## Introduction

On October 12, 1984, President Ronald Reagan signed a continuing appropriations resolution that carried, buried deep inside its second title, the most ambitious restructuring of federal punishment in the twentieth century. The Sentencing Reform Act of 1984 abolished federal parole, created the United States Sentencing Commission, ordered the Commission to write binding guidelines that would squeeze judicial discretion into a grid of months, and replaced early release with supervised release, a new form of court-imposed control that began only after the prison term ended. It became law without a separate floor vote in either chamber, riding a must-pass spending bill less than four weeks before a presidential election.

![The Sentencing Reform Act of 1984: the Commission, the guidelines grid, and the end of parole - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-49.webp)

The measure's parentage was its most striking feature. Its chief architect was Edward Kennedy, the liberal senator who saw unguided judicial discretion as a vehicle for racial and economic discrimination. Its indispensable champion was Strom Thurmond, the conservative senator who saw the same discretion as a vehicle for leniency. The two men agreed on the mechanism while disagreeing on the goal: Kennedy wanted uniformity to pull harsh outliers inward, Thurmond wanted uniformity to pull lenient outliers upward. Between them they built majorities neither could have assembled alone, and the machinery they built delivered the uniformity both demanded at a severity only one side had sought.

The Act attacked the indeterminate sentencing system on every front. Judges had imposed broad brackets and the Parole Commission had chosen the actual release date, so the sentence announced in court bore little relation to the time served. The new regime fixed the sentence, capped good conduct credit at a modest level, and subjected every federal sentence to ranges written by the Commission, binding on judges and reviewable on appeal. For roughly two decades the guidelines ruled federal courtrooms, until the Supreme Court's decisions in Mistretta, Apprendi, Blakely, and Booker tested the structure and converted the mandate into advice. Meanwhile mandatory minimums enacted alongside the guidelines set statutory floors the Commission could not lower, and the federal prison population grew more than eightfold across three decades.

This guide traces the full arc: the unlikely coalition and the appropriations vehicle that carried the bill, the machinery of the Commission and the sentencing table, the abolition of parole and the invention of supervised release, the constitutional challenge that made the guidelines advisory, and the evidence on severity, disparity, and the sources of each. What emerges is a case study in how a coalition that agrees on a mechanism while disagreeing on a goal produces, in the end, the goal of whoever controls the mechanism afterward.

## The unlikely coalition and the vehicle

The Sentencing Reform Act of 1984 reached the statute books through one of the most improbable legislative partnerships of the modern era. Its chief architect was Edward Kennedy, the liberal Democratic senator from Massachusetts, who treated unguided judicial discretion as an engine of racial and economic discrimination. Its indispensable parliamentary champion was Strom Thurmond, the conservative Republican senator from South Carolina, who treated the same unguided discretion as an engine of leniency. The two men agreed on almost nothing about criminal justice except the diagnosis that federal sentencing was lawless and the prescription that a commission should write binding rules to tame it. Between them they assembled majorities that neither could have built alone, and the machinery they built delivered the uniformity both demanded at a severity that only one side had originally sought.

The idea that eventually became the Act did not begin in a committee room. It began in 1972 with Criminal Sentences: Law Without Order, a book by Marvin Frankel, a federal district judge in the Southern District of New York, who argued that the nearly unchecked power judges exercised at sentencing was intolerable in a society professing devotion to the rule of law. Frankel proposed an administrative body, a commission on sentencing, charged with enacting binding guides for sentencing courts. His proposal gave reformers a concrete institutional design, and it found its legislative patron in Kennedy, who in 1975 introduced S. 2699 in the 94th Congress, a bill calling for the abolition of parole and the creation of a federal sentencing commission to produce guidelines. That bill went nowhere, but it established the template that would govern every subsequent effort: determinate sentences, no parole, written ranges, and appellate review of the result.

### Opposite grievances, one remedy

What made the template politically viable was that it promised to cure two different diseases with a single instrument. For liberals, the disease was disparity. By the mid-1970s, a growing body of sentencing research documented that defendants convicted of similar offenses received wildly different punishments depending on the judge they drew, the district in which they were charged, and the race and sex of the defendant. Congress would later cite studies finding racial, gender, inter-judge, and inter-district disparities as the factual foundation for reform. Liberal reformers argued that indeterminate sentencing had failed as rehabilitation, created anxiety and uncertainty among prisoners, and was fundamentally at odds with ideals of equality and the rule of law. To them, discretion was the problem because it was unguided, and unguided power reproduced the biases of the society that wielded it.

For conservatives, the disease was leniency. Critics on the political right expressed dissatisfaction with the perceived softness of sentencing judges and parole officials. Under the indeterminate regime, a judge imposed a nominal sentence and the parole authorities decided how much of it the offender would actually serve, with many defendants serving only about one-third of the term announced in open court. The public heard a sentence of ten years and the offender served three, a practice that reformers on the right called dishonest and that made every announced sentence a fiction. To conservatives, discretion was the problem because it was merciful, and merciful power released dangerous offenders early.

Both camps therefore converged on the same remedy for opposite reasons. Liberals wanted rules to pull the outliers inward and end discriminatory harshness. Conservatives wanted rules to push the outliers inward and end indulgent softness. The guidelines promised both: a grid of offense levels and criminal history categories that would narrow the range any judge could impose, a requirement that the judge sentence within the range or state reasons for departure, and appellate review to police the boundaries. Abolishing parole served both camps at once. For liberals, it eliminated a second, unreviewable layer of discretion that operated behind closed doors. For conservatives, it guaranteed that the sentence imposed would be the sentence served. The convergence was genuine, and it was also tactical. Kennedy needed conservative votes to move a bill that the law enforcement establishment might otherwise have treated as a liberal experiment in leniency. Thurmond needed liberal votes to move a bill that civil rights organizations might otherwise have treated as a device for locking up more defendants for longer. Each man lent the other his credibility.

### The late-seventies high-water mark

Kennedy first tried to carry sentencing reform inside a comprehensive rewrite of the federal criminal code. S. 1437, the Criminal Code Reform Act, introduced on May 2, 1977, gathered cosponsors that advertised the coalition Kennedy was building: Kennedy himself, Hubert Humphrey of Minnesota, Orrin Hatch of Utah, and Thurmond of South Carolina. The Judiciary Committee reported the bill on November 15, 1977, and the Senate debated it across nine days in January 1978 before passing it on January 30 by a vote of 72 to 15. The margin suggested a durable consensus. It was an illusion.

The bill died in the House, which never acted on it before the 95th Congress adjourned. The failure exposed the structural weakness of the reformers' position. A full recodification of Title 18 touched every interest group with a stake in federal criminal law, and the House Judiciary Committee had neither the appetite nor the calendar to process it. The sentencing provisions, which had enjoyed genuine bipartisan support in the Senate, were dragged down by the weight of the code rewrite that carried them. Reformers drew the lesson that would shape the next decade: sentencing reform would have to travel either alone or inside a vehicle too urgent for the House to ignore.

The 1978 vote nevertheless mattered because it put the Senate on record. Seventy-two senators had voted for a bill that abolished parole and created a sentencing commission, and that vote became the baseline from which later majorities were rebuilt. When Kennedy reintroduced his proposals in the Congresses that followed, he no longer had to persuade his colleagues that the idea was respectable. He had to find a legislative train moving fast enough to carry it.

### The apprenticeship years

The six years between the 1978 Senate vote and the 1984 enactment were not fallow. Kennedy kept the proposal alive through successive Congresses, and each iteration refined the design while shifting the coalition's center of gravity. In the 96th Congress, the sentencing provisions traveled once more inside a code rewrite, S. 1722, the Criminal Code Reform Act of 1979. Its Part III set out a new sentencing structure complete with a United States Sentencing Commission, guideline ranges for categories of offenses and defendants, departure only for circumstances the Commission had not adequately considered, stated reasons for sentences imposed outside the range, and the need to avoid unwarranted disparity as an explicit sentencing factor. The Judiciary Committee reported the bill on January 17, 1980, but the full Senate never voted on it, and the code-rewrite strategy died a second death. The lesson of 1978 was reinforced with interest: the sentencing provisions would have to stand on their own.

They did. In the 97th Congress, the proposal appeared as a freestanding measure, S. 1555, the Criminal Sentencing Reform Act of 1981, introduced on July 31, 1981. The bill created the Commission as an independent body in the judicial branch, directed it to promulgate guideline ranges and policy statements, required judges to sentence within the range absent unconsidered aggravating or mitigating circumstances, and required stated reasons for departures. It also directed the Commission to assure substantial terms of imprisonment for specified categories of offenders: those with two or more prior felony convictions, those whose offenses formed part of a pattern of criminal conduct yielding substantial income, managerial participants in racketeering conspiracies, and those who committed crimes of violence while on release. The directive revealed how far the project had traveled from its origins. The bill still spoke the liberal language of avoiding unwarranted disparity, but it carried explicit instructions to imprison the habitual, the organized, and the violent for substantial terms. The severity ratchet was being written into the blueprints before the building existed. S. 1555 did not become law, but it completed the transformation of the proposal from a liberal reform project into a bipartisan crime-control measure, and it established the freestanding-bill form that would carry the provisions to enactment.

Kennedy and Thurmond divided the labor of the coalition along the lines of their strengths. Kennedy supplied the intellectual architecture, dating to his 1975 bill, and the liberal legitimacy that kept civil liberties organizations from mobilizing against the measure. Thurmond supplied the conservative legitimacy that kept law enforcement organizations from denouncing it as soft, and in the 98th Congress he supplied the Senate vehicle itself. S. 1762, the Comprehensive Crime Control Act of 1984, which Thurmond sponsored and introduced in August 1983, carried the sentencing provisions as its Title II under the short title Sentencing Reform Act of 1983. By the time the 98th Congress convened, the sentencing provisions no longer needed Kennedy to carry them alone. They had become one component of the crime package that the Reagan administration wanted and that the Judiciary Committee's Republican majority was prepared to move.

### Rebuilding in the Reagan years

The early 1980s changed the political weather in ways that favored the reformers. The Reagan administration arrived committed to a tougher federal posture on crime, and Thurmond, as chairman of the Senate Judiciary Committee, became the administration's principal ally on criminal legislation. The sentencing reformers split their proposal from the doomed code-rewrite project and advanced it as a freestanding measure. In the 98th Congress, the sentencing provisions appeared as Title II of S. 829, Thurmond's Comprehensive Crime Control Act of 1983, bearing the short title Sentencing Reform Act of 1983. A companion freestanding bill, S. 668, titled the Sentencing Reform Act of 1984, passed the Senate on February 2, 1984, by a vote of 85 to 3, an even wider margin than the 1978 vote and a sign that the coalition had survived the change of administrations and deepened.

The House, which had killed the 1978 effort by inaction, was slower but not immobile. The House Judiciary Subcommittee on Criminal Justice held hearings on the sentencing title across May 1984, and Representative Hamilton Fish IV of New York carried the House version of the broader crime package. On June 4, 1984, H.R. 5773, the House's Sentencing Reform Act of 1984, was introduced and referred to the Judiciary Committee. The two chambers were working from substantially similar texts, and the remaining question was not whether a crime bill could pass but whether the House would pass one before the election-year clock ran out.

That clock was the reformers' greatest asset. By the autumn of 1984, a large package of crime legislation, including the sentencing provisions, bail reform, forfeiture expansion, and insanity defense reform, had cleared committee work in both chambers but had not reached either floor as a unified bill. With adjournment approaching and members eager to campaign on crime, the package needed a vehicle that could not be delayed, amended to death, or quietly shelved. The reformers found it in the appropriations process.

### A vehicle of opportunity

On September 17, 1984, Representative Jamie Whitten of Mississippi, chairman of the House Appropriations Committee, introduced H.J. Res. 648, a joint resolution making continuing appropriations for fiscal year 1985. A continuing resolution is must-pass legislation. Without it, the government would shut down at the start of the fiscal year, and no member wanted to explain a shutdown to voters weeks before an election. Into this unkillable vehicle the House folded the entire crime package. The tactic of carrying substantive policy on must-pass spending legislation is examined in [omnibus-bills-and-riders](/2022/04/15/omnibus-bills-and-riders/). On September 25, 1984, the House passed H.J. Res. 648 by a vote of 316 to 91, with an amendment incorporating the language of H.R. 5963, a crime bill identical to H.R. 6031 that the Judiciary Subcommittee on Crime had approved in July. The Senate took up the resolution and passed it on October 4 by voice vote. A conference committee reconciled the differences and reported on October 10. The House agreed to the conference report that same day by a vote of 252 to 60, and the Senate agreed on October 11 by a vote of 78 to 11. President Ronald Reagan signed the resolution on October 12, 1984, and it became Public Law 98-473.

The crime provisions rode inside the resolution as Title II, styled the Comprehensive Crime Control Act of 1984, and the sentencing provisions rode inside that title as its own chapter, the Sentencing Reform Act of 1984. The structure reflected the bill's legislative genealogy. The Senate's original vehicle had been S. 1762, Thurmond's Comprehensive Crime Control Act of 1984, introduced in August 1983, and the House's vehicle had been the H.R. 5963 and H.R. 6031 pair. When the conference committee assembled the final text, it preserved the architecture of those bills inside the appropriations resolution, so that a sentencing reform first imagined by a liberal judge and first introduced by a liberal senator became law as one chapter of a conservative crime package enacted through a spending bill. The procedure drew criticism as legislating by ambush, and the criticism had force. A measure that restructured the entire federal sentencing system received no separate floor vote in either chamber in its final form. It passed because the continuing resolution had to pass, and members who might have balked at individual provisions were unwilling to vote against funding the government to register their objections.

The timing was not incidental. Reagan signed the resolution on October 12, 1984, less than four weeks before the presidential election of November 6, with members of both chambers about to return to their districts to campaign. A comprehensive crime bill was valuable currency in an election year, and the continuing resolution offered a way to mint it without the delays and amendment fights of separate floor consideration. The procedure also insulated the package from the ordinary scrutiny that a freestanding bill of comparable scope would have attracted. A measure that restructured the entire federal sentencing system became law without ever receiving a separate vote in either chamber in its final form, carried past every procedural checkpoint by the one consideration no member could vote against.

The vehicle also shaped the substance. A freestanding sentencing bill would have invited amendments from every faction with a view on punishment, and the delicate Kennedy-Thurmond compromise might not have survived open amendment. Inside the crime package, the sentencing provisions were protected by the package's political logic. Opponents of the guidelines could not strip them without unraveling the coalition behind bail reform, forfeiture, and the other titles, and supporters of the package as a whole had no incentive to reopen a bargain that had taken seven years to assemble. The appropriations vehicle thus did double duty. It guaranteed passage, and it froze the compromise in place.

### The machinery ran upward

The compromise that the vehicle protected contained a quiet asymmetry, and the asymmetry determined the character of the system the Act created. Liberals had joined the coalition to reduce disparity, expecting that uniformity would mean sentences converging toward a reasoned middle. Conservatives had joined to increase severity, expecting that uniformity would mean the lenient outliers being pulled up to the norm. The enacted machinery satisfied the second expectation more fully than the first. The guidelines that the new United States Sentencing Commission wrote were anchored in an analysis of past sentencing practice, but the statute layered directives, mandatory minimums, and congressional amendment instructions that pushed the levels upward, and the abolition of parole meant that the longer nominal sentences were actually served rather than discounted by early release. Uniformity was achieved, but it was achieved at the high end of historical practice rather than at its center.

This outcome was not an accident of drafting. It was the price of the coalition. Kennedy could not have passed the bill without Thurmond, and Thurmond's support was conditioned on a system that punished more severely, not merely more evenly. The statute's purposes of sentencing, codified at 18 U.S.C. 3553(a)(2), dropped rehabilitation from the recognized goals of punishment, a change that removed the principal philosophical justification for leniency and individualized treatment. The requirement that the Commission reflect the seriousness of the offense, promote respect for the law, and provide just punishment gave the Commission mandates that pointed upward far more reliably than they pointed toward mercy. Kate Stith and Steve Y. Koh, in their legislative history of the guidelines, traced how the political dynamics of the enactment process converted a reform conceived as a check on disparity into an engine of severity, as each successive legislative intervention raised the floor a little higher.

The severity ratchet was reinforced by parallel legislation that Congress enacted alongside and after the guidelines. In the same year it passed the Sentencing Reform Act, Congress established mandatory minimum penalties for certain drug and gun offenses, and the 1986 Anti-Drug Abuse Act added mandatory five- and ten-year prison terms tied to precise drug quantities. These statutes operated alongside the guidelines, setting floors beneath which the Commission's ranges could not fall and ensuring that the convergence the reformers had promised would settle at higher levels than past practice. The Commission, instructed to reflect the seriousness Congress attached to offenses, received its clearest signals about seriousness from statutes that kept equating it with severity. The guidelines thus entered a statutory environment in which every new enactment pushed the same direction, and the machinery that liberals had designed to cure discrimination became the transmission belt for a sentencing severity that neither camp had fully anticipated.

The irony was complete. A reform movement launched by a judge who called sentencing lawless and championed by a senator who wanted to end discrimination produced a system that sentenced more uniformly and more harshly than the regime it replaced. The liberals got the rules they wanted, and the rules delivered the severity the conservatives wanted. Both sides could claim victory, and for several years both did, until the consequences of the severity the machinery produced began to generate a new generation of critics who had not been present at its creation.

### The review window

One feature of the 1984 enactment is easily overlooked and helps explain the system's later trajectory. Congress did not put the Sentencing Reform Act into immediate effect. Section 235 of Public Law 98-473 held the Act's operative provisions in abeyance pending the drafting of the guidelines themselves. The Commission was given until April 13, 1987, to transmit its initial guidelines to Congress, and the statute provided for six months of congressional review, after which the guidelines would take effect automatically unless Congress enacted legislation to the contrary. The Commission submitted its initial guidelines on April 13, 1987. Congress held hearings, debated the product, and declined to disapprove it. The guidelines therefore took effect by operation of law on November 1, 1987, applying to all offenses committed on or after that date.

The delayed effective date served the coalition's purposes in 1984. Senators who were uneasy about voting for a sentencing system whose content did not yet exist could reassure themselves and their constituents that Congress would have a final look before the rules bound anyone. The six-month review window was supposed to be the moment of democratic accountability, the point at which the elected branches would examine what the unelected Commission had produced. In practice, the window functioned as a ratchet. Disapproving the guidelines would have required affirmative legislation, which would have required the President's signature or a veto-proof majority, and no coalition existed to do the work of writing an alternative. The guidelines that the Commission produced, with their severity anchored in the political bargains of the mid-1980s, slid into effect through congressional inaction, the same inaction that had killed the 1978 bill, working in the reform's favor. The system that Kennedy and Thurmond had built together began binding federal judges on the first day of November 1987, and the unlikely coalition that had created it dissolved into the ordinary partisan divisions it had briefly transcended.

## The machinery: the commission and the guidelines

The Sentencing Reform Act of 1984 built two things at once: an institution to write the rules and a mechanism for applying them. The institution was the United States Sentencing Commission, a new body charged with converting congressional intent into numerical form. The mechanism was the guidelines themselves, a grid that would translate the facts of each case into a range of months. Everything about the design reflected the central complaint the Act was meant to answer, namely that federal sentencing had become a lottery in which the judge drawn mattered more than the crime committed. The Commission would supply the uniformity. The grid would supply the transparency. A narrow escape hatch for atypical cases would supply the flexibility that made the rest tolerable.

### An agency lodged in the judicial branch

Congress created the Commission at 28 U.S.C. 991(a) as an independent commission in the judicial branch of the United States. That placement was deliberate. The Act removed sentencing policy from the diffuse hands of hundreds of district judges, but it did not hand the power to the executive branch either. An agency lodged within the judiciary, staffed in part by judges, suggested that sentencing remained at bottom a judicial function even as it became a regulated one.

The statute fixed the Commission at seven voting members and one nonvoting member. The President appoints the voting members by and with the advice and consent of the Senate, but only after consultation with a roster of interested parties that the statute names: representatives of judges, prosecuting attorneys, defense attorneys, law enforcement officials, senior citizens, victims of crime, and others interested in the criminal justice process. One member serves as Chair, appointed with Senate confirmation, and three serve as Vice Chairs designated by the President. At least three of the seven must be federal judges, selected after the President considers a list of six judges recommended by the Judicial Conference of the United States. No more than four members may belong to the same political party, and of the three Vice Chairs no more than two may share a party. The Attorney General, or the Attorney General's designee, sits as an ex officio, nonvoting member, giving the executive a voice without a vote. Members serve with an unusual degree of insulation: the President may remove them only for neglect of duty or malfeasance in office or for other good cause shown.

The purposes Congress assigned the Commission, set out at 28 U.S.C. 991(b), framed the entire enterprise. The Commission was to establish sentencing policies and practices for the federal criminal justice system that would assure the meeting of the purposes of sentencing laid out in 18 U.S.C. 3553(a)(2), provide certainty and fairness in meeting those purposes, avoid unwarranted sentencing disparities among defendants with similar records convicted of similar conduct, and preserve enough flexibility to permit individualized sentences where mitigating or aggravating factors warranted something different. The tension between those last two clauses, between avoiding disparity and permitting individualization, became the defining problem of everything the Commission produced.

### The sentencing table

The guidelines converted offense and offender characteristics into numbers, and the numbers into months. The final product was the sentencing table, a grid with forty three offense levels forming the vertical axis and six criminal history categories forming the horizontal axis, for two hundred fifty eight cells in all. At the intersection of a defendant's offense level and criminal history category sat a range expressed in months. A defendant at offense level twenty four with a criminal history category of one faced a range of fifty one to sixty three months. The same offense level paired with category six produced one hundred to one hundred twenty five months. The same conduct, read against a different record, nearly doubled the exposure.

The offense level came first. Each offense guideline assigned a base offense level, and specific offense characteristics then moved the number up or down to reflect the harm or culpability involved in the particular crime. A fraud guideline, for example, raised the level as the dollar loss grew. A drug guideline raised it as the quantity grew. Chapter Three of the manual then applied adjustments that cut across offenses: increases for an aggravating role in the offense or for obstructing the administration of justice, decreases for acceptance of responsibility. The result was a total offense level that measured the seriousness of the conduct as the guidelines defined it.

The criminal history category measured the defendant's record. Prior sentences earned points, with longer sentences earning more, and the total placed the defendant in one of six categories, from category one for little or no record to category six for an extensive one. Additional points applied when the defendant committed the instant offense while under another criminal justice sentence, such as probation, parole, or supervised release. The premise was transparent and conventional: a defendant who kept returning to crime deserved a longer sentence, both because earlier punishments had failed to deter and because the public needed protection from further offenses.

The ranges in the cells were deliberately narrow. At 28 U.S.C. 994(b)(2) Congress required that the maximum of any range not exceed the minimum by more than the greater of twenty five percent or six months, with an exception allowing a life maximum where the minimum reached thirty years. That narrowness was the point. If the range for a given offense level and history category spanned only a quarter of the minimum, judges could not produce the wide disparities of the preguidelines era even when they disagreed about the right sentence within the range. The table was also divided into zones that governed what kinds of sentences were available. At the low end, in zones where the minimum was zero or a few months, probation or alternatives to incarceration remained possible. At the high end, in the zone covering the great majority of cells, the sentence had to be served as imprisonment. The design thus encoded not only duration but also the threshold choice between liberty and confinement.

### The statutory purposes of sentencing

The grid was a means, not an end. The ends were codified at 18 U.S.C. 3553(a)(2), which Congress wrote into the Act as the definitive statement of why federal courts punish. The court was required to impose a sentence sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with four purposes. The first, sometimes called retribution, required the sentence to reflect the seriousness of the offense, to promote respect for the law, and to provide just punishment. The second required adequate deterrence of criminal conduct, both for the defendant being sentenced and for others who might consider similar conduct. The third, incapacitation, required the protection of the public from further crimes of the defendant. The fourth, rehabilitation, required the provision of needed educational or vocational training, medical care, or other correctional treatment in the most effective manner. That opening formulation, sufficient but not greater than necessary, imposed what came to be called the parsimony principle: the sentence should do the work the purposes demanded and no more.

Section 3553(a) then listed the factors the court had to consider in choosing the particular sentence. First came the nature and circumstances of the offense and the history and characteristics of the defendant. Second came the four purposes themselves. Third came the kinds of sentences available. Fourth came the kinds of sentence and the sentencing range established by the guidelines for the applicable category of offense and the applicable category of defendant. Fifth came any pertinent policy statement issued by the Commission. Sixth came the need to avoid unwarranted sentence disparities among defendants with similar records found guilty of similar conduct. Seventh came the need to provide restitution to any victims of the offense. The guidelines range was thus one factor among seven, but under the original scheme it occupied a commanding position, because a separate provision made it binding.

The Commission's introduction to the first manual was candid about a difficulty the empirical method had been enlisted to solve alongside the practical one. Commentators had pressed the Commission to choose among competing philosophies of punishment, to declare whether retribution or the forward looking aims of deterrence, incapacitation, and rehabilitation would govern. The Commission declined the invitation, observing that a definitive choice would have been profoundly difficult and would have diminished the acceptance the guidelines needed to function. As a practical matter, the Commission argued, the competing philosophies usually pointed to the same result in concrete cases, and the empirical approach allowed the Commission to proceed without resolving the theoretical dispute. The guidelines thus embodied all four purposes at once, a compromise that made the system workable while leaving the deeper disagreement intact for judges to navigate case by case within the narrow bands the table allowed.

### Congressional direction to the Commission

The Act did not simply create the Commission and leave it to its own devices. At 28 U.S.C. 994 Congress wrote a long set of duties and directives that told the Commission how to build the guidelines. The Commission was to promulgate guidelines for use in determining whether to impose probation, a fine, or imprisonment. In establishing sentencing ranges, it was to set them consistently with all pertinent provisions of title 18, the criminal code the ranges would operate within, and to observe the narrow-range rule already described. Congress specified the categories of considerations the Commission had to weigh. For offenses, it listed matters such as the grade of the offense, the circumstances under which it was committed, the nature and degree of the harm caused, and the community view of the gravity of the offense. For defendants, it listed age, education, vocational skills, mental and emotional condition, physical condition including drug dependence, previous employment record, family ties and responsibilities, community ties, role in the offense, criminal history, and degree of dependence upon criminal activity for a livelihood.

Congress also drew boundaries around what the Commission could consider. The guidelines and policy statements were to be entirely neutral as to race, sex, national origin, creed, and socioeconomic status. They were to reflect the general inappropriateness of considering a defendant's education, vocational skills, employment record, family ties and responsibilities, and community ties in recommending imprisonment or its length. They were to reflect the general appropriateness of a sentence other than imprisonment for a first offender convicted of a crime that was not violent and not otherwise serious. The Commission was to assure that recidivist patterns, the need for crime control, incapacitation, deterrence, and rehabilitation all found expression in the rules it wrote.

Some directives were more pointed. At 28 U.S.C. 994(h) Congress ordered the Commission to ensure that the guidelines specified a sentence to a term of imprisonment at or near the maximum term authorized for certain career offenders: defendants at least eighteen years old whose instant offense was a felony crime of violence or a controlled substance offense and who had two prior felony convictions for such offenses. The Commission implemented that command at section 4B1.1 of the guidelines manual, which recalculated both the offense level and the criminal history category for qualifying defendants, often producing a range dramatically higher than the ordinary calculation would have yielded. The legislative history showed that Congress had considered writing the career offender rule directly into statute as a mandatory minimum and had chosen instead to direct the Commission, on the theory that the guidelines process could implement the policy more consistently and rationally. The choice illustrated the relationship Congress envisioned: detailed instruction flowing downward, expert implementation returning upward.

### The empirical method of the first Commission

Faced with the philosophical question of what sentences ought to be, the original Commission, chaired by Judge William Wilkins with then Judge Stephen Breyer among its members, chose an empirical method. Rather than deriving sentences from theories of punishment, the Commission studied what federal judges had actually been doing. Its analysts drew on ten thousand presentence investigations, on the distinctions drawn in substantive criminal statutes, on the United States Parole Commission's guidelines and the statistics those guidelines generated, and on other relevant sources, in order to determine which distinctions mattered in existing practice. The Commission then accepted, modified, or rationalized the more important of those distinctions. Its Supplementary Report on the Initial Sentencing Guidelines, issued in 1987, set out the comparison between estimates of preguidelines practice and the sentences the guidelines would produce.

The Commission was explicit that it did not consider itself bound by past practice. It did not simply codify the averages. It departed from the data at various points for various reasons: to rationalize distinctions that were defensible, to adjust for the abolition of parole, which had previously allowed early release that the new determinate sentences would not, and to conform the guidelines to congressional judgments that had intervened since the data were collected. But past practice supplied the starting point and the vocabulary. Guideline sentences in many instances approximated the average preguidelines sentence, and adherence to the guidelines would eliminate the wide disparity that had been the Act's target. Where a high percentage of defendants had received probation under prior practice, the Commission built specific offense characteristics to separate those who had received probation from those who had received prison. In some instances the Commission substituted short, certain terms of incarceration for a prior practice in which similar offenders had received anything from probation to several years, on the theory that regularity itself was a value.

The method had a consequence Congress had not fully anticipated. By grounding the guidelines in past practice, the Commission produced a system that was conservative in the literal sense: it preserved much of the severity structure of the era it measured, then Congress kept adding to it.

The empirical project also faced a complication that the averages alone could not solve. The Act abolished parole for federal offenders and replaced it with determinate sentences subject to limited good time credit, which meant that the time defendants had actually served under the old regime was systematically shorter than the sentences judges had imposed. A preguidelines judge might have imposed ten years knowing the Parole Commission would likely release the defendant well before the term expired. The Commission therefore had to translate its estimates of past practice into a world where the sentence imposed and the sentence served were nearly the same thing, adjusting its starting points upward to account for the disappearance of the parole discount. The result was a subtle ratcheting effect built into the methodology itself: guidelines derived from sentences imposed in an era of parole, recalibrated for an era without it, produced ranges that exceeded the time most offenders had actually spent in prison under the system the data described.

### Congressional overrides

The Act gave Congress two ways to override the Commission, and Congress used both. The first was direct legislation altering the sentencing landscape, which the Commission then had to absorb into the guidelines. The Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986 established mandatory minimum penalties for drug offenses and a one hundred to one ratio between the quantities of powder cocaine and crack cocaine needed to trigger those minimums. The Commission calibrated the drug quantity table in the guidelines to correspond with that statutory ratio, embedding a congressional policy judgment at the heart of the most frequently applied guideline in the federal system.

The second was the formal disapproval of proposed amendments. Under the amendment process at 28 U.S.C. 994(p), the Commission submitted proposed guideline amendments to Congress, which took effect unless Congress acted to block them. In 1995 the Commission voted to equalize the treatment of crack and powder cocaine in the drug guidelines, recommending a one to one ratio on the ground that the existing disparity overstated the difference in harm and created a perception of unfairness, particularly given the racial composition of crack defendants. Congress rejected the proposal, the first and only time it formally disapproved a set of proposed amendments, and in the disapproval legislation directed the Commission to ensure that crack sentences generally exceeded powder sentences for like quantities. The episode demonstrated where ultimate authority lay. The Commission could study, recommend, and reason from data, but Congress could simply say no.

A third form of override arrived with the PROTECT Act of 2003. Concerned that downward departures were eroding the guidelines' uniformity, Congress bypassed the Commission's amendment process and wrote departure policy directly into law. The Act expanded de novo appellate review to cover all departures and directed the Commission to reduce the frequency of downward departures, which the Commission did by amending its policy statements to prohibit or restrict departure on grounds such as plea agreements, acceptance of responsibility, minor role, gambling addiction, and family ties, among others. Congress had thus authored portions of the guidelines manual itself. The pattern across two decades was consistent: congressional directives to the Commission moved in one direction, toward higher sentences and tighter constraints, a one way ratchet that the Commission, for all its expertise, could not reverse.

### The departure valve under the original 18 U.S.C. 3553(b)

Against that tightly calibrated structure, the original 18 U.S.C. 3553(b) supplied the only lawful way out. Its command was mandatory: the court shall impose a sentence of the kind, and within the range, referred to in subsection (a)(4), which pointed to the guideline range. The exception was narrow. A judge could depart only upon finding that there existed an aggravating or mitigating circumstance of a kind, or to a degree, not adequately taken into consideration by the Sentencing Commission in formulating the guidelines, and that the circumstance should result in a sentence different from the one the range described. The statute further confined the inquiry: in deciding whether the Commission had adequately considered a circumstance, the court could look only to the sentencing guidelines, the Commission's policy statements, and its official commentary. Nothing else counted.

The Commission's introduction to the guidelines manual explained the theory behind the provision through the concept of the heartland. Each guideline was understood to carve out a heartland of typical cases embodying the conduct the guideline described. The departure power existed for the atypical case, the one to which a guideline linguistically applied but whose conduct differed significantly from the norm. The Commission expected courts to follow the guidelines in most cases and to depart only in the rare instance where the defendant's actions fell outside that heartland. Departures grounded in factors the guidelines never mentioned were expected to be highly infrequent.

Courts applying the provision developed a two part inquiry. First, the judge had to identify a particular aggravating or mitigating circumstance that the guidelines had failed to take into account. Second, the judge had to determine that the circumstance warranted a sentence different from the one the guidelines prescribed. The framework received its authoritative form in Koon v. United States, decided in 1996, where the Supreme Court instructed sentencing courts to classify the factor proposed as a basis for departure. If the guidelines forbade consideration of the factor, as they did for race, sex, national origin, creed, religion, and socioeconomic status, departure on that basis was impermissible. If the guidelines encouraged the factor but had not fully accounted for it, departure was authorized to the extent the guideline left room. If the factor was discouraged, departure required that it be present to an exceptional degree that made the case different from the ordinary case where the factor appeared. If the factor went unmentioned, the court had to examine the structure and theory of the relevant guidelines and of the guidelines as a whole to decide whether the case had left the heartland.

The guidelines manual supplemented the statute with its own departure provisions. Section 5K2.0 restated the statutory standard as a policy statement governing departures generally. Section 5K1.1 authorized departure upon the government's motion when the defendant provided substantial assistance in the investigation or prosecution of another person, creating the cooperation departure that became one of the most consequential features of federal practice. The original scheme also built in accountability for the use of the power. Section 3553(c) required the judge to state in open court the specific reasons for imposing a sentence outside the range, and section 3742 gave the defendant the right to appeal an upward departure and the government the right to appeal a downward one, with appellate courts reviewing for reasonableness. The machinery was thus complete: a Commission to write the rules, a table to apply them, a statute to make them binding, and a narrowly guarded door for the case the rules did not fit.

## Parole abolished, truth in sentencing, and supervised release

### The indeterminate system and the end of parole

For most of the twentieth century, federal sentencing operated on an indeterminate model. A judge, working within a broad statutory range, imposed not a fixed term but a bracket, for example five to twenty-five years. Within that bracket, the actual time the defendant would spend behind bars remained unsettled at the moment of sentencing. The judge determined the parole eligibility date and the outer boundary of confinement, subject to good time deductions, and the United States Parole Commission decided the real release date. Release before the maximum depended on the Commission's judgment, guided by its own decision-making guidelines, that the prisoner was rehabilitated or no longer posed a danger. Punishment was thus split between two institutions: the court announced a range, and an executive-branch body chose the moment of freedom.

The model rested on a rehabilitative theory of punishment. An open-ended sentence functioned as an individualized treatment plan, adjustable to the speed of the offender's reform. The promise of release at the early end of the range gave inmates an incentive to behave and to participate in programming. But the discretion that made the system flexible also made it opaque. Two defendants convicted of similar conduct could serve sharply different terms, because release turned on the Commission's discretionary assessment rather than on anything fixed at sentencing. The sentencing judge never knew with confidence how long the sentence imposed would last, and neither did the defendant. Critics charged that the system produced unwarranted disparity and that parole decisions, made inside institutions by an agency applying guidelines of its own devising, lacked the procedural safeguards of a courtroom. The judge's sentence was a starting point for negotiation among institutions, not a measure of time to be served.

The Sentencing Reform Act ended this arrangement for new offenses. Persons convicted of federal offenses committed on or after November 1, 1987 serve determinate terms under the sentencing guidelines and are not eligible for parole consideration. The date was not the date of the Act's enactment in 1984. Congress built in a transition period, and the guidelines took effect on November 1, 1987, so abolition runs with the offense date rather than the sentencing date. A sentence imposed after the Act for a pre-November 1987 offense remains an old-law sentence with parole available. The distinction matters because the ex post facto clause forbids applying a harsher post-1984 regime to conduct that predates it, which is why the line had to be drawn by offense and why the old system could not simply be switched off.

The Parole Commission itself was slated for extinction. The Act provided for its elimination on November 1, 1992, five years after the guidelines took effect. That phaseout schedule proved unrealistic. Thousands of prisoners sentenced before November 1, 1987 had not completed their sentences by 1992, and constitutional doctrine barred the government from stripping them of parole consideration. Had the Commission been allowed to terminate as scheduled, old-law prisoners could have claimed that their sentences were being unconstitutionally lengthened by a law enacted after their offenses, and some would have sought immediate release, a prospect members of Congress described as a danger to public safety. Congress responded with the Judicial Improvements Act of 1990, which extended the Commission's life to November 1, 1997. When that date approached with roughly 6,700 parole-eligible old-law defendants still in the federal system, Congress passed the Parole Commission Phaseout Act of 1996, carrying the authorization to November 1, 2002 while reducing the Commission's size to match its shrinking workload. When 2002 arrived with old-law offenders still in custody, Congress extended the Commission again, for three years at a time in 2008 and 2011 and through subsequent reauthorizations, with a 2020 extension setting expiration at November 1, 2022. The institution Congress meant to sunset became a permanent caretaker of a dwindling caseload.

The Commission's residual jurisdiction tells the story of the transition. It retained authority over federal offenders sentenced before November 1, 1987, and Congress later added jurisdiction over offenders convicted under District of Columbia law as well as District offenders on supervised release, after the D.C. Revitalization Act of 1997 transferred District corrections functions to the federal government. Each extension debate rehearsed the same logic: the old-law population was finite and shrinking, but no other federal agency could constitutionally assume the parole function, so the Commission had to survive until the last old-law offender finished. The long tail of pre-1987 sentences turned a five-year phaseout into a multi-decade wind-down, and the majority of the Commission's later workload concerned District of Columbia offenders rather than the federal old-law population it was created to shepherd out of existence.

The mechanics of the phaseout explain why Congress could never simply let the authorization lapse. The Act required that old-law inmates be given release dates three to six months before the Commission's scheduled expiration, a provision meant to force an orderly wind-down. In practice it functioned as a tripwire: every time the expiration date approached with old-law prisoners still in custody, the Department of Justice warned that the mandate would compel the setting of release dates for prisoners whose parole decisions remained unresolved, and Congress responded with another extension rather than accept the alternative. The provision thus converted the Commission's sunset into a recurring legislative event. What was designed as a five-year transition became a standing item on the Judiciary Committees' calendars, revisited every two to five years as the authorization neared its end.

### Truth in sentencing

With parole gone, the sentence pronounced in court became, in substance, the sentence served. That was the point. Under 18 U.S.C. 3624(a), a prisoner is released by the Bureau of Prisons on the date of expiration of the term of imprisonment, less any credited time. The only statutory reduction available in the new system is good conduct time, capped by 18 U.S.C. 3624(b) at up to 54 days for each year of the sentence imposed, conditioned on the Bureau's determination that the prisoner displayed exemplary compliance with institutional disciplinary regulations. The Bureau may award lesser credit or none at all when compliance falls short, and credit not earned may not later be granted. The statute also directs the Bureau to consider whether the prisoner has earned or is making satisfactory progress toward earning a high school diploma or equivalent. The ceiling is modest: roughly a 15 percent reduction in the best case, a fraction of what parole could once accomplish.

The contrast with the pre-1984 world is stark. Under the old regime, early release flowed through two channels. The Parole Commission could release a prisoner well before the judicial maximum, sometimes at a small fraction of the nominal sentence, based on its assessment of rehabilitation. On top of that, statutory and extra good time provisions shortened sentences further. The sentence imposed was therefore a starting point for negotiation among institutions, not a measure of time to be served. A defendant sentenced to twenty years might serve seven. After 1987, the channels collapsed to one: a fixed term, reduced only by the good time ceiling. The number the judge announced acquired a meaning it had never possessed, namely an approximate calendar duration that the defendant, the victim, and the public could all rely upon.

Even the 54-day figure contains a subtlety worth attention. The statute speaks of 54 days for each year of the sentence imposed, but the Bureau of Prisons has long calculated the credit against time actually served rather than the nominal sentence, a reading the Supreme Court sustained in Barber v. Thomas. The practical consequence is that the effective benefit falls short of 54 days per imposed year. In the Court's illustration, a perfectly behaved prisoner serving a ten-year sentence earns 470 days of credit rather than the 540 days that a straight reading of "per year of sentence imposed" would yield, because no credit accrues for time the prisoner will not actually spend in custody. The reduction is real but bounded, and bounded deliberately: Congress wanted the punishment announced in the courtroom to resemble the punishment inflicted in the prison.

The intellectual case for abolition reached beyond complaints about disparity. The indeterminate system was built on the rehabilitative ideal, the proposition that punishment should be calibrated to reform the offender and that release should follow reform. By the late twentieth century that ideal had lost its hold on penal theory. The Act's supporters argued that the rehabilitative model gave parole authorities a mandate they could not reliably execute, since no one could say with confidence when an offender was "rehabilitated," and that the resulting decisions were inevitably impressionistic. The Act replaced rehabilitation as the organizing purpose of the release decision with the purposes set out in section 3553(a): retribution, deterrence, incapacitation, and rehabilitation as one purpose among several rather than the master principle. Once the release decision was no longer about judging inner transformation, there was little left for a parole board to do that a judge, working within guidelines, could not do at sentencing.

This is the doctrine of truth in sentencing. The label describes a simple proposition, that the punishment announced should resemble the punishment inflicted. Indeterminate sentencing violated the proposition twice over, first by letting the judge announce a range whose lower bound the prisoner might never reach, and second by letting the Commission release the prisoner at a point the judge never specified. The determinate sentence, trimmed only by capped good conduct credit, restored the connection between the courtroom and the cell. Whether the connection produced fairer outcomes was debated then and remains debated, but the structural change is not in doubt: discretion over the release date moved from the parole authority to the sentencing judge, and the judge's number became the operative fact.

### Supervised release

The Act did not simply delete post-release supervision from the system; it replaced parole with a different institution, supervised release, codified at 18 U.S.C. 3583. The court, when imposing a sentence of imprisonment for a felony or misdemeanor, may include as part of the sentence a requirement that the defendant serve a term of supervised release after imprisonment, and must do so when a statute requires it or when the defendant has been convicted for the first time of a domestic violence crime as defined in section 3561(b). The key phrase is "after imprisonment." Supervised release does not begin until the custodial term ends. It is not a continuation of the sentence and it is not parole under another name. As the Sentencing Commission's policy guidance puts it, unlike parole, a term of supervised release does not replace a portion of the sentence of imprisonment; it is an order of supervision in addition to any term of imprisonment imposed by the court.

The differences from parole run deep. Parole shortened the time behind bars: the prisoner served part of the sentence in the community under supervision, and the unserved remainder hung over the parolee as leverage for compliance. Supervised release adds time beyond the prison term. A defendant sentenced to sixty months of imprisonment plus three years of supervised release faces up to eight years under federal control, not five. The institution that decides also differs. Parole decisions belonged to the Parole Commission, an executive agency applying its own guidelines. Supervised release is imposed by the sentencing court, its length and conditions chosen by the judge under the factors set out in section 3553(a), and violations are adjudicated by courts rather than by a parole board. The locus of power shifted from agency to judiciary, consistent with the Act's broader transfer of sentencing authority from discretionary institutions to the courtroom and to the guidelines that constrain it.

Congress set the authorized lengths of supervised release by offense class in 18 U.S.C. 3583(b): not more than five years for a Class A or Class B felony, not more than three years for a Class C or Class D felony, and not more than one year for a Class E felony or for a misdemeanor other than a petty offense. Individual statutes may require or extend supervised release terms for particular offenses, and the court must impose the term when the statute commands it, but the class-based caps supply the default architecture. The structure mirrors the Act's general approach of calibrating post-conviction consequences to the gravity of the offense while leaving the precise term to judicial determination within the statutory ceiling. The court weighs the familiar sentencing factors in choosing the length and in setting conditions, which must be consistent with any pertinent policy statements issued by the Sentencing Commission. The statute requires the court to order, as an explicit condition of every term, that the defendant not commit another federal, state, or local crime during the term, and it authorizes additional standard and special conditions, including drug testing, tailored to the defendant's circumstances and the nature of the offense. The probation officer must provide the defendant with a written statement setting forth all the conditions, stated clearly enough to guide the defendant's conduct. Because supervised release is imposed by the court rather than granted by an agency, its conditions carry the character of a judicial order from the outset, and violating them is a breach of that order rather than a failure to satisfy an agency's release criteria.

Probation survived the reform as a separate alternative, codified at 18 U.S.C. 3561, and the distinction between probation and supervised release illuminates both devices. Probation is imposed instead of imprisonment: the court sentences the defendant to probation for a felony for not less than one nor more than five years, for a misdemeanor not more than five years, and for an infraction not more than one year. The probationer avoids custody entirely unless probation is revoked. Supervised release, by contrast, is imposed in addition to imprisonment and follows it. The two devices occupy opposite ends of the sentencing choice. Probation substitutes supervision for the prison term; supervised release appends supervision to the prison term. Confusing them collapses the very distinction the Act drew between front-end leniency and back-end control, and it mistakes a sentence that avoids prison for a sentence that extends federal authority beyond prison.

### Revocation and why the distinction matters

The teeth of supervised release lie in revocation. Under 18 U.S.C. 3583(e)(3), when the court finds by a preponderance of the evidence that the defendant violated a condition of supervised release, it may revoke the term and require the defendant to serve in prison all or part of the term of supervised release authorized by statute for the offense, without credit for time previously served on postrelease supervision. The statute sets class-based caps on the revocation term: not more than five years if the underlying offense is a Class A felony, not more than three years for a Class B felony, not more than two years for a Class C or D felony, and not more than one year in any other case. The caps turn on the seriousness of the original offense of conviction, not on the seriousness of the violation itself. The court has other options short of revocation as well: it may terminate the term early when the defendant's conduct and the interest of justice warrant it, extend the term if less than the maximum was imposed, or modify, reduce, or enlarge the conditions at any point before the term expires.

Revocation proceedings themselves reflect the Act's judicialization of post-release control. The hearing takes place before the court, not before an agency panel, and the court must find the violation by a preponderance of the evidence. The Sixth Amendment's jury trial, confrontation, and assistance-of-counsel guarantees do not attach to the revocation hearing in the way they attach to a criminal trial, because revocation is treated as an aspect of sentencing rather than as a new prosecution. The statute also creates mandatory revocation triggers: under section 3583(g), the court must revoke when the defendant possesses a controlled substance, possesses a firearm in violation of federal law, or refuses to comply with drug testing. The preponderance standard and the mandatory triggers give the revocation power a different texture from parole revocation, which belonged to the Commission's discretionary judgment. Here the court applies fixed statutory commands, and the defendant's liberty turns on findings made under judicial procedures rather than on an agency's assessment of rehabilitative progress.

The critical point is what revocation adds. A parole violator under the old system could be returned to prison to serve the unserved remainder of the original sentence; the custody was the residue of a term already imposed, and it could not exceed what the sentence still contained. A supervised release violator faces something different: a fresh term of imprisonment, authorized by the statute at the time of the original sentencing, imposed because the defendant breached the conditions of post-release supervision. The statute expressly denies credit for time already spent on supervision, so the revocation term stacks on top of the completed prison term rather than drawing from its remainder. Because the revocation sanction is keyed to the offense class rather than to the unserved balance of the original sentence, the defendant's aggregate time in custody can exceed the statutory maximum for the underlying offense. Courts have sustained this architecture against the objection that it punishes the defendant twice for the same conduct, treating postrevocation penalties as part of the penalty for the original conviction rather than as a new punishment for the violation.

The class-based cap applies to each revocation separately. The statute provides that the defendant may not be required to serve more than the stated maximum "on any such revocation," which means that successive violations can produce successive terms of revocation custody, each within the cap. A defendant who violates supervised release, serves a revocation term, is placed on a further term of supervised release, and violates again may be revoked again, up to the cap each time. Under parole, by contrast, the unserved remainder of the original sentence set an absolute ceiling on what return to custody could cost; once that remainder was exhausted, the sentence was spent. Supervised release has no equivalent exhaustion point within the revocation framework. The leverage the system holds over the defendant does not diminish with each use, because each revocation draws on fresh statutory authority rather than on a depleting remainder. This is the sharpest expression of the conceptual break: parole's discipline was bounded by the sentence already imposed, while supervised release's discipline renews itself with each breach.

This is why the distinction between parole and supervised release matters in practice and not only in theory. Parole was a mechanism of early release: it let the prisoner out before the sentence expired and held the balance of the sentence as security for good behavior. Supervised release is a mechanism of extended control: it keeps the defendant under federal authority after the sentence expires and holds additional prison time as security for compliance. The first system made the prison term elastic downward; the second makes the period of federal supervision elastic upward. A defendant who violates supervised release conditions does not merely forfeit an early release already granted; the defendant incurs new custody that would never have existed had the violation not occurred. The difference reflects the Act's wager that certainty at sentencing, paired with court-managed supervision afterward, serves deterrence and public protection better than the old bargain of a long nominal sentence and an early, agency-granted release.

The three reforms form a single design. Abolishing parole fixed the meaning of the sentence. Truth in sentencing, through the capped good conduct provision, preserved that meaning against erosion. Supervised release replaced the supervisory function parole had performed, but on the court's terms and after, rather than during, the custodial term. Each piece presupposes the others. Determinate sentencing without post-release supervision would have left high-risk offenders entirely unsupervised the day their terms expired. Post-release supervision structured as parole would have reintroduced the very indeterminacy the Act meant to kill. Supervised release, imposed by judges, capped by statute, and revocable into fresh custody, was Congress's answer to the question parole had once answered: what happens when the prison gates open.

## The Constitutional Challenge and the Advisory Turn

The mandatory guidelines that the Sentencing Reform Act of 1984 created did not survive their first fifteen years unchallenged. Two distinct lines of constitutional attack tested the statute, and each produced a different answer. The cases behind this line of attack are surveyed in [sentencing-law-supreme-court-cases](/2018/03/15/sentencing-law-supreme-court-cases/). The first, decided in Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361 (1989), asked whether Congress could create the United States Sentencing Commission at all. The second, running from Apprendi v. New Jersey through United States v. Booker, asked whether the mandatory application of the guidelines the Commission wrote could coexist with the Sixth Amendment right to a jury trial. The second line changed federal sentencing far more than the first, and its result is easy to misstate. The statute was never struck down. Only the provision that made the guidelines binding was severed, leaving the Commission, the guidelines, and the rest of the Act intact.

### The structural attack fails in Mistretta

The challenge in Mistretta came from John Mistretta, who had been convicted of selling cocaine in the Western District of Missouri and was sentenced under the new guidelines. He argued that the entire scheme was unconstitutional on two structural grounds. First, he contended that Congress had delegated excessive legislative power to the Commission in violation of the nondelegation doctrine. Second, he argued that placing a rulemaking body inside the judicial branch, staffing it in part with sitting Article III judges appointed by the President and removable by the President for cause, violated the separation of powers. The district court upheld the guidelines, and the Supreme Court granted certiorari before judgment in the Eighth Circuit to resolve the constitutional questions without delay.

Justice Blackmun wrote for an eight-justice majority that sustained the arrangement in full. On nondelegation, the Court applied the familiar intelligible principle test and found that Congress had supplied the Commission with detailed statutory direction rather than a blank commission. The Act specified the purposes of sentencing that the guidelines were to serve, identified the offense and offender characteristics the Commission was to consider, directed the creation of offense categories and corresponding ranges, and constrained the permitted spread between the top and bottom of each range. The majority acknowledged that the power given to the Commission was comprehensive and concerned matters of great significance, but it treated that breadth as a practical necessity: Congress could not feasibly write hundreds of offense-specific ranges itself, and the delegation left the broad policy choices to Congress while assigning the laborious work of translating those choices into a grid to an expert body. The Court stated that it had upheld delegations under broad standards without deviation since 1935, and it harbored no doubt that the guidance here satisfied constitutional requirements.

On separation of powers, the majority rejected the claim that lodging the Commission in the judicial branch compromised either the judiciary or the political branches. The Court described the Commission as an independent body located within the judicial branch for administrative convenience, not as an entity exercising judicial power. The judges who served on it did so in an individual capacity, drawing on their accumulated experience with sentencing rather than acting as representatives of the judiciary or as agents of the executive. Presidential appointment with Senate confirmation and removal for cause did not convert the Commission into an executive agency, the majority reasoned, because those features were consistent with the kind of practical cooperation among the branches that the Constitution permits when the branches pursue a shared goal. The structural protections of the Constitution, in the majority's view, were flexible enough to accommodate a body that performed a legislative-type rulemaking function from a judicial-branch address.

Justice Scalia dissented alone. His objection was not that the intelligible principle test was flunked but that the test was beside the point. In his view, the guidelines had the force and effect of laws: they bound judges, determined the length of prison terms, and displaced the discretion the Sentencing Reform Act had supposedly preserved. Congress had therefore transferred genuine legislative power to a body that was neither Congress nor an executive agency exercising law-execution authority, and it had done so in a form that let Article III judges write the laws they would later apply. Scalia called the Commission a junior-varsity Congress and treated the case as an instance of pure delegation, for which no degree of guiding standards could compensate. He also insisted that the structural violation was aggravated, not cured, by the participation of sitting judges, since lawmaking by judges struck at the reason the Constitution vests legislative power in a separate, electorally accountable branch. The majority's tolerance for interbranch cooperation was, to the dissent, a license for the branches to trade away the very separations the Constitution made nonnegotiable.

The practical effect of Mistretta was to clear the structural field. The Commission was constitutional, the guidelines were constitutional, and the mandatory system Congress had designed could operate. Nothing in the decision, however, addressed the jury trial question that would later unravel the mandatory character of the system. Mistretta asked who could make the rules; the cases that followed asked who could find the facts that triggered them.

### The jury trial line: Apprendi and Blakely

The second line of attack began outside the federal system. In Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466 (2000), Charles Apprendi pleaded guilty to second-degree possession of a firearm for an unlawful purpose, an offense carrying a five-to-ten-year range, after firing shots into the home of an African-American family. A New Jersey hate crime statute permitted the trial judge to enhance the sentence if the judge found by a preponderance of the evidence that the defendant had acted with a purpose to intimidate on account of race. The judge made that finding and imposed a twelve-year term, two years beyond the maximum authorized for the offense of conviction. The New Jersey courts affirmed.

Justice Stevens, writing for a five-justice majority, reversed. The Court held that, other than the fact of a prior conviction, any fact that increases the penalty for a crime beyond the prescribed statutory maximum must be submitted to a jury and proved beyond a reasonable doubt. The majority rooted the rule in the due process requirement of proof beyond a reasonable doubt and in the Sixth Amendment guarantees of notice and jury trial, reasoning that a fact which exposes a defendant to greater punishment functions as an element of a more serious offense even when the legislature labels it a sentencing factor. The prior-conviction exception, retained from Almendarez-Torres v. United States, survived because a prior conviction had itself been established through procedures carrying the right to jury trial. Justice Scalia concurred separately to emphasize the jury's historic role as a check on the state's power to punish, and Justice Thomas concurred to argue that the logic extended to any fact that increased punishment, including facts triggering mandatory minimums.

The dissents gave the majority's rule as much attention as the majority gave itself, and for good reason: they forecast the collision with the federal guidelines. Justice O'Connor, joined by the Chief Justice and Justices Kennedy and Breyer, warned that the rule's logic could not be contained to hate crime enhancements and would destabilize determinate sentencing schemes, including the federal guidelines, that depended on judicial factfinding. Justice Breyer, joined by the Chief Justice, added a functional objection: legislatures needed room to guide judicial discretion without converting every sentencing consideration into a jury issue, and the majority's formalism threatened to undo two decades of sentencing reform. The majority answered that constitutional rights were not subject to balancing against administrative convenience, but the dissents had correctly identified where the doctrine was headed.

Four years later, Blakely v. Washington, 542 U.S. 296 (2004), carried the Apprendi rule into a guidelines system. Ralph Blakely pleaded guilty to kidnapping his estranged wife. The facts admitted in his plea supported a standard-range maximum of fifty-three months under Washington's sentencing guidelines, but the trial judge imposed an exceptional sentence of ninety months after finding that Blakely had acted with deliberate cruelty, a statutorily enumerated aggravating factor. Justice Scalia wrote for a five-justice majority that the sentence violated the Sixth Amendment. The decisive move was definitional: the relevant statutory maximum for Apprendi purposes, the Court held, was not the maximum sentence a judge might impose after finding additional facts, but the maximum the judge could impose solely on the basis of the facts reflected in the jury verdict or admitted by the defendant. Because Washington law required the exceptional sentence to rest on findings beyond those admitted in the plea, the ninety-month term exceeded the Blakely maximum, and the judge-found facts that authorized it had to go to a jury.

The Blakely majority expressly noted that the federal guidelines were not before the Court, but the implication was unmistakable and the dissenters said so at volume. Justice O'Connor, in dissent, described the decision as a Number 10 earthquake for sentencing law, predicting that its logic would reach the federal guidelines and every other determinate system built on judicial factfinding. Justice Kennedy, also dissenting, and Justice Breyer, in a separate dissent, elaborated the institutional stakes: mandatory guidelines systems would either have to submit vast numbers of sentencing facts to juries, an enterprise the dissents treated as unworkable, or abandon the mandatory character that made them effective against disparity. The majority, again, declined to let administrability dilute the jury right. Blakely thus presented the federal system with a binary choice that Booker would have to resolve: keep the mandatory guidelines and jury-try the facts, or keep judicial factfinding and surrender the mandate.

### Booker splits in two

United States v. Booker, 543 U.S. 220 (2005), decided January 12, 2005, answered the question Blakely had left open, and it did so through an unusual procedural device: two majority opinions, joined by different five-justice coalitions, addressing the constitutional question and the remedial question separately. The cases consolidated for decision involved Freddie Booker, sentenced in the Seventh Circuit to thirty years based in part on judge-found drug quantities, and Duncan Fanfan, whose case came up from the First Circuit before judgment. Both sentences rested on facts found by judges under the mandatory federal guidelines, and both tested whether Blakely applied to the federal system.

The constitutional majority, in an opinion by Justice Stevens joined by Justices Scalia, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg, held that it did. There was no constitutionally significant distinction between the Washington guidelines invalidated in Blakely and the federal guidelines: both were mandatory systems in which judge-found facts raised the ceiling on punishment beyond what the jury's verdict or the defendant's admissions alone authorized. The combination of mandatory guidelines and judicial factfinding therefore violated the Sixth Amendment, and the Court retained the prior-conviction exception without further discussion. The dissenters on this question, led by Justice Breyer and joined by the Chief Justice and Justices O'Connor and Kennedy, together with a separate dissent by Justice Thomas, argued that the federal guidelines differed from the Washington scheme in ways that mattered, including the role of the Sentencing Commission as an expert body and the guidelines' grounding in the jury-authorized statutory range. The majority treated those distinctions as irrelevant to the jury right as Blakely had defined it.

The remedial majority then confronted what to do about the violation, and here the coalitions scrambled. Justice Breyer wrote the remedial opinion, joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, and Ginsburg. The Court considered two candidate remedies. The first, favored by the dissenters from the remedial holding, would have engrafted the Sixth Amendment jury requirement onto the existing mandatory system, submitting guidelines facts to juries while leaving the mandate intact. The second, which the Court adopted, severed and excised the statutory provisions that made the guidelines binding. The remedial majority concluded that Congress would have preferred excision to total invalidation of the Act, and excision to a jury-driven mandatory system that Congress had never designed and whose complexity would have defeated the uniformity the Act sought. Severance, the opinion reasoned, had to remove only what was incompatible with the constitutional holding while preserving the portions of the statute that could function independently and that remained consistent with Congress's basic objectives.

Two provisions were therefore severed: 18 U.S.C. 3553(b)(1), which directed sentencing courts to impose sentences within the applicable guidelines range absent justifying circumstances, and 18 U.S.C. 3742(e), which set the standards of appellate review, including de novo review of departures, that depended on the guidelines' mandatory character. With those provisions excised, the guidelines became effectively advisory. Sentencing courts were still required to calculate the applicable range correctly and to consider it alongside the other sentencing factors set out in 18 U.S.C. 3553(a), and they were still directed to impose sentences sufficient but not greater than necessary to serve the statutory purposes of sentencing. Appellate review, shorn of the de novo standard, was recast as review for reasonableness. The Commission, the guidelines manual, the statutory purposes, and the presentence and supervised-release machinery all survived; only the binding force of the range did not.

The remedial dissents objected with equal force from opposite directions. Justice Stevens, joined by Justice Souter and in large part by Justice Scalia, argued that the Court had no warrant to rewrite the statute on Congress's behalf: the proper remedy was to keep the guidelines mandatory and submit the triggering facts to juries, a remedy the dissenters maintained was workable and far closer to what Congress had enacted. Justice Thomas and Justice Breyer, dissenting in part from the constitutional holding, would not have reached the remedial question at all. The result was a decision in which no single five-justice group agreed on both the violation and the fix, yet the fix commanded a majority and became law. The odd alignment, with Justice Ginsburg supplying the fifth vote for the remedy while Justice Scalia voted against it, reflected a pragmatic judgment that an advisory system preserved more of Congress's design than either alternative.

### The advisory-era doctrine: Rita, Gall, and Kimbrough

Booker's remedial opinion left the lower courts with two open questions: how much weight the advisory guidelines should carry, and how searching appellate review for reasonableness should be. Three decisions in 2007 supplied the answers, and together they defined the advisory era.

Rita v. United States, 551 U.S. 338 (2007), addressed the first question from the appellate side. Victor Rita sought a below-range sentence based on his physical condition, his likely vulnerability in prison, and his military service; the district court imposed thirty-three months, the bottom of the thirty-three-to-forty-one-month range. The Fourth Circuit affirmed, applying its rule that a within-range sentence was presumptively reasonable. Justice Breyer, writing for the majority, held that courts of appeals may apply such a presumption. The presumption was permissible, the Court reasoned, because it reflected the dual consideration the guidelines embodied: the Commission had shaped the ranges to carry out the same section 3553(a) objectives that sentencing judges were instructed to apply, drawing on extensive empirical study of past sentencing practice, so a sentence reflecting both the judge's and the Commission's judgment about those objectives was likely to be reasonable. Critically, the presumption was an appellate presumption only. It did not bind, it did not shift any burden, and the sentencing court itself was forbidden to presume the guidelines range reasonable; the district judge had to make an individualized determination under section 3553(a) in every case. Justice Stevens concurred to emphasize the narrowness of the holding, Justice Scalia concurred in part, and Justice Souter dissented, warning that even a nonbinding appellate presumption would exert gravitational pull toward the range and recreate mandatory guidelines in practice. The majority's answer to that concern was structural: because the guidelines were advisory, no presumption applied at the moment of sentencing could convert advice into command.

Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38 (2007), addressed review of sentences outside the range. Brian Gall had participated in an ecstasy distribution conspiracy while in college, withdrew from it, and built a law-abiding life before his indictment years later; the district court sentenced him to thirty-six months of probation though the guidelines recommended thirty to thirty-seven months of imprisonment. The Eighth Circuit reversed, holding that so large a variance required extraordinary circumstances. Justice Stevens, for the majority, rejected that approach and the rigid mathematical formulas some circuits had used to calibrate the strength of justification to the percentage of departure. All sentences, whether inside, just outside, or significantly outside the range, were to be reviewed under a deferential abuse-of-discretion standard. The Court laid out the procedure district judges were to follow: correctly calculate the guidelines range as the starting point and initial benchmark, hear the parties' arguments, consider all of the section 3553(a) factors, and explain the sentence, with the explanation expected to grow more substantial as the variance grew larger. An appellate court could consider the extent of the deviation, but it could not demand extraordinary circumstances or apply a percentage-based formula, because either approach came too close to an impermissible presumption that outside-range sentences were unreasonable. Procedural soundness came first: a court that miscalculated the range, treated the guidelines as mandatory, or failed to consider the statutory factors committed procedural error before the substance of the sentence was ever reached. Justice Scalia concurred to note that judge-found facts would continue to raise Sixth Amendment questions in individual cases, a reservation that underscored how much of Booker's constitutional tension the advisory remedy had eased without eliminating.

Kimbrough v. United States, 552 U.S. 85 (2007), completed the trilogy by addressing policy disagreement directly. Derrick Kimbrough's guidelines range for a crack cocaine offense was 228 to 270 months, driven by the 100-to-1 quantity ratio that treated crack as one hundred times more severely than powder cocaine; the district court called the resulting range greater than necessary and imposed the 180-month statutory minimum. The Fourth Circuit vacated, holding under circuit precedent that a sentence outside the range was per se unreasonable when based on disagreement with the crack-powder disparity. Justice Ginsburg, writing for a seven-justice majority, reversed. The cocaine guidelines, like all guidelines, were advisory, and a district court did not abuse its discretion by concluding in a particular case that the crack-powder disparity yielded a sentence greater than necessary to serve the objectives of sentencing. The majority gave the point added weight by observing that the drug guidelines, unlike much of the manual, were not the product of the Commission's empirical study of past practice but were keyed instead to the statutory mandatory minimums Congress had set, which diminished the claim that they embodied the Commission's institutional expertise. Justices Thomas and Alito dissented, arguing that the disparity reflected a congressional policy judgment that district courts were not free to second-guess. The majority's response was that the statute's parsimony instruction and the directive to avoid unwarranted disparity authorized individualized judgment even about guideline policies, and that nothing in the advisory scheme made any particular guideline ratio effectively mandatory.

### What survived

The through line of these decisions is narrower than the drama of the litigation suggested. Mistretta sustained the Commission's existence and Congress's power to create it; Apprendi and Blakely established that judge-found facts could not raise sentences under a mandatory system; Booker severed the mandate while preserving everything else. The guidelines did not become unconstitutional, and the Sentencing Reform Act was not invalidated. What fell was a single statutory command, 18 U.S.C. 3553(b)(1), together with the appellate review standard that depended on it. The Commission continued to write guidelines, judges continued to calculate ranges and give them respectful consideration, and appellate courts continued to review sentences for reasonableness under the abuse-of-discretion standard Gall prescribed, with Rita's presumption available for within-range sentences and Kimbrough's license for policy-based variance available for sentences outside them.

The advisory turn thus preserved the statute's central ambition while changing its mechanism. Congress had sought to reduce unwarranted disparity without eliminating individualized judgment, and the post-Booker doctrine pursued the same end through persuasion rather than compulsion: the guidelines remained the starting point and the benchmark, anchored in the Commission's expertise and in section 3553(a), but the sentencing judge retained the final responsibility to fit the sentence to the offense and the offender. The constitutional challenge ended not with the demolition of the Sentencing Reform Act but with its conversion, by judicial surgery, into the advisory system its severed provisions had once forbidden.

## Mandatory minimums, severity, and what the evidence shows

### The second machinery beside the guidelines

The guidelines did not operate alone for long. Two years after the Sentencing Reform Act of 1984 reached the statute books, Congress enacted the Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986 ([anti-drug-abuse-acts-1986-1988](/2018/06/15/anti-drug-abuse-acts-1986-1988/)), which imposed five- and ten-year mandatory minimum sentences tied to drug type and quantity, and followed with the Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1988, which extended mandatory minimums further, including a five-year minimum for simple possession of crack cocaine, the only drug punished that way, and new minimums reaching drug trafficking conspiracies. These provisions, appearing in statutes such as 21 U.S.C. 841(b), operate beside the guideline ranges and frequently displace them. The statutory-floor machinery they belong to is examined in [mandatory-minimum-sentencing-law](/2018/02/01/mandatory-minimum-sentencing-law/). Section 5G1.1(b) of the guidelines manual provides that when application of the guidelines yields a range below the statutory minimum, the statutory minimum becomes the guideline sentence. The floor is not advisory, and it does not yield to the arithmetic of the sentencing table.

The Commission did not resist this arrangement. It built around it. In response to the 1986 Act, the Commission set the quantity thresholds in the Drug Quantity Table so that the five- and ten-year minimums correspond to base offense levels 26 and 32, with the resulting guideline ranges beginning just above the statutory floors. The signal from Congress, expressed through the minimums themselves and through its continuing oversight of the Commission, was that guideline ranges were expected to reflect the severity of the new statutes, and the Commission complied. The consequence is structural and often misunderstood. Guideline reform alone cannot move many of the sentences in question, because the floors are statutory and the body that writes the guidelines cannot lower a statute. The only routes beneath a mandatory minimum are the narrow exceptions Congress itself created: the safety valve, added by the Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994 for certain low-level, nonviolent drug offenders, and sentences below the minimum on government motion for substantial assistance, authorized by 18 U.S.C. 3553(e) and reflected in guidelines section 5K1.1.

The 1991 special report of the Commission to Congress on mandatory minimums documented how concentrated this machinery is. From 1984 through 1990, 59,780 cases were sentenced under statutes containing mandatory minimum provisions, and four drug and weapons statutes accounted for approximately 94 percent of them: 21 U.S.C. 841 for manufacture and distribution of controlled substances, 21 U.S.C. 844 for possession, 21 U.S.C. 960 for importation and exportation, and 18 U.S.C. 924(c) for carrying a firearm during a drug or violent crime. More than half of the sixty mandatory minimum statutes on the books during that period were never used at all, and six more were used six or fewer times. The apparatus of severity was broad on paper and narrow in practice, but where it was used, it was decisive, and it has grown: a Congressional Research Service review of the Commission's 2011 follow-up report counted 195 mandatory minimum statutes, against the 60 identified in 1991.

The 1988 Act widened the net as well as deepening it. By attaching mandatory minimum penalties to drug trafficking conspiracies, it brought everyone involved in a trafficking organization within the federal government's reach, from organizers to peripheral participants, and exposed each of them to the same statutory floors as principals. The 1991 report also recorded how prosecutors viewed these floors: as valuable tools in obtaining guilty pleas. A mandatory minimum is leverage at the bargaining table, and its application turns on charging decisions that the guidelines do not govern. The Commission found that mandatory minimum sentences were not applied as expected to all cases meeting the statutory criteria of eligibility, which meant that two offenders facing the same statutory floor could receive very different treatment depending on decisions made before sentencing began.

### Disentangling the sources of severity

Anyone who attributes the growth of federal sentences to the guidelines alone tells a story that is partly true and significantly confounded. The Commission's own 2004 assessment, Fifteen Years of Guidelines Sentencing, found that between 1987 and 1989, the first years in which a majority of federal offenders were sentenced under the guidelines, the average prison time expected to be served almost doubled. By 1992 it had more than doubled, from 26 months in 1986 to 59 months in 1992. The report then refused to credit the guidelines with the whole increase. The abolition of parole, the enactment of mandatory minimum penalty provisions, and changes in the types of offenders sentenced in federal court, the Commission wrote, all contributed to increased severity along with implementation of the guidelines, and the influence of each varied among offenses. Honest measurement has to separate four forces that arrived together: the guideline ranges, the statutory floors, the end of parole, and the changing composition of the federal docket.

Each force has its own fingerprint. The abolition of parole, the SRA's own doing, meant that the typical federal offender sentenced in 2002 would spend about twice as long in prison as the typical offender sentenced before the Act, increasing from just under 25 months to almost 50 months, because the Act required offenders to serve at least 85 percent of the sentence imposed. Mandatory minimums left a different mark. Drug trafficking offenders, whose sentences were shaped directly by the 1986 and 1988 Acts, saw time actually served rise two and a half times, to an average of 80 months, immediately after guidelines implementation, and the average remained more than twice its 1984 level in 2001 even after a slight downturn in the late 1990s that the Commission attributed in part to the safety valve. The guidelines left a third mark, visible in the probability of going to prison at all: in 1987, 69 percent of federal offenders were sentenced to prison, and by 2002 the figure was 86 percent. And beneath all three ran the fourth force, the docket itself. Drug trafficking offenders, whose cases are dominated by mandatory minimums and the quantity-driven guideline structure built on top of them, numbered 25,835 in 2002, or 40.4 percent of the entire federal criminal docket. When drug sentences move, the federal average moves with them.

The pattern was not uniform across offense types, which is itself evidence against a single-cause story. For violent offenses, sentences imposed actually decreased in the guidelines era, yet time served increased anyway, because the abolition of parole meant that more of each sentence imposed was actually served. For manslaughter, the two effects canceled out: lower sentences imposed offset by the end of parole left time served roughly unchanged. For immigration offenses, both forces pushed in the same direction, and time served increased substantially. These differences show why the aggregate doubling of time served cannot be read as the footprint of any one reform. The guideline ranges set the starting point, the mandatory minimums set the floor in drug and firearms cases, and parole abolition ensured that nearly all of whatever was imposed was actually served.

There is a further entanglement that the Congressional Research Service flagged. In the brief window before the 1986 Act repopulated federal law with mandatory minimums, the guidelines themselves operated, in the Service's words, essentially but briefly to establish a mandatory minimum term of imprisonment where none had existed before. The Commission anchored the drug ranges to the new statutory floors almost as soon as Congress built them, so the two systems have been impossible to separate in practice ever since. Any claim that the guidelines caused federal sentence growth, without accounting for the mandatory minimums enacted alongside them, overstates the Commission's role and understates Congress's.

### The crack-to-powder ratio and the Commission's long dissent

The clearest case study in how mandatory minimums overrode guideline judgment involves cocaine. The 1986 Act required 100 times as much powder cocaine as crack cocaine to trigger the same mandatory minimum: five grams of crack or 500 grams of powder brought a five-year minimum, and 50 grams of crack or five kilograms of powder brought a ten-year minimum. The 1988 Act made first-time simple possession of crack punishable by a mandatory minimum, the only drug so punished. Congress wrote these quantities into statute at a moment of intense political pressure over crack, and the Commission then embedded the ratio in the guidelines, setting the Drug Quantity Table thresholds so that guideline ranges tracked the statutory scheme.

The Commission then spent more than a decade telling Congress the ratio was indefensible. In February 1995, in the first of what would become four reports on federal cocaine sentencing policy ordered under the 1994 crime act, the Commission recommended against the 100-to-1 quantity ratio. In 1997 it unanimously concluded the disparity was no longer justified. In 2001 it recommended replacing the 100-to-1 ratio with a 20-to-1 ratio, raising the crack threshold to at least 25 grams. The 2004 Fifteen Years assessment criticized the ratio again, noting the stark racial impact: in 2002, 81 percent of offenders sentenced for crack trafficking were African-American, and the average term of imprisonment for crack was 119 months, compared with 78 months for powder cocaine, 25 months longer than for methamphetamine and 81 months longer than for heroin. The Commission reported that when the ratio was implemented, the majority of crack defendants receiving sentences greater than five years were low-level street dealers, and that for no other drug were low-level dealers subject to such harsh sentences. It also found that recidivism rates for drug-trafficking career offenders were much lower than those of other offenders at the same criminal history level, leading it to conclude that criminal history category was a less perfect measure of recidivism risk for such defendants. The eventual congressional response came in the Fair Sentencing Act of 2010, which raised the crack quantity thresholds for the five- and ten-year minimums from 5 to 28 grams and from 50 to 280 grams and repealed the mandatory minimum for simple possession of crack, narrowing the statutory ratio to roughly 18-to-1. A later round of federal sentencing adjustment is profiled in [first-step-act-2018-guide](/2018/01/15/first-step-act-2018-guide/). The ratio thus stands as the starkest illustration of the relationship the 1984 Act created: Congress legislated severity, the Commission built guidelines on top of it, and the Commission's own research then documented the damage while Congress took fifteen years to respond in part.

When the Commission finally reduced crack cocaine guideline penalties in 2007, and voted to apply the reduction retroactively beginning in 2008, the change illustrated the limits of guideline reform. Commission estimates put the reduction at roughly 27 months off the sentences then being served and 15 to 16 months for future sentences, but the statutory mandatory minimums enacted in 1986 and 1988 remained exactly where Congress had left them. The guidelines could soften the range above the floor; they could not move the floor. This is the durable meaning of section 5G1.1(b) in practice: the guidelines are the instrument, but the statute sets the bottom.

### Disparity: what the numbers showed

The SRA's central promise was to reduce unwarranted disparity, and the Commission's 2004 assessment tested that promise with unusual candor. Studies dividing judicial influence into primary judge effects, meaning the tendency of some judges to be harsher or more lenient across all cases, and interaction effects, meaning harsher or more lenient treatment of particular offense types, found that the primary judge effect was small in both the preguidelines and guidelines eras but was reduced by between a third and half under the guidelines, from 2.32 to 1.24 percent among judges who sentenced in both periods. Interaction effects were about three to five times larger than primary effects and were reduced for most judges, though not among those who sentenced in both periods. The influence of judges was reduced for drug, fraud, firearm, and larceny offenses, though immigration and robbery offenses showed no reduction. A matched-group study comparing comparable offenders before and after implementation found narrower sentence distributions under the guidelines, and a reanalysis requested by Congress and performed by the General Accounting Office replicated the basic finding while concluding the evidence was insufficient to establish clearly that the guidelines had reduced disparity.

The assessment also documented where disparity went rather than whether it vanished. Regional differences in drug trafficking sentences increased from the preguidelines to the guidelines era. Discretion migrated to stages before sentencing, where charging and plea decisions determine which charges, and therefore which mandatory minimums and guideline provisions, apply. Substantial assistance departures, the escape hatch from mandatory minimums, formalized a new disparity: the Commission's report concluded that these mechanisms had a greater adverse impact on Black offenders than the judicial factors of the discretionary system had. The Commission's 1991 special report had already warned that mandatory minimums would work against the disparity goal. The lack of uniform application of mandatory minimums, it concluded, creates unwarranted disparity in sentencing and compromises the potential of the guidelines system to reduce disparity. The report found that substantial assistance departures, which allow judges to sentence below an otherwise applicable minimum, were more likely to be granted to white defendants than to Black or Hispanic defendants, and the Commission could identify no relevant factors, such as the severity of the offense or the extent of the cooperation, to explain the difference. A 1998 study by Commission staff confirmed the pattern: controlling for offense, offender, judicial, and guideline factors, Hispanic defendants were seven percentage points less likely than non-Hispanic defendants to receive a substantial assistance departure. The uniformity the statute sought arrived, but the discretion it removed from judges reappeared in the hands of prosecutors, with the same unequal results distributed along the same racial lines.

Later Commission research confirmed that the disparity problem outlived the mandatory guidelines era. The November 2017 report Demographic Differences in Sentencing, updating the Commission's 2012 Booker report, found that after controlling for a wide variety of sentencing factors, Black male offenders received sentences on average 19.1 percent longer than similarly situated White male offenders during fiscal years 2012 through 2016, a gap that had remained relatively unchanged across the periods studied. Black male offenders were 21.2 percent less likely than White male offenders to receive a non-government-sponsored downward departure or variance, and when they did receive one, their sentences were 16.8 percent longer than those of White male offenders who received the same relief. Within the applicable guideline range, the gap was 7.9 percent, while there was no statistically significant difference among offenders who received substantial assistance departures. The Commission's November 2023 update, covering fiscal years 2017 through 2021 and more than 300,000 offenders, found the disparity had not closed: Black males received sentences 13.4 percent longer than White males, with the gap concentrated in the decision whether to incarcerate at all. The guidelines narrowed the differences that judges had introduced; they did not eliminate the differences the system as a whole produced.

### Severity and the federal prison population

The aggregate consequence is visible in the size of the federal prison system. The federal prison population stood at approximately 24,000 to 25,000 inmates in fiscal year 1980, a level at which it had remained, with fluctuations, since 1940. It then began a nearly unabated three-decade increase, reaching nearly 219,000 in fiscal year 2012, an increase of more than 800 percent. The Bureau of Prisons director testified in 2013 that the agency's population was a little more than 60,000 when he joined as a correctional officer in 1988. The Congressional Research Service, reviewing the causes, reported that the Commission, the Bureau, and scholars had identified the same drivers: increases in the number of federal offenses subject to mandatory minimum sentences, changes to the federal criminal code that made more crimes federal offenses, and the elimination of parole. Growth was fastest in the 1990s and 2000s, averaging roughly 7,600 and 7,500 additional inmates per fiscal year respectively, compared with about 3,700 per year during the 1980s. The timing matters. The steepest growth followed the combination of the 1984 Act and the mandatory minimums of 1986 and 1988, not the Act alone. Those drivers compound rather than merely add. The director of the Bureau of Prisons testified in 2013 that the war on drugs in the early 1980s was a significant driver of the growth, bringing more offenders into the system, and once inside, the combination of longer sentences imposed, higher statutory floors, and the end of parole kept them there longer. Annual growth averaged about 3,700 inmates per fiscal year during the 1980s, then roughly doubled, averaging about 7,600 per year in the 1990s and about 7,500 per year in the 2000s. By fiscal year 2012, when the population reached nearly 219,000, the system held more than eight times as many people as it had when the Sentencing Reform Act was signed, after a nearly unabated three-decade increase.

### Two reforms in one machine

The statute's history contains a paradox that the evidence sharpens rather than dissolves. The Sentencing Reform Act emerged from two distinct grievances that happened to agree on the instrument. One critique held that federal sentencing was arbitrary: similarly situated defendants received markedly different sentences depending on the judge before whom they appeared, with race and resources shadowing the differences, and uniformity was the remedy. The other critique held that federal sentencing was lenient: indeterminate sentences and parole meant that the sentence a judge imposed bore little relation to the time an offender served, and certainty and severity were the remedy. Both camps supported the same machinery: a commission that would write binding ranges, the abolition of parole, and punishment that was certain and severe. The Commission itself later acknowledged that increasing the certainty and severity of punishment were clear goals of the SRA, alongside the reduction of unwarranted disparity.

The tilt toward severity was not an accident of drafting. It followed from who got to set the numbers. The disparity coalition's instrument was the binding range, which constrained judges; the severity coalition's instruments were parole abolition and the statutory minimums, which constrained outcomes. When the Commission wrote the ranges, it did so under Congress's direction, and for drugs it built the ranges upward from the mandatory minimums rather than downward from past practice. The Commission's original design for many offenses drew on historical sentencing data, which is why violent-offense ranges tracked prior practice, but for drugs the 1986 Act supplied the anchor, and that anchor had been set by the same legislature that was overseeing the Commission. Uniformity meant that every judge applied the same table; severity meant the table itself started at the statutory floor. Both coalitions got uniformity. Only one got the numbers it wanted.

Once the machinery existed, the two goals did not share equally in its operation. A commission responsive to congressional direction wrote ranges anchored to mandatory minimums enacted in 1986 and 1988, embedded the 100-to-1 crack ratio in the Drug Quantity Table, and operated in a system where statutory floors, not guideline ranges, determined the longest sentences. The uniformity both coalitions had sought arrived, but it arrived at the severity only one of them had sought. A fair accounting weighs the two promises separately. On uniformity among judges, the Commission's measurements show real but limited progress: primary judge effects fell by a third to a half, sentence distributions narrowed for matched offenders, and most judges showed reduced interaction effects. On severity, there is no ambiguity about direction: average time served doubled, the likelihood of imprisonment rose, and the federal prison population grew more than eightfold over three decades. On racial disparity, the record is the hardest to defend: the 100-to-1 ratio produced a 119-month average for crack against 78 months for powder with an 81 percent Black defendant population in 2002, and the Commission's 2017 and 2023 reports found Black men still receiving sentences 19.1 and 13.4 percent longer than similarly situated white men. The disparity the statute set out to cure was reduced in one of its forms and preserved or deepened in others. Inter-judge variation narrowed while discretion migrated to prosecutors and cooperation decisions, and the racial disparities reappeared in the mechanisms of departure and charging that the new system created. The guidelines did not cause all of this, and the evidence does not permit saying so. Mandatory minimums, parole abolition, and the changing docket each did their share. But the 1984 Act built the machine in which all of them operated, and it built that machine to value uniformity above all else.

## Conclusion

The Sentencing Reform Act stands as the most complete legislative redesign of federal punishment in American history, and its record invites a verdict measured in two currencies. On uniformity among judges, the evidence shows real but bounded progress: primary judge effects fell by a third to a half, sentence distributions narrowed, and the wide lottery of the preguidelines era contracted into the narrow bands of the sentencing table. On severity, there is no ambiguity about direction: average time served doubled, the probability of imprisonment rose, and the federal prison population grew more than eightfold in three decades, driven by the combination of the guidelines, mandatory minimums, and the end of parole. On racial disparity, the record is the hardest to defend: discretion migrated from judges to prosecutors and cooperation decisions, and the differences reappeared in the new system's mechanisms along the same racial lines the old system had displayed.

Two reforms in one statute: the disparity critique and the leniency critique produced the same machinery, and once guidelines were written by a commission responsive to congressional direction, the uniformity both sides wanted arrived at the severity only one side had sought. That is the namable claim this profile supports, and it carries a warning that travels well beyond sentencing law. A statute profile showing that a coalition agreeing on a mechanism while disagreeing on a goal produces the goal of whoever controls the mechanism afterward.

The lesson for readers of legislation is practical rather than philosophical. Study the institution a statute creates, the directives it gives that institution, and the direction in which those directives can be exercised. The 1984 Act's coalition agreed on a commission, a grid, and the end of parole; the commission then operated under congressional direction that moved in one direction only, toward higher sentences and tighter constraints. The judges kept their courtrooms, the Commission kept its manuals, and Congress kept the power to say no. Whoever controls the mechanism afterward writes the numbers. ### Q: What did the Sentencing Reform Act create?

The Sentencing Reform Act created the United States Sentencing Commission, an independent body in the judicial branch charged with writing sentencing guidelines for the federal system. It created the guidelines themselves, a grid that converts offense level and criminal history category into a narrow range of months for each sentence. It abolished federal parole for offenses committed on or after November 1, 1987, replacing indeterminate sentences with determinate terms subject only to capped good conduct credit. It created supervised release, a term of court-imposed supervision that follows imprisonment rather than replacing part of it. It established the purposes of sentencing at 18 U.S.C. 3553(a)(2), required judges to state reasons for sentences outside the range, created appellate review of sentences, and supplied a departure mechanism for atypical cases the Commission had not adequately considered.

### Q: Did the Sentencing Reform Act abolish federal parole?

Yes. The Act abolished parole for federal offenses committed on or after November 1, 1987, the date the guidelines took effect. Offenders sentenced under the new regime serve determinate terms with only limited good conduct credit, and no Parole Commission decides an earlier release date. The abolition ran by offense date rather than sentencing date, because the ex post facto clause barred applying the harsher regime to earlier conduct, so pre-November 1987 offenders remained eligible for parole under the old system. Congress originally scheduled the Parole Commission for elimination in 1992, but the old-law caseload made the schedule impossible, and repeated extensions carried the Commission forward for decades. The abolition was central to the Act's design: without parole, the sentence imposed became the sentence served, a change the statute's supporters called truth in sentencing.

### Q: Why did liberals and conservatives both support the Sentencing Reform Act?

Liberals supported the Act because they saw unguided judicial discretion as an engine of disparity, producing sentences that varied with the judge, the district, and the race and sex of the defendant. Binding guidelines promised to pull harsh outliers inward and end discriminatory punishment. Conservatives supported it because they saw the same discretion as an engine of leniency: judges imposed long nominal sentences and parole authorities released offenders after a fraction of the term. The guidelines and parole abolition promised to pull lenient outliers upward and guarantee that the sentence imposed was the sentence served. Edward Kennedy supplied the liberal architecture and legitimacy, while Strom Thurmond supplied conservative votes and carried the provisions inside his crime package. Each side lent the other its credibility, and the coalition held because the mechanism served both grievances even though the goals pointed in opposite directions.

### Q: How was the Sentencing Reform Act enacted on an appropriations bill?

The sentencing provisions traveled inside a continuing appropriations resolution, H.J. Res. 648, introduced in September 1984 to fund the government for fiscal year 1985. The House folded its crime package, including the sentencing title, into the resolution on September 25, and the Senate passed it on October 4. A conference committee reconciled the chambers' versions, and President Ronald Reagan signed the resolution on October 12, 1984, making it Public Law 98-473. The crime provisions rode inside as Title II, the Comprehensive Crime Control Act of 1984, and the sentencing provisions rode inside that title as the Sentencing Reform Act. The vehicle guaranteed passage because no member would vote against funding the government weeks before an election, but it also meant the measure never received a separate floor vote in either chamber in its final form, a procedure critics described as legislating by ambush.

### Q: What did Mistretta decide about the Sentencing Reform Act?

In Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361 (1989), the Supreme Court upheld the Act against structural constitutional attack. John Mistretta argued that Congress had delegated excessive legislative power to the Sentencing Commission and that placing a rulemaking body in the judicial branch, staffed partly by judges appointed and removable by the President, violated the separation of powers. An eight-justice majority, in an opinion by Justice Blackmun, rejected both claims. On delegation, the Court held that the Act supplied an intelligible principle through its detailed directives on sentencing purposes, offense and offender characteristics, and the permitted spread of guideline ranges. On separation of powers, the Court treated the Commission as an independent body lodged in the judicial branch for administrative convenience, not as an exercise of judicial power. Justice Scalia dissented alone, calling the Commission a junior-varsity Congress. The decision cleared the structural field, though it left the jury trial question for later cases.

### Q: Are Sentencing Reform Act guidelines still mandatory?

No. The guidelines became effectively advisory in United States v. Booker, 543 U.S. 220 (2005). The Court held that mandatory guidelines combined with judicial factfinding violated the Sixth Amendment right to a jury trial, because judge-found facts raised the ceiling on punishment beyond what the jury verdict authorized. Rather than submit guidelines facts to juries, the remedial majority severed the provision that made the guidelines binding, 18 U.S.C. 3553(b)(1), along with the appellate review standards that depended on it. Judges must still calculate the range correctly and consider it alongside the sentencing factors at 18 U.S.C. 3553(a), and appellate courts review sentences for reasonableness under a deferential abuse-of-discretion standard. Within-range sentences may receive a presumption of reasonableness on appeal, but the sentencing judge may not presume the range reasonable and may vary from it, including on policy disagreement with a guideline.

### Q: What is supervised release under the Sentencing Reform Act?

Supervised release, codified at 18 U.S.C. 3583, is a term of court-imposed supervision that follows imprisonment. It replaced parole's supervisory function but works in the opposite direction: parole shortened the prison term by releasing the offender early, while supervised release adds a period of federal control after the custodial term ends. A defendant sentenced to sixty months of imprisonment plus three years of supervised release faces up to eight years under federal authority. Congress set authorized lengths by offense class, up to five years for the most serious felonies, and the sentencing judge chooses the length and conditions within the statutory ceiling. Violations are adjudicated by the court under a preponderance standard, and revocation can produce a fresh term of imprisonment keyed to the offense class, imposed because the defendant breached the conditions of supervision rather than as residue of the original sentence.

### Q: Who serves on the commission created by the Sentencing Reform Act?

The Commission has seven voting members and one nonvoting member. The President appoints the voting members with Senate confirmation, after consultation with representatives of judges, prosecutors, defense attorneys, law enforcement, victims, and others interested in the criminal justice process. At least three members must be federal judges, selected after the President considers a list recommended by the Judicial Conference. No more than four members may belong to the same political party. One member serves as Chair and three as Vice Chairs, with party limits on the Vice Chairs as well. The Attorney General, or a designee, sits as an ex officio nonvoting member, giving the executive a voice without a vote. Members are insulated from political pressure: the President may remove them only for neglect of duty, malfeasance, or other good cause shown.

### Q: What are the statutory purposes of sentencing under the Act?

The Act codified the purposes of federal sentencing at 18 U.S.C. 3553(a)(2). A court must impose a sentence sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to reflect the seriousness of the offense, promote respect for the law, and provide just punishment; to afford adequate deterrence; to protect the public from further crimes of the defendant; and to provide the defendant with needed training, medical care, or other correctional treatment. The opening formulation, sufficient but not greater than necessary, is known as the parsimony principle. Notably, rehabilitation appears as one purpose among several rather than the master principle, a deliberate break from the indeterminate era, when punishment was organized around reforming the offender. The Commission's original guidelines declined to choose among the competing philosophies, arguing that they usually pointed to the same result in concrete cases, and built the system to embody all four at once.

### Q: How does the federal sentencing table work?

The sentencing table is a grid with forty three offense levels on the vertical axis and six criminal history categories on the horizontal axis, producing two hundred fifty eight cells. Each offense guideline assigns a base offense level, adjusted upward or downward by specific offense characteristics such as dollar loss or drug quantity, plus cross-cutting adjustments for aggravating role, obstruction, or acceptance of responsibility. Criminal history points from prior sentences place the defendant in one of six categories. The intersection of offense level and category yields a range in months, deliberately narrow: Congress required that no range maximum exceed its minimum by more than twenty five percent or six months, with a limited exception. A defendant at offense level twenty four in the lowest history category faces fifty one to sixty three months, while the same level in the highest category yields one hundred to one hundred twenty five.

### Q: What was the 25 percent rule in the guidelines?

The 25 percent rule, at 28 U.S.C. 994(b)(2), required that the maximum of any guideline range not exceed the minimum by more than the greater of twenty five percent or six months, with an exception allowing a life maximum where the minimum reached thirty years. The narrowness was the point of the reform: if the range for a given offense level and criminal history category spanned only a quarter of the minimum, judges could not reproduce the wide disparities of the preguidelines era even when they disagreed about the right sentence within the range. The table was further divided into zones governing the type of sentence available. At the low end, probation or alternatives to incarceration remained possible; at the high end, covering most cells, the sentence had to be served as imprisonment. The rule thus constrained not only how long a sentence could be but also whether custody was required at all.

### Q: What was the departure power under the original guidelines?

Under the original 18 U.S.C. 3553(b), the court was required to impose a sentence within the guideline range, with one narrow exception: departure was permitted only upon finding an aggravating or mitigating circumstance of a kind, or to a degree, not adequately taken into consideration by the Commission, and only the guidelines, policy statements, and official commentary could be consulted to decide whether the Commission had considered it. The Commission explained the provision through the heartland concept: each guideline carved out a heartland of typical cases, and departure existed for the atypical case whose conduct differed significantly from the norm. In Koon v. United States (1996), the Supreme Court instructed courts to classify the proposed departure factor as forbidden, encouraged, discouraged, or unmentioned, with different standards for each. The statute also created accountability: judges had to state reasons in open court for outside-range sentences, and both sides could appeal departures.

### Q: What did Booker decide about the federal guidelines?

United States v. Booker, 543 U.S. 220 (2005), decided through two majority opinions by different five-justice coalitions, ended the mandatory guidelines era. The constitutional majority held that the combination of mandatory guidelines and judicial factfinding violated the Sixth Amendment: judge-found facts raised the punishment ceiling beyond what the jury verdict or admitted facts authorized, extending the rule of Blakely v. Washington to the federal system. The remedial majority then severed 18 U.S.C. 3553(b)(1), the provision making the guidelines binding, and 18 U.S.C. 3742(e), the appellate review standards that depended on it. The Commission, the guidelines manual, the statutory purposes, and the supervised-release machinery all survived; only the binding force of the range fell. Sentencing courts must still calculate the range correctly and consider it under 18 U.S.C. 3553(a), with appellate review recast as review for reasonableness.

### Q: How does revocation of supervised release work?

When a court finds by a preponderance of the evidence that a defendant violated a condition of supervised release, it may revoke the term and require the defendant to serve in prison all or part of the supervised release term authorized for the offense, without credit for time previously spent on supervision. The statute caps each revocation term by offense class: five years for a Class A felony, three for Class B, two for Class C or D, and one year otherwise, with the cap applying separately to each revocation, so successive violations can produce successive terms. Because the revocation sanction draws on fresh statutory authority rather than the unserved remainder of the original sentence, aggregate custody can exceed the statutory maximum for the underlying offense, a structure courts have sustained against double punishment objections. The statute also creates mandatory revocation triggers, including possession of a controlled substance or a firearm and refusal of drug testing.

### Q: What is good conduct time under the Sentencing Reform Act?

With parole abolished, the only statutory reduction of a determinate sentence is good conduct time, capped at 54 days for each year of the sentence imposed under 18 U.S.C. 3624(b), conditioned on exemplary compliance with institutional disciplinary regulations. The Bureau of Prisons may award less or none when compliance falls short, and unearned credit cannot be granted later. The statute also directs the Bureau to consider progress toward a high school diploma or equivalent. In Barber v. Thomas, the Supreme Court sustained the Bureau's practice of calculating the credit against time actually served rather than the nominal sentence, so the effective benefit falls short of 54 days per imposed year: a perfectly behaved prisoner serving ten years earns 470 days rather than 540. The ceiling is modest by design, roughly a 15 percent reduction at best, a fraction of what parole could once accomplish, so that the punishment announced in the courtroom resembles the punishment inflicted.

### Q: What was the 100-to-1 crack to powder cocaine ratio?

The Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986 required 100 times as much powder cocaine as crack cocaine to trigger the same mandatory minimum: five grams of crack or 500 grams of powder brought a five-year minimum, and 50 grams of crack or five kilograms of powder brought ten years. The 1988 Act made first-time simple possession of crack punishable by a mandatory minimum, the only drug so treated. The Sentencing Commission embedded the ratio in the Drug Quantity Table, then spent more than a decade telling Congress it was indefensible, recommending its elimination in 1995, a 20-to-1 replacement in 2001, and documenting stark racial impact: in 2002, 81 percent of crack trafficking offenders were African-American, and the average crack term was 119 months against 78 for powder. Congress rejected the Commission's 1995 amendment proposal and finally narrowed the statutory ratio to roughly 18-to-1 in the Fair Sentencing Act of 2010.

### Q: Did the Sentencing Reform Act reduce unwarranted disparity?

The Commission's 2004 assessment found real but limited progress. Primary judge effects, the tendency of some judges to be harsher or more lenient across all cases, were reduced by between a third and a half, and matched-group studies showed narrower sentence distributions under the guidelines, a finding the General Accounting Office replicated while cautioning that the evidence was insufficient to establish the reduction clearly. But disparity migrated rather than vanished. Regional differences in drug sentences increased, and discretion shifted to charging and plea decisions that determine which mandatory minimums apply. Substantial assistance departures, the escape hatch from mandatory minimums, fell unevenly along racial lines: the Commission found no offense-related explanation for the gap. Later research found Black men still received sentences 19.1 percent longer than similarly situated white men in fiscal years 2012 through 2016, and 13.4 percent longer in 2017 through 2021. Inter-judge variation narrowed; systemic disparity persisted.

### Q: What happened to the United States Parole Commission after parole was abolished?

The Act scheduled the Parole Commission for elimination on November 1, 1992, five years after the guidelines took effect, but the phaseout proved impossible. Thousands of offenders sentenced before November 1, 1987 remained in custody with constitutionally protected parole eligibility, and no other federal agency could assume the function. Congress extended the Commission to 1997, then to 2002 with a reduced size, then in three-year increments, with a 2020 extension setting expiration at November 1, 2022. The Act's tripwire provision, requiring release dates for old-law inmates three to six months before each scheduled expiration, forced the recurring extensions: the Department of Justice warned that letting the authorization lapse would compel release dates for prisoners whose parole decisions were unresolved. The Commission's residual jurisdiction also grew to cover District of Columbia offenders after the 1997 D.C. Revitalization Act transferred District corrections functions to the federal government.

### Q: How did mandatory minimums interact with the guidelines?

Mandatory minimums enacted in 1986 and 1988 operate beside the guideline ranges and frequently displace them. Under guidelines section 5G1.1(b), when the guidelines yield a range below the statutory minimum, the minimum becomes the guideline sentence, and the floor is not advisory. The Commission built around this arrangement, setting the Drug Quantity Table so that the five- and ten-year minimums correspond to base offense levels 26 and 32, with ranges beginning just above the statutory floors. The consequence is structural: guideline reform alone cannot move many sentences, because the floors are statutory and the Commission cannot lower a statute. The only routes beneath a minimum are the safety valve for certain low-level nonviolent drug offenders, added in 1994, and sentences below the minimum on government motion for substantial assistance under 18 U.S.C. 3553(e). A 1991 Commission report found the minimums were not applied uniformly, creating the unwarranted disparity the guidelines were meant to cure.

### Q: What is the parsimony principle in federal sentencing?

The parsimony principle comes from the opening instruction of 18 U.S.C. 3553(a): the court must impose a sentence sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes of sentencing. The sentence should do the work the purposes demand and no more. The principle survived Booker's transformation of the guidelines from mandatory to advisory: the remedial opinion kept the instruction that courts impose sentences sufficient but not greater than necessary to serve the statutory purposes. It played a central role in Kimbrough v. United States (2007), where the district court called a crack cocaine guideline range greater than necessary and imposed the statutory minimum instead; the Supreme Court held that a judge does not abuse discretion by concluding in a particular case that a guideline yields a sentence greater than necessary. The principle thus operates as the statute's built-in ceiling on punishment, even as the guidelines supply its starting benchmark.
