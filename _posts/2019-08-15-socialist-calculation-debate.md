The socialist calculation debate is usually reported as a single argument with a single winner, and that report is the first thing a careful reader has to unlearn. Two distinct objections were advanced in sequence, by different men, in different decades, against different targets. The first held that a socialist economy could not calculate: without private ownership of the means of production there would be no prices for producer goods, and without such prices no common measure by which alternative uses of a resource could be compared. The second held something subtler and harder: that even if prices could be constructed, the knowledge an allocating authority would need does not exist in any form that could be delivered to it. The two objections require different answers, and the celebrated answer to the first does not touch the second. Almost every confident verdict on this debate, in either direction, is produced by merging the two objections into one and then grading a reply against a challenge it was never built to meet.

![Two columns of a seminar ledger showing the two objections table, price tickets and a telegraph key, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-104.webp)

This article keeps the objections apart from its first page to its last. It states the original claim in the precise form its author gave it, including the two precisions that popular accounts drop: the original claim was confined to producer goods, and to calculation rather than motivation. It then sets out the proposed solution, the trial and error method associated above all with Oskar Lange, in the strong form its author intended, statue joke and all. It follows the argument as it changed hands and changed character across the two decades that followed, when Friedrich Hayek restated the difficulty as a problem of knowledge rather than of arithmetic. And it closes with the two observations that discipline every verdict: that the question of who won depends entirely on which objection is being graded, and that the proposed solution was never implemented by any socialist state, so the fate of those states cannot be entered as evidence about it.

The debate matters beyond its own history because it is the cleanest case in modern economics of an argument that moved while everyone was watching. The challenge of 1920 was answered, formally and on its own terms, within two decades. By the time the answer arrived, the challenge had been reformulated by its own side into something the answer did not address. Partisans of each camp then spent half a century claiming victory, and both claims were, in a sense this article will make exact, true: true of different objections. The shifted objection, the migration of the argument from a claim about the impossibility of calculation to a claim about the dispersal of knowledge, is the key that makes the whole record intelligible, and it is the claim around which everything below is organised.

## Two objections under one name: the calculation debate separated

Begin with the separation itself, since everything else in the article hangs on it. The calculation objection, as Ludwig von Mises stated it in 1920, is a claim about the existence of a measuring instrument. A modern economy commits resources to competing uses: steel to rails or to ships, labour to mining or to milling, land to wheat or to housing. Choosing rationally among such alternatives requires comparing their values in a common unit, and in a market economy that unit is supplied by money prices, which arise from actual exchanges between owners. Abolish private ownership of producer goods, Mises argued, and the exchanges cease; where nothing is exchanged, no price forms; where no price forms, the planner who must decide between the railway and the ship is comparing quantities of unlike things with no scale on which to weigh them. The objection is not that planners would be lazy, corrupt, or ill intentioned. It is that they would be innumerate in a specific and incurable way, however devoted and however intelligent.

The knowledge objection, as Hayek developed it through the nineteen thirties and stated canonically in 1945, grants for the sake of argument that prices of a sort could be announced and equations of a sort could be written. Its claim concerns the raw material any such apparatus would have to work on. The information relevant to allocation, Hayek argued, is not a stock of statistics waiting in filing cabinets. It is dispersed among millions of participants, much of it local, fleeting, and tacit, and it is generated by the very process of acting in markets rather than existing beforehand, available for collection. A central board cannot be given this knowledge, because in large part it does not exist in a form that could be given. The price system matters, on this account, not because it computes an optimum that a sufficiently large office could compute instead, but because it lets each participant adjust to changes he knows nothing about, guided by a handful of signals.

Hold those two statements side by side and the shape of the whole controversy becomes visible. An answer that supplies the missing measuring instrument answers Mises. It does nothing for Hayek's point unless it also shows how the board acquires, continuously and at the speed of events, the dispersed material the instrument is supposed to process. The trial and error solution, examined below, does the first of these things with genuine elegance. Whether it does the second is the question on which the debate's second life turned, and the answer given by its critics was that the solution presupposes exactly what the second objection denies: that the centre can observe the relevant surpluses and shortages, promptly and accurately enough to act on them. The sections that follow take each element in turn, in the order the participants produced them, and grade each claim only against the objection it was actually answering.

One further discipline governs the article throughout, and it concerns comparison. Arguments about planned and market allocation do not have the same force in every domain, and an unqualified comparison across all domains is not a comparison at all. Moving standardised bulk commodities along established routes is one problem; supplying consumer goods whose appeal shifts with fashion and season is another; committing resources to infrastructure whose returns arrive across generations is a third. Wherever the article weighs the two systems against each other, it names the domain it is weighing them in, and the reader should hold it to that standard, because the participants themselves were not always so careful, and some of the debate's confusion is traceable to verdicts reached in one domain being quoted as verdicts about all three.

## Before the challenge: the formal possibility conceded in advance

The debate is often introduced as though the question it asks had been posed for the first time in 1920, by the man who answered it in the negative. The record is more interesting. By 1920 the formal structure of the problem had already been described, and its formal solubility had already been asserted, by writers working inside the mainstream of economic theory. The challenge of that year was powerful precisely because it did not dispute the formalism. It disputed whether the formalism could ever be fed.

The central figure in this prehistory is Enrico Barone, whose essay on the ministry of production in the collectivist state appeared in an Italian journal in 1908. Barone asked what a collectivist ministry would have to do in order to allocate resources as a competitive economy does, and his answer was austere: it would have to solve, in substance, the same system of equations that describes a competitive equilibrium. The conditions for maximum collective welfare could be written down for an economy without private property in producer goods, just as they could for an economy built on such property. The categories of price, wage, rent, and interest would reappear inside the collectivist state under whatever names the ministry chose to give them, because they are not ornaments of capitalism but the shape any rational allocation must take when resources are scarce and uses compete.

### What did the early paper establish before the debate began?

It established that the equations describing an efficient allocation could be written down for a collectivist economy just as for a competitive one, so the formal problem was soluble in principle. What it did not establish was any procedure by which a ministry could discover the numbers those equations required.

That distinction, between writing the equations and discovering their terms, is the hinge on which the next forty years turned, and Barone himself saw both sides of it. His essay is remembered for the concession, and the concession was real: after 1908, no informed participant could claim that socialist allocation was a logical absurdity, a contradiction in terms. The system of conditions an efficient collectivist allocation would have to satisfy existed on paper, and a socialist theorist could point to it. But Barone was equally clear, in the same essay, that solving the system in practice was another matter entirely. The ministry could not determine the coefficients a priori, from its armchair; it would have to experiment, and experiment on a scale whose waste he contemplated with something like horror. The formal possibility and the practical difficulty were both present at the creation, in a single text, and the subsequent debate can be read as the two sides of Barone's essay separating into two camps, each quoting the half it needed.

Barone was not alone in the prehistory. Vilfredo Pareto, whose work on general equilibrium supplied much of the mathematics in the background, had likewise observed that the equations of an optimum could be stated for a collectivist organisation of production. Friedrich von Wieser, writing on value a generation earlier, had argued that the categories of valuation would persist under communism, since scarcity does not consult constitutions. And in 1902 the Dutch economist Nicolaas Pierson had posed the value problem for a socialist society in a form close enough to the later challenge that Mises, to his credit, acknowledged the anticipation when he came to write. The problem of how a commonwealth without markets would value its resources was, by 1920, an old and recognised difficulty, discussed by men who cannot be recruited into either later camp without violence to the record.

What none of these precursors had done was press the difficulty into a verdict. Pierson posed the problem and left it as a problem. Barone described the ministry's task and doubted its feasibility while affirming its logic. The step from difficulty to impossibility, from a hard question to a closed one, was the step taken in 1920, and it was taken with full knowledge of the prehistory. Mises knew Barone's result and did not deny it. His claim was that the paper solution concealed the operative question: where, in an economy without exchange of producer goods, would the numbers come from? The equations of equilibrium describe a state in which every price is already known. A market economy arrives at its prices by a process, the higgling of owners risking their own property. A collectivist ministry, handed the equations as a description of its destination, still lacks the process. That is the form in which the challenge entered the debate, and keeping it in view prevents the commonest of all errors in this literature, the supposition that the calculation objection denied something that had been proved in 1908. It denied nothing of the kind. It relocated the dispute from logic to genesis: not whether the destination can be described, but whether there is a road.

## The challenge: no prices where nothing is exchanged

The essay that opened the debate proper appeared in 1920, in a German journal of social science, under a title that translates as economic calculation in the socialist commonwealth. Its author, Ludwig von Mises, was a Viennese economist of the liberal school, writing in a city where socialist administration was not a speculation but a municipal fact, and where the experience of the war economy, with its central boards and its allocations in kind, was fresh in every economist's mind. Two years later he expanded the argument into a book on the socialist commonwealth that carried the case to a wider public, and it is the essay's core contention, sharpened rather than altered in the book, that the debate inherited.

The contention can be stated as a chain with four links, and the article will keep to the chain because most misreporting of the debate begins by breaking it. First link: rational choice among alternative uses of a resource requires a common measure of the values at stake. Second link: in a developed economy that measure is money price, and money prices for producer goods arise only where such goods are actually exchanged. Third link: exchange of producer goods requires that they be owned, since a transfer between two offices of the same state, neither of which can gain or lose by the terms, is an administrative movement and not an exchange in the sense that generates a price. Fourth link: a socialist commonwealth, defined by common ownership of the instruments of production, therefore lacks money prices for producer goods, and with them lacks the instrument by which the relative urgency of competing uses could be compared. The conclusion followed with the remorselessness its author intended: "Where there is no free market, there is no pricing mechanism", and where there is no pricing mechanism, allocation among producer goods is not rational calculation but groping.

### What did the original calculation objection claim?

that without private ownership of producer goods there are no exchanges of them, therefore no money prices for them, and therefore no way to compare the value of alternative uses of a resource. The claim concerned producer goods specifically and calculation rather than motivation.

Notice what the chain does not require. It does not require that socialist administrators be stupid, lazy, or corrupt; a board of saints and geniuses faces the same missing instrument. It does not require that nothing be produced; goods would be produced, as they are produced in any administered system, by instruction, by habit, and by guess. It does not even require that consumer goods be unpriced, a point the next section takes up in detail. The claim is narrow enough to be precise and strong enough to be interesting: a specific input to rational decision, the money price of producer goods, is generated by a specific institution, exchange between owners, and cannot be generated where that institution has been abolished by definition. Critics who answer a broader and cruder claim, that socialism would produce nothing or that socialists cannot count, are answering a pamphlet rather than the essay.

The essay also engaged, and dismissed, the main alternative its author could see on the horizon of 1920: calculation in kind. The war economies had allocated by physical quantities, and Otto Neurath, the most systematic advocate of a moneyless economy, argued from that experience that a socialist administration could plan in natura, comparing tons, hours, and hectares directly without the mediation of money. Mises replied that physical quantities are exactly what cannot be compared across kinds. A plan that needs steel, coal, labour, and time in various combinations for various ends must reduce these to a common denominator before the word rational can attach to its choices, and tons do not reduce to hours by any operation arithmetic knows. Barter between goods in kind multiplies the comparisons beyond any office's capacity, and it is precisely this multiplication that money prices exist to collapse. The reply has worn well as a piece of logic. What it did not yet engage, because its author had not yet been answered, was the possibility that prices might be supplied to a socialist economy by an act of administration rather than by exchange, which is the possibility the next decade produced.

It is worth pausing on the tone of the original challenge, because the tone explains part of its reception. Mises did not present the calculation problem as a difficulty to be weighed among others, to be set against the moral attractions of socialism in some general balance. He presented it as a demonstration that the thing proposed could not be done at all, and he drew the conclusion in the largest terms available to him, treating the socialist project as refuted not in its ethics but in its engineering. That absolutism was a provocation, and it worked as provocations do: it forced a generation of socialist economists to think seriously about the economics of the order they were proposing, some of them for the first time. The man who would do most to answer the challenge said as much himself, in the joke this article reaches in due course. But absolutism also fixed the debate's terms in a way that later distorted its reporting, because a claim stated as total impossibility invites a reply stated as total vindication, and the space between those two totals, where the interesting truth about the debate resides, was left for later writers to map.

## Two precisions the popular version drops

Every durable argument acquires a popular version, and the popular version of the calculation objection is wrong in two specific ways that matter for grading everything that follows. Restoring the precisions is not pedantry. The proposed solution was designed against the precise claim, and it can only be assessed against that claim; a reader carrying the popular version will judge the solution either too weak or too strong, and will misjudge it either way.

The first precision concerns the goods at issue. Mises's chain runs through producer goods: machines, raw materials, intermediate products, land, the instruments with which other goods are made. Consumer goods stand outside the chain's grip, because nothing in the socialist programme as its mainstream understood it required abolishing personal property in the goods of consumption, and where individuals buy and sell, or even merely choose among goods offered at posted prices with their wages, prices for consumer goods can form and can register the pattern of preference. The planner of the original objection is thus not pictured as ignorant of what consumers want. He is pictured as unable to cost the means of giving it to them, unable to say whether satisfying a want one way rather than another husbanded or wasted the community's stock of instruments. A debate about loaves can proceed; a debate about whether the bakery's oven should have been a loom cannot.

### Why did the objection concern producer goods rather than consumer goods?

Because consumer goods could still be bought and sold by individuals under the proposed arrangements, their prices would form in exchange and register preference. Producer goods, transferred between state organs without owners on either side, would change hands without any exchange in which a price could arise.

The second precision concerns the faculty at issue. The objection is about calculation, not motivation. It does not say that managers without ownership will shirk, that workers without wages graded to effort will idle, or that innovators without profit will sleep. Those are incentive claims, and they belong to a neighbouring controversy with its own literature, treated elsewhere in this series in the article on [the incentive problem in socialism](/2019/11/01/incentive-problem-socialism/). Mises's 1920 argument deliberately brackets all of them. Grant the socialist commonwealth a population of tireless and public spirited administrators; grant its managers the diligence of owners and its workers the zeal of volunteers; the objection stands untouched, because diligence does not manufacture a measuring rod. Confusing the two claims has been the single most productive source of cross purposes in the debate's long life: defenders of planning answer the incentive claim with evidence of motivation under public ownership, declare the objection met, and never notice that the objection they were set is still standing where it stood.

### How does a claim about calculation differ from a claim about motivation?

A motivation claim says that people without ownership will not work, save, or take risks as the system requires. A calculation claim says something colder: that even a population of perfectly willing administrators, working with complete devotion, would lack the numerical instrument needed to choose between alternative uses of resources.

There is a third precision, quieter than the other two, that the article flags here and develops later. The objection of 1920 is directed at a commonwealth that has abolished exchange in producer goods entirely. It derives its force from the completeness of the abolition: no remnant market, no tolerated private production in the instruments of production, no foreign price lists close enough to borrow. Arrangements that retain some exchanges, some private or cooperative production of instruments, some importation of price information from surrounding markets, stand partly outside the chain's reach, and the degree to which they stand outside it is a matter of how much genuine exchange they retain. This is why the objection, for all its absolutist tone, functions in careful hands as a gradient rather than a gate: it presses hardest on the most complete abolition, and its pressure relaxes as real exchange re-enters. The proposed solution exploited exactly this gradient, by proposing to reintroduce the forms of exchange: prices, consumer and labour markets, accounting in money, while withholding the ownership that was supposed to be their source. Whether the forms can do the work without the substance is the question the nineteen thirties asked.

## The proposed solution: prices announced, then corrected by trial and error

The answer that reorganised the debate arrived in the middle nineteen thirties, but its essential device had been sketched a few years earlier, in an unlikely venue. In his presidential address to the American Economic Association at the end of 1928, published the following year, Fred M. Taylor proposed that a socialist state need not solve its valuation problem by pure reason. It could proceed as markets proceed, by announcing prices and correcting them in the light of what happened next. The state would set accounting prices for factors of production, observe whether at those prices the quantities demanded and supplied matched, and revise. Taylor called the method one of trial and error, and the phrase stuck to the whole subsequent construction. The address was brief and its author did not build the full edifice, but the device at its heart, the parametric price adjusted by observed imbalance, is the load bearing idea of the solution that followed.

### Who proposed parametric prices before the famous solution, and in what setting?

An American economist proposed them in a presidential address to his professional association late in the nineteen twenties, suggesting that a socialist state could announce prices, watch the resulting shortages and surpluses, and adjust them as a market does, without owning the adjustment process to any private hand.

The edifice was built by Oskar Lange, a Polish economist then working in the United States, in a two part essay on the economic theory of socialism published in 1936 and 1937. Lange's construction accepts the framework of the challenge more completely than most of the challenge's other targets did, and that acceptance is the source of its strength. He grants that rational allocation requires prices in the broad sense, indices of the terms on which alternatives can be substituted for one another. He grants that these indices must guide the decisions of individual plant managers, who cannot wait for a ministry to compose each establishment's plan from first principles. He grants even the form of the equilibrium conditions Barone had written down. His disagreement is confined to a single point, the genesis of the prices. Mises had held that prices for producer goods can arise only from exchange between private owners. Lange replied that a price, functionally considered, is a term of transformation announced to decision makers, and that a central planning board can announce such terms without owning anything, then correct them by the same evidence a market uses: the physical surplus or shortage that appears when the announced terms are wrong.

The construction's moving parts deserve to be set out slowly, because later argument turned on each of them. Consumers remain free: they spend money incomes on consumer goods in shops whose prices they take as given, and their choices register demand exactly as in a market economy. Workers remain free in the choice of occupation, so wages form in a labour market and register the relative attractiveness and scarcity of kinds of work. Enterprises are publicly owned, and each is run by a manager who treats the prices announced by the board as parameters, data he may not bargain over but must obey in a specific way. The board announces prices for producer goods, capital goods, and resources. It watches the consequences. Where a good is in shortage, its stocks falling and queues forming, the board raises its price; where a good accumulates unsold, the board lowers it. Iteration by iteration, the price list gropes toward the configuration at which quantities offered and quantities sought coincide. The board, in Lange's deliberately provocative formulation, performs the function of the market, and performs it, he suggested, with advantages: it can see the whole field, take account of effects no individual trader internalises, and adjust with a deliberateness that the anarchy of production lacks.

### What two rules were the managers of enterprises instructed to follow?

They were told to combine their factors of production so that the average cost of each unit was as low as possible, and to expand or contract output until the cost of one more unit equalled the price the planning board had set, treating that price as a parameter beyond their influence.

The two rules are the construction's transmission belt, and they repay attention. The first rule, minimise average cost, forces each manager to substitute among factors exactly as a competitive entrepreneur does, using the announced prices as the weights: a factor whose announced price rises will be economised, a factor whose price falls will be used more freely, and this substitution is what connects the board's price list to the physical allocation of resources across the whole economy. The second rule, produce where price equals marginal cost, fixes the scale of each plant's output, expanding production while an extra unit costs less than the announced price and contracting it when the extra unit costs more. A manager obeying both rules, in an economy whose board adjusts prices toward balance, reproduces by instruction the outcome that competition reproduces by survival. Lange's claim was not that managers would be angels. It was that the rules, plus accounting in money, plus the board's revisions, made the angels unnecessary: the system's information circulated in prices, and obedience to the rules was auditable in accounts.

One element of the construction is often omitted in summary and should not be, because it answers the objection's strongest intuitive form. The rate of interest, and with it the allocation of investment, was not left to chance or to political appetite. The board was to set a rate of interest as it set other prices, raising it when the demand for capital funds outran the supply the community's saving provided, lowering it when funds accumulated unclaimed, and the total of investment was to be governed by that price rather than decreed in physical terms. Whether a board could in practice hold an interest rate against the importunity of ministries wanting investment is a political question the construction brackets; within the construction, the capital market's function is performed by the same device as every other market's function, an announced price corrected by observed imbalance. The socialist economy of the model is thus not a command economy with prices painted on. It is an economy of genuine markets for consumer goods and labour, genuine accounting everywhere, and a simulated market, parametric and iterative, exactly at the point where Mises had said simulation was impossible: the pricing of the means of production.

Lange knew precisely whose objection he was answering, and he marked the debt in the most quoted joke in the literature of planning: "a statue of Professor Mises ought to occupy an honourable place" in the great hall of the ministry of socialisation, he wrote, since the challenge had forced socialists to abandon a naive faith that allocation under socialism would somehow arrange itself, and to construct the economics of their system with the rigour the challenge demanded. The gratitude was genuine and the wit was deliberate.

### What did the solution's author propose should stand in the planning ministry?

He proposed, in print and with undisguised pleasure, that a statue of his Austrian opponent should stand in the hall of the ministry of socialisation, because the opponent's challenge had forced socialists to think seriously about the economics of their own system for the first time in its history.

Lange was not the construction's only builder, and the solution as the profession received it was a collective work of the middle thirties. Abba Lerner, in a series of papers and later in a systematic treatise on the economics of control, developed the marginal rules with unmatched clarity and pressed them toward their logical form, insisting that the rules for efficient allocation were the same under any ownership and that the case for socialism stood or fell elsewhere, on distribution and monopoly. H. D. Dickinson, who had earlier sketched a socialist economy working through a kind of organised exchange, gave the model an institutional dress in his book on the economics of socialism at the decade's end. E. F. M. Durbin and others carried versions of the argument into the British debate. The family resemblance among these constructions matters more than their differences: all of them answer Mises by separating the price from the owner, by treating price as a function that an administration can perform, and by relying on observed physical imbalance as the signal that drives revision. The solution the profession debated, credited, and largely accepted was this family, with Lange's essay as its canonical statement.

Not every socialist economist welcomed the construction, and the dissent from within is part of the record the article must keep. Maurice Dobb, the Cambridge Marxist, regarded the whole market socialist design with suspicion, as a surrender of planning's essence to the categories of the system it was meant to replace. On Dobb's view, the point of planning was to decide the great proportions of the economy, the rate of investment, the balance between industry and agriculture, the location of new capacity, by conscious collective choice in physical and social terms, not to simulate a market whose outcomes the planners had supposedly transcended. A board that spends its life mimicking the price system has, for Dobb, accepted the enemy's criterion of rationality. The disagreement is instructive because it reveals what the trial and error solution is and is not. It is a proof that allocation can be rational, on the equilibrium conditions' own definition of rationality, under public ownership of producer goods. It is not a vindication of planning as its more ambitious advocates conceived it, and its author, to do him justice, never claimed that it settled the larger questions of dynamics, innovation, or political power that surround the economic core. Those questions were waiting, and one of them was about to be asked by the objection's new owner.

### What must a trial and error board be able to observe?

It must observe stocks: whether goods accumulate unsold in warehouses or vanish from shelves faster than they are replaced. Those accumulations and shortages are its only signals, and the method works only if the signals arrive quickly, honestly, and in a form the board can translate into revised prices.

That last requirement, stated innocently inside the solution's own exposition, is where the second objection would fasten. The board's method is a loop: announce, observe, revise. Everything depends on the observation step, on the surpluses and shortages being visible, legible, and timely, and on their being sufficient statistics for the revisions required. In the formal model they are, because the model endows the board with the economy's data as a premise. The question Hayek would press is whether the premise describes anything in the world: whether the relevant facts present themselves to any observer as stocks and queues at all, or whether they live somewhere else entirely, in the expectations, hunches, and local circumstances of the people doing the producing and the buying, upstream of any signal a board could read. The solution had answered the objection of 1920. The objection was already changing into the one it could not answer.

## The two objections table

The article has now assembled both objections and the proposed answer, and this is the point at which to fix them in a single view. The two objections table below is the article's findable artifact, the instrument a reader can carry away and apply to any account of the debate encountered elsewhere. Its columns are chosen to force the separation the article defends. Each objection is stated in its own terms; the answer that would satisfy it is specified independently of the answer that was actually offered; and the verdict column records what follows when the two are compared without conflation. Read across a row and you have one argument, complete. Read down the final column and you have the article's thesis.

Two features of the table deserve emphasis before the article proceeds. The first is that the rows are not symmetrical in their fates, and the asymmetry is the finding. The calculation objection was answered, and answered by the means its own logic invited: it demanded a measuring instrument, and an instrument was designed. The knowledge objection has not been answered, and the reason is structural rather than accidental: it denies the availability of the input every proposed instrument requires, so each new instrument proposed against it must begin by assuming the input exists. The second feature is that the table's final column assigns each of the two famous victory claims to its proper row. The claim that the debate refuted socialism is a claim about the first row inflated into a claim about both. The claim that the debate vindicated socialism is the same row's claim read in the opposite direction, inflated the same way. Deflate both to their rows and both become true, modest, and compatible, which is the condition the article calls the shifted objection and the next sections establish in detail.

## The shift: the objection changes hands and changes form

The transformation of the objection was not a single event but a migration, spread across a decade, visible in the sequence of one man's publications and in the changing centre of gravity of the argument as a whole. Friedrich Hayek entered the controversy as the editor of a collective volume on collectivist economic planning in 1935, which made the original challenge available in English and surrounded it with contributions pressing its case. At that stage Hayek's own emphasis still lay close to his teacher's: the practical impossibility of the calculations a planning board would face, the millions of equations, the sheer administrative enormity of solving them. The emphasis was quantitative. The board could not compute fast enough. It is the emphasis of a man still fighting on the first objection's ground, and it was vulnerable to exactly the reply Lange was preparing, since a board that does not solve equations at all, but gropes by trial and error, is not refuted by the observation that the equations are numerous.

The migration begins in Hayek's 1937 essay on economics and knowledge, one of the most consequential methodological papers of the century, and it begins as a question about his own side's theory. The pure logic of choice, Hayek argued, treats knowledge as given: the equilibrium of the textbooks describes a state in which every participant's plans are consistent with every other's, on the assumption that the relevant facts are known to all of them in the form the theory requires. But the empirical problem of a real economy is precisely how such consistency could ever come about, how the knowledge that is in fact divided among millions of minds, no one of whom holds more than a fragment, could be brought into sufficient alignment for plans to mesh. Equilibrium analysis assumes the answer. The economic problem of society, restated in this light, is not the allocation of given resources among given ends by a mind that surveys both. It is the utilisation of knowledge which is not given to anyone in its totality. With that sentence the objection had changed its object: from the absence of prices to the dispersal of the material prices would have to summarise.

The 1940 essay on the competitive solution applied the new standpoint directly to Lange's construction, and its criticisms have the precision of the new standpoint throughout. Trial and error, Hayek observed, is a fair description of how a market adjusts when the underlying data are stable enough for the iterations to converge; but the data of a real economy are not stable, they change continuously and at innumerable points, so the board would be solving for a configuration that had ceased to exist before its revisions took effect. The managers of the model, moreover, are instructed to treat prices as parameters, yet the real work of enterprise consists in deciding exactly the things the model holds fixed: what to produce, in what quality, by which of several not yet costed methods, against which anticipated shift in demand. A construction that assigns those decisions to no one has not solved the problem of direction; it has defined the problem away by assuming its preconditions. And the board's information, on inspection, is the model's gift to itself: the surpluses and shortages the board observes are aggregates, arrived after the fact, stripped of the local detail that would tell it why a shortage appeared and whether it will persist.

### How does the knowledge objection differ from the calculation objection in what it denies?

The first denies that a common measure for comparing alternatives can exist without exchange between owners. The second grants that measures and equations can be constructed, and denies instead that the information any such construction needs can be gathered into one place, because much of it exists only in use.

The canonical statement came in 1945, in an essay on the use of knowledge in society that has since become one of the most reprinted pieces in all of economics. Its argument can be carried in three movements. First, the knowledge that matters for economic decisions is overwhelmingly not scientific knowledge, not the contents of textbooks and statistics, but what its author called "the knowledge of the particular circumstances of time and place": the half empty lorry returning from a delivery, the machine that will stand idle next week, the local surplus of a material that a man on the spot can put to a use no distant office would think of. Second, this knowledge cannot be transmitted to a central authority in statistical form, because statistics are constructed by abstracting from the minor differences between items, and it is exactly those minor differences, the ones the statistician discards, on which good decisions on the spot depend. Third, the price system is the evolved device that solves the problem thus posed: it compresses the relevant information into indices that each actor can consult without understanding their causes, so that when tin grows scarcer, users who know nothing of mines or of new uses for tin economise on it anyway, guided by a figure. The system works, in Hayek's famous image, as a kind of system of telecommunications, and its marvel is how little any participant needs to know in order to act appropriately on changes he cannot see.

### What kind of knowledge did the canonical statement say the price system economises?

It said the knowledge that matters is of particular circumstances of time and place: the empty wagon returning, the machine standing idle for an hour, the local glut and the local dearth. Prices let strangers act on such knowledge without possessing it, by watching a few indices and adjusting their plans.

With the 1945 essay the shift was complete, and the article can now name what had happened with the precision the namable claim requires. The shifted objection is this: the argument moved from the impossibility of calculation to the impossibility of centralising knowledge, and since the proposed solution answered the first and not the second, both the claim that socialism was refuted and the claim that the refutation was answered are true of different objections. Every element of the confusion that surrounds this debate in the textbooks flows from ignoring the shift. Accounts that celebrate Lange's victory are grading the reply against the 1920 challenge, on which it genuinely scores. Accounts that celebrate the Austrian victory are grading the challenge of 1945 against replies designed for its predecessor, on which it genuinely stands. The debate did not have a winner because the debate, as a single contest, ceased to exist in the middle nineteen thirties; what continued under its name were two contests, sharing a cast, a vocabulary, and almost nothing else.

Two clarifications keep the naming honest. The first is that the shift was a migration, not a repudiation: Hayek never withdrew the calculation objection, and Mises, in his own later work, absorbed elements of the knowledge theme into his broader case, treating the market as a process rather than a state. The participants themselves experienced the change as a deepening. It is the article, looking back, that insists on the separation, because grading requires it. The second clarification is that the shift was not a retreat to weaker ground. The knowledge objection is the stronger of the two, in the precise sense that it survives the concession of everything the first objection's opponents established. Grant the equations, grant the announced prices, grant the rules and the accounting and the board's good faith: the question of where the board's information comes from, and whether it can arrive in usable form at usable speed, remains exactly where it was. An objection that concedes the whole of the opposing construction and still stands is not a fallback position. It is the argument's centre of gravity, discovered late.

## The knowledge objection at full strength

The discipline of this series requires that the strongest objection to an article's governing verdict be stated at full strength, in its author's own terms, before any answer is attempted, and the requirement is doubly binding here because the article's verdict is that this objection stands. A verdict that an argument is unanswered is worthless if the argument has been understated to make the verdict easy. What follows is therefore the knowledge objection as strongly as it can be put, assembled from its author's statements across the nineteen thirties and forties and from the logic those statements share, and the reader who finishes the section unpersuaded by the article's later qualifications will at least have met the objection itself rather than a summary of it.

The objection begins from a claim about what economic knowledge is. The data of the textbooks, the given resources, the given tastes, the given techniques, are given to no one. What exists in the world is a vast scatter of particular knowledge, held by particular people, in forms fitted to their situations: the shipper who knows that a vessel is sailing half empty and can carry a cargo at little extra cost; the estate agent who knows of a property mispriced because its owner must sell this month; the foreman who knows that a machine's temperamental fault can be coaxed through one more production run; the shopkeeper who knows that a line will sell in his street that would not sell two streets away. This knowledge is not a sample of some central stock. It is the stock, and its characteristics are the whole difficulty. Much of it is knowledge of the fleeting moment, valuable for hours and worthless by next quarter. Much of it is tacit, embodied in skills and recognitions the holder could not state as propositions if his life depended on the stating. And much of it does not exist at all until the moment of action creates it, when a man trying something discovers what he could not have reported in advance, because there was nothing yet to report.

From that claim the objection draws its conclusion about institutions. Knowledge of this character cannot be collected. A board that wishes to allocate rationally must first be informed, and the channels of information available to it, returns, statistics, reports, inspections, transmit only what can be articulated, aggregated, and sent, which is to say they transmit a residue from which the decisive particulars have been filtered out by the very act of transmission. The man on the spot cannot cable his judgement; he can only act on it, and he will act on it only if the institutional arrangement leaves the decision, and some share of its consequences, with him. This is the sense in which the objection says the knowledge does not exist in collectible form. It is not hidden, the way a document is hidden in a drawer. It is unformed, the way a remark is unformed before the conversation that draws it out, and the conversation that draws out economic knowledge is exchange itself, undertaken by people acting on their own account, at their own risk, with their own resources in their hands. Remove the exchange and you do not merely lose the knowledge's transmission. You lose a large part of its generation, the discoveries that acting produces and that no survey, however diligent, can substitute for, because the survey's questions are composed from what is already known to be worth asking.

The objection's final movement concerns what prices are for. On the calculation objection's telling, prices are measuring instruments, and the question is who can manufacture them. On the knowledge objection's telling, prices are something more economical and more strange: they are a device for making most knowledge unnecessary. The user of tin who sees its price rise does not need to know whether a mine has flooded or a new use has been found; the figure instructs him to economise, and he does, and the adjustment propagates through thousands of such decisions without any mind comprehending the whole. The price system thus coordinates the fragments of knowledge without centralising them, leaving each fragment where it lives, in the head and hands of the person positioned to use it, and extracting from it only the thin signal that others need. A planning board, by contrast, must centralise in order to decide at all, and centralisation is the one operation the material cannot survive. That is the objection at full strength: not that the board's arithmetic would be too slow, not that its officials would be too corruptible, but that the world it would need to know is constituted in a way that defeats being known from a centre, and that the only known device for using that world of knowledge whole is the process the board exists to replace.

### Why can dispersed knowledge not simply be reported upward to a board?

Because reporting presupposes that the holder can state what he knows, and much practical knowledge is embodied in habit, timing, and judgement that the holder himself could not write down. What cannot be articulated cannot be transmitted, and what cannot be transmitted cannot be entered into any central calculation.

Two corollaries of the objection deserve statement while it stands at full strength, because they govern the shape of any possible answer. The first is that the objection is comparative in a specific way: it does not claim that markets use knowledge perfectly, only that they use a kind of knowledge that central direction structurally cannot reach, so the relevant comparison is not between an ideal plan and an actual market but between two imperfect users of an imperfectly knowable world, one of which has access to a stratum of fact the other cannot touch. Market outcomes can be wasteful, myopic, and unjust while every word of the objection remains true. The second corollary is that the objection scales with the complexity and pace of the economy it is applied to. In a simple economy of few goods and settled techniques, the stratum of local and tacit knowledge is thin, and a centre's ignorance costs little. In an economy of differentiated products, rapid technical change, and shifting tastes, the stratum is most of the territory. The objection is therefore not a slogan to be applied at uniform strength to every case, and the next section's answer consists largely in mapping where its pressure is weak, which is the only honest way to establish where it is strong.

## Where the objection does not reach: allocation that needs less of that knowledge

An objection stated at full strength earns the right to a discriminating answer, and the discriminating answer to the knowledge objection is a map of its weak ground. There are kinds of allocation problem that do not depend, or depend only slightly, on the stratum of dispersed and tacit knowledge the objection describes. Identifying them is not a debating manoeuvre. It is what the objection itself invites, since its author built the argument on the character of the knowledge involved, and where the knowledge involved is of a different character, public, stable, and statable, the argument's engine does not turn over.

Consider first the allocation problems of settled technique and standardised output. Where a product is homogeneous, its production function well understood, and demand for it stable and predictable in aggregate, the knowledge required for rational allocation is engineering knowledge and statistical knowledge, both of which exist in articulable form and can be centralised without fatal loss. Moving bulk commodities along established routes, grain, coal, ore, oil, through networks whose capacities and costs are documented, is a problem of this kind. So is the expansion of a proven plant design to a second and third site, where the uncertainties that defeat planners have already been burned away by the first site's operation. Electricity systems were in fact dispatched centrally, in market economies as in planned ones, by engineers solving exactly such problems in real time, because the physics of a grid does not hide its data in the tacit judgements of millions of holders. The knowledge objection does not claim these cases, and a defence of planning that confines itself to them is not refuted by it. What the defence may not do is generalise from them, because the economy as a whole is not a grid.

### Which kinds of allocation problem depend least on dispersed local knowledge?

Those with stable and well understood technologies, standardised outputs, and objectives that can be stated in physical terms: moving bulk commodities along known routes, expanding a proven plant design, or mobilising for a single overriding goal. Where the technique is settled and the target is singular, central direction loses least.

Consider next the allocation problems of mobilisation, where a society subordinates allocation to a single overriding objective. War economies in market societies suspended the price mechanism across wide territories and allocated by priority and command, and they did so without the collapse the calculation objection might have predicted, for reasons the knowledge framework itself explains. A single objective collapses the problem of comparing values: when victory outranks every other use, the planner does not need prices to tell him that bombers precede bunting. The technique of the relevant industries was largely settled, the outputs standardised, and the time horizon short enough that the costs of misallocation, though real and documented, could be paid from the stock of slack a peacetime economy carries. Mobilisation shows that central allocation can function, under conditions of singular purpose and settled technique, for a limited period, at a price in waste that the emergency makes acceptable. It does not show that the same apparatus can allocate a peacetime economy's full diversity of goods across years of changing taste and technique, and the participants in the calculation debate who cited the war economies, on either side, were usually careful to say which of those propositions they took the evidence to support.

A third weak ground deserves notice because it is the one modern readers most often reach for: the interior of the large firm. Corporations of continental scale allocate resources internally by administrative decision, across product lines and continents, without internal markets for most of what they move, and they do not collapse for want of calculation. The observation is correct and its limits are instructive. The firm plans inside a surrounding price system that supplies it with the very indices the objection is about: it buys its inputs at market prices, sells its outputs against market tests, borrows capital at a market rate, and can compare its divisions' performance in money terms because every boundary transaction is priced from outside. Its internal planning is an island of administration in an ocean of exchange, and the ocean is doing epistemic work for the island at every point. The example therefore cuts both ways, and in the debate it mostly cut against those who brandished it: it shows administration functioning exactly where, and only where, the knowledge problem has been pre solved by the prices the firm did not have to create.

The map's remaining territory is where the objection presses, and the article marks it as plainly as it marked the weak ground. Wherever the allocation problem concerns goods whose character is not standardised, whose demand turns on taste, novelty, and local circumstance, and whose production methods are themselves in flux, the knowledge involved is precisely the dispersed, tacit, and moment bound kind. There the centre's statistics arrive late and aggregated, the survey's categories lag the reality they describe, and the cost of ignorance is not a rounding error but the substance of the decision.

### In which domain does the knowledge objection press hardest?

It presses hardest where preferences change quickly and products differentiate: consumer goods whose appeal turns on fashion, timing, and local taste. There the relevant knowledge is created and destroyed daily, held by no one for long, and a centre revising prices at intervals is steering by a wake rather than by the water ahead.

Between the weak ground and the hard ground lies the great middle of an actual economy, and honesty requires the article to say what the map shows there. Most allocation in a developed economy is neither grid dispatch nor fashion. It is the steady production of differentiated but familiar goods, by techniques that evolve gradually, for consumers whose tastes drift rather than leap. In that middle territory the knowledge objection operates as a tax on central direction rather than a prohibition: the centre can function, but it functions with systematically worse information than the dispersed alternative, and the losses compound through every substitution its prices get slightly wrong. How heavy the tax is, in which industries, at which scale, are empirical questions the debate's participants mostly did not ask, because both camps preferred the cleaner war of principle. The map this section has drawn is the article's attempt to state the answer the objection actually supports: not that central allocation is impossible, nor that it is unproblematic, but that its competence is domain specific, strongest where knowledge is public and settled, weakest where knowledge is private and fleeting, and burdened between. That answer, however, is an answer about degree and domain. The objection in its general form, as a claim about the economy as a whole, still awaits its answer, and the next section says so without varnish.

## The concession, and the condition that would overturn it

The verdict of this article on the knowledge objection is that, for the general case, it has not been answered. The concession should be made cleanly, because the article has demanded clean concessions from every participant it has examined. The trial and error construction does not answer it, since the construction's observation step presupposes the availability of the knowledge in dispute. The computational proposals of later decades do not answer it, since faster processing of given data leaves untouched the question of how the data, in their decisive local and tacit forms, are to be given. The argument from the large firm does not answer it, for the reason the previous section gave. The argument from mobilisation does not answer it beyond its own narrow conditions. What has been established against the objection is a map of domains where its force is small, and a demonstration that economies are mixtures of such domains with others where its force is large. That is a genuine intellectual gain, and it converts a slogan into a research programme. It is not an answer to the general claim, and the literature's habit of presenting domain mapping as refutation is one more instance of the conflation this article exists to correct.

Because the cluster to which this article belongs requires every verdict to carry the condition of its own reversal, the defeater is stated here in full. The verdict that the knowledge objection stands would be defeated by a demonstration that the information required for allocation can be generated and updated without the actors' voluntary participation in exchange, or by a working national-scale implementation of a planning model that adjusts to dispersed local change at the speed markets do. Either exhibit would do the work. The first would show that the generation of the relevant knowledge is not bound to the process of exchange itself, that some instrument of observation or elicitation can draw out, continuously and in usable form, what the objection says exists only in the using. The second would show it in the only way that finally settles arguments in this field, by construction and operation, the way the existence of a bridge settles an argument about whether a river can be spanned at a given point. Neither exhibit has been produced. The verdict therefore stands, and it stands in the specific and limited form the article has given it: the objection is unanswered for the general case, answered nowhere, and weakened, legitimately and permanently, in the domains where knowledge is public, stable, and few in kind.

The reader should notice the asymmetry the defeater creates between the article's two rows. The calculation objection's verdict carries no such outstanding condition, because the answering exhibit was produced, in the model of the nineteen thirties, and accepted by the profession that graded it. The knowledge objection's verdict is explicitly provisional, hostage to a demonstration or a construction that the future could in principle supply. That difference in the texture of the two verdicts, one closed and one open, is itself part of the shifted objection's lesson. The debate's first phase ended. Its second phase has not ended, because the condition of its ending has been specified and not met, and an argument whose ending condition is known but unmet is in a different state from an argument that merely continues from habit. It is waiting, and what it is waiting for is exactly stated.

## Who is held to have won: the first telling, in which the solution prevails

Ask who won the calculation debate and the answer depends on when the question is put, because the debate has been graded at least three times by three different juries, and the juries did not agree. The first grading, by the mid-century economic profession, awarded the debate to the solution, and it did so for reasons internal to the profession's own development that a reader must understand before dismissing the verdict as fashion. Economics in those decades was consolidating around the theory of general equilibrium, a framework in which an economy is described by a system of simultaneous equations and a market outcome is a solution to that system. Within such a framework, Lange's construction was not merely plausible; it was almost a corollary. If the market's achievement is a solution to the equations, and if the equations can be stated for a collectivist economy, as Barone had shown before the debate began, then a board that mimics the market's adjustment process stands in the same relation to the solution as the market does. The objection of 1920 appeared, to eyes formed by that framework, to have confused the institutional clothing of the price system with its logical skeleton, and the skeleton had been shown to be wearable by a socialist body.

The verdict's most striking witness was Joseph Schumpeter, no friend of socialism's politics and the century's most celebrated analyst of capitalism's dynamism, who in his 1942 book on capitalism, socialism, and democracy answered the question whether socialism could work with a flat affirmative and treated the calculation difficulty as a problem of logic that had been settled. When the author of the theory of creative destruction declares the blueprint question closed, the profession listens. Abram Bergson's authoritative survey of the welfare economics literature, published in the middle forties, consolidated the judgement: the formal theory of allocation under socialism was a solved chapter, continuous with the theory of allocation anywhere. Textbook writers absorbed the verdict and transmitted it for a generation, and in Samuelson's great textbook the debate appears, where it appears at all, as a historical episode with a known outcome. Generations of students learned that Mises had raised a problem and Lange had solved it, in the way they learned that other famous problems had been raised and solved, and the lesson had the authority of the discipline's whole mid-century apparatus behind it.

### Why did the mid-century profession judge the proposed solution decisive?

Because the profession's own theory of the market was the same system of equations the solution borrowed. If a competitive equilibrium could be described by simultaneous equations, and a board could grope toward the same solution by adjusting prices, then the objection seemed answered inside the framework both sides accepted.

It is important, for the grading this article eventually gives, to see that the mid-century verdict was not stupid. It was the correct verdict on the objection it was grading. The objection of 1920, in its precise form, claimed that without exchange between owners, producer goods could carry no prices, and rational comparison of alternatives would therefore be impossible. Lange supplied prices without owners and rules for their use, and the claim, in that form, does not survive the supply. Where the mid-century jury erred was not in its logic but in its docket. It graded the debate as though the debate were still the one Mises had opened, at the very moment when the debate's ablest living critic had moved it elsewhere. Hayek's knowledge argument was available in print from 1937 and in canonical form from 1945, squarely inside the period of the profession's consolidation, and the profession largely did not grade it, because its framework had no obvious place for the argument to stand. Equilibrium theory treats knowledge as a premise. An objection whose entire content is that the premise is false in the way that matters could not be processed by the framework; it could only be ignored by it, and ignored it mostly was, filed as philosophy or as Austrian eccentricity. The first telling of the debate's outcome is thus a story about the power of a framework to decide which arguments count as arguments, and the reader who understands that story holds the key to the second telling, in which a later generation went back and graded the paper the first jury had set aside.

## The second telling: the revisionists reopen the file

The reopening began in the nineteen eighties, in a literature that reread the debate's texts with an historian's patience and announced that the received verdict rested on a misdescription of what the participants had argued. Its central scholarly work was Don Lavoie's study of rivalry and central planning, published in 1985, and its central claim was that the debate had been misunderstood by its own scorekeepers. On Lavoie's reading, the Austrian case was never, at its deepest level, a claim about the arithmetic of equilibrium. Mises and Hayek were theorists of the market as a process, a rivalrous activity in which entrepreneurs, alert to discrepancies no one had catalogued, continually discover the data that equilibrium theory assumes to be given. Lange and his allies, by contrast, answered inside the equilibrium framework, and their answer was impeccable there, because there the data are given by hypothesis. The two sides had not disagreed about a theorem. They had talked past each other across a difference in what they took an economy to be, and the profession, sharing the equilibrium side's conception, had naturally recorded the exchange as a victory for that side.

### What did the revisionist literature argue about the debate's outcome?

It argued that the profession had graded the wrong examination: the solution answered the objection of 1920 while the debate's later and stronger objection, concerning knowledge and discovery, was never addressed by it, and that the shared equilibrium framework had hidden the questions that actually divided the participants.

The revisionist case drew strength from rereading the shift this article has already traced. On the revisionist telling, Hayek's migration from calculation to knowledge was not a change of subject but the debate's real destination, the point at which the Austrian argument found its true object: not the impossibility of solving equations, which no careful Austrian needed to deny after Barone, but the impossibility of assembling, at any centre, the knowledge that the solving would require and that the market process generates as it goes. Israel Kirzner's work on entrepreneurship and discovery, developed from the nineteen seventies, supplied the positive counterpart: the market's essential service is not allocation among known alternatives but the discovery of alternatives not yet known to exist, a service that no procedure working from given data can replicate, since discovery is precisely the correction of the data. Read through this lens, the trial and error board is not a rough approximation of the market. It is a device that presupposes the market's discoveries have already been made and delivered to it, and then performs, adequately, the least problematic part of the market's work.

The revisionists also pressed a point about the debate's personnel that flatters neither camp's mythology. Mises himself, they argued, had never rested his case on the equilibrium arithmetic his opponents answered; his 1920 essay was about the genesis of prices in exchange, a process claim from the start, and his later work developed the process view into a general theory of human action. If that reading is right, then even the first objection was partly misgraded by the jury that awarded it to Lange, because the essay's deepest stratum, the claim that prices are products of a process and not entries in a table, survived the construction of the table. The article does not need to award the revisionists this stronger claim, and the counter-revisionists contest it vigorously, as the next section shows. What the revisionist literature established beyond serious dispute is the weaker and sufficient point: that the debate contained two objections, that the second was neither raised nor answered by the exchange the profession had graded, and that any verdict on the debate as a whole must therefore be a double verdict. That establishment is the scholarly form of this article's namable claim, and it is why the shifted objection is not a journalist's simplification but the considered finding of the debate's most careful modern readers.

## The third telling: the counter-revision and the open file

No revision of a famous verdict goes unanswered, and the revisionist reading of the calculation debate met a counter-revision that keeps the file open to this day. Its arguments are of three kinds, and a fair article must give each its weight, because the article's own verdict, that the knowledge objection stands unanswered for the general case, is a verdict the counter-revisionists partly share and partly resist, and locating the exact line of their resistance is the surest way to locate the verdict's proper boundaries.

The first counter-argument is textual. The revisionists, it is said, overdraw the separation between the two Austrian principals and between the debate's two phases. Mises did press calculation in a form close to the arithmetic his opponents answered, and he also pressed, in the same texts and with equal vehemence, claims about knowledge, about the impossibility of the planner surveying the whole, about incentives and the spirit of enterprise, in a mixture that resists the tidy two objection scheme. Hayek, for his part, acknowledged debts and continuities that the revisionist drama of a moved argument tends to flatten. There is force in this. The two objections table is an instrument of analysis, not a photograph, and like every instrument it sharpens some edges the original left soft. The article's defence of its table is the defence of any good map: the separation is real in the logic even where it is blurred in the texts, because the two claims have different satisfaction conditions, and an answer can meet one while missing the other regardless of how their authors interleaved them on the page.

### What do the counter-revisionists reply to that reading?

They reply that the later objection was not as separate as the revisionists claim, that the first critic himself pressed questions of knowledge and incentive alongside calculation, and that computational and institutional proposals since have narrowed the gap the knowledge argument opened, even if they have not closed it.

The second counter-argument is technological, and it is the one with the longest future. The knowledge objection was framed against the information technology of the nineteen forties: paper returns, aggregate statistics, the telegraph and the filing cabinet. Since then the capacity to gather, transmit, and process data has grown by orders of magnitude that the debate's participants could not have imagined, and a line of writers, among them Allin Cottrell and W. Paul Cockshott in their work of the nineteen nineties, has argued that the objection's practical force has decayed with the technology. On their account, a modern planning apparatus could maintain the detailed physical and labour time accounts whose absence Mises lamented, could poll the economy's stocks and flows continuously rather than by periodic return, and could compute allocations for millions of products with machines whose speed converts Barone's nightmare of equations into a routine. Lange himself, late in life, supplied the counter-revision with its favourite quotation: in an essay of 1967 on the computer and the market, he observed that the electronic computer could now solve in seconds the equations his trial and error procedure had been designed to grope toward, and he described the market process, with an old man's evenhandedness, as a computing device of a pre electronic age. If the inventor of the solution thought the computer had overtaken his device, the objection that the device was too slow loses its remaining force.

The article's assessment of the technological counter-argument follows the map drawn earlier. Computing power answers, completely, the arithmetical stratum of the original problem: the equations can be solved, the accounts can be kept, the stocks can be polled. It does not answer the knowledge objection in its strong form, because the strong form is not about the speed of processing data but about the existence of the data in processable form. The tacit judgement of the foreman, the unarticulated sense of the buyer, the opportunity that exists only in the moment someone acts on it: these are not records awaiting faster collection. They are, on the objection's account, generated by participation in exchange and partly extinguished by its absence, and a faster computer connected to a quieter economy processes the quiet, not the economy that might have been. The counter-revisionists reply, in turn, that this strong form proves too much, that firms and platforms already elicit and aggregate dispersed information at scales Hayek never envisaged, through mechanisms that are neither classical markets nor classical plans. That reply identifies the live frontier of the argument, and the article records it as exactly that: a frontier, where the objection's pressure is being tested by institutional inventions its author did not foresee, not a refutation, since the inventions in question operate inside, and draw their data from, the surrounding system of exchange whose replacement is the question at issue.

The third counter-argument is the most philosophical, and it questions the objection's standard of success. To say the knowledge objection stands, the counter-revisionist notes, is to compare planning against a market order credited with using all the knowledge there is. But markets, too, decide in ignorance: they generate bubbles, neglect whole categories of cost, and discover some opportunities only by bankrupting the discoverers. If the standard is the full utilisation of dispersed knowledge, the market fails it as well, and the objection degenerates into the observation that omniscience is unavailable, which no planner ever claimed. The fair standard is comparative performance across the domains the article has mapped, and on that standard the verdict must be rendered industry by industry and era by era, not once for the economy as a whole. The article accepts the correction in part and has built its answer section on it: the map of domains is precisely the comparative rendering demanded. Where the article parts from the counter-argument is at its final step. A general claim was made in this debate, by the objection's author, about the general case, and the general claim has a specified defeater, stated in the concession section above. Until the defeater's condition is met, the honest report is that the general claim stands and the comparative work continues underneath it. Both things can be true at once, and in this debate, learning to hold two such things at once is most of what there is to learn.

## The model that was never built

There remains the observation that the brief for this article calls available to all parties, and it is the observation that disciplines the debate's most common rhetorical move. The trial and error model, the construction that answered the calculation objection and won the profession's mid-century verdict, was never implemented. No socialist state organised its economy on Lange's design. No planning board announced parametric prices and revised them by observed shortage and surplus while instructing its managers to minimise average cost and produce where price equalled marginal cost. The model existed in journals, in textbooks, in the syllabi of two generations, and nowhere else. It was the most successful unbuilt institution in the history of economic thought.

What the socialist states built instead was a different machine, and the difference is not a detail. Soviet planning, whose actual operation this series examines in its article on [Soviet planning and how it actually worked](/2019/04/15/soviet-planning-how-it-worked/), allocated predominantly by command and by quantity: output targets in physical terms, input allocations by entitlement, prices fixed administratively for years at a time and functioning as accounting conventions rather than as signals, and a continuous process of bargaining between ministries and enterprises over the size of targets and the generosity of allocations. Whatever the merits or defects of that machine, and they are the subject of their own literature, it was not Lange's. Its prices did not grope. Its managers were not instructed to equate price and marginal cost; they were instructed to fulfil the plan's quantities, and their careers turned on fulfilment. The family resemblance between the model and the reality is confined to the single feature of public ownership, which is the one feature the model shared with every socialist arrangement whatever.

### Why does the collapse of the Soviet system fail as evidence in this debate?

Because the model under discussion was never the system that collapsed. Soviet planning allocated by command, quantity targets, and bargaining between ministries, not by parametric prices adjusted through trial and error, so its failure tests the command method and cannot be entered as a result for or against the unbuilt design.

The consequence for the debate is strict, and the article states it as a rule of evidence. The collapse of the Soviet system at the end of the nineteen eighties is evidence about the command method of planning, about the political economy of the states that practised it, and about much else. It is not evidence about the trial and error model, because a test requires that the thing tested be the thing built, and the thing built was something else. Polemic that cites the collapse as the calculation debate's final score commits the same conflation as polemic that cites Lange as socialism's vindication, in the opposite direction and with larger props. The symmetry is worth dwelling on, because it is the article's whole method in miniature. Each side of this controversy keeps a trophy that belongs to a different contest: the one side waves a model that was never tried, the other waves a collapse that never tested it, and the trophies cancel, leaving the arguments themselves, which is where this article has insisted on keeping the reader's attention from its first paragraph.

Why was the model never built? The question is historical rather than logical, and the answers historians give are themselves instructive about the debate's limits. The states that might have built it were governed by parties whose conception of planning was Dobb's rather than Lange's: planning as the conscious direction of the economy's great proportions, an instrument of transformation and of power, not a simulation of the market they had abolished on purpose. To such governments the market socialist design looked like a capitulation dressed as a technique, a proposal to govern by mimicking the enemy. The one serious approach toward elements of the design, in the market reforms attempted in parts of Eastern Europe in the nineteen sixties, mixed parametric ideas with retained commands in proportions that satisfy no one's model, and the episode is claimed, predictably, by every camp as evidence for its own reading. The article records the episode and declines the claims. A hybrid built under political constraint, operated briefly, and abandoned under political pressure tests the politics of reform under party states. It does not test the construction of 1936, and the discipline of saying what a piece of evidence is evidence about, which this debate teaches better than any other in economics, requires the refusal.

## What the socialists had assumed before the challenge

To understand why the essay of 1920 landed with the force it did, a reader has to recover what socialist thought had previously assumed about the economics of its own project, and the recovery is embarrassing to the tradition in a way its historians have not always faced. Marx himself had written almost nothing about the allocative mechanics of the society he predicted. His critique of capitalism is a theory of how that system distributes, accumulates, and breaks down; it is not a manual for the administration of its successor, and the few passages that touch administration, the labour certificates of the Critique of the Gotha Programme, the remarks on bookkeeping in the second volume of Capital, concern the distribution of consumption goods in a first phase, not the pricing of producer goods in an industrial economy. The silence was partly principled: Marx distrusted recipes for the cook shops of the future, and regarded detailed blueprints as utopian in the bad sense. But the effect of the silence was that the movement inherited a destination without a route map, and filled the gap with an assumption so comfortable it was rarely stated: that modern capitalism was itself building the apparatus of planning, in its trusts, its cartels, its great integrated firms, and that socialism would inherit the apparatus as one inherits a house, needing only to change the name on the door and the purposes in the front room.

The assumption had distinguished sponsors. Thinkers across the socialist spectrum pointed to the wartime mobilisation of 1914 to 1918 as the proof of concept: the belligerent states had directed production, rationed consumption, and allocated raw materials by administrative decision, and the sky had not fallen. If a state could plan for destruction, ran the argument, it could plan for construction, and with better motives. Lenin's writings of 1917 gave the assumption its most influential form, treating accounting and control as the whole secret of the new order's administration and the apparatus of the banks and syndicates as a machine built by capitalism and awaiting only new hands. The calculation essay of 1920 was aimed, with full deliberation, at exactly this complacency. Its author's contention was that the inherited house had no staircase: the apparatus of the trusts and the war boards worked, insofar as it worked, inside a price system that surrounded and supplied it, and a society that abolished the surrounding system would find the apparatus mute, its dials disconnected from the reality they had measured. The war economies, on this reading, were not planning's proof but its most flattering disguise, islands of command sustained by oceans of market, exactly as the large firm would later be.

The challenge's reception inside the socialist camp ran the full scale from dismissal to conversion, and the scale is worth recording because it explains the solution's eventual shape. Some dismissed the essay as a scholastic trick, a definition of rationality rigged to exclude the answer. Some, particularly in the German speaking world where the essay circulated first, treated it as the most serious objection the movement had ever faced and set about answering it in kind; the debate in the journals of the nineteen twenties, involving figures such as Karl Polanyi, who proposed a functionalist accounting for a guild socialist order, and the advocates of calculation in kind around Neurath, was richer and more various than the later textbook summary suggests. And some drew the conclusion that would define the solution: that the objection could only be answered by conceding its framework, by showing that the functions it declared impossible without ownership could be performed by design. Lange's construction is unintelligible except as the product of that third response. It is the work of a socialist who had read the enemy's essay and decided that the enemy had asked a real question. The statue joke is the public form of that decision, and the profession's later verdict cannot be understood without it: the solution persuaded because it was built by a man who had first been persuaded that there was something to solve.

## The first objection in its author's later form

Mises did not leave his objection in its 1920 form, and fairness to the debate requires following him as he restated it across the following three decades, because the restatement complicates the tidy separation this article defends, in ways the counter-revisionists have rightly pressed. In his great treatise of 1949, the argument reappears inside a general theory of action, and its centre of gravity has visibly shifted from the existence of prices to the process that generates them. Monetary calculation, in the later work, is described as the intellectual division of labour's essential instrument: it is what allows each participant in an extended economy to orient himself, to appraise, to compare his project's claims on resources against the claims of every rival project, in a common medium. The entrepreneur of the treatise is not a solver of equations. He is an appraiser of an uncertain future, staking resources on judgements about what consumers will want and what techniques will serve, and prices are the residue and the guide of millions of such appraisals. On this telling, a board that announces prices is not merely using a different instrument for the same task. It is attempting appraisal without appraisers, judgement at a point where no one stands to gain by judging well or lose by judging badly in the currency the system itself recognises.

The later restatement matters to the grading for two reasons. First, it narrows the distance between the two principals of the Austrian side: the Mises of 1949 speaks a language of process, appraisal, and dispersed judgement that sits much closer to Hayek's knowledge argument than the Mises of the textbook summary, the pure logician of calculation, ever did. The revisionist historians made this convergence the centre of their case, and they were right to find it in the texts. Second, and against the revisionists' stronger claim, the restatement does not dissolve the first objection into the second. The claim of 1920 retains its own satisfaction condition throughout Mises's work: supply genuine prices for producer goods, usable by decision makers for the comparison of alternatives, and that claim, as stated, is met. Lange's construction purports to supply exactly that, and the later Mises's rejoinder is not that the construction fails the 1920 test but that it fails a deeper one, about appraisal, risk, and the entrepreneurial function, which belongs to the debate's second phase and its widening penumbra. The article's table survives the complication, but it survives as what it is: an analytical separation imposed on a literature whose authors moved, deepened, and overlapped, drawn because grading is impossible without it, and offered with the caveat, now recorded twice, that the texts themselves are less tidy than the instrument that grades them.

## Discovery: the function neither the objection nor the answer supplies

There is a third act to the debate's inner logic, quieter than the shift from calculation to knowledge, and the article pauses over it because it explains where the argument's frontier now lies. Both the original objection and the proposed solution, for all their differences, describe an economy whose contents are already known: a list of goods, a set of techniques, a pattern of wants, with the problem being to find the allocation that fits them together. Hayek's knowledge argument disturbed that picture by pointing to knowledge that exists but is scattered. The discovery argument, developed most fully by Israel Kirzner from the nineteen seventies, disturbs it further, by pointing to knowledge that does not yet exist anywhere, not even scattered: the opportunity no one has noticed, the use for a material no one has imagined, the want that will only be recognised when someone first supplies its object. An economy is not only a puzzle of fitting given pieces. It is a process that continually manufactures new pieces, and the manufacturing is done by people alert to the gaps in the existing pattern, people whose alertness is rewarded, in a market order, by the profit of being first.

### What does the price system do, on the knowledge account, besides compute?

It discovers. Rivalry between independent holders of partial knowledge brings possibilities into view that no participant intended and no survey could have listed in advance: new uses for a material, new combinations of factors, new wants themselves. A system that only computes from given data cannot reproduce that generative function.

The discovery argument reframes both rows of the article's table. Against the calculation objection's answer, it observes that trial and error with announced prices can, at best, grope toward equilibrium within a given menu; it contains no agent whose business is to enlarge the menu, no one rewarded for noticing that the board's categories omit a possibility. The board can learn that a listed good is short. It cannot learn, from its returns, about the good that was never listed. Against a complacent reading of the knowledge objection, the discovery argument adds that even a perfect transmission system for existing knowledge would leave the generative problem untouched: the difficulty is not only that the centre cannot receive what the periphery knows, but that much of what the economy will come to know is created by the periphery's acting, and the centre's plan, by fixing the terms of action in advance, suppresses part of the creation. This is the deepest stratum of the Austrian case, and it is the stratum least touched by the technological counter-argument, since computation over a menu, however fast, does not compose new entries for it. Whether some institutional invention, neither classical market nor classical plan, could house the discovery function while socialising ownership is the question the debate's heirs are actually arguing about, usually without knowing that the argument is a century old and has a name.

## The anatomy of the observation step

Return, with the discovery argument in hand, to the moving part of the proposed solution on which everything depends: the board's observation of surplus and shortage. The model's loop requires the board to know, for each of thousands upon thousands of producer goods, whether the announced price has cleared the market, and to know it soon enough that the revision addresses the situation that provoked it rather than its successor. In the formal construction this knowledge is a premise, costless and complete. In any imaginable administration it is an achievement, and examining what the achievement would involve shows why the knowledge objection fastened on this joint in the machine.

A shortage, first, is not a fact that announces itself in a single voice. It appears as lengthening delivery times, as substitutions improvised by managers, as quality quietly degraded to stretch a short input, as orders refused and queues disguised as waiting lists, as bribes and favours where the official channel fails. Some of these appearances register in stock figures and many do not; a warehouse can be full of a good that is, for practical purposes, in shortage, because what fills it is the wrong specification, the wrong size, the wrong alloy, delivered to the wrong region in the wrong month. The board's aggregate for the good reports balance. The economy's users experience scarcity. The gap between those two reports is not a failure of honesty that better auditing could close, though auditing would help at the margin. It is a consequence of aggregation itself, of the compression that turns a million local facts into one figure, discarding, as the 1945 essay warned, exactly the minor differences on which usability turns. A price formed in exchange suffers no such compression at the point of decision, because the decision maker confronts the specific lot, in the specific place, and his willingness to pay registers the particulars that the statistic cannot carry.

Time is the second difficulty, and it compounds the first. The board's revisions are periodic by nature; prices are gazetted, circulated, and entered into accounts. Between revisions, the world moves. Demand shifts with season and fashion and the news; supply shifts with breakdowns, weather, and the thousand accidents of production. The market's prices move continuously, vulgarly, without permission, and their very restlessness is the mechanism by which dispersed knowledge is brought to bear before it decays. A board revising quarterly is steering, as the article said of the fashion trades, by the wake. For slow moving bulk goods the wake is a fair guide, since the ship's course changes slowly; for the differentiated middle of the economy it is a systematically stale one; and the staleness is not an implementation defect that a better board would remedy, because the periodicity is imposed by the act of central announcement itself. A price that must be everyone's price, printed and promulgated, cannot also be a price that changes by the hour in one valley and not the next. The model's elegance depends on treating the announced price as both universal and current, and in a changing world it cannot indefinitely be both.

None of this refutes the construction on its own ground, and the article repeats the grading discipline: as an answer to the claim that prices for producer goods cannot exist without owners, the construction stands. What the anatomy shows is the precise location at which the second objection enters the machine the first objection provoked, not as an external slogan but as a question about the machine's fuel line. The board must be fed with knowledge it cannot gather in the form it needs. Every subsequent proposal in this debate, computational, institutional, hybrid, is best read as an attempt to repair that fuel line, and the article's defeater is simply the standard a successful repair would have to meet.

## Three economies inside one argument: bulk, taste, and time

The article promised, at its outset, to name the domain whenever it compared the two systems, and this section pays the promise in full by walking the comparison through the three domains the brief for the debate distinguishes. The exercise matters because the debate's participants repeatedly won arguments in one domain and banked them in another, and because a reader equipped with the threefold map can locate any new claim about planning and markets, including the computational claims of the present century, in the territory where it belongs.

The first domain is that of standardised bulk commodities and the networks that move them. Coal, grain, crude oil, standard grades of steel, container capacity on established shipping routes: here the goods are homogeneous enough that a ton is a ton, the techniques of production and transport are mature, and demand, while it fluctuates, fluctuates in aggregate patterns that statistics capture tolerably well. In this domain the knowledge objection's pressure is at its weakest and the calculation objection's answer is at its most complete. Central coordination of bulk flows was practised extensively inside market economies, by wartime boards, by nationalised industries, and by the logistics departments of great firms, with results that no honest critic can call chaos. The failures of planned economies in this domain were real but they were characteristically failures of incentive, of maintenance, of the politics of targets, matters for the neighbouring controversy, rather than failures of the centre to know what needed moving. A verdict formed here will be comparatively kind to planning, and the kindness is earned, within the domain.

The second domain is consumer goods under changing preference, and here the valences reverse. The goods are differentiated, their appeal is perishable, and the knowledge that governs good allocation is the fleeting, local, largely tacit kind the 1945 essay placed at the centre of economic life. The historical record of the planned economies is at its most damning in this domain, and the damnation is epistemic before it is anything else: the assortments were wrong, the sizes were wrong, the styles arrived after their season, the queues and the gluts coexisted for the same categories of goods in different shops, because the signals reaching the centre were aggregates stripped of the particulars that constitute consumer reality. Market economies are not elegant here either; they waste fortunes on products nobody wants and bury their errors in bankruptcy and remainder bins. But their errors are corrected by the same process that commits them, at the speed of the shop floor, by actors holding the relevant knowledge in their hands. The centre's errors in this domain were corrected, when they were corrected, at the speed of the plan's revision, which is to say at the speed of the wake. Form the verdict in this territory and it will be severe, and the severity is earned there.

The third domain is long horizon investment: the dams, the grids, the steel towns, the research programmes, the commitments whose returns arrive across decades and whose value depends on a future no participant can inspect. Here the comparison surprises readers formed by the first two domains, because the market's knowledge advantage attenuates with the horizon. Prices are eloquent about the present and the near future; about the state of demand in thirty years they say almost nothing, and market actors deciding under such uncertainty rely on convention, imitation, and nerve as much as on information. Central direction, for its part, can at least hold a long intention steady, mobilise savings at a rate a fragmented capital market might not volunteer, and coordinate the complementary investments, the railway with the mine with the town, whose joint value exceeds their separate ones. The planned economies' genuine achievements cluster in this domain, as do their genuine catastrophes, the vast projects that embodied a mistaken intention held steadily for decades. The knowledge objection does not vanish here; the centre still cannot know the future it is building for. But the market cannot know it either, and the objection, which is a claim about a knowledge asymmetry, loses its edge where the asymmetry fades. A verdict formed in this domain will be mixed and sombre in both directions, and it will be about judgement under radical uncertainty rather than about calculation or knowledge at all.

The threefold map now permits a precise statement of the article's comparative finding. The debate's famous question, can planning beat the market, has no single answer because it is three questions wearing one coat, and the contemporary form of that question has an article of its own in this series, on [whether planning can beat the market](/2019/10/15/can-planning-beat-the-market/). In bulk and networks, planning of the trial and error kind, and even cruder kinds, can match the market's epistemic performance and sometimes exceed it in coordination. In consumer goods under changing taste, the knowledge objection states a durable asymmetry that no participant answered. In long horizon commitment, the contest is between two forms of blindness, and the winner varies with the quality of the judgement each system can bring to bear. The participants in the original debate can be graded against the map with profit. Mises wrote as though the second domain were the whole economy. Lange wrote as though the first were. Hayek, alone among the principals, built the domain structure into his argument's logic, since his objection scales with the dispersal and perishability of the knowledge involved, which is one reason his version of the case has outlasted the others and why the article's verdict follows its contour.

## The neighbour at the border: incentives and the limits of this article

Every account of the calculation debate must at some point refuse a temptation, and the refusal belongs in the open. The temptation is to let the neighbouring controversy about incentives flood across the border, because in the historical record of the planned economies the two failures, of knowledge and of motivation, are so thoroughly entangled that no archive can fully separate them. Managers who concealed capacity from the planning ministry were responding to incentives; the concealment also starved the centre of knowledge. Workers whose effort could not be elicited by wages graded to scarcity were an incentive problem; their indifference also falsified the labour data on which allocation depended. A historian of the Soviet economy who tried to assign each failure to one column would falsify the record, and this article does not try. What it insists on is the analytical separation, for the debate it examines, because the participants themselves observed it and because grading is impossible without it. The calculation objection was framed to survive the assumption of perfect motivation. The knowledge objection was framed to stand even when every administrator's goodwill is granted in full. The solutions and replies were framed against those purified claims. A reader who wants the controversy in which motivation is the central term will find it in this series under its own heading, in the article already linked above, and will find there the mirror discipline observed: that article does not grade the calculation debate either. The family of critiques to which both belong, and the map of their relations, is drawn in the cluster's opening survey, [the complete guide to the critiques of Marxism](/2019/08/01/critiques-of-marxism-complete-guide/), and the replies the Marxist tradition has made to the whole family are collected in the article on [Marxist replies to the standard critiques](/2019/12/01/marxist-replies-to-critiques/). The two thinkers whose names head the critical side of this debate receive their own full treatment in the article on [Mises and Hayek against Marx](/2019/09/01/mises-hayek-against-marx/), where their arguments against Marx's economics as a whole, of which the calculation and knowledge objections are the most durable parts, are weighed together.

## Calculation in kind: the moneyless alternative and its fate

Before the trial and error solution defined the debate's centre, another answer to the challenge had its decade, and the article restores it because its fate clarifies what the successful answer had to concede. The answer was calculation in kind: the proposal that a socialist economy dispense with money and prices altogether and plan directly in physical quantities, balancing tons against hours against hectares in a great ledger of nature. Its most systematic advocate was Otto Neurath, philosopher, sociologist, and administrator in the brief Bavarian and Austrian experiments after the Great War, who argued from the war economies that money was a historical encumbrance rather than a logical necessity, and that a mature administration could compare plans by their physical and human consequences, stated in kind, without reducing them to a monetary common denominator. The proposal had a moral energy the price debate lacked. Neurath's in kind calculus was meant to keep visible what money conceals: that a plan which maximises monetary value may waste lives, landscapes, and health, while a plan stated in kind must look its consequences in the face.

Mises's reply to calculation in kind was the coldest passage of the 1920 essay, and as pure logic it has never been answered on its own level. To compare two plans in kind is to compare bundles of unlike quantities, and the comparison requires weights: how many tons of coal against how many hours of labour against how much risk of flood from the dam. Weights are valuations. A system that refuses money does not abolish valuation; it performs valuation without a common unit, which is to say it performs it implicitly, inconsistently, and beyond audit, since the trade offs are buried in the planner's unexamined intuitions rather than stated in figures that others can check. The war economies that inspired Neurath had in fact leaned throughout on the price systems surrounding them, valuing their requisitions in the money of the markets they had suspended, and the one attempt to run a large economy substantially in kind, the war communism of the Russian civil war years, collapsed into barter, requisition, and famine with a speed that even its administrators conceded by retreating to the market under the New Economic Policy. The episode is not, by the article's own rule of evidence, a test of Lange's model, which did not yet exist. It is a test of the moneyless alternative, and the alternative failed it.

The historical significance of the in kind episode for the main debate is the concession it extracted from the socialist side. After Neurath, the serious socialist economists abandoned the moneyless design and accepted the challenge's central premise: that a complex economy needs a common denominator, that prices or something performing their function are indispensable, and that the argument must be conducted on the ground of how prices can exist under socialism rather than whether they are needed. Lange's construction is the monument of that concession. It does not defend the abolition of the price system; it rebuilds the price system under new ownership. The distance between the socialist economics of 1920 and that of 1936 is the distance the calculation objection travelled into its opponents' camp, and it is the first and least disputable of the debate's outcomes. Whatever else was won or lost, the objection permanently changed what a defensible socialism had to contain: money, accounting, prices for producer goods, and a mechanism, real or simulated, by which those prices could move. Socialisms that dispense with these things continued to be preached, but after the nineteen thirties they were preached without an economics, and the profession, including its socialist members, knew it.

## The thirties: why the debate burned when it did

Arguments have their seasons, and the calculation debate burned in the nineteen thirties because the world was supplying it with evidence and audience at once. The capitalist economies were in depression, their price systems apparently coordinating millions into idleness beside unmet needs, while the Soviet Union, insulated from the slump by its separation from world markets, was industrialising at a pace that impressed observers who agreed about nothing else. Planning was in the air far beyond the socialist parties: in the corporatist experiments of continental Europe, in the early New Deal's enthusiasm for codes and authorities, in the British vogue for political and economic planning that crossed party lines, in the Webbs' vast admiring anatomy of the Soviet experiment. To ask whether planning could calculate was, in that decade, to ask the practical question of the age in its most theoretical form, and the participants knew the size of their audience. Lange wrote for a profession that was deciding what to teach the administrators of the future. Hayek wrote at the London School of Economics, which had become, under Lionel Robbins, the beachhead of the Austrian ideas in the English speaking world, in daily institutional rivalry with a Cambridge where the planning idea had powerful friends.

The political charge of the setting explains two features of the debate that puzzle readers who meet it in the calm of the textbooks. The first is its courtesy. The principals were, by the standards of ideological controversy, remarkably civil: Lange's statue joke is a tribute across the barricade, Mises acknowledged Pierson's anticipation of his own argument, Hayek's criticisms of Lange are models of precision without contempt, and Lange, in return, treated the Austrian challenge as the making of his subject. The civility was not merely temperamental. These were economists who shared a technical culture, read the same journals, and understood that they were jointly constructing the theory of a system none of them governed, and the shared culture held even as the political world outside the seminar room descended toward catastrophe. The second feature is the debate's inconclusiveness at the level of allegiance. Nobody changed sides. Mises died unpersuaded that a price could be announced into existence; Lange died a socialist who had served as a Polish ambassador and central banker without ever administering his own model; Hayek's later work generalised the knowledge argument into a theory of spontaneous order that left the calculation debate behind as a special case. The debate changed the theory of everyone who watched it and the allegiance of no one who fought it, which is perhaps the normal outcome of arguments conducted at this level of honesty, and one reason the article grades claims rather than crowns champions.

The decade's end scattered the cast. War closed the journals' exchange; Lange spent the war years in America and returned to a Poland whose new order had no use for market socialism; Mises emigrated to New York and wrote his treatise in a country whose profession politely ignored it; Hayek, his 1945 essay a triumph, turned to political theory and the defence of the market order as a civilisation. The debate they left behind entered the textbooks in the frozen form the next section examines, and the freezing, not the fighting, is what fixed the popular version of who had won.

## The construction under stress: three details its critics pressed

Between the solution's publication and its canonisation, critics pressed on three details of the construction that deserve a section of their own, because each marks a point where the formal model and any possible administration of it come apart, and because the three together show how the knowledge objection grew out of the solution's own machinery rather than arriving from outside. The critics who pressed them included sympathisers as well as opponents, which is why the details carry weight: they are the construction's internal stresses, visible to its friends.

The first detail is the board's knowledge of costs. The two rules instruct the manager to minimise average cost and to equate marginal cost with price, and both instructions presuppose that the manager knows his cost curves, the schedule of what each level of output will cost him in each factor. In a market, that knowledge is itself produced by the process: the entrepreneur learns his costs by producing, erring, and watching his accounts against his rivals' survival. In the construction, the manager's cost knowledge is assumed, and the board's price revisions are driven by quantities, the surpluses and shortages, rather than by any direct inspection of costs. Critics asked what happens when the manager's reported or revealed costs are themselves the board's only window into the industry's possibilities. A board that sets prices from observed quantities, in an economy where the quantities are produced by managers whose costs it cannot independently verify, is steering by instruments the steered themselves manufacture. The point is not fraud, though the neighbouring controversy would have much to say about the manager's reasons for shading his figures. The point is epistemic: the model distributes to its agents a knowledge that the model's own procedure is supposed to generate, and the circle, once noticed, is the knowledge objection in miniature.

The second detail is investment, and it is the construction's most exposed flank. The board sets a rate of interest to equilibrate the demand for capital funds with the supply of saving, and within the model the device is symmetrical with every other price. But the volume and direction of investment is where a socialist economy's defining decisions live: how much of the social product to withhold from consumption, and toward which industries and regions and techniques to direct the withholding. Critics from several directions observed that no board, in an actual socialist state, would in fact leave these magnitudes to an interest rate it had announced, because the magnitudes are the politics of the system, the choices its rulers exist to make. Dobb's dissent, noted earlier, returns here with force: the construction solves the allocation of a given capital stock among given uses while assuming away the decision that planning, as its advocates conceived it, existed to take. And even within the model's own terms, the interest rate equilibrates a market in which the suppliers of funds and the demanders of funds are, in the end, the same state wearing different hats, so the price's information content is thinner than its form suggests. Later defenders of the construction replied that the same is true, in part, of investment under capitalism, where the state and the great corporations also stand on both sides of the capital market. The reply is fair as far as it goes, and it goes exactly as far as the article's domain map predicts: investment is the domain where the asymmetry between the systems is smallest, because the future that investment must price is unknown to everyone.

The third detail is time, again, but now inside the model's logic rather than its administration. Trial and error converges, when it converges, toward the equilibrium appropriate to the data from which the iteration began. The critics of 1940 asked what entitles anyone to expect convergence in an economy whose data change during the iteration, and the question has no answer within the construction, because the construction treats change as an interruption of its method rather than as the medium in which any real allocation swims. A market does not converge either, on this view; it does something else, it adjusts continuously and locally, never arriving and never needing to arrive, and the comparison between a process that approximates a moving target from a centre, in steps, and a process that tracks it from everywhere, continuously, is the knowledge objection translated into dynamics. Lange's defenders answered that the board could revise frequently, that most prices in most markets are in fact sticky and administered, and that the model's steps could be made small and swift enough for practical purposes. The answer has practical force in the slow domains and fades in the fast ones, and the article's map has already recorded the gradient. What the three details jointly establish is the route by which the debate's second phase grew from its first: the objection did not merely change hands in the nineteen thirties. It changed because the solution, examined closely, kept pointing at the place where its own assumptions did the work its mechanism was credited with, and the name for that place, once Hayek had supplied it, was knowledge.

## The concession inside the critique: what the second critic granted

It clarifies the whole debate to notice how much Hayek granted, and the article pauses on the granting because polemical memory has erased it. In the 1940 essay that applied the knowledge standpoint to the proposed solution, Hayek stated explicitly that the formal problem had been solved: the system of equations could be written, the trial and error procedure was a logically valid method of approaching their solution, and the dispute, as he put it in substance, was not about the logic but about the practicability of the method under the conditions of a real economy. The concession was repeated across his writings of the period with a consistency that his later popularisers, eager for a total victory, found inconvenient. The man who is supposed to have refuted socialism in the calculation debate is on record granting that the calculation objection, in its original logical form, had been answered. His case was that the answer was a paper method whose premises about information were false to the world, which is a different and, in his judgement, a fatal objection, but an objection of the second row, not the first.

The granting had a price, and Hayek paid it knowingly. By conceding the formal problem, he surrendered the clean polemical weapon Mises had forged, the claim of impossibility, and accepted a burden of proof that is much harder to discharge in public argument: the burden of showing that a method which works in the model fails in the world for reasons that are structural rather than incidental. Arguments about practicability invite the reply that practice can be improved, that administrators learn, that technology assists, and the whole subsequent history of the debate, including the computational counter-revision examined above, consists of attempts to collect on that invitation. Hayek's wager was that the impracticality was not incidental, that it flowed from the constitution of economic knowledge itself, and therefore that every improvement in practice would run into the same wall at a higher level of expense. The article's verdict records that the wager, for the general case, has not yet been lost: the wall has been mapped, skirted in the domains where it is low, and described with increasing precision, but the demonstration or the construction that would defeat the verdict has not appeared. That is the exact sense, and the only sense, in which this article says the knowledge objection stands.

## The cast in their own terms

The discipline of attribution requires that each participant be given in his own terms before he is graded, and the article has so far honoured the requirement piecemeal. This section gathers the cast into one place, stated as each would have wished to be stated, because a reader who can recite the positions in their owners' voices is immune to most of the debate's secondary literature.

Barone, first in time, is the debate's unwilling founder: an Italian theorist of equilibrium who asked what a ministry of production in a collectivist state would need to work out, showed that the computation had the same shape as the market's solution, and doubted, in the same breath, that any ministry could perform it without ruinous experiment. He belongs to neither camp, and both camps' habit of quoting half his essay is the debate's original sin in miniature. Mises is the challenger: a Viennese liberal for whom the market's price system was the sole known instrument of rational direction in a complex economy, and who held that a commonwealth abolishing exchange in producer goods had thereby abolished the instrument, whatever its administrators' virtues. His claim was about producer goods and about calculation, and he spent thirty years deepening it from a claim about prices into a claim about appraisal and process without ever withdrawing its original form. Taylor is the construction's John the Baptist: an American institutionalist who proposed, in a presidential address, that a socialist state could find its prices by announcing and correcting them, and then left the working out to others. Lange is the answer's author: a socialist economist of the first rank who conceded the challenge's framework, rebuilt the price system as an administrative function, and honoured his opponent with a statue for having forced the rebuilding. Lerner is the answer's logician, who refined its rules and insisted they were ownership neutral, the same for any rational economy. Dobb is the dissenter within the answer's own camp, who held that a planning which mimics the market has surrendered the point of planning, and who kept alive, through the decades of the model's triumph, the alternative conception of planning as conscious direction of the great proportions. Hayek is the objection's second author: Mises's student, who began by pressing the computation's enormity and ended by relocating the entire problem in the dispersal of knowledge, granting the formal solution as he did so. Neurath is the road not taken, the advocate of the moneyless ledger, whose defeat inside the socialist camp marks the challenge's first victory. Lavoie is the debate's historian and revisionist, who taught the profession to see two arguments where it had graded one. Around these principals circles a supporting cast the article has named in place: Pierson the anticipator, Pareto and Wieser in the prehistory, Dickinson and Durbin among the builders, Schumpeter and Bergson among the judges, Kirzner the theorist of discovery, Cottrell and Cockshott among the computational counter-revisionists. No account of the debate that cannot place each of these in a sentence should be trusted with a verdict about any of them.

## Why accounts of this debate differ so much

The debate's secondary literature is a hall of mirrors in which the same events support opposite morals, and the article owes the reader an explanation of the mirrors, since explaining them is the surest inoculation against being fooled by any single reflection. Four mechanisms do most of the work.

The first mechanism is the shift itself. Any account written from one phase of the debate will grade the whole by that phase's standard, and since the phases had different winners, the accounts divide accordingly. A history that ends in 1937, with the solution published and the profession applauding, reports a socialist victory. A history that begins in 1937, with the knowledge objection, reports that the applause was premature and the Austrian case unanswered. Both histories are accurate as chronicles. They differ as verdicts because they are secretly about different arguments, and the reader who has absorbed this article's separation can diagnose any new account in a paragraph by asking which objection its verdict is grading.

The second mechanism is the textbook compression. Textbooks need closed episodes, and the calculation debate entered them during the decades when the profession's verdict favoured the solution, so the closed episode the textbooks transmitted was the solution's victory, often in a paragraph, often without the knowledge objection's existence. Generations of economists therefore carry a memory of a debate they never read, and the memory is not neutral: it is the first jury's verdict, preserved in amber. The revisionist literature of the nineteen eighties was written by men who had discovered, on reading the originals, that their textbooks had misdescribed the debate, and the energy of that literature is the energy of the misdescribed taking their revenge. Counter-revision, in turn, defends the textbooks' instinct that something real was settled, and the argument continues, at one remove, as an argument about what the textbooks may responsibly say.

The third mechanism is political gravity. The calculation debate is one of the few technical controversies in economics whose verdict is popularly taken to settle the largest political question of the twentieth century, and that reputation bends accounts toward totality. A writer who believes the century vindicated the market will grade the debate as a market victory and smooth its complications; a writer who believes the century's planned economies failed for political rather than epistemic reasons will grade it as a socialist victory on points and smooth the other way. The smoothing is the tell. In honest accounts the debate is untidy, because it was: a moved argument, a split verdict, an unbuilt model, a collapse that tested something else. Untidiness in this literature is not a defect of exposition. It is the signature of the record.

The fourth mechanism is the collapse's shadow. After 1989 the debate was reread, by public argument if not by scholars, as a prophecy fulfilled, and the rereading flooded the record with a hindsight the participants did not possess. The collapse made the Austrian side's conclusions look like foresight and the solution look like a dead end, and it did so regardless of the rule of evidence this article has enforced, that the collapsed system was not the system the debate modelled. Scholarly accounts resisted the flood better than journalistic ones, but even scholars write for audiences whose sense of the obvious has been set by events, and the post collapse literature's confidence that the debate is over should be discounted by exactly the amount that its central exhibit is inadmissible. The debate is not over. It is in the state the concession section described: one row closed, one row open, awaiting an exhibit that the future may or may not supply.

## The equations and their appetite

A reader who has followed the article this far deserves a closer look at the object that haunts its first half: the system of equations whose existence Barone asserted and whose solution the board was to grope toward. The equations are those of general equilibrium, the framework Walras built in the nineteenth century and Pareto refined, in which every price in the economy is determined simultaneously by every other, since each market's balance depends on the prices ruling in all the rest. To write the system for an economy is to write one equation for each good, expressing that the quantity supplied equals the quantity demanded, with each quantity itself a function of all prices, all endowments, and all techniques. The system's elegance is its interdependence: nothing is priced alone. Its appetite is the shadow of its elegance. The number of equations grows with the number of distinct goods, and a modern economy distinguishes goods not by the hundreds but by the millions, once specification, location, and date are allowed to make goods distinct, as for purposes of actual delivery they must. Barone's ministry, solving its system, would need the coefficients of every equation: the technical recipes of every production process and the preference orderings behind every demand. The equations describe the destination with perfect clarity and say nothing about where their own contents are to be found, which is why the article has treated them throughout as the debate's most eloquent silence.

The trial and error method was, at bottom, a proposal to let reality fill in the equations' contents iteratively rather than demanding them in advance. The board does not need to know the demand function for a good if it can watch the good's stocks; it does not need the cost function of an industry if managers, obeying the rules, reveal costs in their choices among factors at announced prices. The method's ingenuity is exactly this substitution of observation for knowledge, and its vulnerability is exactly the substitution's limit: observation yields the quantities that appear at the announced prices, in the places and periods the observation covers, and the board must infer the rest, the responses to prices not announced, the demands for goods not offered, the costs of methods not tried. A market makes the same inferences unnecessary by never centralising them; each actor infers only his own neighbourhood, from signals addressed to him, and the system's total knowledge is the sum of neighbourhoods rather than a map of the whole. The equations, in short, are a description of consistency, not a procedure for achieving it, and the debate's first phase was won by the side that supplied a procedure, while its second phase was opened by the side that asked whether the procedure's observations could ever amount to the knowledge the consistency requires. Both questions are about the same equations. That is why the debate feels, to a careful reader, less like two arguments than like one argument photographed at two depths, and why the separation this article enforces is a grading device rather than a claim that the participants were strangers to each other's depths.

## Exile and return: the debate in the postwar decades

Between the profession's mid-century verdict and the revisionist reopening, the calculation debate lived the quiet life of a settled question, and the quiet is itself a chapter of the story, because it shows how a debate can be simultaneously closed in the textbooks and open in the world. The postwar decades were the age of confident economic management in the West and of extensive growth in the East, and in both halves of the world the practical men believed the allocation problem had been domesticated. Western governments steered demand, nationalised the bulk industries of the article's first domain, and planned investment in the commanding sectors without feeling Mises's ghost at their shoulder. Eastern governments expanded output at rates that made the calculation objection look, to many observers, like a scholasticism refuted by the steel statistics. The Austrian school itself was at its lowest ebb, its principals dead or dispersed, its doctrines absent from the great departments. Hayek's 1945 essay survived as a classic read by students who were then taught, in the next lecture, that its author's side had lost the debate the essay concluded. Few episodes in intellectual history show so clearly the difference between a text's survival and its argument's reception.

The return began with the discontents of the nineteen seventies. Stagflation discredited the Western managers' confidence that the economy's knowledge problems had been solved by demand steering, and the Eastern economies' deceleration, as their extensive growth exhausted itself against the intensive problems of quality, assortment, and innovation, revived every question the debate had asked about the second and third domains. Hayek's Nobel memorial prize of 1974, awarded for work on money and fluctuations but received by the world as a rehabilitation, returned his name and his arguments to circulation. The Austrian revival in the American universities produced the scholars who would reread the calculation debate, and the rereading, when it came in the following decade, found an audience prepared by events to doubt the textbook verdict. None of this constitutes evidence in the debate itself, and the article's rules forbid treating the Eastern deceleration as a test of an unbuilt model. But it explains the debate's rhythm: the argument sleeps when the world seems to have answered it by prosperity, and wakes when prosperity falters, because its questions are the permanent ones of economic organisation, and each generation's confidence that they have been settled is itself a datum the next generation's difficulties correct.

## Prices under socialism in practice: the reform episodes

The article has insisted that Lange's model was never built, and the insistence requires a companion section, because two socialist states did build something in the model's neighbourhood, and an honest reader will want to know why the neighbourhood does not count as the address. The episodes are Yugoslavia's market socialism and Hungary's New Economic Mechanism, and they repay examination precisely because they differ from the model, and from each other, in ways that illuminate what the model was.

Yugoslavia, after its rupture with the Soviet bloc in 1948, constructed the most market oriented economy the socialist world produced: enterprises socially owned but managed by workers' councils, trading with each other at prices substantially formed in markets, with banks, interest, and a foreign trade sector exposed to world prices. The Yugoslav experiment is therefore not an implementation of trial and error at all. It is something the calculation debate's framework finds more interesting: a socialism that answered Mises by retaining genuine exchange, genuine markets in producer goods between socially owned firms, and with them the price generating process whose absence the objection had mourned. That the Yugoslav economy functioned, grew, and supplied its consumers markedly better than its command neighbours is evidence, of a rough historical kind, for the objection's central contention, that the price process does work no substitute has matched. That it also generated its own pathologies, unemployment exported as emigration, inflation, regional inequality, soft discipline in its enterprises, belongs to the neighbouring controversies and to the theory of self management, and the article notes them without grading them here. What matters for this debate is the taxonomy: Yugoslavia tested markets under social ownership, not parametric prices under a board, and its lessons belong to the first row's side of the table, as a demonstration that the measuring instrument works when the exchanges that generate it are real.

Hungary's New Economic Mechanism, introduced in 1968, came closer to the model's letter and thereby shows more clearly why the letter was never the model's spirit. The reform abolished compulsory plan directives for most enterprises, freed many prices, and announced that regulators, taxes, credit, and a partially parametric price system, would steer enterprises that remained state owned and state appointed. For a few years Hungary was the socialist economy Western economists visited with hope. Yet the steering never became the model's loop. Prices were freed in categories and refrozen under pressure; the centre retained its grip on investment, on appointments, on the rescue of failing enterprises; and the managers' operative skill became, as observers documented, the negotiation of the regulators rather than obedience to them. The episode's lesson for the debate is about the model's political preconditions, a subject the model itself brackets. A board that announces prices and revises them by observed imbalance must be a board that can let the revisions bite: can let a price rise against a ministry's favourite project, can let an enterprise contract when the rules say contract, can hold its rate of interest steady against pressure from the powerful. Whether such a board can exist inside a state that owns everything and answers for everything is not a question in economics, and the calculation debate, to its credit and its limitation, never pretended it was. The article records the Hungarian episode as the closest approach to the model in practice, and as evidence about the model's unexamined premise, the neutrality of its board, rather than about its logic.

## The answer's author in office: a coda on Lange

There is a biographical irony at the debate's centre that no account should omit, because it concentrates the argument's whole tragedy of unimplementation in a single career. Lange returned to Poland after the war and served the new order in the posts it offered: ambassador to Washington, representative at the United Nations, deputy chairman of the council of state, professor, planner, elder of Polish economics. He wrote on the practice of planning in a command economy, on econometrics, on cybernetics, and in his later work he conceded to the market a larger role in the socialist economy's daily operation than his model had required, arguing in the Polish discussions of the nineteen fifties that consumer goods and much else should be allocated by genuine markets, with planning reserved for the great proportions. The model of 1936, the construction that had answered Mises and won the profession, was never proposed by its own author as the operating system of the state he served, because that state was not asking his question. It was asking how to industrialise a ruined country under a party that regarded the market as the enemy's country, and Lange, who knew better than any man alive what the price system does, spent his practical years administering its absence. His late essay on the computer, with its evenhanded valediction to the market as a computing device of an earlier age, reads in this light as something more than a technical remark. It is the answer's author, at the end of his life, still insisting that the question of his youth was a question about computing, and still declining to record what his career had taught him about the other question, the one his model bracketed: who holds the board, and what the board is for. The debate is richer for his silence, because the silence marks, with biographical precision, the boundary between the argument this article has examined and the politics that surrounded and finally buried it.

## What the debate settled about Marxism itself

A series on Marxism owes its readers a section locating this debate inside the tradition it examines, because the calculation controversy is often misfiled as a debate about Marx, and it is not, quite. Marx proposed no allocative mechanism for the society he expected, and the objection of 1920 was aimed not at his texts but at the programme of the parties that ruled in his name and the theorists who advised them. The debate's bearing on Marxism is therefore indirect and double. On one side, the objection drew strength from the tradition's silence: a movement that had analysed capitalism's allocation in exhaustive detail and its successor's hardly at all was exposed exactly where the exposure hurt, and the exposure was the doing of the tradition's own priorities, which treated the administration of abundance as a detail beneath theory's notice. On the other side, the objection's deepest claims are not about Marx's doctrines at all, and Marxists who answered it were defending a construction, the centrally directed economy, that Marx never specified and that some of his texts, with their emphasis on the free development of individuals and their suspicion of state machinery, sit awkwardly beside. The cluster of articles around this one traces the tradition's replies in full, and the reader who wants the doctrinal accounting will find it in the article on the replies already linked. Here it is enough to fix the filing: the calculation debate is a debate about whether a post capitalist economy can be rationally directed, conducted between liberal critics and socialist economists, on ground Marx left unmapped. Its verdicts bind the programme, not the critique, and a Marxism that absorbed the debate's lessons, retaining prices, genuine markets for consumption goods and for labour, and money accounting as permanent instruments rather than transitional embarrassments, would be answering the debate in the only way the debate recognises: by construction.

## The shifted objection: grading both victory claims

The article can now perform the operation it was built for, and grade, one final time and in one place, the two famous claims whose confusion is the debate's popular legacy. The first claim is that the calculation debate refuted socialism. Graded against the record, the claim is true of the moneyless socialism of calculation in kind, which the objection destroyed and the socialist camp itself abandoned. It is false of the market socialism of the proposed solution, which met the objection in its original form and was judged by the profession to have met it. It is unproven, in the strong form its partisans intend, against socialism as such, because the objection that survives, the knowledge objection, establishes an asymmetry and a domain map rather than an impossibility, and because its own author specified the exhibit that would defeat it, an exhibit whose absence is a fact about the record and not a theorem about the future. The second claim is that the debate vindicated socialism, that the refutation was refuted. Graded against the record, that claim is true of the formal question: after the nineteen thirties, no informed person could say that rational allocation under public ownership was a logical absurdity, and the saying had been the objection's public form. It is false of the knowledge question, which the vindication's champions mostly do not mention, and it is empty as a claim about practice, since the vindicated model was never operated and the systems operated in socialism's name were built to a different design whose record cannot be entered for either side of this debate. Two claims, each true in a row of the table and false as a verdict on the whole: that is the shifted objection's yield, and a reader who carries it away carries the debate itself, in the only form in which it can be carried without loss.

What remains, when both trophies have been returned to their proper contests, is a set of questions that the debate bequeathed in better shape than it found them. Whether prices can be administratively supplied for the slow domains of an economy is settled enough for practical purposes, and the settlement is yes, at a cost in information that varies with the domain. Whether the knowledge that allocation needs can be generated without the process of exchange is open, specified, and awaiting its exhibit. Whether institutions yet undesigned can house the discovery function under social ownership is the live form of the same openness. And whether any board can be constituted that will let its own prices bite against the powerful is the political question the economists bracketed, on which the historical record is eloquent and the theory silent. A debate that leaves its successors four questions of this quality has not failed, whatever its scorecards say. It has done what the best controversies do: it has converted a slogan match into a research programme, and the conversion, not the crown, is its monument.

## What a centre can actually know: statistics and their discontents

The knowledge objection is sometimes met with a gesture at the modern statistical state, as though the sheer volume of data a contemporary administration collects had dissolved the problem of 1945 by accumulation. The gesture deserves a section, because examining what statistics are shows why accumulation does not dissolve it, and the examination is one the objection's author began and his successors have continued. A statistic is not a piece of the world. It is a construction, made by defining a category, assigning the world's items to it, and discarding, by design, every difference among the items that the category does not record. The construction is indispensable; without it no administration, no firm, no science can proceed. But its cost is fixed at the moment of construction: whatever the category discards is invisible to every later user of the figure, and the discarding is done in advance, by the category's designer, in ignorance of the uses to which the figure will be put and the decisions it will be asked to inform. The planner who receives a figure for the output of shoes receives a number from which the sizes, the styles, the regions, the durability, and the season have been abstracted away, because the category of the shoe was built for counting and not for the particular decision, about this factory's next run of this last for this town, that the number will be asked to settle. He can commission finer categories, and does, in an endless refinement whose limit is set not by diligence but by the logic of the instrument: a category fine enough to preserve every decision relevant difference would have one member per item, which is to say it would have ceased to be a statistic and become the world again, unmanageable in the form the centre requires.

Two further properties of statistical knowledge compound the difficulty, and both were visible already in the planned economies' practice. The first is delay compounded by revision: figures are gathered for periods, transmitted upward through layers that each aggregate further, and published into a world that has moved on, so the centre's picture is not only coarse but old, a portrait of an economy that no longer exists in the form portrayed. The second is endogeneity: the figures are produced by the administered themselves, whose standing depends on them, so the data arrive pre stressed by the interests of their producers, a difficulty the neighbouring controversy claims as its own but whose epistemic consequence, a centre that cannot trust its instruments' readings, belongs to this debate as well. Neither property is an accusation against statisticians, who in the planned economies were often the most clear eyed critics of their own figures. Both are properties of the channel, and the channel is the objection's subject. Against this analysis the market's alternative is not better statistics. It is the refusal to need them at the centre: the price that confronts a decision maker is not a report about the world, aggregated and delayed, but a term offered in the world, at the moment and place of decision, by a counterparty whose own knowledge is embodied in the offer. The distinction between a report and an offer, between knowledge about the economy and knowledge in it, is the whole of the knowledge objection in one contrast, and no increase in the volume or velocity of reports converts them into offers.

## Tacit knowledge: the debate's philosophical root

The knowledge objection drew, in its author's hands, on an insight that philosophy was developing in parallel and that a later thinker gave its canonical name. Michael Polanyi, chemist turned philosopher, and brother of the Karl Polanyi who had proposed a socialist accounting of his own in the debate's first decade, argued that we can know more than we can tell: that skilled performance, the chemist's feel for a reaction, the clinician's recognition of a syndrome, the craftsman's judgement of a material, rests on a subsidiary awareness of particulars that the performer cannot specify, and that this tacit dimension is not a defect of primitive knowledge awaiting articulation but the permanent structure of all knowing, science included. The bearing on the calculation debate is direct, and the article draws it explicitly because it converts the knowledge objection from an economist's conjecture into a claim about the constitution of knowledge itself. If much of what the economy's participants know is tacit in Polanyi's sense, then the proposal to centralise the knowledge fails not for want of bandwidth but for want of anything to send: the holder himself does not possess his knowledge in transmissible form, and no questionnaire, however ingeniously designed by the board, can extract what the holder could not state to save his enterprise. He can only exercise it, in place, in the act. An institutional order that wants the benefit of that knowledge must therefore leave the acts, and enough of their consequences, where the knowledge lives, which is the knowledge objection restated as a theorem of epistemology.

The lineage runs backward as well as forward, and tracing it shows the objection's roots in the classical tradition its author claimed. Adam Smith's division of labour has a shadow that Smith himself noticed: the division of knowledge, the fact that an extended economy works by entrusting each fragment of the total task to a mind that knows its fragment intimately and the whole not at all. Hayek, in his later work, made the parallel explicit, treating the price system as the institutional answer to the division of knowledge exactly as the market is the answer to the division of labour, and treating the planner's predicament as the attempt to reverse the division of knowledge while keeping the division of labour's fruits. Whether the parallel is a discovery or a construction of hindsight matters less than its clarifying force. The economy the objection describes is one in which knowledge is not a stock that happens to be scattered, the way coins are scattered in a field, to be gathered by a sufficiently thorough search. It is a division, functional and self reproducing, in which each fragment exists because the arrangement sustains it, and the arrangement is the very process the board exists to replace. Gather the fragments into one office and you do not obtain the whole. You obtain the office's fragments, plus the memory of a whole that the gathering dissolved.

## Dobb against Lange: the argument inside the answer

The article has twice touched the dissent of Maurice Dobb, and it now gives the dissent its own section, because the quarrel between Dobb and the market socialists is the debate's most instructive internal division: two socialists, agreed on the destination, divided on whether the proposed road led there or somewhere else entirely. Dobb's position, developed from the nineteen thirties to his late writings on the theory of planning, was that the trial and error construction had conceded the argument's soul while winning its letter. A socialist economy, on his view, is not a market economy with public owners. It is an economy in which the great decisions, the rate of accumulation, the balance between heavy industry and consumption, the siting of new productive capacity, the direction of technical change, are taken consciously and politically, in the light of social priorities that no price system registers because they concern the shape of the future rather than the allocation of the present. To equip such an economy with a simulated market and instruct it to obey the market's rules is, for Dobb, to install the old system's criterion at the new system's heart: the plan becomes a device for reproducing, with friction and delay, outcomes the market would have produced anyway, and the transformation that justified the revolution evaporates into an accounting technique.

Lange's implicit reply, and the explicit reply of his school, was that Dobb had confused two separable questions: the determination of the economy's great proportions, which the construction always left to political decision, since the board that sets the accumulation rate and the broad investment pattern is a political organ; and the allocation of resources within those proportions, where the price mechanism's services are indispensable and its use implies no surrender. A general staff decides that the army shall advance on the left; it does not follow that every battalion's rations should be allocated by the same deliberation that chose the strategy. The reply is strong, and the history of the command economies is in part the history of what happened to systems that refused it, attempting to deliberate the battalions' rations at the centre and losing the strategy in the paperwork. But Dobb's counter question survives the reply, and it is the question the Hungarian episode later posed in practice: whether the boundary between the great proportions and the daily allocation can be held by the institutions that must hold it, whether a state that reserves the great decisions will in fact refrain from reaching past them when the daily outcomes displease it, as they regularly will. The economists of the debate had no theory of that boundary's defence, because the theory required is a theory of the state, and the debate, on all sides, proceeded as if the board of its models were a function rather than a power. The article flags the absence here, in the section where it bites hardest, and leaves it flagged: the calculation debate solved and dissolved many things, but the political theory of the planning board is not among them, and the reader who goes looking for it in this literature will find the models' most consequential silence.

## Who decides the future's share: saving, interest, and the model's political gap

One decision in the construction deserves extraction from the previous section's argument, because it concentrates the political gap in a single variable. In a market economy the division of the social product between consumption and investment emerges, untidily and with much injustice, from the saving decisions of households and firms and the borrowing decisions of investors, mediated by the rate of interest. In the trial and error construction the division is, by design, a decision of the board: the community's saving is whatever the board's investment plan requires, and the interest rate is set to clear a market in which the board stands, ultimately, on both sides. Lange was candid about this. The construction's consumers are sovereign over the composition of consumption and entirely without voice over its total, which the board determines as the residual of its accumulation decision. Critics at the time, and not only liberals, noticed what the candour disclosed: the model's citizens are free exactly where freedom costs the system nothing and unfree exactly where the system's defining choice is made, and the choice is made by an organ whose own constitution the model does not discuss. Defenders replied, correctly, that democratic determination of the saving rate is not obviously a defect, that market economies also take the decision politically whenever governments run surpluses or deficits and central banks set the price of credit, and that a socialist commonwealth could submit the accumulation rate to its parliament as it submits a budget. The reply is available and the article records it. What the reply cannot supply from within the construction is the mechanism by which the parliament, or the board, would learn the citizens' preferences between present and future consumption, since the construction has abolished the market in which such preferences were previously revealed, and a vote reveals them, if at all, in the coarsest possible grain, once, for everything, bundled with every other question on the ballot. The knowledge objection thus has a political corollary that its author, in his later political writings, developed at length: the dispersal of knowledge and the dispersal of decision are the same fact viewed from two sides, and a system that centralises the second must pretend to a knowledge, of the future's value to the present's owners, that the first dispersal makes unavailable to anyone. The calculation debate began as an argument about prices for steel. Its longest shadow falls across the question of who may decide what the living owe the unborn, and the article is content to end its analytical sections with that shadow in view.

## The reader's instrument: grading claims that arrive wearing this debate's clothes

The debate's vocabulary circulates far beyond its literature, and claims arrive constantly, in journalism, in manifestos, in the prospectuses of technologies, wearing its conclusions as credentials. The article closes its analytical work by handing the reader the instrument it has used throughout, in a form portable enough to apply at sight. When a claim cites the calculation debate, ask four questions of it, in order.

First, which objection is being graded? A claim that socialism was refuted must specify whether the refutation is of the moneyless design, of the market socialist construction, or of public ownership in every form, and the claim's value falls as its specification widens beyond the first. A claim that the refutation failed must specify whether the failure is formal, and therefore complete, or practical, and therefore hostage to the domains. Second, what is the domain? A demonstration drawn from bulk logistics does not travel to fashion; a failure drawn from fashion does not travel to bulk; and a claim that declines to name its domain has declined to be graded, which is itself a grade. Third, what is the evidence's object? Models test consistency, episodes test the systems actually built, and collapses test the systems that collapsed; a claim that offers one kind of evidence for another kind of conclusion has committed the debate's characteristic fallacy, and naming the fallacy usually ends the argument. Fourth, where is the defeater? A verdict that cannot state what would overturn it is not a verdict but a loyalty, and this debate, alone among the century's great economic controversies, has the distinction that its ablest participants stated their defeaters in advance: the challenger by specifying the instrument whose supply would answer him, the answer's author by building the instrument, the second critic by conceding the formal point and naming the practical one. Claims that arrive without defeaters are not this debate's heirs, whatever vocabulary they borrow, and the reader who applies the four questions will find that most contemporary invocations of the debate dissolve under the second of them. The instrument is the article's parting gift, and it is also, not coincidentally, the method by which the article itself asks to be judged.

## The debate in the mirror: what each side taught the other

It is customary to end accounts of controversies with the victor's spoils, and this article has refused the custom on principle. It ends its analytical sections instead with an accounting of instruction, because the calculation debate's most durable product is what its participants taught each other, and the teaching ran in both directions with a completeness rare in ideological war. The socialists learned, from the challenger, that their project had an economics or had nothing: that prices, money, and accounting were not bourgeois decorations to be discarded with the bourgeoisie but instruments any complex order must possess or replace, and the lesson permanently rebuilt socialist economics in the image of the objection it answered. The liberals learned, from the answer, that their system's defence could not rest on the impossibility of the alternative: that the price mechanism's virtues had to be stated positively, as a theory of knowledge and discovery, rather than negatively, as the absence of a rival, and the restatement permanently rebuilt liberal economics too, giving it the epistemic depth that its twentieth century renaissance ran on. And both camps learned, from the debate's long inconclusive afterlife, the lesson this article has made its method: that an argument which changes its object mid course will generate two histories and two trophies, and that the only defence against the resulting confusion is the unglamorous discipline of grading each claim against the objection it actually answers. The participants were, most of them, better than their followers in exactly this respect. They read each other. The statue joke, the acknowledged anticipation, the conceded formal point: the debate's courtesies were not decorations on its substance but part of the substance, the marks of men who understood that an opponent's argument, stated at full strength, is the only kind worth answering. A series on Marxism, which has had to apply the same discipline to friendlier material throughout, can close this article by recording that the calculation debate remains the tradition's most instructive defeat and its critics' most instructive victory, instructive in both cases because neither was total, and because the boundary between them, drawn carefully, is where the knowledge is.

## The debate inside the planning world: Kantorovich and the shadow price

The calculation debate is usually told as an argument between Vienna and the West, but its most poignant chapter unfolded inside the planning world itself, largely unread by the debaters of the journals, and the article restores it because it tests the debate's categories against the one audience that could not treat them as academic. In 1939 a young Soviet mathematician, Leonid Kantorovich, consulted by a plywood trust about the scheduling of its machines, generalised the consultation into a method: linear programming, the mathematics of optimising an objective under constraints, and with it a set of auxiliary numbers he called resolving multipliers, one attached to each constraint, measuring exactly how much the objective would gain if that constraint were relaxed by a unit. The multipliers are, in substance, prices: shadow prices, arising not from exchange but from the structure of an optimal plan itself, telling the planner the scarcity value of every resource in the plan's own terms. Here, from inside the Soviet system and from pure mathematics, came an answer to the calculation problem of a kind neither Mises nor Lange had envisaged: not prices announced and groped with, but prices deduced, as the dual of the planning problem, from the plan's own optimum.

The fate of the discovery is the chapter's lesson. Kantorovich's method implied that rational planning was possible in principle and that it required the planner to respect valuations his ideology told him were bourgeois categories; worse, the multipliers contradicted the labour theory of value as an accounting doctrine, since they priced land, capital, and time by scarcity rather than by embodied labour. The work was published in a small edition, its economic applications were resisted by the planning establishment and by orthodox economists, and its author spent decades in partial eclipse, applying his mathematics where it was permitted, in transport and cutting problems, while the economy he might have reformed allocated by the bargaining and the balances this series describes elsewhere. Only in the nineteen sixties did the optimal planning school around him revive in the Soviet debates, and only in 1975 did the Nobel memorial prize, shared with Tjalling Koopmans, who had developed the same mathematics in the West, announce to the world what had been available in Leningrad in 1939. For the calculation debate the episode cuts in several directions at once, and the article lets it. Against the first objection it shows that the measuring instrument can be generated by mathematics as well as by markets, a genuine extension of Barone's concession. Against the knowledge objection it shows nothing at all, since the multipliers presuppose that the planner already possesses the economy's technical coefficients and objective, the given data whose givenness is the second objection's whole complaint; a linear programme is only as knowledgeable as its matrix. And against both camps' self images it shows the debate's questions being settled, in the place where they mattered most, by neither argument nor evidence but by the political admissibility of the answer, a verdict on the century that the economists, in their courtesy, mostly spared each other.

## Four words that changed meaning

Controversies that last a century often do so because their central words migrate, and a reader of this debate needs a small glossary of the migrations, since half the cross purposes in the literature are lexical before they are logical. The first word is price. For the challenger of 1920 a price is an exchange ratio, a fact generated by acts of buying and selling between owners, inseparable from the process that produces it. For the answer's author a price is an index, a term of transformation, any number that confronts a decision maker as the cost of one alternative in terms of another, whatever its origin. The debate's first phase is, in one of its aspects, the contest between these two definitions, and the profession awarded it to the second because the second is the one its own theory used. The second word is calculation. In the challenger's usage it names the concrete act of comparing alternatives in money before committing resources, the entrepreneur's daily arithmetic. In the textbooks it swelled to mean the whole problem of allocation, and the swelling is responsible for the popular version's crudity, in which the objection becomes the claim that socialists cannot do sums. The third word is rational. The objection never claimed that a planned economy would be irrational in the sense of mad or arbitrary; it claimed that its choices could not be rational in the specific sense of demonstrably economical, answerable to a measure that others could audit. Much indignant reply has been spent refuting the madness the objection never alleged. The fourth word is knowledge, whose migration is the debate itself: from the first phase, where it barely appears, the participants assuming that the facts are where facts live, in ledgers and statistics, to the second, where it becomes the contested territory, dispersed, tacit, and ungatherable. A reader who watches the four words can date any contribution to the debate by its vocabulary alone, and can perform the article's separation unaided, since the rows of the two objections table are, at bottom, the positions the words had reached when each row was written.

## The missing participant: the consumer at the debate's edge

For a controversy about the organisation of production, the calculation debate is strikingly silent about the person production is for, and the silence is worth a section because the consumer's position differs instructively across the positions. In the challenger's framework the consumer is sovereign in a strict sense: his spending, and his abstention, are the ultimate data of the system, the valuations from which every price of producer goods is derived at several removes, and the entrepreneur's art is the art of anticipating him. Abolish the consumer's market and, on this account, you do not merely inconvenience shoppers; you cut the root from which the whole price structure grows, since the value of a machine is the discounted value of the consumptions it will serve. The proposed solution accepted the consumer's sovereignty in form and preserved it in fact for the composition of consumption: its citizens choose freely among consumer goods at market prices, and their choices instruct the board through the same surplus and shortage signals that govern everything else. What the solution withdrew, as the section on saving showed, was the consumer's voice over the division between present and future, and over the economy's direction in the large. The consumer of the model is a voter in the shops and a subject in the plan, and the model's defenders and critics have argued ever since about whether the second status corrupts the first, whether a board that decides the future's share can be trusted to read the present's signals innocently, or whether its preferences will bend the signals toward the future it has already chosen.

The knowledge objection gives the consumer a third role, stranger than either: he is a knower who does not know what he knows. His wants are not a table of data he could hand to a survey. They are revealed, and partly created, in the act of choosing among actual offers at actual prices, and they change under his own hand as incomes, fashions, and the offers themselves change. A planning board that asks him in advance what he will want is asking for a report on a country he will only discover by travelling in it, and his honest answers, aggregated, are the statistics whose limits the article has examined. None of this makes the consumer wise, and the debate's liberals were not romantics about him; his choices can be manipulated, short sighted, and formed by the very producers who claim to serve them, as the critics of advertising insisted at the time. The point is narrower and survives the concession: whatever the consumer's defects as a judge, he is the only site where the knowledge of his wants exists in usable form, and an allocation system must either consult him continuously, at the point of choice, or proceed on a portrait of him that ages from the moment it is painted. The debate's three positions can be restated, in the end, as three theories of that portrait: that it cannot be painted without the market's sitting, that it can be painted by a board watching the sitter's behaviour in the shops, and that the sitting is the whole of the matter and the portrait a superstition. The article's verdicts distribute themselves accordingly, and the reader who has followed the argument can perform the distribution unaided.

## The objection that outgrew its conclusion: markets are not ownership

One consequence of the shift deserves extraction before the personas, because it reverses the political polarity that popular memory assigns to the debate. The first objection, if sound, was an argument about ownership: no private owners, no exchanges, no prices, no calculation. Its conclusion indicted public ownership as such. The second objection, if sound, is an argument about the locus of decision: knowledge lives in dispersal, and allocation must be so arranged that decisions are taken where the knowledge is. That conclusion indicts centralisation, not ownership, and the difference is not a nuance. A socialism of genuinely independent, socially owned enterprises, trading in real markets at prices they make rather than receive, concedes everything the second objection demands while retaining the public ownership the first objection attacked; the Yugoslav experiment, whatever its other fortunes, was an attempt to inhabit exactly that logical space. Conversely, a capitalism of vast integrated corporations and administered internal transfer prices exhibits, inside its own borders, the epistemic pathologies the second objection describes, mitigated only by the surrounding market in which the corporations swim. The knowledge objection, taken seriously, thus dissolves the debate's original battle lines and redraws them along an axis its first phase never contemplated, the axis of dispersal against concentration, which cuts across the ownership axis at right angles. This is why the debate continues to discomfort its own partisans' heirs. The heirs of the challenger must explain why their argument, in its strongest surviving form, does not deliver the conclusion about property they most want from it. The heirs of the answer must explain why their construction, which conceded the market's forms while withholding its substance, stopped at the threshold the second objection marks, when crossing it, into real markets under social ownership, was available all along. The article leaves both explanations to their owners. It records the redrawing as the debate's final analytical yield: the shifted objection shifted more than the argument. It shifted the question, from who owns the means of production to where decisions can competently be taken, and the second question, unlike the first, has no party.

## The answer's counterstroke: the board as the better auctioneer

The proposed solution was not left defenceless against the knowledge critique, and its author's counterstroke deserves a section, because it turns the critique's own machinery against the market with a dexterity that the debate's later summaries rarely credit. Lange observed that the trial and error he proposed was not an imitation of some process the market performs perfectly. It was an imitation of the process by which the textbooks said the market reaches equilibrium, the auctioneer's groping, and real markets perform that groping badly: slowly, through the costly medium of actual production undertaken at wrong prices, with monopolies obstructing the adjustment and whole industries adjusting in herds that overshoot. A planning board, Lange argued, could perform the same groping on paper, or in its accounts, revising its announced prices in the light of accounting surpluses and shortages without waiting for the physical waste of the market's method, and could therefore reach the equilibrium configuration faster and more cheaply than the economy of private owners, which must pay for its information in bankruptcies. The board, on this telling, is not a clumsy substitute for the auctioneer. It is the auctioneer rationalised, the Walrasian fiction made into an office and improved in the making.

The counterstroke has genuine force against the first phase's terms, and even against a careless version of the second. If the market's own adjustment is a slow, wasteful, monopolised approximation, then the comparison the knowledge objection needs is not between the board's approximations and an ideal but between two approximations, and the board's might win in sectors where its information, though coarse, is at least comprehensive, while the market's, though fine, is fragmented and strategically distorted. The knowledge objection's mature form has an answer to this, and the article has already given its substance: the market's adjustments are not approximations to a configuration that exists independently of them, because the data to which both processes respond are partly created by the responding, and the board's paper groping, conducted over data delivered by others, cannot replicate the discoveries that the market's costly groping generates as its by product. But the exchange is worth staging in full because it marks the debate's highest level, the point at which both sides were arguing about the dynamics of adjustment rather than the statics of allocation, and because Lange's counterstroke permanently improved the market's defenders' case by forcing them to abandon the auctioneer. After Lange, no sophisticated defender of the market could rest on equilibrium's attainment. The defence had to become a theory of process, of discovery, of knowledge in use, which is to say it had to become Hayek's and Kirzner's theory. The answer thus educated its opponents exactly as the challenge had educated its author, and the debate's spiral of instruction, each side's best argument becoming the other side's next premise, is the deepest reason it remains the model controversy of its science.

## The shared idealisations: what both sides assumed

A controversy can be shaped as much by what its parties share as by what divides them, and the calculation debate's shared assumptions repay an audit, because several of the debate's dead ends were dug by common ground. The first shared assumption was competition. The challenger imagined a market of many owners, none able to dictate prices; the answer's author imagined the same market as the standard his board must match. The capitalism of their century, of cartels, trusts, and administered prices, appears in the debate mostly as a rumour from outside the seminar room, and critics at the time, including some of the answer's own allies, remarked that a board competing against the textbook's market faced a sterner test than a board competing against the actual one, with its monopolies idle capacity and depressions. The second shared assumption was the benevolence, or at least the neutrality, of the directing intelligence on one's own side: the challenger's entrepreneurs are disciplined by loss into serving the consumer, the answer's board is disciplined by its rules into serving the plan's rationality, and neither side spent much theory on the directing intelligence's own appetites, though the neighbouring controversies were full of them. The third shared assumption was the givenness of the ends: both sides took the pattern of wants as data, differing only on whether the data could be known, and the possibility that an economic system might be judged by the wants it forms, rather than by its efficiency in serving the wants it finds, entered the debate only at its edges, in Dobb's insistence that planning exists to change what people can want, and in the critics of advertising who made the symmetrical point against the market. The fourth shared assumption was the nation as the unit: the debate's economies are closed, self sufficient stages on which the drama of calculation plays out, though the actual socialist economies traded, borrowed prices from the world market for their foreign sector, and thereby quietly solved, for tradable goods, a part of the problem the debate treated as absolute, a point the more careful participants noticed and the polemical literature forgot. The audit's yield is not that the participants were naive; their idealisations were instruments, chosen to isolate the variable under dispute, and the isolation is what made the debate soluble in parts. The yield is a reading rule: when a claim from this debate is carried into the world, the shared assumptions travel with it, invisible as luggage, and the claim's value in the world depends on how much of the luggage the world will accept. The article's domain map is one attempt to inspect the luggage. The reader now has the habit, and the habit is the article's real conclusion.

## The debate abroad: planning's pupils in the postcolonial world

The calculation debate's questions did not stay in the Europe that posed them, and a series read by students of the Indian examinations, among others, should record the debate's second life in the planning of the postcolonial world, where its abstractions were cashed as policy within a generation. India's Second Five Year Plan, framed in the middle nineteen fifties around the model of the statistician P. C. Mahalanobis, was the most intellectually ambitious attempt outside the socialist bloc to direct an economy's structure by plan, and its design reproduced, in a mixed economy's idiom, the exact choices this debate had theorised. The Mahalanobis model assigned the commanding decision, the share of investment and its concentration in the capital goods industries, to the state, on reasoning Dobb would have recognised: that the pattern of the future must be chosen consciously because no price system registers the claims of a development that has not yet occurred. Its critics, among them the Bombay economists C. N. Vakil and P. R. Brahmananda, replied in terms the debate had also prepared: that the model neglected the supply of wage goods, the food and cloth whose shortage would tax the whole design through inflation, and that the plan's physical proportions, set without a working calculus of scarcities at the margin, would generate exactly the imbalances the calculation literature predicted for allocation by proportion rather than by price. India kept its markets, its private sector, and its price system alongside the plan, so the episode tests nothing in the debate's strict sense, and the article enters it under the same discipline as the Yugoslav and Hungarian episodes. What it shows is the debate's migration from the journals into the chancelleries: by the nineteen fifties, every serious planning discussion anywhere was conducted in a vocabulary, of proportions and prices, of consumer sovereignty and accumulation rates, of shadow prices and scarcity values, that the controversy of the nineteen thirties had minted. The debaters had lost control of their argument, as arguers do when they win the only victory that matters, the victory of setting the terms in which their successors are obliged to think.

## A note on the sources: a debate conducted in translation

A final scholarly caution belongs in the open, because this debate, more than most, was conducted across languages, and some of its misunderstandings are translation's children. The challenge of 1920 was written in German and reached the English speaking profession in full only in 1935, in the translation Hayek published in his collective volume, fifteen years after its composition and after its author's views had themselves developed; a generation of Anglophone readers therefore met the objection in the form its editor's camp selected, framed by an introduction written from within the second phase. The answer's author, a Pole writing in English for a British journal, composed in his second language with a lucidity that conceals the feat, and his key terms bear the marks of the crossing: his price as a term of transformation is a concept formed between languages, in the space where the German Preis and the economist's index had not yet been distinguished by his readers. Barone wrote in Italian and was read, for decades, in the translation of 1935, excerpted where the excerpt served. Kantorovich wrote in Russian and was read in the West a generation late. The article does not raise these facts as an excuse for agnosticism; the arguments survive their crossings, and the article has graded them without discount. It raises them because the debate's historians keep discovering that passages fought over for decades read differently in their first languages, and because a series that has insisted throughout on taking locked material verbatim owes its readers the admission that the canon of this controversy exists, for most of us, in versions. The admission costs the verdicts nothing. The two objections are distinguishable in any language, which is the final evidence that the distinction is in the arguments and not in their wording.

## The debate's Russian shadow: the argument the participants were watching

Behind the Viennese and London exchanges stood an experiment that both sides watched and neither could use as clean evidence, and it earns a section of its own here because the experiment's internal argument reproduced the debate's structure inside the socialist camp itself, a decade before the journals staged it. The Russia of war communism had attempted, under the pressure of civil war and conviction in roughly equal measure, the abolition of money's role in the state sector, allocation by requisition and entitlement, and the direction of industry from a single centre. The system's collapse into barter, black markets, and peasant withdrawal forced the retreat of 1921, the New Economic Policy, which restored markets, money, and private trade while the state retained the commanding heights. The retreat was defended by its authors as a breathing space and denounced by its critics as a surrender, but its epistemic meaning was plain to every economist who watched: a great state had tried to allocate without the price system and had reinstated the price system, under a government ideologically committed to its abolition, because allocation without it had failed at the level of bread. The essay of 1920 was written with this experiment beginning before its author's eyes, and the confidence of its tone owes something to the spectacle: Mises believed he was theorising a failure the world was already witnessing.

The Soviet economists of the nineteen twenties then conducted, among themselves, a debate that mirrors the Western one with uncanny fidelity and almost no contact. The question of the plan and the market, of whether the state sector should be governed by a central design or by the discipline of exchange with the peasant economy, divided Bukharin from Preobrazhensky, and with them the party. Preobrazhensky's theory of primitive socialist accumulation, which proposed to fund industrialisation by squeezing the private sector through deliberately unequal exchange, was in its way a calculation doctrine: it assumed the centre could set the terms of trade between the sectors and harvest the difference, an assumption about the centre's knowledge and power that the subsequent famine of collectivisation tested in blood. Bukharin's gradualism, by contrast, rested on the market link between state industry and peasant agriculture and on prices that would coax rather than command the countryside's surplus. The Western debate's categories map onto the Russian one imperfectly, since the Russian argument was about transition and power rather than about a stationary socialist economy's logic, but the mapping is close enough to instruct: wherever the argument was held, in Vienna or in Moscow, the same fork appeared, between those who trusted the centre's knowledge and those who trusted the process's, and the fork, not the flag, is the debate's true subject. The Russian fork was settled by force, and its settlement built the command economy whose record the Western debate would later be forbidden, by this article's rule of evidence, to use. The Western fork was settled by print, and its unsettlement is the reason this article exists.

## A worked example: tin, and what a price knows

Abstract claims about knowledge earn their keep in examples, and the debate's canonical example deserves to be walked through slowly, because its mechanics are the knowledge objection in miniature. Suppose, following the 1945 essay's own illustration, that some new use for tin is discovered, or some established source of tin fails, so that tin has become scarcer relative to the wants it serves. In a market order, almost nobody needs to know this. The price of tin rises. The canner who knows nothing of mines or of the new use sees the figure, and the figure instructs him: economise, substitute, redesign the can, recover the scrap. He obeys without understanding, and his obedience, multiplied across every user of tin in the world, rations the diminished stock toward its most valued uses with a precision no administrator could match, because the rationing is performed by the very people who hold the knowledge of what their uses are worth. The information that travelled was a single number. The knowledge that acted never travelled at all; it stayed in the canner's head, where it lived, and the price system's achievement was to make its travelling unnecessary. Now pose the board's problem in the same case. The board must learn that tin is scarcer, which its statistics will eventually report; must decide which uses to curtail, which requires knowing the value of tin in canning against its value in solder, in bearings, in foil, knowledge that exists nowhere except dispersed among the users; must issue the curtailments and verify compliance; and must do all this at a speed that matches the scarcity's own evolution. At every step the board needs, in transmissible form, what the market needed only in situ. The example is small and the economy is not, and the multiplication of the example across every material, every component, every locality, and every week is the knowledge objection's full form, which is why its author rested so much weight on so humble a metal.

Set beside the example its legitimate counterweight, from the domain where the objection sleeps. An electricity grid's dispatcher, allocating generation across a network minute by minute, performs central direction of a complex system in real time, and performs it well, in market economies and planned ones alike. His success is not a refutation of the tin example but its complement, because the grid's knowledge is of the articulable kind: the loads, the capacities, the costs of generation at each station are engineering data, measurable, transmissible, and stable in form, and the objective, meet the load at least cost with the system intact, is singular and statable. The dispatcher's centre knows what it needs because what it needs can be known centrally. The tin user's centre cannot, because what it needs is the comparative value of uses, which is not a fact about tin but a fact about everyone's purposes. The two examples together are the article's domain map at bench scale, and a reader who can say why the dispatcher succeeds where the tin board fails has understood the knowledge objection better than most of the literature that cites it.

## The formalist turn: why the profession stopped listening

The mid-century verdict in the solution's favour had a second act that the article should record, because it explains why the knowledge objection, available in print throughout, failed for decades to get a hearing in the profession's main rooms. After the war, economics underwent its formalist turn: the construction, with mathematical rigour, of the general equilibrium theory whose informal shape the debate had used. The Arrow Debreu model of 1954 completed the programme, specifying with full precision the conditions under which a competitive economy achieves an efficient allocation, and in that completed theory the debate's first phase reads as a preliminary skirmish the victors had long absorbed. Lange's construction is, from the formalist summit, obviously sound: of course the equations can be written for any ownership, of course an iterative price adjustment can be described. The knowledge objection, from the same summit, is not so much wrong as inaudible. It does not deny any theorem. It questions the theory's premise, that the data are given, and a research programme has no internal procedure for hearing a question about its own premises, since the premises are what make its questions statable. The objection was therefore filed, by a profession busy with the most successful formal programme in its history, under philosophy, under Austrianism, under anything but economics, and the filing held until the programme's own difficulties, and the world's, reopened the question of what the data are and who can have them. The episode carries a warning that exceeds this debate. A discipline's verdicts are partly verdicts about arguments and partly verdicts about which arguments its instruments can process, and the two are easily confused by the discipline itself, especially in its decades of triumph. The calculation debate's historiography, with its confident mid-century closure and its embarrassed late-century reopening, is the standing exhibit of the confusion, and the article has treated the profession's first verdict with respect for its logic and reserve about its jurisdiction for exactly that reason.

## The permanent deposits: what the debate left inside economics

Whatever its verdicts, the debate deposited permanent assets in the science that hosted it, and an inventory of the deposits is the right penultimate accounting. The first deposit is the theory of shadow prices: the recognition, which Kantorovich and Koopmans formalised, that every constrained optimum generates its own system of valuations, so that any rational plan implies prices whether or not it displays them, and any displayed price system can be audited against the plan it implies. Cost benefit analysis, project appraisal in development economics, the pricing rules of the postwar nationalised industries, which were debated in Britain and France in explicit descent from Lerner's work, all draw on this deposit; the modern state's daily practice of valuing what it does not buy and sell is the calculation debate, domesticated and put to work. The second deposit is mechanism design, the late twentieth century's formal theory of institutions as information processors, whose founders acknowledged the calculation debate as their problem's source. When Leonid Hurwicz, who had known the debate's literature from his youth, asked how the informational burden of an allocation mechanism could be measured and compared, he was asking Hayek's question with mathematics, and the Nobel recognised the descent. The third deposit is negative and equally valuable: the permanent discrediting of allocation by proportion, of the belief that an economy's structure can be designed in physical terms without a calculus of scarcity, which no serious economics, of any political family, has entertained since. The fourth deposit is the debate's method itself, the discipline of the steelman and the stated defeater, practised by its principals at a level their century rarely matched and preserved in their texts as a standard the reader can apply, as this article has tried to apply it, to the debate's own inheritors. Sciences remember their controversies by what the controversies built. By that measure this one, which built the theory of prices as information, the mathematics of planning's hidden valuations, and the formal study of institutions as knowledge devices, while failing to settle the political question that occasioned it, ranks among the most productive failures in the history of economic thought.

## Money and the unit of account: the proposals the challenge eliminated

Beneath the debate about prices runs a quieter debate about the unit in which a socialist economy would reckon, and the article surfaces it because several once serious proposals died there, and their deaths explain the shape of everything that survived. If money prices for producer goods are unavailable, perhaps the commonwealth can reckon in some other unit, and the candidates were canvassed in the debate's first decades with an ingenuity the textbooks have forgotten. Labour time was the senior candidate, with a pedigree in the classical economists and in Marx's own distribution scheme for socialism's first phase: let every product be valued by the labour hours embodied in it, and let the accounts be kept in hours. The candidate fails on a fact its proponents knew and discounted, that labour is not homogeneous. An hour of the surgeon's time and an hour of the porter's are equal as durations and unequal as scarcities, and a unit that prices them alike misprices everything they touch; to correct the unit by grading labours is to reintroduce, by the back door, the wage differentials of a labour market, which is to concede that the unit must be formed by the process it was meant to replace. Energy was proposed, in the technocratic enthusiasms of the period, and fails more crudely, since the economy's scarcities are not proportions of energy. Utility units fail for want of any operation that measures them. Money alone survived the canvass, and it survived for a reason the challenger stated precisely: money is not a unit imposed on exchange from outside but the medium exchange itself generates, the good against which all others are actually traded, so its prices are records of real substitutions rather than entries in a chosen metric. The proposed solution accepted the verdict completely, which is why its economy keeps money, money wages, money prices, and money accounting throughout, differing from a market economy in the ownership of its enterprises and the authorship of its producer goods prices and in nothing else monetary. Readers who meet the claim that socialism abolishes money are meeting a position the calculation debate retired, on the socialist side's own motion, before the Second World War.

## The unpriced world: external effects and the board's claimed advantage

The article has graded the proposed solution strictly, and fairness requires a section for the advantage its author claimed most confidently and its critics answered least completely: the treatment of effects that markets do not price. A market economy's prices register the costs and benefits that fall on the parties to each exchange, and they are silent about the costs imposed on third parties, the smoke over the town, the river's burden, the congestion of the road, and about benefits that accrue to people who cannot be charged. Lange argued that a planning board, seeing the whole field, could correct its prices for such effects as a matter of routine, building the social cost into the announced terms where the market leaves it out, and that this capacity was not an ornament of his construction but one of its principal superiorities. The argument is real, and its descendants govern the modern practice of environmental pricing in economies of every ownership type. The knowledge objection's reply is also real, and it is the reply this article's analysis predicts: to correct a price for an external effect, the board must know the effect's magnitude in the same common measure as everything else, and the magnitude is characteristically the kind of knowledge, dispersed, contested, and valuation laden, that the objection says does not arrive at centres in usable form. The board can decree that smoke shall count; what it shall count for, in the currency of the plan, is a judgement that the price system it replaced also never made, since the market's silence on smoke is not a measurement of zero but an absence of measurement. The honest grading is therefore double. Against the market, the construction scores a genuine point of principle: an institution exists that could, in form, internalise what the market structurally ignores. Against the construction, the objection scores its standing point: the form's exercise requires the knowledge whose availability is in dispute, and the history of administered pricing for external effects, in all systems, is a history of magnitudes set by politics wearing the costume of measurement. The article leaves the point scored to both sides, as the record leaves it, and notes the section as a model of the debate's maturity at its best: an advantage conceded, a difficulty conceded in return, and the argument advanced by the exchange.

## Disequilibrium as the normal state: the critique beneath both positions

Beneath the debate's two phases lies a discomfort that neither phase fully faced, and a complete account must name it, because the debate's ablest heirs built their work on the naming. Both the objection and the answer, for all their opposition, speak the language of equilibrium: the challenger denies that the planned economy can find the equilibrium configuration, the answer supplies a method for finding it, and and Hayek's restated objection denies that such knowledge can be centralised at all. The shared term is the configuration itself, the state in which all plans mesh and all markets clear, and the discomfort is that no actual economy is ever in it, or observably travelling toward it. Expectations about the future, on which every production decision depends, are not data that could be correct or incorrect against a configuration; they are conjectures that the future will partly falsify whatever anyone does, and the falsification is not noise around the economy's real life but a large part of its real life. The economist George Shackle, and in a different idiom Lachmann with his image of the kaleidic society, in which the pattern is rearranged by every shift of expectation, pressed this discomfort into a critique of the framework the whole debate shared. On the kaleidic view, the market's virtue is not that it approximates equilibrium but that it is a procedure for living without one, for coordinating conjectures that will mostly be wrong in ways no one can foresee, and the board's defect is not that it computes badly but that a plan, by its nature, must pretend the conjectures are knowledge, must freeze a pattern of expectations into targets and entitlements whose rigidity then meets a future that has declined to be frozen. The article records this stratum without grading the debate by it, since the participants did not conduct their argument there, and importing a later framework as the standard would violate the discipline the article has kept throughout. But the stratum explains the debate's strange afterlife better than any other single fact: the argument survives its own terms because the discomfort beneath the terms keeps generating new versions of it, in the theory of expectations, in the study of entrepreneurship, in every subsequent quarrel about whether coordination is a state to be reached or a process to be housed. The shifted objection shifted the argument from calculation to knowledge. The kaleidic critique suggests a further shift, from knowledge to expectation, waiting in the debate's future as the second shift waited in its past, and the article is content to point at the door without opening it.

## Uncertainty and appraisal: the debate's American cousin

The calculation debate had a contemporary American cousin whose kinship the participants only partly recognised, and the article notes the cousin because the family resemblance sharpens both arguments. In 1921, one year after the challenge, Frank Knight published his theory of profit and uncertainty, distinguishing risk, which can be insured because its probabilities are known, from uncertainty proper, which cannot, because the situations it concerns are unique and their probabilities do not exist to be known. Profit, on Knight's account, is the return to bearing the second kind: to committing resources on judgements about a future that no calculus governs. The bearing on the calculation debate is exact. The entrepreneur of the Austrian argument, appraising, staking, and answering for the stake, is Knight's uncertainty bearer in Viennese dress, and the planning board's deepest difficulty, on this showing, is not information but judgement under uncertainty that cannot be pooled, insured, or delegated upward, because the man who delegates the judgement has not transferred the uncertainty, only disowned it. Knight himself drew the moral cautiously, and his own politics were more equivocal than his Austrian contemporaries', but the analytical point entered the debate's bloodstream and remains in it: a system of allocation must somewhere house the function of committing resources to an unknowable future on fallible judgement, and must arrange for the judges to be selected, corrected, and replaced by the consequences of their judging. Markets house the function in owners and their hired appraisers, and correct them by loss. The proposed solution houses it in the board and its managers, and corrects them by the surplus and shortage signals whose adequacy the knowledge objection disputes. The debate, restated in Knight's vocabulary, asks whether judgement under uncertainty can be institutionalised in an office without the office's judgements decaying into the very guesses the challenger predicted, and on that question, as on the knowledge question it translates, the article's verdict stands where it stood: the decay is documented in the systems that tried command, the office of the model was never tried, and the general claim awaits its exhibit.

## The rules at work: marginal cost pricing in the nationalised industries

The proposed solution's most concrete afterlife was not in any socialist state but in the public enterprises of the mixed economies, where its rules were adopted, debated, and administered as practical pricing doctrine, and the episode deserves a section because it is the nearest thing to a field test the construction ever received. The nationalised industries of postwar Britain and France, coal, rail, electricity, gas, were public enterprises operating inside a market economy, selling their outputs to customers who could sometimes choose and sometimes not, buying their inputs at market prices, and answerable to ministers for their finances. What prices should such enterprises charge? The debate's deposit supplied the answer that the textbooks canonised: the rules associated with Lerner and with the French engineer economists around Marcel Boiteux, that price should equal marginal cost, with departures handled by taxation or subsidy rather than by distorting the price, since the price's work is to confront every user with the true cost of his marginal demand. White papers were written, select committees deliberated, and for a generation the pricing of the commanding heights in the mixed economies was conducted in a vocabulary minted in the calculation debate, by officials most of whom had never heard of Mises.

The field test's results instruct in both directions, as the article's method leads one to expect. Where the industries' outputs were standardised and their costs engineering facts, electricity above all, marginal cost pricing functioned as a rational discipline and exposed, by contrast, the arbitrariness of the average cost conventions it replaced. Where the outputs were differentiated, the demand shifting, and the costs entangled with politics, the rules decayed in administration exactly as the knowledge objection's logic predicts: the marginal costs to which prices were to be equated were not facts the enterprises possessed but constructions they negotiated, and the ministers who were to hold the prices against pressure held them, in the main, until the pressure arrived. The episode is not a test of the model of 1936, whose board governed a whole economy and not a sector swimming in market prices, and the article enters it under the standing rule. It is a test of the model's rules at the scale where they could be tried, and its mixed result, success in the standardised domain and decay in the differentiated and political ones, is the article's domain map wearing administrative dress. Few ideas in economics have had so practical a posterity and so ambiguous a report card, and the ambiguity is not a failure of the episode's administrators. It is the debate's own ambiguity, encountered at work.

## Schumpeter's paradox: the judge who would not join either army

One figure stands apart from the debate's armies in a way that illuminates both, and the article pauses on him because his position is the strongest evidence that the debate's questions were genuinely separable from its politics. Joseph Schumpeter believed that capitalism would not survive, that socialism would succeed it, and that the transition would be a civilisational loss; he also believed, and said in print at the height of the controversy, that the calculation objection was without force, that the socialist blueprint presented no logical difficulty that its administrators could not in principle resolve. The combination scandalised his admirers in both camps and is unintelligible on the popular version of the debate, in which the calculation question is the whole argument and a man's answer to it assigns his side. On the article's version the combination is coherent and even distinguished. Schumpeter graded the first objection and found it answered, exactly as the profession did. His pessimism about capitalism rested on entirely different ground, on the sociology of the entrepreneur's obsolescence and the intellectual class's hostility, matters on which the calculation debate has no bearing. And his confidence about the blueprint coexisted with a famous reservation about the practice, since he doubted whether the socialist order could reproduce the cultural preconditions of the efficiency it would inherit, a doubt that belongs to no row of the table and anticipates the political gap this article flagged in the construction. Schumpeter is the debate's control case: a mind of the first order, hostile to socialism's promise and certain of its arrival, who nonetheless awarded the calculation argument to the socialists because that is where he judged the argument to lie. A literature that cannot accommodate his verdict without accusing him of confusion is a literature that has merged the questions he kept apart, and the article offers his example, as it offered the statue and the concession, as a standing rebuke to the merger.

## How this debate could end: demonstration, construction, dissolution

The article's concession section stated the condition under which its central verdict would fall, and it is fitting, before the closing sections, to set that condition among the debate's possible endings as a whole, since a controversy of this age invites the question of what its ending would even look like. Three endings are conceivable, and they are not equally available to the debate's two rows. The first ending is demonstration: a proof, accepted by the competent, that one side's claim holds. The first row achieved this ending in its formal register, which is why its grading is stable; the profession demonstrated to its own satisfaction that the equations could be written and the prices announced, and the satisfaction has never been withdrawn. The second row cannot achieve this ending, because its claim concerns the constitution of knowledge in the world, and the world does not submit to demonstration; claims of its kind are ended, when they end, by the second route. That route is construction: the building of the thing whose possibility is disputed, which settles the dispute by existing. The trial and error model never received this ending, and the knowledge verdict's defeater specifies exactly the construction that would supply it, a planning system working at national scale and matching the market's speed of adjustment to dispersed change. The third ending is dissolution: the question ceases to be asked, because the world that made it urgent has passed. The debate approached dissolution in the profession's quiet decades, and the approach proved temporary, because the question the debate asks, where knowledge sits and who may therefore decide, is generated by every new form of economic organisation and cannot be retired by fashion. The article's forecast, offered as such and graded by its own instrument, is that the first row stays closed, the second row stays open pending construction, and the debate as a whole ends, if it ends, by the slow conversion of its questions into the ordinary working problems of institutional design, where they already live under other names. A controversy that dissolves into the foundations of its science has not been lost by anyone. It has been absorbed, which is the most complete victory an argument can have and the one neither of this debate's armies thought to claim.

## The men in the room: Vienna, and the seminar where the objection was raised

The debate's ideas had addresses, and recovering them explains the argument's peculiar intimacy, the sense, unmistakable in the texts, that the participants were answering people and not positions. The objection was raised in a city where its author conducted, through the nineteen twenties and early thirties, a private seminar that became one of the seedbeds of modern economics. Mises's Privatseminar met in his office at the Vienna Chamber of Commerce, and around its table sat, at various times, Hayek, Gottfried Haberler, Fritz Machlup, Oskar Morgenstern, and a generation of Central European economists who carried the discussion into exile and into the universities of two continents. The seminar's discipline was the debate's discipline: a paper was read, the argument was taken apart without regard to its author's standing, and the obligation to state the opposing case fairly was the price of admission. Hayek absorbed the calculation problem at that table as a young man, as his teacher's cause, and his later transformation of it was performed by someone who had heard the original argued aloud, in its author's presence, across years. The knowledge objection's courtesy toward the calculation objection, its careful preservation of what the first claim had established, is seminar courtesy become method.

The answer's author worked at a greater distance and in a colder medium, and the difference shows in the texts. Lange encountered the challenge in print, in the journals, and answered it as print is answered, by construction rather than by conversation; there is no record of the two principals meeting as equals across a table, and the statue joke, that most personal of gestures, was performed for an audience of readers who would never see its subject. The debate's geography is thus part of its explanation. One side argued in a room, where positions evolve by concession and refinement across an evening; the other answered in essays, where positions are built whole and defended entire. The shift from calculation to knowledge happened in the room's tradition, by the slow migration of a school thinking aloud across a decade. The answer's fixity, its quality of a designed object complete at publication, is the essay's form made argument. Neither form is superior, and the debate needed both: the room to deepen the question, the essay to build the machine that forced the deepening. Intellectual history written without the addresses misses this, and reads as temperament what was in fact furniture.

## The problem of quality: where the price list runs out

One difficulty of administered pricing deserves its own section because it sits exactly on the border between the debate's two objections and illuminates both. A price list prices categories: a grade of steel, a class of shoe, a standard room. The goods of the world, however, vary continuously beneath their categories, in durability, finish, fit, and a hundred dimensions that buyers weigh and sellers shade. In a market, the variation is policed by the buyer's refusal: a producer who degrades the quality inside a category finds his goods refused at the category's price, or discounted by the haggling that real markets permit, and the price that emerges is always, in part, a price of the actual quality delivered. A board that fixes the category's price has no such instrument. Its managers, instructed to meet their targets at the announced prices, discover that the cheapest way to satisfy a category is to let the quality inside it slide toward the category's floor, since the plan's arithmetic counts the unit and not the unit's worth. The result, documented across the planned economies in everything from shoes that split to housing whose fittings failed in their first year, is conventionally filed under incentives, and the filing is half right. The other half is epistemic, and it belongs to this debate. Quality is knowledge of the tacit and particular kind: it lives in the inspection, the handling, the reputation, the local comparison, none of which a category can carry to a centre. The board cannot price what its categories cannot say, and it cannot multiply its categories without limit, because each refinement multiplies the prices it must announce, observe, and revise, until the price list approaches the size of the world it was meant to summarise. The market's solution is characteristically indirect: it lets quality be priced by the parties who can see it, in transactions whose terms adjust to the actual goods. The problem of quality is thus the knowledge objection at the level of the single commodity, and it explains a pattern the aggregate record shows everywhere: the planned economies were least bad at goods whose quality is a number, tonnage, purity, voltage, and worst at goods whose quality is a judgement, which is to say at most of the goods by which their citizens judged them.

## The participants in retrospect: what the principals later said

The debate's principals lived long enough to grade their own controversy, and their retrospections, gathered here, form a coda the article would be poorer without. Hayek, in his later years, identified the knowledge essay of 1945 as the work he regarded as his most durable contribution to economics proper, the discovery, as he put it in substance, that the price system's function is the communication of knowledge, and he treated the calculation debate as the occasion that had forced the discovery on him. The judgement is striking from the man the popular version casts as the debate's winner by refutation: in his own accounting, what he won was not a refutation but an insight, and the insight was about the market's epistemology rather than about socialism's impossibility. The answer's author, in his, never withdrew the construction and never claimed for it more than its logic contained; his late reflections treat the market increasingly as an instrument a socialist economy should use rather than simulate, a migration toward his opponents that the polemical literature, committed to fixed positions, has largely declined to record. The challenger, to the end, conceded nothing, and his intransigence has its own evidentiary value: the man who knew the answer's construction best, and had every intellectual incentive to find its flaw precisely, located the flaw where his student had located it, in the provenance of the board's information and the absence of genuine appraisal, and not in the construction's logic. Three principals, three retrospections, and not one of them matches the popular version's scorecard. The article has followed the principals rather than the scorecard throughout, and it rests its grading, finally, on theirs: the formal question answered, the knowledge question opened, the political question untouched, and a century's argument the richer for having been conducted by men who could tell the difference.

## The model's quiet hero: what the manager is assumed to know

Every construction has a figure who does its hardest work unnoticed, and in the proposed solution that figure is the enterprise manager. The board announces and revises; the consumers choose; the theory attends to both. The manager, between them, is where the construction's knowledge actually has to live, and examining his assumed endowments shows, once more, the precise joint at which the second objection enters. The manager must know his plant's possibilities in a form the two rules can use: he must know, for each level of output, what it will cost in each factor, which is to say he must possess the cost schedule of his enterprise as a coherent object, before the production it describes has occurred. In a market economy no one possesses cost schedules in that form. The entrepreneur discovers his costs by incurring them, revises his beliefs by his accounts and his rivals' survival, and operates throughout on estimates whose errors are corrected, when they are corrected, by loss. His knowledge is real but it is knowledge in the making, inseparable from the process of acting on it, and a large part of it is the tacit knowledge of his trade that the article has already examined. The construction requires the same man, retitled manager, to hold the finished version of that knowledge as a premise, and to hold it in a form he can act on under rules that forbid him the market's instruments of discovery: he may not bid for factors against other managers beyond the announced prices, may not shade his output's quality or timing in response to his reading of demand, may not, in short, do most of the things by which his market counterpart finds out what his costs and his customers are. The rules that make him auditable make him blind in specific and predictable directions, and the board that reads his results is reading the behaviour of a man it has deliberately deprived of the means of knowing more. None of this touches the construction's logic, which may assume what it needs. It touches, with surgical precision, the construction's claim to describe a possible administration, and and it is in this form that the knowledge objection was pressed by the critics who knew the model best, several of whom had begun as its builders. The manager is the model's quiet hero because the model asks him to be the market's entrepreneur with the market removed, and then grades the economy on his performance.

## The welfare function: the debate's formal ghost

The debate's formalisation, in the hands of the mid-century theorists who consolidated the solution's victory, introduced an object whose quiet presence conceals the controversy's largest unasked question, and the article ends its analytical sections by asking it. Welfare economics, as Bergson and Samuelson built it, evaluates allocations against a social welfare function: a complete ranking of the community's possible states, specifying how the goods of different persons, and the goods of the present against the future, are to be weighed. Given such a function, the theory of the second best and all its refinements follow, and the planning board's task is well defined: maximise the function subject to the economy's constraints. The formalism is impeccable and the ghost is visible the moment one asks the obvious question. Where does the board get the function? In the market's theory the question does not arise in that form, because no one needs the function: the allocation emerges from the separate choices of the owners of resources, and its evaluation, if anyone attempts it, is a spectator's exercise. In the board's theory the function is an input, as necessary as the technical coefficients and far harder to supply, since it encodes every distributive and intertemporal judgement the community's life involves. It cannot be read off from consumer choices in the shops, which register wants at given incomes for present goods and are silent about the weighing of one citizen's want against another's and of both against their grandchildren's. It cannot be supplied by the price system the board replaces, because constructing it was, in part, the point of replacing that system. In practice it is supplied by the board itself, or by the political authority behind the board, which is to say that the formalism's most important input is a political decision wearing a function's notation. The calculation debate's participants mostly declined to pursue the ghost, Mises because his subjectivism denied that any such function could exist as knowledge, Lange because his construction prudently confined itself to efficiency given the ends. The article pursues it one step only, far enough to mark the place: beneath the argument about whether a planned economy can know enough to allocate lies the argument about whether anyone can know, in the required form, what the allocation is for, and that argument, between the theory of value and the theory of the state, is the one the twentieth century's economics bequeathed to its successor sciences still open. The calculation debate's final lesson may be that its two famous questions were the tractable ones.

## The scandal that armed the answer: idle men beside idle machines

No account of the debate's middle decade is complete without the spectacle that stood behind the answer's reception, because the proposed solution persuaded a profession that was watching its own system's most spectacular failure of coordination. The Great Depression presented, to any eye, a calculation failure of the market order itself: millions unemployed beside factories standing idle, wants unmet beside capacities unused, the price system apparently unable to bring together the men who needed work, the machines that needed men, and the consumers who needed the machines' products. The socialists of the debate did not fail to draw the moral. If the market's claim was that its prices coordinate the economy's dispersed knowledge into a coherent pattern, the nineteen thirties displayed a pattern coherent only as waste, and the board's promise, to coordinate by intention what the market had failed to coordinate by signal, acquired a plausibility that no seminar argument could have given it. Lange's construction arrived in a profession whose confidence in the automatic virtues of its subject had been broken by events, and part of its triumph was timing.

The critics' reply to the spectacle is part of the record too, and the article gives it, because it taught the debate's second phase one of its distinctions. The Depression, on the Austrian reading, was not the price system failing to calculate but the price system prevented from adjusting, by rigidities, by monetary mismanagement, by the interventions that froze the signals mid correction; and in any case a failure of macroeconomic coordination, of the economy's aggregate tempo, is a different failure from the microeconomic one the calculation debate concerned, the comparison of alternative uses at the margin. A board might conceivably keep men and machines together in work, by spending and direction, while remaining quite unable to say whether the work it found them was the work the community's resources were best fitted for. Full employment of resources in the wrong proportions is not rational allocation, and the history of the command economies, which achieved the fullest employment of all and misallocated on a continental scale, stands as the distinction's grim illustration. The exchange matters to the grading because it fixed the debate's scope for good: the calculation controversy is about the pattern of production, not its volume, about the composition of output rather than the level of activity, and claims that import the Depression, in either direction, are grading the debate on a question it never asked. The article has enforced that scope throughout, and records the spectacle here, in its place, as the wind that filled the answer's sails without being part of its rigging.

## Kornai: the debate's heir inside the planning world

The calculation debate's most important heir wrote from inside the world the debate theorised about, and his work closes the article's survey of the argument's lives because it transformed the debate's questions into a theory of the systems that had ignored them. János Kornai, a Hungarian economist formed in the reform discussions of the nineteen fifties, spent a career anatomising the economy his country actually had. His early work on overcentralisation documented, from within, the bargaining, the plan tautness, and the information pathologies of the command system with an insider's evidence. His Economics of Shortage, published in 1980, then proposed the debate's most consequential amendment: that the chronic shortage of the classical socialist economy was not a failure of its planners' knowledge or diligence but a systemic property, generated by the soft budget constraint, the enterprise's well founded expectation that losses would be covered and survival guaranteed, which destroyed at the root any pressure to economise, to innovate, or to report truthfully upward. Kornai's framework absorbs the calculation debate and passes beyond it. Against the first objection it is silent, granting the terrain. Against the knowledge objection it is an ally with a difference: the centre's ignorance, on Kornai's showing, is not only a fact about knowledge's dispersal but a product of the system's own incentives, since the information the centre receives is manufactured by subordinates whose survival depends on its content. And against the proposed solution it poses the question the solution never faced: not whether the board's prices could guide managers who obeyed the rules, but what institution could make obedience, truthfulness, and economy the rational course for the managers themselves. Kornai called his mature position a critique of both the market fundamentalists and the planning fundamentalists of his youth, and his trajectory, from reform economist to the theorist of systemic shortage, is the calculation debate's longest shadow: the argument that began in Vienna as a claim about prices ended in Budapest as a theory of why the systems built in defiance of it could neither know nor be told what they were doing. The article leaves the development there, in the hands of the heir, and turns to its closing sections with the debate's map complete: two objections, one answer, a concession, a defeater, and a century of systems and scholars grading themselves against all five.

## The American chapter: the answer's author among the econometricians

The debate's strangest setting was neither Vienna nor London but Chicago, where its geography produced an irony no novelist would risk. Lange, driven from Europe by the war's approach, taught at the University of Chicago from 1938, in the same years and nearly the same corridors in which the Cowles Commission for Research in Economics was building the econometric programme that promised to estimate, from statistical data, the very structures of demand and production that a planning board would need to know. Tjalling Koopmans, the Dutch physicist turned economist who directed Cowles's research, developed there his activity analysis of production and allocation, the Western twin of Kantorovich's Leningrad mathematics, shadow prices and all. For a few years, therefore, the world's best hope of the board's knowledge problem being an estimation problem, soluble by sufficiently careful statistics, sat in one American city alongside the man whose construction most needed it solved. The econometricians were not planners; their programme served a market economy's forecasting and policy. But their existence changed the debate's weather. If the economy's coefficients could be estimated, if demand functions and production functions could be recovered from data by the new statistical methods, then the knowledge the board required might be a research programme rather than an impossibility, and several of the debate's mid-century participants, watching Cowles, drew exactly that comfort.

The comfort deserves the article's scrutiny, because the distinction it blurs is the debate's own. Estimation recovers the parameters of a structure assumed stable enough to have generated the data: it learns the economy that was, in the form the econometrician's model permits. The knowledge objection concerns, in large part, what such recovery cannot reach: the local and tacit material that never enters the data, and the responses to situations that have not yet occurred, on which allocation for the future depends. An estimated demand function is a portrait of past choosers under past prices; the board needs the future's answers to prices it has not announced for goods that do not yet exist. Koopmans himself, to his credit, understood the limits of the formal apparatus he had built, and his later writings on the state of economic science are models of methodological humility. The Chicago chapter's second irony completes the first. The university that sheltered the answer's author and the econometric hope became, in the postwar decades, the intellectual fortress of the market order, the home of the price theory tradition that taught generations to treat the price system as the economy's irreplaceable nervous system. The debate's American chapter thus contains its whole subsequent history in miniature: the construction, the hope that data would redeem it, the distinction that hope overlooked, and the institutional victory, in the end, of the side that made the distinction its creed. Lange left Chicago in 1945 for the Polish diplomatic service and the career this article has already traced. He left behind a city that would spend the next half century grading his construction more harshly than any other, in the university where he had taught it, which is perhaps the purest instance in the whole record of the debate's governing law: that arguments are graded by whoever holds the framework, and frameworks are held by institutions, and institutions, unlike arguments, can simply wait for their opponents to go home.

## A case study in how arguments move: the debate read by the philosophy of science

The calculation debate has a second literature, smaller than the first, in which it figures not as an economic controversy but as a specimen: the philosophy of science's favourite economic example of how a research programme survives refutation by changing its claims. The specimen repays a section, because the philosophical reading both illuminates the debate and risks traducing it, and the article's readers should have the reading and the risk together. In the vocabulary Imre Lakatos gave the philosophy of science, a research programme consists of a hard core of commitments protected by a belt of auxiliary hypotheses, and it progresses when its shifts predict novel facts and degenerates when its shifts merely accommodate defeats. The Austrian programme's shift, from the impossibility of calculation to the dispersal of knowledge, reads in this vocabulary as a textbook problem shift, and the two camps of the debate's historians divide, predictably, on its classification. The revisionists read the shift as progressive: the new objection explained everything the old one had, the failure of administered economies to handle assortment and innovation among them, and predicted the pattern of the command economies' decay, their competence in bulk and their incompetence in the differentiated domains, with an accuracy the equilibrium framework could not match. The counter-revisionists read it as the programme's protective belt at work, a retreat from a falsified strong claim to an unfalsifiable weaker one, since a claim about knowledge that cannot be gathered is conveniently immune to any particular failure of gathering.

The article's grading of the philosophical quarrel follows its grading of the economic one. The knowledge objection is not unfalsifiable, because its author and this article have stated its defeater: a demonstration or a construction would overturn it, and the specification is concrete enough that its non fulfilment across a century of motivated attempts is evidence and not evasion. A claim that names its executioner and survives is not evading the test; it is passing the only test its kind admits. At the same time, the philosophical reading convicts the debate's popularisers on both sides of the exact sin Lakatos diagnosed: treating a programme's shift as either a confession or a coronation, when the record shows what shifts in live arguments usually are, the discovery, under pressure, of what the argument was actually about. The calculation debate's participants discovered, across twenty five years, that their subject was not arithmetic but knowledge, and the discovery is the debate's contribution to the theory of their science as much as to the science itself. A student of any discipline can be set down in front of this record with profit, because the pattern recurs wherever arguments are serious: the challenge that is answered, the answer that reveals the deeper challenge, the verdicts that lag a generation behind the migration. The article has called the pattern the shifted objection and built its grading on it. The philosophers call it a problem shift and build theories on it. The names differ; the discipline is the same, and it is the discipline of never grading an argument against a version of itself that its author has already, and honourably, surpassed.

## The debate's late heir: coupon socialism and the conceded objection

The debate produced one more construction, two generations after the answer, that shows better than any commentary how completely the shifted objection had redrawn the argument's map. In the nineteen nineties the economist John Roemer proposed a design for a market socialism of a new type, in which citizens would hold equal portfolios of coupons, exchangeable for shares in publicly accountable funds that would own the economy's firms, with the firms trading in genuine markets, at genuine prices, managed by managers answerable to the funds, and with profits distributed to the citizen coupon holders as a social dividend. The design's interest for this article is not its feasibility, which its critics disputed from every side, but its concessions. Roemer's socialism does not simulate the market. It keeps the market, entire, for producer goods as for everything else, and socialises not the allocation mechanism but the distribution of its returns, by equalising the ownership claims from which profit flows. The calculation objection is answered by surrendering to it completely: prices will be made by exchange or not at all. The knowledge objection is answered the same way: decisions will be taken where the knowledge sits, by firms in markets, or not at all. What remains socialist in the design is the one thing neither objection ever touched, the distribution of the income from capital, which the debate's whole history shows to have been separable from the mechanism of allocation all along. The article draws the moral without pressing the design. By the century's end, the most rigorous socialist construction on offer had absorbed both objections as premises and relocated socialism's claim to the single terrain the objections left free. The shifted objection had completed its work: it had moved the argument, and then it had moved the programme the argument was about, and the distance between the moneyless ledger of 1920 and the coupon portfolios of 1994 is the measure of what one controversy, honestly conducted, can do to the side that loses its first phase and learns from the loss.

There is a fitting symmetry in ending the survey here. The debate began with a socialist tradition that assumed allocation would arrange itself and a critic who denied that any arrangement was possible. It ends, three quarters of a century later, with the tradition's ablest designer building his socialism out of the critic's own instruments, markets, prices, and dispersed decision, and reserving his doctrine's distinctiveness for the distribution of the proceeds. Between those two points lies everything this article has graded: an objection answered, an objection standing, a model unbuilt, and a programme transformed by its opponents' questions more thoroughly than by its own answers. That is the record, and the record is the verdict.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

Teach this debate as the cleanest available specimen of an argument that changed its object while keeping its name, and your students will carry the lesson into every controversy they meet. Begin with the merger their textbooks have already performed, and make the first seminar an exercise in separation: give the class the two objections table with the final column blank, and let them discover, from the primary texts, why no single verdict fills it. The statue joke and the concession of 1945 are your two best exhibits, the one showing a controversialist honouring the opponent who forced his best work, the other showing a controversialist surrendering the strong claim to keep the true one, and students who have handled both exhibits have learned more about intellectual conduct than any methods lecture teaches. Set the grading exercise in writing: two verdicts on the debate, drawn from real textbooks, each to be marked against the rows it secretly grades. Close the sequence by assigning the defeater as an essay in itself, since a student who can state what would overturn a verdict has understood the verdict, and a class that can do it for both rows has understood the debate.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Your live questions in this field sit at three frontiers. The first is formal: mechanism design, the debate's mathematical heir, continues to ask how much information an allocation institution must move in order to function, and its results are the nearest thing the knowledge objection has to a proof theory. The second is institutional: the large platforms and logistics systems that elicit dispersed information at scales the debaters never imagined are testing, in production, whether the eliciting can be detached from the exchange that was supposed to generate the information, and the article's defeater is the standard your reading of their claims should apply. The third is archival: the debate's texts still reward collation across their languages and editions, the Kantorovich papers above all, where the planning world's own calculation theory waits in partial translation. For the contemporary form of the argument, where the computational claim is pressed in full, this series treats it separately in the article on whether planning can beat the market, already linked in the body above; the present article's contribution to your work is the grading instrument, and the standing reminder that the field's most cited verdict was delivered by a jury grading a different objection from the one its subject had become.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the socialist calculation debate?

It is the controversy, opened in 1920 and still not closed, over whether an economy that has abolished private ownership of productive assets can allocate its resources rationally. The controversy proceeded in two phases. In the first, the charge was that such an economy could not perform the elementary act of comparing alternative uses of a resource, because the prices needed for the comparison arise only from exchange between owners. In the second, the charge became that the information any allocator would require is scattered through the population in forms no centre can gather. Every famous verdict on the debate grades one phase or the other, which is why the verdicts contradict each other.

### Q: What did the original calculation objection claim?

It claimed that rational choice among competing uses of the means of production needs a common measure, that money prices supply that measure, and that prices for machines, materials, and land form only where such goods are genuinely bought and sold. Public ownership ends the buying and selling, so the prices vanish, and with them the planner's ability to say whether a given use of a resource is worth more than the use it displaces. The claim was aimed at producer goods alone, and at the act of reckoning, not at anyone's willingness to work.

### Q: What was the proposed solution to the calculation problem?

The solution, published in the middle nineteen thirties and associated with the Polish economist Oskar Lange, proposed that a central board announce prices for producer goods and revise them by watching physical results. Where stocks ran down and buyers queued, the price was to rise; where goods piled up unwanted, it was to fall. Managers of the publicly owned enterprises were given two standing instructions: keep the average cost of output as low as the announced prices allowed, and adjust the scale of production until the cost of an additional unit matched the price. Consumers and workers, meanwhile, kept free choice in genuine markets.

### Q: How did the objection shift to knowledge?

The shift happened across the nineteen thirties, in the work of the Austrian economist Friedrich Hayek, and it happened because the proposed solution had met the original charge on its own ground. Hayek first questioned whether the profession's theory had anything to say about how the facts an equilibrium requires ever become known to anyone. He then argued, against the trial and error design, that its board would need its revisions to chase data that change while the revisions are being made. By 1945 the objection had been restated completely: the difficulty was no longer the absence of a measuring rod but the dispersal of what any rod would have to measure.

### Q: Who is held to have won the calculation debate?

It depends on which jury is asked, and the honest answer names the juries. The mid-century profession awarded the debate to the proposed solution, because within its own equilibrium framework the solution was complete. The revisionist scholarship of the nineteen eighties awarded it to the critics, on the ground that the objection which mattered by then, the knowledge objection, had never been addressed by the winning answer. A counter-revision disputes the revisionists' reading of the texts and points to later computational proposals. The article's own position is that the question has no single answer, because the debate it names contains two objections with different fates.

### Q: Was the trial and error model ever implemented?

No. No state organised its economy on the design: no board announced parametric prices and corrected them by observed shortage and surplus while its managers followed the two rules. The economies built in socialism's name allocated by command, by physical targets, and by negotiation between ministries and enterprises, which is a different machine sharing only the feature of public ownership. Two states approached parts of the neighbourhood, Yugoslavia by keeping genuine markets between socially owned firms and Hungary by a reform that freed some prices while retaining command over investment and appointments, but the design itself, as published, was never operated anywhere.

### Q: Does the Soviet collapse settle the calculation debate?

No, and the reason is a rule about evidence rather than a judgement about the Soviet record. A collapse can only test the system that was actually built, and the system that collapsed allocated by command and quantity targets, not by the announced and corrected prices of the proposed design. The design and the collapsed system share public ownership and almost nothing else. The collapse is therefore strong evidence about command planning and about the states that practised it, and it is no evidence at all, in either direction, about the model the calculation debate spent its second decade discussing.

### Q: Was the formal problem ever in doubt?

After 1908, no. In that year an Italian economist showed that the equations of an efficient allocation take the same form for a collectivist economy as for a competitive one, so the problem was never one of pure logic for any informed participant. The challenger of 1920 knew the result and built around it: his claim was never that the destination could not be described, but that a society without exchange in producer goods had no road to it, no process that would generate the numbers the equations need. Doubts about the formal problem belong to the debate's prehistory, not to the debate.

### Q: Why do accounts of this debate differ so much?

Because the accounts are written from different phases of an argument that moved, preserved by textbooks that froze one phase's verdict, and bent by the political weight the debate is made to carry. A history ending with the solution's publication reports a socialist success; a history beginning with the knowledge objection reports an Austrian one; both chronicles can be accurate while their verdicts collide. Add the shadow of a collapse that tested a different system from the one under discussion, and the divergence is fully explained without accusing any party of bad faith. The divergence is the predictable residue of grading a moved argument as though it had stood still.

### Q: What would answer the knowledge objection?

Two exhibits would do it, and the article states them as the verdict's defeater. The first is a demonstration that the information allocation needs can be produced and kept up to date without people generating it through their own participation in exchange. The second is a construction: a planning system, working at the scale of a national economy, that adjusts to scattered local change as rapidly as markets adjust. Either would overturn the verdict that the objection stands. Neither has been produced, and the verdict is therefore provisional in a specified way, which distinguishes it from a mere refusal to be persuaded.

### Q: How does the shifted objection grade the claims made for the debate's outcome?

It grades each claim down to the objection it actually answers. The famous refutation claim survives only against the moneyless design that the socialist camp itself abandoned, fails against the market socialist construction that met the original objection, and remains unproven as a claim about every possible socialism, since the surviving objection maps domains rather than issuing impossibilities. The claim that the refutation was itself refuted survives as a verdict on the formal question and fails as a verdict on the knowledge question, which it usually does not mention. Deflated to their proper rows, the two famous claims stop contradicting each other.

### Q: What should a student separate between calculating an allocation and knowing what to allocate?

Calculating an allocation means checking a proposed pattern of production against a common measure: given the list of goods, the techniques, and the ends, do the figures cohere, and which pattern does the arithmetic prefer. Knowing what to allocate is the prior and harder problem: discovering which goods should exist, which techniques are possible, and what the ends are worth to the people who hold them, when much of that knowledge is scattered, tacit, and created by acting. A student who keeps the two apart can see why an answer to the first problem, however complete, leaves the second untouched.

### Q: Why did the argument move from calculation to knowledge?

Because the first objection was met, and a met objection stops being the argument's frontier. Once announced prices and managerial rules had shown how calculation could proceed without owners, continuing to press the impossibility of calculation would have defended ground the other side had already taken. The critics' genuine remaining difficulty lay elsewhere, in the provenance of the information any calculating device must consume, and the argument migrated to the difficulty that was still alive. The migration was a deepening rather than a retreat: the new objection concedes everything the old one's opponents established and stands anyway, which is the mark of a stronger position, not a weaker one.

### Q: How does the two objections table order the claims?

The table orders the claims by giving each objection its own row and refusing to let either borrow the other's fate. Across a row the reader finds what the objection asserts, who asserted it, what evidence or construction would satisfy it, whether that satisfaction was ever supplied, and what follows for the verdict. Down the final column the two famous victory claims are assigned to the rows where they belong, the refutation to the first and the vindication to the same row read the other way, leaving the second row's claim standing apart. The ordering is the article's argument in miniature: separation first, grading second, verdict last.

### Q: What separates answering the calculation objection from answering the knowledge objection?

The two answers differ in what they must supply. Answering the calculation objection requires supplying an instrument: prices for producer goods that managers can actually use, generated without private owners. That instrument was designed in the nineteen thirties, and its design is the answer. Answering the knowledge objection requires supplying the instrument's fuel: the dispersed, largely unspoken information about circumstances, techniques, and wants, delivered to whoever decides, in usable form and as fast as circumstances change. No design has supplied that, because the objection's claim is that the fuel is created by the very process of exchange the instrument was built to replace.
