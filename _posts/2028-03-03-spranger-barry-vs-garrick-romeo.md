In the autumn of 1750 the London stage ran an experiment it has never repeated under such controlled conditions: the same play, in substantially the same text, on the same nights, in the two patent theatres, with the age's two leading tragedians divided between the houses in the same part. Spranger Barry played Romeo at Covent Garden, opposite Susannah Cibber. David Garrick played Romeo at Drury Lane, opposite George Anne Bellamy. Because nearly every other variable was held constant, the town's judgment on the two men survives as something rarer than gossip: a comparative verdict on how Romeo should be acted, delivered scene by scene by an audience that could walk from one theatre to the other and test its memory the following evening. Simultaneity is what gives the comparison its evidentiary force, and it deserves a sentence of its own. Acting judgments usually decay into incomparability within a season, because memory revises what it retains and no two performances share an audience in the same state of attention. The autumn of 1750 abolished the decay. The same town, in the same weeks, with the same text in its ears from the night before, could hold Barry's orchard against Garrick's while both were warm, and the partisanship the contest excited, so often treated as noise in the record, is better treated as its sensitivity: partisans attend more closely than neutrals, return more often, and compare more exactly, and a verdict formed under partisan heat by audiences shuttling between the houses is a verdict formed under laboratory attention. The verdict was split, and the split is the valuable part of the record. Barry was preferred as the lover, ardent and tender in the wooing. Garrick was preferred in the passion and grief that close the play. This article argues that the split was not a failure of the town's judgment but its finest precision, because the part of Romeo is written in two registers that make opposite demands on an actor's instrument, and that the palm, weighed across the whole role and not by single scenes, belongs to Barry: the play spends its capital in the wooing, and the catastrophe only wounds an audience that has first believed the love. Garrick's supremacy in the tomb is conceded scene by scene in what follows, and then weighed and found insufficient to overturn the larger verdict, for a reason his own career supplies: the tomb scene in which he triumphed was one he had himself rewritten to give the actor more time upon the stage, while the wooing in which Barry beat him is Shakespeare's unaided writing.

The comparison also carries a lesson that outlasts both men. Every later Romeo inherits the same divided assignment, and the history of the part after 1750, from John Philip Kemble's statuesque Verona to Johnston Forbes-Robertson's late Victorian lover, reads as a sequence of attempts to solve the problem the contest exposed: how one actor is to be, by turns, the most musical of Shakespeare's young lovers and the most frantic of his mourners, when the gifts for the two tasks pull in opposite directions. The 1750 evidence remains the cleanest map of that problem, because it was produced by rivalry under controlled conditions rather than by theory. What follows examines the part's two registers in the text, the two instruments that met them, the documented record of each man's Romeo, and the quarrel among the witnesses, before reaching the verdict the evidence supports.

## What Kind of Part Is Romeo? The Two Registers of the Role

Romeo enters the play performing love rather than feeling it, and the text says so in its own voice. The lovesick boy of the first act speaks in ready-made antitheses, "O brawling love! O loving hate!" (1.1.176), a young man reciting the Petrarchan script for Rosaline, a woman the audience never sees. Mercutio names the performance for what it is when he urges his friend, "You are a lover; borrow Cupid's wings / And soar with them above a common bound" (1.4.17-18): Romeo, at the rise of the action, is an amateur of Cupid's wardrobe. Friar Laurence delivers the play's own diagnosis when the boy arrives at dawn still fluent and sleepless: "Young men's love then lies / Not truly in their hearts, but in their eyes" (2.3.67-68). The dramatic task of the role is the conversion of that recited passion into a felt one, and the conversion is registered in the verse itself. The actor who cannot make the early fluency sound hollow will have nowhere to grow; the actor who plays the early scenes as sincere will flatten the very arc the contest of 1750 was fought over.

The first register, the wooing, is lyric, sustained, and architectural. At the Capulet feast Romeo's language builds shared structures with a stranger: the sonnet the lovers exchange, "If I profane with my unworthiest hand" (1.5.92), is a formal poem completed by two voices in alternation, and it asks of its speaker not fire but measure, a lover composing in real time and inviting completion. His first sight of Juliet is a held chord rather than an outburst: "O, she doth teach the torches to burn bright!" (1.5.44). The orchard scene extends the demand across the play's longest sustained lyric movement, from "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks?" (2.2.2) to the parting exchange, "Good night, good night! Parting is such sweet sorrow" (2.2.185). These scenes reward the actor who can sustain a line's music without breaking it into effects, who can let tenderness accumulate at the speed of verse rather than gesture. They are written for a voice that persuades by dwelling.

The shared sonnet concentrates the first register's whole demand into fourteen lines, and it is worth pausing on its mechanics because the scene became, in the contest, a kind of assay. Romeo opens with a conceit, his hand a pilgrim at a holy shrine, and Juliet does not merely receive the poem but corrects and extends it, granting the pilgrim his prayer while relocating its terms, until the form itself negotiates the kiss: "Saints do not move, though grant for prayers' sake" (1.5.104), and his answer takes the grant. The acting problem is exquisite. The verse is a game with rules, and it must be played as a game, each speaker visibly thinking inside the form; yet the game is also a seduction, and it must land as feeling, each line's wit arriving warm. An actor who plays the wit alone makes the scene a madrigal, clever and cold. An actor who plays the feeling alone makes it a blur, the form's articulation lost. The scene asks for ardour with its intelligence showing and intelligence with its ardour undimmed, at conversational speed, in rhyme. That the town awarded the wooing to Barry should not be read as awarding him the feeling against Garrick's intelligence in this scene; it should be read as finding that his ardour carried the form's thinking inside it, the rhymes discovered rather than delivered, while Garrick's intelligence, audible as intelligence, kept reminding the house that a performance was occurring. In a scene whose fiction is two strangers inventing a poem, the reminder is the one unforgivable effect, and the sonnet is where the contest's whole logic can be watched in miniature.

The second register is the mourning, and it is written in the opposite grain: convulsive, interrupted, and fast. When the sentence of banishment falls, Romeo collapses in Friar Laurence's cell in a speech that veers between pleading and self-laceration: "There is no world without Verona walls, / But purgatory, torture, hell itself" (3.3.17-18), and, at the Friar's offer of philosophy, "Be merciful, say 'death'" is the drift of a boy who hears comfort as cruelty. The Friar's rebuke anatomizes the performance problem exactly: "Art thou a man? Thy form cries out thou art; / Thy tears are womanish" (3.3.109-110). This Romeo is not sustaining a mood but ricocheting among them, and the verse demands an actor of rapid transition, of starts and breaks. The same demand returns at Mantua, where grief compresses into a single detonation, "Is it e'en so? Then I defy you, stars!" (5.1.24), and in the tomb, where the long final speech moves through wonder, tenderness, resolve, and a farewell tenderness again within a hundred lines. An actor built for the first register will find the second exhausting in the wrong way, spending his sustained tone on material that keeps interrupting itself. An actor built for the second will find the first register exposing, because lyric wooing cannot be fragmented into points without losing the persuasion that is its whole effect.

### What in the text makes Romeo two parts rather than one?

The registers are distributed by the plot's hinge, not blended through it. Before Mercutio's death, Romeo's assignments are the feast, the orchard, the marriage, and the dawn parting, all lyric occasions in sustained verse shared with Juliet. After the hinge, his assignments are the cell, the Mantuan resolve, and the tomb, all solitary or confrontational, in fractured verse.

The role therefore tests sustained musical wooing first and convulsive grief second, in sequence, and an actor's strength in either half is weak evidence about the other.

The dawn parting of Act 3, Scene 5 is the hinge scene in miniature, and it is the most instructive passage in the part for the Barry and Garrick question, because both registers sound inside a single short scene. Juliet's "It was the nightingale, and not the lark" (3.5.2) opens a lyric duet of the orchard kind, two voices bargaining with the morning. Within twenty lines Romeo has swung to the second register's absolute: "Let me be ta'en, let me be put to death" (3.5.17), a lover offering his life with a completeness that anticipates the tomb. The scene is a test any Romeo must pass in both directions at once, first dwelling and then detonating, and it foreshadows the verdict of 1750 with uncanny neatness: the town would divide the role between the man who could dwell and the man who could detonate, and quarrel for two centuries over which half of this scene, and of the role, mattered more.

Two further features of the writing sharpen the division, and both were legible to the audience of 1750 even if no pamphlet states them in terms. The first is the marriage scene, Act 2, Scene 6, where Romeo's first register is put under the pressure of joy rather than longing and nearly fails the test the Friar sets it. The boy arrives at the cell in a transport, and the Friar must school him in real time: "These violent delights have violent ends" (2.6.9) is a warning addressed to a lover whose feeling runs ahead of every occasion, and the scene asks its Romeo to register correction without losing ardour, to be chastened and transported in the same breath. It is a miniature of the whole role's difficulty, ardour and governance demanded simultaneously, and it explains why the town's witnesses kept returning to the language of nature when they praised Barry: the scene cannot be pointed into brilliance, because its effect is a balance held, not a series of effects struck. A sustaining instrument holds it by temperament. A transitional instrument must manufacture the appearance of holding it, and manufacture, in this scene, is detectable.

The second feature is an absence. Romeo is denied the bravura set piece that the period's acting reputations were built on. Mercutio has the Queen Mab speech, a solo display so self-sufficient that Henry Woodward's performance of it earned its own bestselling print in 1753, engraved by W. Herbert, while the play's Romeo has nothing of the kind: no aria, no mad scene, no single speech in which the actor may step forward and exhibit. His excellence is distributed across duets and reactions, in the sonnet shared line by line with Juliet, in listening at a window, in answering a friar, in receiving news. The part therefore resists the century's usual machinery of fame, the isolated great moment, and rewards instead the actor whose quality is continuous. This structural fact quietly governs the whole comparison. Garrick, the supreme manufacturer of great moments, was competing in the one part that offers its Romeo no moment to manufacture, while Barry, whose quality was a climate rather than a weather event, found the part's distribution of labour exactly suited to his gift. The 1750 verdict begins to look less like a preference between two men than like the part itself voting.

Between the two registers the part also keeps a third, smaller mode, and it matters to the comparison because it is the mode in which the lover is neither singing nor breaking but doing. The Romeo of the ladder commission in Act 2, Scene 4 is brisk, secretive, and funny, a plotter arranging cords and hours with the Nurse in prose-paced exchanges that ask for timing rather than tone. The mode is brief, but it is the hinge of credibility between the registers: an audience must believe that the dreamer of the orchard can also organize a marriage before noon, or the plot's machinery creaks. Both men of 1750 could play it, Garrick by native quickness and Barry by the conspiratorial warmth that made his confidences feel like admissions, and the town's witnesses, significantly, never divided the palm over these scenes. The contest was fought where the part is extreme, in its longest lyricism and its deepest fracture, and the undivided middle is a useful control: where the writing made no extreme demand, the two instruments served it indistinguishably, and the legend of their difference dissolves. The difference was real only where the text is real in two opposite ways at once, which is to say in the scenes by which Romeos are still judged.

Understanding the part this way disciplines the whole comparison that follows. The question of 1750 was never which man was the greater actor in the abstract; Charles Churchill's Rosciad had already settled the general chair for Garrick a decade later, and no serious witness disputed Garrick's wider supremacy across the whole repertory. The question was narrower and more interesting: which man's instrument matched which half of a divided role, and which half of the role a Romeo should be judged by. Those are the questions the documented record can answer, and they are the questions this article keeps in view.

## Two Instruments: Barry's Figure and Voice Against Garrick's Fire

Spranger Barry was born in Dublin on 23 November 1719, a silversmith's son brought up to his father's trade, and he came to the stage with the two assets that cannot be taught: a person of striking beauty and a voice of natural music. The eleventh edition of the Encyclopaedia Britannica, digesting the period's record, calls him "a singularly handsome man, with the advantage of height which Garrick lacked", and Churchill's Rosciad, a hostile witness where Barry is concerned, concedes the figure before attacking the execution: "In person taller than the common size, / Behold where Barry draws admiring eyes!" (The Rosciad, 1761). Height in a lover is not decoration. Romeo spends his great scenes at a vertical disadvantage of the fiction's own making, below a window, beneath a balcony rail, looking up; the orchard scene is staged yearning toward a height the lover cannot reach, and a tall actor converts that geometry into presence, filling the lower stage as the fixed point toward which the scene's desire travels. A contemporary female theatregoer, whose remark the anecdotal record preserved because it compressed the town's judgment into a single image, put the figure's effect beyond improvement: had she been Juliet to Garrick's Romeo, so impassioned was he that she would have expected him to climb up to her; had she been Juliet to Barry's Romeo, so tender and seductive was he that she would certainly have come down to him. The wit of the remark is its precision about direction. Garrick's Romeo exerts force upward, striving; Barry's exerts attraction downward, drawing. The play's geometry sides with attraction, since its lover is answered, not rewarded for climbing: Juliet descends to the marriage, to the parting, and at last, in the tomb, to the dagger's level.

The voice completed the instrument. The period's descriptions of Barry's speaking return to the same cluster of words: melodious, musical, tender, insinuating. His was a voice for sustained utterance, for letting a speech's feeling gather along an unbroken line, which is the exact technical requirement of the first register. But the same instrument carried a documented defect, and Churchill, again the hostile witness whose concessions are therefore worth the most, located it with a satirist's accuracy. When Barry's passions laboured for violent expression, Churchill wrote, the voice failed the figure: "His voice comes forth, like Echo from her cell, / To swell the tempest needful aid denies, / And all adown the stage in feeble murmurs dies." The charge is specific and mechanical: Barry's voice, ravishing at lyric pressure, thinned when the verse demanded tempest. Churchill even turned the defect into a compliment about the opposite gift, asking who else could give "such well applauded tenderness to Lear" (The Rosciad, 1761). Tenderness was the signature; force was the tax. A Romeo anatomized by this evidence predicts the 1750 verdict almost line for line: the wooing, where the voice's music works at its natural pressure, would be Barry's; the tempest scenes, the cell and the tomb's rages against fortune, would strain him exactly where Churchill said the strain would come.

### Why did Barry's voice fail him exactly where Garrick's caught fire?

Because the voices were built at opposite pressures. Barry's instrument worked by sustaining tone, and sustained tone is spent, not strengthened, by interruption; when verse breaks into starts and self-interruption, as Romeo's after the banishment, the voice must smooth the breaks away or thin out. Garrick's instrument was the reverse: a transitional delivery whose effects lived in the breaks themselves.

Verse written in fractures therefore sounded, from Garrick, like thought in the act of happening, which is the precise illusion the second register requires.

David Garrick, born at Hereford in 1717, two years Barry's senior, carried the opposite endowment and the opposite defect. He stood about five feet four inches; contemporaries described him as low in stature, and the jibe-structure of the period's wit returns to his size as reliably as it returns to Barry's height. What the small figure housed was the most mobile expressive instrument of the century: dark, brilliant eyes, a face of extraordinary quickness, and a delivery built on transition, the famous starts and pauses by which a passion was seen to arrive rather than declared. Richard Cumberland, recalling Garrick's first effect beside the statuesque Quin, described "little Garrick, young and light and alive in every muscle and feature", stepping across a century in a single scene. That aliveness is the second register's exact requirement. Romeo in the cell, Romeo at Mantua, Romeo in the tomb must be watched changing, and Garrick's art was the art of visible change. The cost was paid in the first register. A lover whose effects arrive by transition will keep arriving, and the orchard scene punishes arrival: its persuasion is cumulative and still, and an audience watching a wooer of starts and points may admire the acting while ceasing to believe the courtship. The town's eventual judgment that Garrick's Romeo was impassioned where Barry's was persuasive is the sound of that cost being entered in the ledger.

One further asymmetry completes the instruments, and it is the one the contest made impossible to ignore. Barry's endowment was natural and Garrick's was manufactured by genius out of recalcitrant material, and eighteenth-century audiences, trained by the period's criticism to prize nature in acting, could feel the difference without naming it. Barry looked and sounded like what Romeo is supposed to be, a beautiful young man in the grip of a first passion; Garrick looked like what Romeo is not supposed to be, a small, intense man of thirty-three playing a boy, and had to make the audience forget the mismatch by force of art. In most parts the triumph of art over mismatch is the greater glory, and the period awarded it to Garrick across his repertory. In this part the mismatch argument runs the other way, because Romeo is the one great role in which being, rather than seeming, is the rarest effect: the lover must be believed before he is admired, and belief is what Barry's unforced instrument supplied in the scenes where belief is decided.

The formation of the two instruments explains their distribution of gifts, because neither man arrived at his manner by accident. Barry was a tradesman's son with no theatrical inheritance, raised to the silversmith's bench in Dublin, and his rise was the rise of an endowment discovered rather than a technique acquired: the Smock Alley engagement of 1744 prospered at once on the strength of what he was, and his subsequent career shows the same pattern of a nature finding its registers one by one, the Moor in 1746, the lover by 1748, Lear's tenderness thereafter. Nothing in his record suggests a student of acting; everything suggests an instrument whose limits were the limits of the nature itself, which is why Churchill's anatomy of him reads like a description of weather. Garrick, by contrast, was the century's great student of his own effects, the actor who rehearsed transitions before mirrors of company opinion, whose celebrated start in Hamlet, the recoil at the Ghost that the period took as the signature of his naturalism, was a prepared mechanism refined across hundreds of repetitions. His method was observation converted into mechanism: watch how feeling actually arrives in a face, then build the machinery that reproduces the arrival nightly. The method is the more admirable achievement and the more portable one, since machinery can be taught, and Garrick's revolution in English acting is precisely the history of his machinery becoming the profession's standard. But machinery has a signature its operator cannot remove. It produces effects at points, in sequence, and an audience of connoisseurs, which the London audience of 1750 supremely was, learns to see the joins. In the wooing scenes the joins are the one thing the illusion cannot afford, because a lover perceived to be assembling his tenderness has already lost the argument the scene is making. Barry's tenderness had no joins to see. That, in the end, is the technical content of the word nature in the period's praise of him, and it is a content the modern reader can verify against the text: the orchard scene is written in periods too long for points, and it was Barry's voice, not Garrick's ingenuity, that was built to the period's measure.

Behind both instruments stood the critical word of the age, nature, and the contest was, among other things, a referendum on what the word should mean. The old school both men displaced, the statuesque declamation of James Quin, had located dignity in a measured, chant-like delivery that announced passion from a platform of composure; Cumberland's memory of Garrick bounding on beside the pasteboard Quin, stepping over a century in a single scene, is the period's own image of that school's fall. Against Quin, both Barry and Garrick were naturalists. But their naturalisms were different substances sharing a label. Garrick's nature was verisimilitude achieved: the studied reproduction of feeling's outward signs, so exact that audiences mistook the mechanism for the thing itself. Barry's nature was identity: the outward signs were the thing itself, requiring no reproduction, because the man and the lover's feeling occupied the same body without an interpreter between them. The town of 1750, fluent in the vocabulary, understood that it was being offered nature at two removes in the one house and nature at none in the other, and its palm records which remove it preferred in a lover. The preference was part-specific, and the period knew it: in Richard III, in Hamlet, in the repertory's villains and madmen, the manufactured nature of Garrick outran any identity an actor could bring, because no actor is a usurper or a melancholy prince by endowment. In Romeo alone, among the great parts, an actor could simply be the thing, young, beautiful, and enamoured in his own person, and Barry was. The contest's deepest teaching is that the crown of nature changes meaning with the part, and that criticism which ignores the part's demands will misread every verdict the period left behind.

## Before the Contest: Barry's Romeo at Garrick's Own Theatre

The rivalry of 1750 was the second act of a story that began inside Garrick's own company, and the order of events matters because it establishes whose part Romeo was before it became a battlefield. Barry's first London appearance was made in 1746 as Othello at Drury Lane, and the Moor at once disclosed the shape of the coming quarrel: Garrick, who had himself played Othello, gave the part up after seeing what Barry made of it, an early instance of the manager ceding a role to the instrument it fitted. At Drury Lane, in Hamlet and Macbeth, the two men alternated, and the alternation bred the jealousy the Britannica record names plainly: Barry aroused Garrick's resentment "by his success as Romeo". The success belonged to the season of 1748, when Garrick mounted his adaptation of the tragedy at Drury Lane and cast, not himself, but Barry as Romeo opposite Susannah Cibber's Juliet. The adaptation itself, its descent from Theophilus Cibber's 1744 version, its suppression of Rosaline, and its rebuilt tomb scene, is the subject of the companion study of [David Garrick's adaptation of 1748](/2027/08/27/david-garrick-adaptation-1748/), and is treated here at the single passage the comparison requires: Garrick the adapter wrote a Romeo for Barry's instrument, and for two seasons the most famous lover in London was Garrick's employee, speaking Garrick's text, in Garrick's theatre, while Garrick watched from the manager's box. No account of 1750 that begins with the contest itself can explain its heat. The contest was a manager reclaiming a part he had owned only as property, from the actor who owned it as performance.

The Victoria and Albert Museum's catalogue of the Wilson painting preserves the casting's sharpest irony in a single clause: Garrick "wrote his adaptation for Spranger Barry and Susannah Cibber, who played the lovers in 1748 at Drury Lane". Adaptations are composed toward instruments, and a version built for Barry's sustaining voice and Cibber's pathos will carry their measures in its bones: the wooing arranged for dwelling, the tomb arranged for a recognition scene in which two supreme pathetic performers could play against each other at length. When Garrick stepped into his own composition in 1750, he was therefore acting inside a suit cut for another man's figure, in the wooing scenes above all, and some part of the town's verdict is a tailor's verdict: the garment fitted its intended wearer. Garrick's countervailing advantage was the adapter's knowledge of every seam, and in the tomb, the scene most thoroughly his own manufacture, the advantage told. But a Romeo is not judged by his best-fitting scene; he is judged by the fit across an evening, and the evening's longest clothes were Barry's.

The secession of 1750 converted that private tension into public war. Barry and Cibber left Drury Lane for Covent Garden, John Rich's house, carrying the play with them in the town's imagination, and when Covent Garden advertised its new stars in Garrick's version of the tragedy for 28 September 1750, Garrick answered by announcing himself as Romeo for the same night, opposite Bellamy, in his own version at his own house. The event's full record, the raids and counter-raids between the companies, the rival funeral processions, and the box-office evidence, belongs to the study of [the 1750 Romeo and Juliet stage war](/2027/09/03/1750-romeo-and-juliet-stage-war/) and is held to this passage here. What the comparison requires from the event is only its experimental design and its duration. Both houses played the tragedy on the same nights, twelve performances at Covent Garden and thirteen at Drury Lane, the thirteenth a Drury Lane flourish after Covent Garden withdrew its production when Cibber tired. Audiences with money and leisure could, and did, attend both houses in alternation, carrying the previous night's Romeo in the ear while watching his rival. No other acting comparison of the century was ever conducted under conditions that controlled so well for text, timing, and audience.

One feature of the design deserves emphasis because it disciplines every verdict quoted later in this article: both Romeos spoke Garrick's adaptation. The Covent Garden production was not Shakespeare unmediated against Drury Lane's doctored text; Rich's company had acquired the rights in Garrick's version the previous year, and the version's distinguishing marks, the Rosaline material reassigned to Juliet, the funeral procession and dirge, and above all the tomb scene rebuilt so that Juliet wakes while Romeo still lives, were common property of both stages during the contest. The comparison of the two actors is therefore unusually pure. Whatever Barry did in the added tomb conversation, he did inside Garrick's invention; whatever Garrick did in the orchard, he did inside his own arrangement of Shakespeare's wooing. When the town preferred Barry in the love scenes, it preferred him on the adapter's own ground, a fact that should be held in mind when the weighing of the verdict arrives.

One preparation for the contest deserves record because it explains the audience's competence. For four seasons before 1750 the London town had been trained in comparative judgment of these two men by the Drury Lane alternation itself. In Hamlet and in Macbeth the pair had shared parts across the same weeks, Barry's majestic person and musical utterance set against Garrick's restless intelligence in role after role, until the town's playgoing class had acquired the habit, and the vocabulary, of part-by-part adjudication between them. The Romeo contest did not invent its audience's method; it inherited an audience already practised in exactly this sport, already possessed of the categories, figure against fire, nature against art, into which the new evidence would be sorted. The speed with which the split verdict formed in the autumn of 1750, and the precision of its allocation, are the products of that training. London had been rehearsing the comparison for years on other parts; Romeo merely gave the rehearsal its perfect subject, the one part in the repertory whose writing is itself divided between the two men's home territories. When the verdict came in split, it came in the shape of everything the town had already learned about its two tragedians, applied to the role that fitted the lesson best.

## Spranger Barry's Romeo: The Tender Lover Anatomized

Barry's Romeo rested on a single theatrical proposition, which the witnesses state in different vocabularies and never contradict: that the lover's persuasion should be an emanation of the man rather than a display of the actor. His height and beauty did the silent half of the work before he spoke, and his voice did the rest by refusing to seem to work at all. The period's most considered modern summary, the Garrick Club's biographical record of Barry, states the outcome in terms that leave little room for refinement: "In the controversy over the better Romeo, though Garrick was admired for his great passion, the palm went to Barry, whose natural physical beauty and grace more suited him to the role." The sentence is exact about the grounds of the award. The palm went not to the finer technique but to the finer suitability, and suitability in a lover is not a consolation prize; it is the substance of the illusion, because the audience of a love tragedy must first grant the premise that this man, on sight, is the one for whom a guarded daughter would stake her life. Barry's Romeo granted the premise at his entrance.

The wooing scenes showed what the instrument did with its advantage. In the orchard scene, the part's longest stretch of sustained lyric address, Barry's method was dwelling: the verse allowed to unroll at the pace of a man listening as much as speaking, the compliments shaped as discoveries rather than compliments. The anecdotal tradition's best evidence, the theatregoer's remark about coming down from the balcony, is evidence about this scene above all, and its logic deserves slow reading. She does not say that Barry's Romeo was the more moving in grief, or the more intelligent, or the more correct. She says that his tenderness reversed the scene's physics: the woman at the window, placed by the fiction out of reach, would have surrendered the advantage of height and come down to him. That is a judgment about erotic credibility delivered as stage geometry, and it identifies the precise effect the first register exists to produce. The orchard scene is a negotiation in which Juliet, not Romeo, holds the power; she sets the terms, names the danger, and proposes the marriage. A Romeo who strives upward, all visible effort, fights the scene's distribution of power. A Romeo whose tenderness draws downward completes it. Barry, by every account that survives, completed it.

### Which single scene did contemporaries treat as the wooing test?

The orchard scene, Act 2, Scene 2, the balcony scene, because it is the part's longest lyric persuasion, conducted at a disadvantage of height and darkness against a partner controlling the terms. Elliot's print of 1759, showing Barry with Nossiter, fixes them in this scene: the image trade, which sold what the town remembered, remembered Barry at the window.

It is no accident that the image chose this scene rather than any other, since the orchard is where the two Romeos' difference was most continuously on display, sustained across the play's longest stretch of wooing rather than concentrated in a single effect.

The feast and the sonnet belonged to the same economy. The shared sonnet of Act 1, Scene 5 is a duet in the strictest sense, fourteen lines whose rhymes pass between the speakers like a ball kept in the air, and its effect depends on the Romeo's capacity to make composition sound like discovery, to seem to find each answering rhyme at the moment of speaking it. Cibber's Juliet was the ideal partner for this work, the period's supreme actress of pathos, and the partnership matters to the comparison: Barry's tenderness had, in Cibber, a respondent whose own gift was susceptibility, so that the wooing at Covent Garden played as a circuit completed at both ends. At Drury Lane, Bellamy's Juliet, vivid and spirited, answered Garrick's fire with fire, and the circuit there ran hotter and faster. The town's divided memory of the two productions is partly a memory of two chemistries, and an honest anatomy of the contest must enter the Juliets in evidence before sentencing the Romeos: Barry wooed the actress best built to be wooed in his manner, and part of his palm is hers.

Against this, the documented failure of Barry's Romeo stands in the second register, exactly where Churchill's anatomy predicted it. The tempest passages, the cell scene's collapse and the tomb's rages, asked his voice for the convulsive force it could not supply without thinning, and the town's reservation about his later scenes is the consistent corollary of its rapture about his early ones. Barry's way with grief was elegiac rather than frantic: he mourned beautifully where the text, after the banishment, asks for a boy coming apart. The distinction is visible in the shape of his fame after 1750. His Romeo did not remain a single season's marvel but settled into a durable possession, played opposite a succession of Juliets, most fatefully opposite Nossiter, his pupil and partner, at Covent Garden from October 1753, and fixed in the public memory by the 1759 print in the balcony scene. The part fitted him so completely that the century's shorthand, as the chronicle tradition records it, made his Romeo the standard of the role, the performance against which the very idea of the lover was measured. Durability of that kind is itself critical evidence: novelties fade in a season, and possessions endure because audiences keep recognizing the thing itself in them.

The repertory Barry built at Covent Garden after the secession shows the Romeo persona being spent, deliberately, across a line of kindred parts: Lothario in The Fair Penitent, the seducer whose name became the language's shorthand for the irresistible lover; Jaffeir and Pierre in Venice Preserv'd, the friend and the conspirator divided between ardour and honour; Osmyn in Zara; and in time the tragic summits of Lear and Douglas. The Garrick Club's record lists the sequence as the establishment of a major player and a continuing rival, and its significance for the comparison is that the town kept buying Barry in the lover's register for years after the contest, in part after part where ardent persuasion was the assignment. A verdict delivered in one autumn might be a fashion; a decade of casting in the verdict's direction is a market's confirmation. Garrick's repertory across the same decade ran the other way, toward the parts of intellect, villainy, and convulsion, Richard, Hamlet, Macbeth, Lear, in which his supremacy was the age's commonplace. The two careers, read as casting decisions made by managers who knew their assets, ratify the town's allocation from opposite sides: each man was thereafter sold in the register the contest had awarded him, and Romeo, the part that straddles both registers, remained the disputed border territory between two otherwise settled kingdoms.

The afterlife of Barry's Romeo inside his own career supplies the confirmation that the palm was a property judgment and not a season's enthusiasm. In October 1753, at Covent Garden, Barry presented a new Juliet of his own training, the eighteen-year-old Maria Isabella Nossiter, in the part Cibber had made her own, and Cibber herself attended the performance, an act of professional witness the anecdotal record preserved because it understood the ceremony involved: the palm's partner inspecting the succession. The pairing prospered, in the play and beyond it, and the Romeo that had won the town in 1750 was still Barry's calling card when Elliot's print fixed the couple at the window in 1759. Between those dates the part travelled with him through his Covent Garden seasons as a settled possession, the one role in which his supremacy was treated by managers and audiences as a premise rather than a claim. Garrick's Romeo, by contrast, generated no succession and no school; it remained a feat, datable to a single autumn, remembered through a painting. The distinction between a possession and a feat is the distinction the whole comparison turns on. A feat astonishes and is filed; a possession is inhabited, and other actors measure themselves against the inhabitant. Barry inhabited Romeo for the better part of a decade in the town's sight. The century's later Romeos were compared, in the first instance, not to the manager who had won the thirteenth night but to the lover who had won the palm, and that comparative afterlife is the practical meaning of the verdict the town delivered.

## David Garrick's Romeo: Passion Against the Grain of the Part

Garrick came to Romeo late, unwillingly, and on challenge, and the circumstances are written into the performance's documented character. He was thirty-three years old, the manager of Drury Lane, the acknowledged head of his profession, and he had never played the part, because at his own theatre the part had belonged to his subordinate. His assumption of it on 28 September 1750 was an act of management before it was an act of art: the bills had promised Barry at the rival house, and only Garrick's own name could hold Drury Lane's audience against the seceders. The performance he gave solved the problem his endowment set him by the means his endowment supplied. Where Barry's Romeo emanated, Garrick's acted: a lover of starts, transitions, and visible thought, whose passion was legible in the face's weather and the body's quicksilver changes. The Garrick Club summary's phrase for the town's response, "admired for his great passion", locates the admiration precisely; passion was the quality nobody denied him, in this part or any other. What the town withheld was the separate, prior concession that Barry won by existing: the belief that the lover's tenderness was the man's nature rather than the actor's achievement.

The second register was where the achievement told. In the cell scene, Garrick's art of transition met verse written in transitions, and the witnesses' general verdict on his later scenes reflects a genuine technical superiority. Romeo's collapse under the banishment sentence is a sequence of emotional reversals, from incredulity through pleading to a despair that the Friar must physically and rhetorically reassemble, and an actor whose genius was the visible arrival of feeling could play the sequence as a single continuous event in the mind. The Mantuan detonation, "Then I defy you, stars!" (5.1.24), is a one-line part in itself, and it is Garrick's kind of line: a passion arriving all at once, in the open, with the body as its instrument. Barry's sustaining voice, set against such moments, produced pathos where the text wants detonation, and the town heard the difference nightly. The modern scholarly tradition, in the RSC edition materials of Hugh Macrae Richmond, preserves the resulting allocation in a single sentence: Barry was more praised as an ardent lover, while Garrick was felt to be better suited to the tragic aspects of the role. Ardent lover to Barry; tragic aspects to Garrick. The sentence has the balance of a treaty, and like a treaty it records where each army stood when the fighting stopped.

Even in Shakespeare's unaltered text, the tomb speech is built to Garrick's measure, and the point deserves its weight because it keeps the comparison honest about the adapter's advantage. Romeo's final speech is a sequence of addresses, to the vault's mouth, to Tybalt's corpse with an apology, to Juliet's beauty with wonder, to his own resolve with a toast, each unit turning the feeling a quarter-circle from the last. It is transitional writing, a part for an actor whose genius is the visible arrival of successive states, and Garrick would have been formidable in it had he never touched the text. What his adaptation added was not the opportunity but the duration and the witness: more turns of the wheel, and a living face at the end of them. The distinction matters for the weighing. Garrick's tomb supremacy rests partly on Shakespeare's construction, which any Romeo inherits, and partly on his own enlargement, which only his Romeo enjoyed on equal nights, since Barry spoke the same enlarged text at Covent Garden and could not make its pathos his citadel. The enlargement, tested in the rival's mouth, proved to be an advantage of fit rather than of authorship: the added conversation rewarded the transitional instrument that had designed it, and taxed the sustaining instrument it had not been designed for. An adapter who builds a scene for his own kind of acting and then watches it defeat the age's greatest sustaining voice has demonstrated something about the scene as well as about the voices, and the town's verdict, awarding him the scene and Barry the role, priced the demonstration exactly.

The tomb scene was Garrick's citadel, and its fortifications were his own building. Shakespeare's Romeo dies in ignorance, drinking to a Juliet he believes dead lines before she wakes; Garrick's adaptation, following the path Cibber's version had opened, slowed the poison's work so that Juliet wakes while Romeo lives, and the lovers are granted a final conversation of mutual recognition. The Victoria and Albert Museum's account of Benjamin Wilson's painting of the scene states the outcome flatly: Garrick's performance "was judged the more successful, particularly in the pathos of the tomb scene". The judgment is inseparable from the stage picture Wilson's immensely popular print of 1753 fixed in the public memory: Garrick's Romeo upright in the lighted tomb, arm raised in shock at the waking Juliet, Paris dead in the foreground. Garrick the actor triumphed in the scene; Garrick the adapter had first enlarged the scene's actor-proof opportunities, extending the dying man's stage time and giving him a partner to play against in place of a corpse. Francis Gentleman's Dramatic Censor of 1770, auditing the adaptation a generation later, praised the alteration in superlative terms while conceding of its added conversation that it was not, in strict necessity, "absolutely essential": the period's own criticism thus distinguished, as this article does, between the scene's theatrical power and its textual necessity, and awarded the power to Garrick with the reservation recorded.

### What did Garrick's altered ending give his Romeo that Shakespeare's text denies him?

Time, and a living partner. Shakespeare's Romeo spends his death speech addressing a woman he believes a corpse, and dies unreconciled to the truth by minutes. Garrick's alteration delays the poison, wakes Juliet, and converts the death into a dialogue of recognition, multiplying the dying man's opportunities for pathos and giving him the period's most celebrated stage picture.

The advantage was real, and it was the adapter's gift to the actor, in this case the same man.

The Drury Lane partnership supplies its own evidence, and it cuts in Garrick's favour in one respect that an honest anatomy must enter. George Anne Bellamy, his Juliet, published her Apology in 1785, a memoir of her theatrical life that preserves the season of 1750 from inside the Drury Lane pairing, and the partnership she describes was a working instrument of mutual adjustment rather than a mismatch: her Juliet was young, vivid, and spirited, a respondent who met Garrick's tempo instead of moderating it, so that the Drury Lane wooing played at a heat the Covent Garden version deliberately declined. Audiences who preferred Drury Lane's first acts were not simply erring; they were preferring a different theatrical good, excitement over persuasion, and the town's split verdict contains their testimony as well as the palm's. What the palm's award to Barry establishes is not that Garrick's wooing failed but that, weighed as wooing, as the manufacture of belief in a first passion, heat ranked below tenderness in the judgment of the disinterested summaries. The distinction preserves Garrick's honour and Barry's crown at once, which is why the period's record, read whole, sustains it so comfortably.

Garrick's age in the part deserves the same even handling, because it is the point on which careless summary most often convicts him unfairly. He was thirty-three, playing a boy whose age the play leaves unnumbered but whose whole presentation is of adolescence, and Barry at thirty was scarcely better placed in years. The difference was not age but the appearance of age. Garrick's quicksilver vivacity, the "young and light" quality Cumberland remembered from 1741, still served him in 1750 as a kind of theatrical youth, and nobody's testimony complains that his Romeo looked old; the complaint, where it forms, is that his Romeo looked busy, that the machinery of feeling stayed visible in a part whose lover must seem to feel before he seems to act. Barry, taller, graver, and slower, read as the elder man in manner and the younger in illusion, because stillness is the stage's great de-ager and Barry's Romeo stood inside the stillness his voice made. The paradox is worth retaining for the lessons section: in this part, the younger effect belongs to the actor who does less, and Garrick's supreme technical resource, his density of action, was the one asset the wooing taxed rather than rewarded.

There remains the question of what Garrick's Romeo did to the play's temperature at Drury Lane across thirteen nights, and here the managerial and acting records merge. The production was a triumph of his house: the funeral procession and dirge, not promised in his bills, arrived as a surprise effect of exactly the kind his showmanship specialized in, and Tate Wilkinson, the Covent Garden loyalist who was a stage-struck boy in that autumn, conceded across his allegiance that Garrick's unpromised procession gave "a striking effect and an agreeable surprise" (Memoirs of his own life, 1790). A witness from the enemy house, testifying to the enemy manager's coup, establishes the Drury Lane evening as an event whose total force exceeded its Romeo. That is the final refinement the comparison requires. Garrick lost the palm for the part while winning the evenings, and he won the evenings partly by means, the procession, the picture, the publicity, that were not acting at all. The town, to its credit, kept the two ledgers separate, and its split verdict is the proof.

None of this should be read as diminishing what Garrick's Romeo was, and the anatomy must record its genuine conquests before weighing them. A performance that held its house for thirteen nights against the most beautiful lover of the age, that generated the century's bestselling theatrical image of the play, and that the manager's own summary tradition remembers as a triumph of pathos, was no failure wearing a crown. Garrick's Romeo was the finest performance of the second register the century produced, and in the tomb it set a standard of enacted grief that the visual record still transmits. The argument of this article is not that his Romeo was poor but that it was partial, and partial in the half of the role that carries less of the play's weight. Romeo is on stage as a lover for the great majority of his scenes; he is a mourner in extremity for a minority of them. A crown divided by scenes will always be disputed by partisans of the tomb, but a crown weighed by the role's whole economy, by where its verse spends its length and where its audience must invest its belief, falls where the town's palm fell. The full weighing belongs to the verdict section below; first, the quarrel among the witnesses must be heard, because the record's disagreements are themselves evidence about what kind of judgment the town delivered.

## The Split Verdict Examined: Poets, Biographers, and the Manufacture of Fame

The evidence for the two Romeos comes in four kinds, and they do not agree, which is why the contest still repays examination. There is the satirist's anatomy, Churchill's Rosciad of 1761, written by a poet with scores to settle and the century's sharpest ear for vocal defect. There is the insider narrative, Thomas Davies's Memoirs of the Life of David Garrick of 1780, written by an actor who had served in the Drury Lane company and became his manager's biographer. There is the partisan monument, Arthur Murphy's Life of David Garrick of 1801, written to fix its subject's supremacy for posterity. And there is the modern scholarly audit, of which Leslie Ritchie's study of Garrick and the mediation of celebrity, with its close reading of the Battle of the Romeos as a contest in media management, is the most instructive for this article's purpose. Read together, the four kinds of evidence produce a verdict more reliable than any of them alone, because their disagreements fall in predictable places and their agreements fall in the one place that matters.

Churchill first, because his testimony is the most hostile and therefore the most probative where it concedes. The Rosciad conducts a formal contest for the chair of the English stage and awards it, in the end, to Garrick: on the general question of the greater actor, the poem is a Garrick document. Yet its Barry stanzas, quoted in part earlier in this article, perform an anatomy whose details no Garrick partisan ever refuted. The figure draws admiring eyes. The tenderness is real and singled out, in Lear, as the quality no rival could supply. The voice, under tempest pressure, comes forth like Echo from her cell and dies in feeble murmurs. A satirist awarding the crown to Garrick while documenting, in Barry, the exact distribution of strengths that the 1750 town reported, tenderness supreme and tempest deficient, is evidence of the strongest kind: the hostile witness confirming the opposing case's central fact. Churchill's anatomy explains the split verdict's mechanism a decade after the event, and nothing in the subsequent record contradicts his account of either instrument.

Davies supplies the narrative spine: the secession, the simultaneous opening on 28 September 1750, the nightly comparison, the twelve nights at both houses, the withdrawal of the Covent Garden production as Cibber tired, and the thirteenth Drury Lane performance that closed the contest as a managerial triumph. His position requires the reader's care. Davies wrote as Garrick's biographer and former employee, and his Memoirs are a monument raised from inside the Drury Lane interest; a narrative so placed might be expected to award the acting palm to its subject along with the managerial one. The received tradition does not show him doing so. The summaries that descend from the period's record, Genest's great digest of the stage's annals among them, preserve the allocation the RSC tradition still repeats: the love scenes to Barry, the tragic weight to Garrick, the palm in the role to the Covent Garden lover. When a subject's own biographer lets the rival's central conquest stand in the record unreversed, the conquest may be taken as established. Davies's value to this article is precisely that restraint: he proves the split was not a Covent Garden pamphlet's invention but the settled account even in the house of the defeated claimant.

Murphy is the source of the counter-tradition, and his Life of 1801 must be read as advocacy. Murphy had known Garrick, acted beside him, quarrelled and reconciled with him, and wrote after both principals were dead to settle the century's theatrical succession in Garrick's favour. In Murphy's handling, the contest of 1750 becomes an episode in a reign: the challenge met, the rival absorbed into the narrative as a foil, the manager's supremacy the moral of every anecdote. The biography's Garrick is a monarch to whom Barry happens. Modern scholarship has taught readers to discount the handling without discarding the facts, and Ritchie's work gives the discount its sharpest modern form. Garrick was not only the contest's Drury Lane principal; he was a proprietor of newspapers, with interests in the St. James's Chronicle, the Public Advertiser, the Morning Post, and the London papers through which theatrical reputations were amplified or starved. Ritchie's study contrasts the two theatres' management of the media in response to the contest, and its implication for the verdict is sobering and specific: the narrative of Garrick's victory, the thirteenth night, the routed secession, survived in the amplified form because the victor owned part of the amplification. The acting palm, by contrast, is the element of the record that media management could not manufacture, since it ran against the interest of the man who controlled the megaphone, and it is the element that the disinterested summaries, from the Britannica notice to the Garrick Club's own biographical record, keep awarding to Barry.

The media war had a verse front as well, and it opens a window on Garrick's management of tone. For the opening of his first Drury Lane season in 1747, Samuel Johnson had supplied a prologue of famous grandeur, the manifesto of a conqueror promising the town a reformation of its stage. The prologue Garrick spoke for the season of 1750 struck, by the record of its text and of its reception, a markedly different posture: defensive, rallying, and intimate, the rhetoric of a manager asking his public to stand by the house rather than of a genius announcing a reign. The shift is Ritchie's argument in miniature. The Garrick of 1750 knew himself to be contesting a narrative as much as a performance, and he managed the narrative with the same professionalism he brought to the procession and the print. None of this impeaches his acting; it impeaches the archive. A verdict that survives a media campaign mounted by the most sophisticated self-publicist of the century, and survives in the campaigner's disfavour, has passed a test of authenticity that no uncontested reputation is ever set.

### Why does the split verdict survive when the contest's result does not?

Because the two records answer different questions, kept by different custodians. The result, thirteen nights against twelve, was a box-office fact preserved by the winning house's publicity. The split verdict was a judgment of acting preserved by critics, satirists, and biographers with no interest to serve, including witnesses in Garrick's own camp. Judgments outlive results when they describe something real.

The two registers of Romeo's part are still there in the text for any reader to test, which is why the verdict the town reached by walking between two theatres can still be verified by walking between two scenes.

The adjudication this article reaches among the four witnesses is therefore as follows. Churchill is believed on mechanism, because his concessions against interest are detailed, technical, and uncontradicted. Davies is believed on narrative, with his interest noted and his restraint on the palm counted in the record's favour. Murphy is believed on Garrick's stature and disbelieved on the contest's meaning, because his biography's design requires the rival to shrink. Ritchie is believed on transmission: the fame of the victory is partly manufactured, and the fame of the palm is not. What survives the cross-examination is a coherent, multiply attested judgment: Barry was the finer Romeo in the scenes where Romeo woos, Garrick the finer in the scenes where Romeo breaks, and the difference lay in instruments whose strengths and defects contemporaries could specify with technical precision. That judgment is the contest's true result, and it is the one the table in the next section sets out in full.

The modern summaries inherit the quarrel and must be adjudicated as firmly as the period witnesses, because a reader meeting the contest for the first time will meet it in one of two sentences that appear to contradict. The Britannica tradition states that Barry's impersonation was preferred by the critics to Garrick's, and the Garrick Club's biographical record awards Barry the palm in terms. The Victoria and Albert Museum's catalogue, by contrast, states that Garrick's performance was judged the more successful. Read carelessly, the two sentences cancel. Read whole, they do not. The museum's sentence completes itself with its qualification: Garrick's was the more successful "particularly in the pathos of the tomb scene", which awards a scene and its pathos, inside an account of a painting of that scene, where the judgment is offered to explain an image of the tomb. The Britannica and Garrick Club sentences award the role across the whole performance, in accounts of Barry's life, where the judgment explains a career. The apparent contradiction is a difference of unit, scene against role, and it dissolves the moment the units are distinguished, which is precisely the distinction the town of 1750 itself observed when it split its verdict. Modern reference works are therefore in deeper agreement than their headlines suggest, and the agreement runs in Barry's favour at the level of the role. Where a modern source does break ranks, it is usually by compression, a sentence forced to carry a campaign's result and a role's palm in one breath, and compression, in this story, always favours the manager, because results compress and judgments do not. The reader who keeps the units separate holds the record as its makers left it.

The pamphlet scrutiny of the season itself, the fourth stratum of the record, confirms the adjudication from below. The contest generated the period's usual weather of print, verses, paragraphs, and at least one formal pamphlet examination, the Remarks on Romeo and Juliet, as illustrated by Garrick, and now represented, issued under the name Theatricus, which took Garrick's Romeo as its professed subject at the moment of performance. The survival of such a title in the bibliographies matters more than its arguments, which the record transmits only in fragments: it demonstrates that the comparison was conducted in print, in public, and in detail, by writers who expected a readership able to follow scene-level discrimination between two Romeos. John Genest's Some Account of the English Stage, the great digest of 1832 through which much of the period's theatrical memory was filtered into the nineteenth century, transmits the contest in the same allocated form, the honours divided by register, and Genest's method, annalistic, playbill-based, and jealous of novelty, gives his transmission a custodial weight distinct from both memoir and biography. When satire, memoir, pamphlet, and annal all deposit the same allocation in the archive, and when the only dissenting stratum is the monument biography written to enthrone one of the parties, the historian's duty is not to average the witnesses but to discount the interested one. Discounted, the record speaks with one voice about the distribution, and reserves its genuine disagreement for the question the distribution cannot answer: which half of a divided role deserves the crown. That question is one of judgment rather than evidence, and it is the question the remaining sections take up, first in the contrast table and then in the lessons the contrast teaches.

## The Verdict in Pictures: Wilson's Tomb and Elliot's Balcony

The contest's most durable judgments were delivered not in prose at all but in the print shops, and the images deserve a section of their own because they function as criticism of a peculiarly honest kind. A theatrical print is a commercial wager on memory: the publisher engraves the moment the town will pay to keep, and a wrong wager loses money. The three prints the contest and its productions generated divide the play among three performers with a neatness no essayist would dare impose. W. Herbert's print of 1753 took Henry Woodward's Mercutio in the Queen Mab speech, the play's one self-sufficient bravura exhibit. Benjamin Wilson's painting and print of the same period took Garrick and Bellamy in the tomb, Romeo upright in the lamplit vault at the instant of Juliet's waking. William Elliot's print of 1759, after Robert Pyle, took Barry and Nossiter in the balcony scene, the lover below the window in the posture of address. Mercutio to the comedian, the tomb to Garrick, the window to Barry: the market's allocation is the town's allocation, struck in copper.

Woodward's share of the division carries its own instruction. Henry Woodward, Drury Lane's great comedian, played Mercutio in Garrick's production, and his Queen Mab was detachable in a way nothing of either Romeo's was: a set speech, complete in itself, performable as an exhibit, and duly exhibited in Herbert's print as a solo portrait. The contrast between Woodward's icon and the lovers' icons measures the structural fact this article has pressed from the start. Mercutio's excellence can be framed alone because the writing gives him a solo; Romeo's excellence cannot, because the writing gives him duets and reactions, and so the Romeos enter the visual record only in relation, at a window below a woman or in a vault above one. The print trade, without theorizing any of it, has left the cleanest possible diagram of the part's construction: the friend with the set piece gets the solo portrait, and the lover, whose art is relational, gets the window and the tomb, the two places where his relations are decided. Barry's window and Garrick's vault are not merely the scenes each man won; they are the two relations, desire and loss, between which the role is stretched, and the prints divide them with an accuracy the pamphlets only approached.

Emily Leach's study of this portraiture, the most substantial modern reading of the three images, argues that the prints affirm the increase in sentimentality Garrick sought to effect through his adaptation, the cult of feeling made visible and saleable. The argument is just about the Wilson print, which sentimentalizes precisely the scene Garrick sentimentalized in the text, and its justice there exposes its limit at the window. Elliot's Barry is also a sentimental image, but the sentiment it sells is Shakespeare's own scene unassisted by any adapter's enlargement, the wooing exactly as the early acts wrote it. The two prints therefore document a competition inside the period's taste that the prose record only implies: Garrick's manufactured sentiment, an added scene engineered for pathos and then fixed as the play's icon, against the unmanufactured sentiment of the balcony, which needed no addition and took its icon from the play's own most famous stage picture. Posterity bought both, in quantity, for a century. But only one of the two images advertises a scene its subject rewrote, and the distinction between the icon that illustrates a performance and the icon that illustrates an appropriation is the last piece of evidence the weighing requires. Garrick's image certifies his authorship of the moment it shows. Barry's image certifies only his presence in a moment every Romeo must face on the text's own terms, and it is the harder certificate to earn.

The visual record also corrects a distortion that the prose verdict, left alone, might encourage. Because the palm went to Barry, summary history sometimes writes Garrick's Romeo down as the contest's loser in every sense that matters. The print evidence forbids the simplification. Wilson's tomb was the single most successful theatrical image of the play in the century, reproduced in every format the market offered, and an image of that reach is not a consolation prize; it is the form in which the eighteenth century's Romeo survived into the nineteenth century's imagination. The two men divide posterity as they divided the town, Barry holding the judgment of the witnesses and Garrick holding the imagination of the descendants, and any verdict that ignores the second holding understates what Garrick's partial triumph amounted to. The weighing that follows therefore proceeds on the full record: prose palm to Barry, receipts and result to Garrick, and the visual field split between the window and the tomb, with the tomb's image the larger commercial fact and the window's image the purer critical one.

## What the Contrast Teaches About Playing Romeo

The first lesson is that Romeo is a casting problem before it is an acting problem, because the role's two registers make demands that training can redistribute but not abolish. A production choosing its Romeo is choosing which half of the part to insure. The 1750 town, presented with both halves insured by different men, refused to pretend the insurance was equal in value: it paid its palm to the wooing. The reason is structural, and it survives every change of style since. The catastrophe of this tragedy is not self-executing. An audience that has merely admired the lover will watch the tomb with respect; an audience that has believed the lover will watch it with grief. Belief is manufactured in the feast, the orchard, and the parting, and it is spent in the cell and the tomb. Garrick's superiority in the spending scenes could not retroactively manufacture the belief his wooing had left underfunded, which is why his greater general genius took the smaller prize in this particular part. The lesson for any Romeo is therefore ordered: win the wooing first, on whatever terms the instrument allows, because the tomb cannot be won by a lover the house has not yet granted.

The second lesson concerns the uses of a text an actor did not write. Garrick compensated for his first-register deficit by rewriting the second register's chief scene, enlarging his own opportunities in the tomb until the scene's pathos could carry the evening's memory. The compensation worked, commercially and iconically, for a century: Garrick's version held the stage, and his Romeo's image outsold his rival's. But the compensation is legible as compensation in the record, and the palm declined to follow the receipts. Later ages reversed the transaction: the nineteenth-century restorations stripped the added conversation away and returned the tomb to Shakespeare's harsher design, in which the lover dies unheard, and the part's difficulty was restored with the text. An actor-manager's power over the script is a power over everything except the verdict, and 1750 is the cleanest early proof.

The third lesson is written in the part's afterlife, where the divided assignment keeps reappearing. The actor-managers who inherited the role inherited its split. In [John Philip Kemble's Verona](/2028/03/10/john-philip-kemble-verona/) the statuesque dignity of the elder tradition addressed the wooing as ceremony, beautiful and remote, a Barry inheritance translated into marble and stripped of Barry's warmth. At the century's other end, the lover of [Forbes-Robertson and the late Victorian Romeo](/2028/03/17/forbes-robertson-victorian-romeo/) recovered the wooing's ardour in a naturalistic key, proof that the first register's claims reassert themselves in every generation's idiom. Neither heir abolished the problem. Kemble's dignity starved the second register's frenzy; Forbes-Robertson's ardour stayed this side of the tomb's detonation. The pattern across two centuries is the same as the pattern across two theatres in a single autumn: the role is larger than any one instrument, and its performances are graded, consciously or not, by which of its halves the instrument serves and whether the production knows it has made the choice.

A fourth lesson is folded inside the first three and deserves extraction, because it converts the comparison from history into method. Romeo's wooing scenes are duets and his catastrophe scenes are solos, and the two disciplines are not the same craft practised at different intensities. In the sonnet, the orchard, and the parting, the actor's effects depend on a partner's responses and must be timed to another performer's breathing; excellence there is relational, a quality of address and reception. In the cell, at Mantua, and in the tomb, the actor is alone with the verse or with the unanswering, and excellence is architectural, a quality of structure sustained without assistance. Barry was the age's great relational actor in this part, his tenderness completed by Cibber's susceptibility across the footlights, while Garrick was its great soloist, his tomb a structure built to stand without help, so much so that he rewrote the scene to give his solo a waking witness. Productions casting Romeo are therefore choosing, whether they know it or not, between a duettist and a soloist for a part that requires both in sequence, and the wisest of them insure the duet first, for the structural reason already given: a failed solo loses a scene, while a failed duet loses the premise on which every later scene, solos included, depends for its meaning. The tomb of an unbelieved lover is a pageant. The tomb of a believed one is the reason the play has outlived every theatre that ever housed it.

A fifth lesson concerns method, and it may be the contest's most portable bequest. Before 1750, acting reputations accumulated; after it, they could be measured. The simultaneous production converted criticism from a chronicle of impressions into something resembling an experiment, with a control, the shared text, and a variable, the man. The period grasped the instrument it had stumbled into, and the comparative format recurs wherever the theatre wants a question answered rather than a career celebrated: parts are doubled within productions, rivals are engaged for the same role in the same season, and the town is invited to grade the difference. Every such exercise descends from the autumn when two patent houses, by accident of a secession, built the laboratory and ran the trial. The trial's protocol deserves its inventor's credit, which belongs to neither actor but to the quarrel itself: John Rich's raid and David Garrick's pride constructed, between them, the most informative acting comparison of the century, and the information survived because the design was sound. Modern productions that alternate casts in the great roles are still running Garrick and Rich's experiment, usually without knowing whose laboratory they have inherited.

### What should a modern Romeo steal from each man?

From Barry, the discipline of dwelling: letting a love speech complete its music without fragmenting it into points, and treating the lover's stillness at the window as an action, the action of deserving to be answered. From Garrick, the discipline of transition: refusing to let grief settle into one sustained tone, and letting each reversal arrive visibly.

The modern Romeo needs Barry's patience in the first half and Garrick's weather in the second, and the 1750 record suggests the patience is the rarer acquisition, because it cannot be simulated by energy.

The verdict, then, stated without remainder. As actors in general, the century was right about Garrick, and this article does not disturb his crown. As Romeo, in the controlled comparison the autumn of 1750 arranged, the palm belongs to Spranger Barry: to the tender ardour of the wooing, which the town preferred in the scenes where the play's belief is decided, over the fire of the grief, which it preferred in the scenes where that belief is spent. The split verdict was the town's way of recording both truths at once. Two and a half centuries of summaries have been unable to improve on its distribution, and the text of the play, read for its two registers, explains why they never will.

## Sources, Prints, and the State of the Record

The record on which this article rests is unusually rich and unusually treacherous, and a short audit of it is owed to any reader who means to use the comparison further. The narrative frame descends from Thomas Davies's Memoirs of the Life of David Garrick (1780), the work of an insider whose interest is declared by his subject. The partisan counterweight is Arthur Murphy's Life of David Garrick (1801). The technical anatomy of the instruments rests on Charles Churchill's Rosciad (1761), whose Barry stanzas are quoted from the poem's text, and on Francis Gentleman's Dramatic Censor (1770) for the adaptation's critical reception. The institutional summaries that transmit the palm to Barry are the eleventh edition of the Encyclopaedia Britannica's notice of Barry and the Garrick Club's biographical record of him; the Victoria and Albert Museum's catalogue supplies the account of Wilson's painting and the judgment of the tomb scene's pathos; Hugh Macrae Richmond's edition materials supply the treaty's allocation of ardent lover and tragic aspects. Leslie Ritchie's study of Garrick and the mediation of celebrity governs the article's treatment of the victory narrative, and the performance totals, twelve nights at Covent Garden and thirteen at Drury Lane, follow the London Stage record as the series' earlier articles established it. Two further custodians deserve named cautions. Tate Wilkinson's Memoirs of 1790 are the work of a Covent Garden partisan who was eleven years old in the contest autumn, a mimic remembering across forty years the house he adored; his testimony is used in this article only where it testifies against his own allegiance, as in his concession about Garrick's procession, the direction in which a boy's loyalty and a memoirist's honesty pull against each other. John Genest's annals of 1832 are used as a filter rather than a source: Genest compiled from playbills, papers, and the memoir literature, and where his allocation of the honours agrees with the satire and the insider narrative, the agreement of a compiler with no dog in the fight closes the evidentiary circle. The anecdotal tradition, finally, is represented in this article by a single exhibit, the theatregoer's balcony remark, admitted because its content is a technical judgment about stage geometry that no partisan interest explains, and because its survival across two and a half centuries of requotation is itself a datum about how the town remembered the direction of the two men's force. Students assembling this material for presentation, for example through the [ReportMedic PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), should be warned that the secondary literature still corrupts one fact with remarkable persistence: Maria Isabella Nossiter, whose Juliet opposite Barry belongs to Covent Garden in October 1753 and whose image with him Elliot's print of 1759 preserves, is repeatedly misassigned in modern summaries, including a scholarly essay on the period's theatrical portraiture, to the Covent Garden company of the 1750 contest itself. The Juliet of 1750 was Susannah Cibber. The error matters because it quietly erases the partnership on which Barry's palm partly rests, replacing the age's greatest pathetic actress with a gifted pupil, and it demonstrates the fragility of even well-kept records when a famous image is allowed to overwrite a playbill. The playbills, the memoirs, and the satire, checked against one another, are the court of appeal; this article has followed them wherever they could be made to agree, and has flagged the one point, the distribution of the acting palm, where the agreement of hostile and friendly witnesses alike is the strongest evidence the case affords. Used with that discipline, the comparison of 1750 remains what it was in its first autumn: the most instructive controlled experiment in the history of English acting, and the standing refutation of every account of Romeo that treats the lover and the mourner as a single assignment.

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Romeo contrast, and what does it set side by side?

Each row pairs the documented evidence for the two instruments at the same point of the role, so that the town's split verdict of 1750 can be inspected test by test rather than accepted as a slogan. Its pattern is consistent from the first row to the last: the wooing tests fall to Barry and the catastrophe tests to Garrick.

### Q: What did Spranger Barry's voice sound like, and why did audiences prize it in a lover?

Barry's voice was remembered by the period's witnesses as melodious, musical, and tender, an instrument whose effects came from sustaining tone along an unbroken line rather than from emphasis or surprise. Audiences prized it in a lover because Romeo's wooing scenes are written as sustained lyric utterance: the orchard scene, the sonnet at the feast, and the dawn parting all ask the speaker to let feeling accumulate at the speed of verse. A voice that dwells persuades a listener that the passion is continuous rather than performed in bursts, and Barry's delivery made the courtship sound like a state of being instead of a sequence of effects, which is the precise illusion the first half of the role exists to create.

### Q: What did Charles Churchill's Rosciad praise and blame in Barry's acting?

Churchill's Rosciad of 1761 praised Barry's figure, "in person taller than the common size", and singled out his tenderness as a quality no rival could supply, asking who else could give such well applauded tenderness to Lear. It blamed his voice under violent pressure: when passion laboured in his breast, Churchill wrote, the voice came forth like Echo from her cell, denied the tempest its needful aid, and died down the stage in feeble murmurs. The poem is a hostile witness, awarding the general crown of the English stage to Garrick, which is why its concessions carry such weight: the satirist who crowned Garrick documented, in Barry, supreme tenderness and a tempest defect, the exact distribution the 1750 audience reported in Romeo.

### Q: How did the two men's heights compare, and why did stature matter to Romeo?

Barry was tall by the standards of his stage, taller than the common size in Churchill's phrase and singularly handsome in the Britannica record's, while Garrick stood about five feet four inches and was described by contemporaries as low in stature, a disparity the period's wits never tired of measuring. Stature mattered to Romeo because the role's geometry is vertical: the lover spends his great wooing scene below a window he cannot reach, gazing upward, and his presence must fill the lower stage as the fixed point of the scene's desire. Barry's height made the yearning statuesque and credible at a glance; Garrick's smallness forced him to replace presence with energy, striving where the fiction rewards the man who draws the beloved down.

### Q: What part did Susannah Cibber's collapse play in ending the contest of 1750?

The Covent Garden production was withdrawn after twelve nights because Susannah Cibber tired under the run, and Drury Lane then played a thirteenth performance alone, which Garrick's interest converted into a narrative of victory. Cibber's exhaustion is the hinge between the contest's two records: it ended the performances, and it did not end the comparison. The acting palm continued to be awarded to Barry in the summaries that followed, from the period's digests to the Britannica notice and the Garrick Club's biographical record, while the thirteenth night survived as a managerial fact. Her collapse therefore decided which house stopped first, and decided nothing about which Romeo the town preferred, a distinction the publicity of the winning house worked to blur.

### Q: When did Garrick first play Romeo, and what forced the manager into the part?

Garrick first played Romeo on 28 September 1750, the opening night of the contest, at Drury Lane opposite George Anne Bellamy. He had managed the play since 1748 without taking the part, because at his own theatre the role belonged to Barry, whose success in it predated the secession. What forced the manager into the part was the secession itself: when Barry and Cibber carried their celebrated pairing to Covent Garden and Rich's house advertised them in Garrick's own version of the tragedy, only Garrick's name at Drury Lane could hold the audience against them. His Romeo was thus born as a counter in a management war, a challenge performance by a thirty-three-year-old actor who had spent two seasons watching the part from the manager's box.

### Q: What did the theatregoer's balcony remark capture about the two performances?

The remark, preserved in the anecdotal record and endlessly requoted because nothing better was ever said on the subject, runs that had the speaker been Juliet to Garrick's Romeo, so impassioned was he that she would have expected him to climb up to her at the balcony, while had she been Juliet to Barry's Romeo, so tender and seductive was he that she would certainly have come down to him. It captures the direction of each man's force. Garrick's Romeo exerted energy upward, striving against the fiction's geometry; Barry's exerted attraction downward, completing the geometry, since Juliet in the play repeatedly descends, to the marriage, to the parting, and at last into the tomb. The quip is a piece of acting criticism disguised as gallantry, and its survival signals how exactly it fitted what the town had seen.

### Q: What did Benjamin Wilson's print of 1753 choose to immortalize about Garrick's Romeo?

Wilson's painting and its bestselling print fixed Garrick's Romeo in the tomb scene of his own adaptation: the lighted vault, Juliet waking on her bier, Romeo upright with arm raised in shock, the dead Paris in the foreground. The choice of moment is the point. The print trade sold what the town remembered, and what it remembered of Garrick was not the wooing but the pathos of the catastrophe, in the scene Garrick had himself enlarged by waking Juliet before the poison finished its work. The image became the century's default picture of the play, reproduced without pause, so that Garrick's Romeo achieved a visual immortality his rival never matched, even though the acting palm of 1750 had gone the other way.

### Q: What does William Elliot's print of 1759 preserve about Barry's Romeo?

Elliot's print of 1759, after Robert Pyle, shows Barry as Romeo with Maria Isabella Nossiter as Juliet in the balcony scene, and it preserves the complementary half of the contest's visual legacy: Barry's Romeo remembered at the window, in the wooing posture, where Garrick's is remembered in the tomb. Together the two prints ratify the split verdict in images, each man fixed forever in the scene the town awarded him. The print also documents Barry's Romeo as a durable possession rather than a single season's wonder, since it pairs him with the Juliet of his later Covent Garden years, the pupil who first played opposite him in October 1753, nine years after his London debut and three years after the contest itself.

### Q: Who was Maria Nossiter, and why is she wrongly placed in the 1750 contest?

Maria Isabella Nossiter was the young actress, Barry's pupil and partner, who first played Juliet opposite his Romeo at Covent Garden in October 1753, taking the part on a night when Susannah Cibber, whose role it had been, watched from the audience. She is wrongly placed in the 1750 contest because Elliot's famous print of the pair in the balcony scene has overwritten the playbills in modern memory: even a scholarly essay on the period's theatrical portraiture assigns Nossiter to Covent Garden's twelve nights of 1750, when the bills name Cibber. The error matters beyond pedantry, since it erases the partnership, Barry opposite the age's greatest pathetic actress, on which the palm of 1750 partly rested, and substitutes a partnership formed three years later.

### Q: How did Barry's Othello prepare the town to judge his Romeo?

Barry's first London appearance, at Drury Lane in 1746, was as Othello, and the performance was so complete that Garrick, who had played the Moor himself, gave the part up after seeing his new recruit in it. The episode taught the town the pattern it would apply in 1750: where a role turned on the credible embodiment of consuming passion in a noble person, Barry's endowment of figure and voice could displace even Garrick's genius. Othello also fixed the association, in the public mind, between Barry and the grand erotic sufferer, the lover whose passion is a climate rather than a series of decisions. His Romeo inherited that expectation and fulfilled its tender half, while the Moor's jealous tempest remained, as Churchill later noted, the register his voice served least well.

### Q: What happened when Barry returned to London in 1767 after a decade away?

Barry returned in 1767 to play at the Haymarket under Samuel Foote and then, with his wife Ann Barry, under Garrick's own management at Drury Lane, where his reappearance in Othello, described in the records as his greatest part, came after ten years' absence from the London stage. The Romeo rivalry was not renewed. The reconciliation is itself a verdict of a kind: the manager who had fought Barry for the lover's crown in 1750 then engaged him for the Moor, the role in which Barry's supremacy had never been contested, while Romeo remained unrevived between them. Barry moved to Covent Garden in 1774 and remained there until his death on 10 January 1777, his fame as the century's Romeo intact and unchallenged by any second contest.

### Q: Which biographer gives the fullest insider narrative of the contest, and what is his testimony worth?

Thomas Davies, whose Memoirs of the Life of David Garrick appeared in 1780, gives the fullest insider narrative: Davies had acted in Garrick's company, knew both camps, and wrote as the manager's biographer with a monument to raise. His testimony is worth the most precisely where it resists his interest. A biographer so placed might be expected to award his subject the acting palm along with the managerial victory of the thirteenth night; the tradition descending from the period's record instead preserves the allocation of the love scenes to Barry and the tragic scenes to Garrick, unreversed in the house of the defeated claimant. When the winning side's own historian lets the rival's central conquest stand, later readers may treat that conquest as established fact rather than as Covent Garden publicity.

### Q: What did Arthur Murphy's Life of Garrick do to the verdict of 1750?

Arthur Murphy's Life of David Garrick, published in 1801, a generation after the event and two decades after Davies, recast the contest as an episode in a reign. Murphy had known and acted with Garrick and wrote to settle the century's succession in his subject's favour, so Barry shrinks in his handling from the winner of the acting palm into a foil whose beauty sets off the monarch's genius, and the thirteenth night swells from a managerial flourish into the story's moral. The biography did not falsify the record; it re-weighted it, and its influence on the nineteenth century's Garrick worship was large. Reading Murphy against Davies, and both against the disinterested summaries that keep awarding Barry the role, is the standard exercise by which the verdict of 1750 is recovered from its monument.

### Q: What does Leslie Ritchie's study show about how the contest's fame was manufactured?

Leslie Ritchie's study of Garrick and the mediation of celebrity examines the Battle of the Romeos as a contest in media management, contrasting the two theatres' handling of publicity in its aftermath. Its sharpest fact is structural: Garrick held interests in newspapers, among them the St. James's Chronicle, the Public Advertiser, and the Morning Post, so the manager whose house claimed victory in 1750 also owned part of the machinery by which the claim was amplified to posterity. The implication redistributes credibility across the whole record. The victory narrative, the routed secession and the triumphant thirteenth night, is the element of the story that interested amplification would produce; the acting palm awarded to Barry, which ran against the amplifier's interest, is the element least likely to have been manufactured, and the one the neutral summaries preserve.

### Q: Why does the part of Romeo punish a voice that cannot sustain passion under pressure?

Because Romeo's verse changes its physics at the hinge of the play. In the wooing scenes the verse moves in long, unbroken periods, and a sustaining voice is rewarded for as long as it can dwell; Churchill's anatomy of Barry describes an instrument perfect for that pressure. After Mercutio's death the verse fractures into starts, curses, pleas, and self-interruption, and the same sustaining voice must either smooth the fractures away, losing the sense, or force the tone and thin it, as Churchill said Barry's did when the tempest demanded aid his voice denied. The punishment is automatic and nightly: the part first crowns the musical voice, then examines it in the one register where music is not enough, which is why so few Romeos in any century have been judged complete.

### Q: What acting advantage did Garrick's altered ending hand to Garrick the actor?

Garrick's adaptation delayed the poison and woke Juliet while Romeo still lived, converting Shakespeare's solitary death speech, addressed to a woman believed dead, into a dialogue of mutual recognition played with a living partner. For Garrick the actor the advantage was threefold: more time in the play's most pathetic situation, a responsive scene partner in place of a corpse, and a culminating stage picture, the upright Romeo arrested by the waking Juliet, that Wilson's print then carried into every print shop in London. Francis Gentleman's Dramatic Censor praised the alteration while conceding the added conversation was not, in strict necessity, "absolutely essential", a reservation that measures the advantage exactly: the alteration was not needed by the tragedy, but it was invaluable to the tragedian.

### Q: How do the modern editors' texts judge the contest the two actors fought?

The great modern editions, Jill Levenson's Oxford text and Brian Gibbons's Arden text among them, print Shakespeare's ending: Romeo dies believing Juliet dead, and the recognition Garrick bought with his alteration is refused. Editorial practice thereby delivers, in print, a judgment the stage of 1750 never faced, since both rival houses played Garrick's version: the words Garrick cut are restored as the standard text, and the scene in which his Romeo triumphed is demoted to a documented adaptation, studied as performance history rather than read as the play. Levenson's editorial method, which treats the promptbook and performance record as evidence about the text, preserves the 1750 performances inside the edition's apparatus even as the edition's text overrules the adapter, the neatest possible settlement of the quarrel between the actor and the author.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch Romeo contrast to argue that Romeo is written in two registers?

An essay should begin from the table's pattern rather than from the contest's gossip: set the wooing rows, the sonnet, the orchard scene, and the dawn parting, against the catastrophe rows, the cell, Mantua, and the tomb, and show that the allocation of the 1750 palm follows the seam exactly. It should then anchor the seam in the verse itself, in the sustained periods of the orchard against the fractured utterance of the banishment scene, citing the Friar's diagnosis that young men's love lies in their eyes rather than in their hearts as the play's own statement of the conversion the role must enact. Churchill's anatomy of Barry's voice supplies the mechanism, and the verdict follows: the part is two assignments sharing a name, and the town of 1750, uniquely placed to test them separately, graded them separately.

### Q: What single lesson does the comparison of 1750 leave for any actor cast as Romeo?

Win the wooing first. The catastrophe of the tragedy spends a belief that only the feast, the orchard scene, and the dawn parting can manufacture, and no superiority in the spending scenes, however brilliant, can retroactively fund it: Garrick, the greater actor of his century, took the contest's receipts and its image while Barry took the role, because the town granted Barry's lover the belief its grief would need. The lesson is not that tenderness outranks passion in some general scale of acting values, but that this part pays its debts in a fixed order. An actor who secures the audience's conviction in the love scenes earns the right to their tears in the tomb; an actor who reverses the order finds the tomb's pathos applauded as acting and remembered as exactly that.
