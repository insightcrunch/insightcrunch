Between the end of the nineteen nineties and the middle of the following decade, voters across Latin America did something they had never done in such a sequence before: they elected governments of the left, one after another, in country after country, until the pink tide had redrawn the political map of a continent. The victories were not copies of one another. A movement rooted in oil wealth and barracks nationalism won in Venezuela; a metalworker's party, built over decades of union struggle, won in Brazil; a coalition of socialists, communists, and Christian democrats governed in Chile; an indigenous and peasant movement carried the presidency in Bolivia; a former guerrilla coalition took office in Uruguay. What they shared was a direction, a vocabulary of sovereignty and social justice, and an electorate exhausted by two decades of market reform. What they did not share, at the outset, was a programme.

That asymmetry is where analysis has to begin, because the most common way of writing about the period gets it backwards. Journalistic accounts usually start from the personalities and the rhetoric, and treat the economics as background. A Marxist reading reverses the order. It asks what material basis made the wave possible, what that basis permitted the governments to do, and what it forbade them to do, and it finds that a single mechanism answers all three questions. The wave coincided with a long rise in the world prices of oil, gas, copper, soy, iron ore, and other primary commodities, and the windfall of resource rent that flowed into state treasuries financed an era of redistribution that never had to confront the ownership of productive wealth. The achievements were real. So were the limits, and they were built into the financing from the first day.

![A map of Latin America marked with commodity export routes and a worksheet of the rent redistribution table, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-72.webp)

This article works through that argument in full. It defines the wave and fixes its dates, surveys the succession of victories and the differences among them, and then spends its centre on the commodity boom and on the category of rent, because the category does the explanatory work. It sets out the rent redistribution table, which compresses the model into one view, and weighs the achievements against the structure that produced them. It then gives the critique that arose inside the left itself, the charge of neo-extractivism, and the story of how governing coalitions fractured over mines, dams, roads, and wells on indigenous territory. The defence of the governments is stated at full strength, in the terms their own proponents used, and answered with a concession that honest criticism owes them. The recession of the wave, when prices turned, closes the account. Readers who want the wider tradition behind these events will find it in the series survey of [Marxism in Latin America](/2018/10/01/marxism-latin-america-guide/); the concern here is narrower and, for that reason, sharper: one wave, one mechanism, and the verdict the mechanism delivers.

## What was the pink tide?

The name came first from journalism. Spanish speakers called it the marea rosa, and the English rendering, pink tide, preserved the point of the metaphor: pink, not red. These were not revolutions on the Cuban model, and most of the governments involved said so themselves. They came to office through elections, governed through constitutions they mostly respected and sometimes rewrote, and left the commanding heights of their economies in private hands. The colour signalled a dilution, and critics on the right used it as a sneer while critics on the revolutionary left used it as a diagnosis. Both usages conceded the same fact, that the wave belonged to a different species from the insurgent left of the nineteen sixties. Its weapon was the ballot box, its instrument was the budget, and its fuel, as this article will show, was rent.

As a definition, then: the pink tide names the succession of left and centre-left electoral victories across Latin America from the late nineteen nineties through the following decade, which expanded social provision and political inclusion during a commodity price boom while largely leaving ownership and productive structures unchanged. Every clause of that definition carries weight. The victories were electoral, which constrained them. The expansion of provision was genuine, which distinguishes the period from mere rhetoric. The boom was the enabling condition, which fixes the period in economic time rather than in the biography of any leader. And the structures left unchanged are the definition's quiet verdict, because a wave that redistributes income while leaving the production of income untouched has announced, in advance, the manner of its own ending.

### Why did contemporaries reach for a colour rather than a doctrine to name it?

Because no doctrine united the governments involved, and everyone involved knew it. A shared label had to describe a direction of travel rather than a destination, and colour did that work: it marked distance from the market orthodoxy that preceded the wave without claiming the full programme that the older left had carried into its defeats.

The metaphor of a tide deserves a moment of suspicion, and the suspicion is itself analytical. Tides are natural events. They arrive without being organised and recede without being defeated, and a vocabulary of tides can smuggle in the suggestion that the wave's rise and fall were weather rather than history. The suggestion should be resisted in both directions. The victories were won by parties and movements with decades of organisation behind them, built through union struggles, land occupations, neighbourhood associations, and the long labour of surviving dictatorship and defeat. Nothing about them was meteorological. And the recession, when it came, had causes that can be named and weighed: a turn in world prices, the exhaustion of a fiscal model, fractures inside the governing coalitions that predated the price turn, and oppositions that had reorganised while in exile from power. Calling the whole episode a tide is a convenience of naming. Explaining it requires the slower vocabulary of revenue, class, and coalition, and that is the vocabulary this article uses.

One further clarification protects everything that follows. To analyse the wave as a single phenomenon is not to claim it was a single project. No coordinating committee sat above the national governments; no shared manifesto bound a Brazilian finance ministry to a Bolivian constituent assembly. The unity of the pink tide is analytical, not organisational: a family of governments facing a similar world market, drawing on a similar revenue windfall, staffed by coalitions with overlapping social bases, and therefore exhibiting similar achievements and similar pathologies. Family resemblance is a real kind of unity, and it is the only kind claimed here. Where the cases diverge, the divergence is itself evidence, because it shows which features of the pattern followed from the shared mechanism and which followed from national conditions that no mechanism overrides.

### Was the pink tide a single coordinated project directed from one capital?

No evidence supports that picture, and the record contradicts it at several points. The governments quarrelled over trade, energy integration, and diplomatic alignment throughout the period; their parties belonged to rival traditions with old grievances; and their economic policies diverged on exchange rates, central bank mandates, and the treatment of foreign capital in ways no central direction would have permitted.

## A closed period, with its dates stated

This article treats the pink tide as a closed historical period, and the treatment is a methodological commitment rather than a courtesy. The period opens with the first electoral victories of the late nineteen nineties and closes in the middle of the twenty tens, when the commodity price rise that financed the wave had reversed, the fiscal settlements built on it had come under decisive strain, and the succession of victories had given way to a succession of defeats, withdrawals, and transformations of the governing coalitions themselves. Within those bounds the article describes governments, policies, and conflicts. Beyond those bounds it does not go. No serving official is named anywhere in what follows. No administration in office at any later date is evaluated, endorsed, or criticised. Where a leader's name would ordinarily identify a government, the government is identified instead by its country and its period, because the argument concerns structures that outlast persons and because the discipline keeps the analysis honest: a claim about a revenue model should not depend on a judgement about a man.

The same discipline governs numbers. The literature on the period is rich in figures, for poverty rates, export earnings, fiscal revenues, growth, and votes, and nearly all of those figures are contested in method or in interpretation, quite apart from the polemics that surround them. This article therefore asserts no figure for any of them. Claims are stated directionally, that poverty fell substantially across the region during the boom years, that revenues rose very sharply, that export baskets grew more concentrated, and the research literature that establishes each direction is named as it is used: the regional studies of the Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean, the household survey literature, the dependency scholarship, and the internal critics of the left whose works are discussed below. A reader who wants the magnitudes can go to those sources, where the methods are argued out in the open. What the argument of this article needs is not magnitudes but mechanisms, and mechanisms do not require a single contested digit.

### Why does the article refuse figures that other accounts quote freely?

Because the figures for this period are entangled with the polemics about it, and because the argument here turns on the direction of flows rather than their size. Rent rose, was redistributed, and fell; ownership stayed where it was. Those claims survive any plausible revision of the statistics, while a case built on a contested percentage collapses with the percentage.

There is a second reason for the closed-period discipline, and it is the reason the brief for this article flags the decay risk as extreme. Writing about governments that arose from movements still active in their countries invites a slide from history into commentary, in which every sentence about the past is read as a coded sentence about the present. The slide corrupts both genres. History written as commentary flatters or wounds living actors instead of explaining deadlocked structures, and commentary dressed as history borrows an authority it has not earned. The remedy applied here is strict periodisation. The wave is described the way a historian describes any finished episode: with its dates on the table, its actors identified by the offices they held within the period, and its outcomes assessed against the alternatives that were actually available to the people who lived it. Written that way, the article should read identically in any year, because nothing in it leans on the reader's knowledge of what came next.

## The succession of victories, and how much they differed

The sequence began in Venezuela at the end of the nineteen nineties, when a movement born from a failed barracks revolt and a collapsed party system won the presidency on a promise to refound the republic. Brazil followed in 2002, when the Workers' Party, after three defeats, finally carried its union founder into office at the head of a coalition that reached well beyond the organised working class. Argentina's turn came through catastrophe: the collapse of 2001 destroyed the credibility of the existing parties, and the government that consolidated from 2003 governed in the name of a recovered national economy and a repudiated debt. Uruguay's Broad Front, a coalition that included former guerrillas alongside social democrats, won in 2004 after decades as the country's patient opposition. Bolivia's election of 2005 brought the Movement for Socialism to power, a party that described itself as the political instrument of indigenous and peasant movements, and its victory remains the period's clearest case of a social movement becoming a government. Chile's socialist-led coalition, which had governed since the dictatorship's end in a register of caution, returned a socialist president in the election of 2005. Ecuador's turn in 2006 elevated a citizens' movement built around a critique of the party system itself. Paraguay's election of 2008 broke six decades of one-party rule. El Salvador's former insurgency won the presidency in 2009 through a moderate journalist candidate. Nicaragua's Sandinistas returned to office through the election of 2006 by a narrower and more negotiated road.

Read as a list, the sequence can mislead, because the governments differed in almost every respect that journalism found interesting. Their attitudes to the United States ranged from organised confrontation to careful cooperation. Their macroeconomic management ranged from heterodox experiment to a fiscal orthodoxy that reassured bond markets. Some rewrote constitutions through constituent assemblies; others governed entirely within inherited constitutional frames. Some nationalised assets in strategic sectors; others changed no ownership at all and boasted of the fact. A reader who stops at this level of description will conclude that the pink tide is a label in search of a phenomenon. The conclusion would be premature. Beneath the variation, the same three conditions recur in case after case: an electorate punishing the parties of the market reform era, a coalition that joined organised labour or peasant movements to urban middle strata frightened by insecurity, and, arriving almost on cue, a world market willing to pay far more than before for what the region dug and grew. The first two conditions explain the victories. The third explains what the victories could and could not do, and it is the one the governments themselves least controlled.

### What did the winning coalitions have in common beneath their national differences?

Each joined constituencies that the market decades had injured in different ways: formal workers whose unions had shrunk, informal workers outside every protection, peasants pressed by imports and land concentration, and middle strata whose savings, salaries, or small businesses had been damaged by crisis. The coalition's breadth was its electoral strength and, later, its governing problem.

The Venezuelan case stands at one edge of the family and should be marked as such. There the governing movement commanded the world's most lucrative single rent stream relative to its ambitions, confronted the old order early and directly, survived a coup attempt and an oil stoppage in the period's opening years, and built parallel institutions with a speed no other case attempted. The Chilean case stands at the opposite edge: a coalition that had already governed through the nineteen nineties, administering the market model with social corrections, and whose pink tide membership rests on its party composition and its social spending rather than on any rupture. Between those edges lie the instructive middle cases. Brazil combined a mass workers' party with an alliance strategy that reassured finance and agribusiness. Bolivia combined a movement government with a fiscal prudence that surprised its critics. Argentina combined a recovery driven by devaluation and soy with a politics of memory and confrontation toward creditors. The family's spread matters because the rent model, to earn its name, must explain the middle cases and the edges with the same mechanism, allowing only for the size of the rent stream and the strength of the coalition that received it. The claim of this article is that it does.

### Why did the wave arrive as elections rather than as insurrections?

The insurgent road had been defeated across the region by the early nineteen nineties, its organisations destroyed, negotiated into peace, or reduced to remnants, while the transitions from dictatorship had made the ballot the recognised currency of political change. Movements that wanted power had to win it in elections, and a generation of organisers learned to build parties, coalitions, and campaigns instead of columns.

## What the wave inherited: the market decades

No account of the pink tide works without the period it answered. From the early nineteen eighties, Latin America lived through a debt crisis and then through the most thorough programme of market reform applied anywhere in the world: privatisation of state enterprises and utilities, trade liberalisation, deregulation of finance, labour reforms that weakened unions and spread informal work, and fiscal disciplines enforced first by creditors and then by law. The programme had names in the literature, the Washington Consensus in its original coinage, neoliberalism in the vocabulary of its critics, and its social record across two decades was the soil in which the wave grew. Growth was modest and unstable, punctuated by crises in Mexico, Brazil, and Argentina. Inequality, already the world's highest by region, stayed extreme. Informal employment expanded until it covered roughly half the urban workforce in many countries. Public services thinned. The political consequence arrived slowly and then all at once: the parties that had administered the reforms, and in several countries the party systems themselves, lost the confidence of their electorates, and the elections of the pink tide were, in the first instance, verdicts on that record.

The inheritance was institutional as well as electoral. The incoming governments of the left took office inside states that had been deliberately narrowed: central banks insulated from political direction, trade and investment treaties that constrained industrial policy, privatised pension systems, and treasuries dependent on commodity-linked revenues and foreign borrowing. They also inherited the social wreckage as a constituency: the informal workers, the unemployed, the pensioners on eroded benefits, and the indigenous movements whose mobilisation through the nineteen nineties, from the Zapatista rising's continental echo to the water and gas wars of Bolivia, had already rehearsed the politics of the coming decade. A Marxist account insists on this ordering. The wave was not an ideological conversion that descended on the region from books or from a single charismatic source. It was the political form taken by the accumulated grievances of the market decades, once those grievances found parties capable of winning elections and a world market capable of paying for the remedies.

### Which grievances did the market decades deposit with the incoming governments?

Insecurity in every register: jobs without contracts or protection, pensions exposed to market collapse, utilities priced beyond poor households, land and water contested by concessionaires, and public institutions visibly captured by the creditors and conglomerates that had bought the privatisations. The incoming governments promised, above all, to make the state answerable to those injuries again.

It is worth pausing on the depth of the institutional constraint, because later judgements of the pink tide often proceed as if the governments had faced a blank page. They faced a page already written on. A government that wished to tax wealth confronted constitutions, treaties, and capital flight. A government that wished to renationalise confronted compensation claims and the loss of the technical staffs the privatisations had dispersed. A government that wished to spend confronted debt stocks and the memory of inflation, which in several countries was not a memory but a living fear among the poor themselves, who had watched hyperinflation devour wages within living memory. None of this excuses the choices the governments made; explanation is not acquittal. But it defines the field on which the choices were made, and it is the premise of the strongest defence those governments later offered for themselves: that within the field as given, redistribution financed by rent was not a failure of nerve but the available move. Whether that defence succeeds is the question the centre of this article is built to answer.

## Two lefts, or one wave read twice

In 2006, with the wave near its height, the Mexican writer and former foreign minister Jorge Castañeda published an influential article distinguishing two lefts in Latin America. One, in his schema, was modern, social-democratic, and market-compatible: it accepted globalisation, fiscal discipline, and open trade, and sought office to humanise the model rather than replace it. The other was nationalist, populist, and redistributive in a statist key: it blamed foreign power for the region's ills, concentrated authority, and treated institutions as obstacles. The schema was taken up eagerly by commentary in the United States and Europe, because it offered a filing system for governments that outsiders found confusing, and because it carried an implicit instruction about which left deserved support. Within the region it has been used ever since, by sympathisers of each supposed camp against the other.

The schema is analytically useful, and the use should be acknowledged before the criticism. It captures a real difference of style and of economic instinct between, at one pole, governments that treated macroeconomic stability as a conquest to be defended and, at the other, governments that treated it as a constraint to be tested. It also captures a real difference in the social composition of coalitions and in their rhetoric about the continent's northern neighbour. A student who cannot see the difference between the Chilean coalition's technocratic caution and the Venezuelan movement's refoundational ambition has missed something true about the period. The two-lefts map, like many maps, is wrong mainly in what it claims to be: not a sketch of tendencies but a partition of the territory into two kinds, each internally uniform.

### What does the two-lefts schema explain well, and where does it begin to mislead?

It explains rhetoric, diplomatic alignment, and macroeconomic temperament, the visible surfaces of the governments. It misleads when it is read as an account of social structure, because governments filed in opposite camps often ran similar revenue models and similar social policies, while governments filed together diverged sharply on ownership, land, and the treatment of their own movements.

The criticism, developed across the regional literature, runs on three tracks. The first is empirical: the supposedly irresponsible camp included governments of notable fiscal caution, which accumulated reserves and ran surpluses through the boom, while the supposedly responsible camp included governments whose social spending, relative to their economies, was modest. The filing system sorted by speeches and then predicted by the sort, and the predictions kept failing. The second track is analytical: the schema treats the difference as one of ideas, of correct and incorrect conceptions of modernity, where a materialist account would look first at what each country sold to the world and how much rent the sale generated. A government financed by petroleum at boom prices had options that a government financed by copper, or by soy, or by a diversified tax base, did not, and much of the observed difference in daring follows the revenue rather than the doctrine. The third track is political: critics charged that the distinction was an external imposition, drawn in the image of what outside observers hoped the region's left would become, and that it flattened the differences within each camp, between parties, movements, and generations, into a morality tale of the good left and the bad. The charge has force. A schema that sorts living coalitions into the modern and the anachronistic has usually smuggled its conclusion into its categories.

### Why do materialist accounts distrust explanations that sort governments by their ideas?

Because the same ideas, held by the same parties, produced restraint in one revenue environment and ambition in another, while opposite ideas produced convergent policies where the revenue environment matched. When the correlation runs with the financing rather than with the doctrine, the explanation that starts from doctrine is describing the decoration and calling it the wall.

For the purposes of this article the two-lefts debate matters in a specific way. It is the main rival explanation of the wave's variation, and the rent model must beat it on its own ground. The rent model does not deny the differences the schema notices; it reassigns their cause. Where the schema sees temperament, the model sees treasury. Where the schema sees a choice between modernity and populism, the model sees governments distributing a windfall under different degrees of pressure from the movements that elected them and different degrees of exposure to the windfall's reversal. The test between the two explanations is predictive. The schema predicts that the responsible left would weather the end of the boom and the populist left would fall with it. The model predicts that survival would track the depth of each government's dependence on rent and the degree to which its social settlement had been converted into durable organisation. The recession of the wave, examined below, fits the second prediction better than the first, which is one reason the model deserves its place at the centre of the analysis.

## The boom: prices, demand, and the revenue windfall

The material basis of the pink tide can be stated in one sentence, and the rest of this section is an unpacking of it. From roughly 2003 to roughly 2013, the world prices of the primary commodities Latin America exports rose to heights not seen in a generation, stayed high for an unusually long plateau, and thereby transferred an enormous flow of income to the region's treasuries. The drivers sat mostly outside the region. China's industrialisation, following its entry into world markets, generated an appetite for iron ore, copper, soy, and petroleum on a scale that reordered commodity markets; financial investors, hunting yield in a world of low interest rates, poured into commodity indices and amplified the movement; supply, after two decades of underinvestment during the low-price years, could not respond quickly. The result was a terms-of-trade windfall for commodity exporters across the developing world, and in Latin America it arrived precisely as the new governments took office, a coincidence of timing that shaped everything they became.

### What financed the Pink Tide governments?

a sustained rise in primary commodity prices from roughly 2003 to 2013, which raised state revenues from resource rents. That allowed large increases in social spending without altering ownership of productive assets, which explains both the scale of the achievements and the fragility of their basis.

The windfall reached treasuries through several channels, and the channels matter because they determined who had to be confronted and who could be left alone. Export taxes, in Argentina's case levied on agricultural shipments, converted part of the soy price directly into revenue. Royalties and reformed hydrocarbon laws, in Bolivia and Ecuador and Venezuela, raised the state's share of oil and gas income from the subsoil. State mining and petroleum companies, where they survived or were rebuilt, remitted surpluses. Windfall-profit provisions and renegotiated contracts captured a further slice from private concessionaires, usually by negotiation rather than expropriation, because the companies, facing prices far above their planning assumptions, could concede a larger state take and still profit. Corporate and income taxes swelled with the general expansion. The striking feature of the whole apparatus is what it did not require: it did not require taxing the domestic wealthy at confiscatory rates, expropriating the banks, or breaking the agrarian property structure. The money arrived from abroad, paid by foreign consumers of the region's soil and subsoil, and it arrived at the finance ministry before it touched any domestic pocket. Redistribution on that basis is the path of least resistance in any class society, and the pink tide took it, in every country, whatever the rhetoric.

### Why did the source of the money matter as much as its amount?

A government financed by taxing its own rich must fight them in parliament, in the courts, and in the currency markets, and it must win those fights before it spends a peso. A government financed by foreign buyers of its exports receives the money first and argues about it afterwards, which reverses the politics entirely and postpones every confrontation with domestic property.

The plateau's length did quiet ideological work. A windfall of two or three years reads as luck; a windfall of a decade reads as a new normal, and budgets, expectations, and political settlements were built on the assumption of permanence. Ministries planned multi-year programmes. Movements calibrated their demands to a treasury that seemed structurally richer. Opponents, for a time, were disarmed by prosperity: it is difficult to persuade an electorate that a model has failed while incomes rise and the shops fill. The length of the boom also explains a feature of the period that purely political accounts struggle with, the wave's long plateau of incumbency, in which government after government won re-election or passed power to a chosen successor. Incumbency in Latin America had historically been a wasting asset, corroded by inflation and crisis. For a decade, across much of the region, it compounded instead. The rent model reads that plateau as the political half-life of the windfall, and predicts, correctly as the record showed, that the compounding would reverse with the prices.

## Rent: the category that organises the case

Rent is an old category of political economy, and the pink tide cannot be understood without it, because rent is not profit and the difference governs the whole story. Profit, in the classical and Marxian accounts, arises in production: capital organises labour, extracts surplus value, and realises it in sale. Rent arises from ownership of a scarce, non-produced condition of production, above all land and subsoil deposits. The owner of a copper deposit or an oilfield does not create the value of what is extracted; the owner charges for access to a gift of geology, and the charge is paid out of the difference between the world price of the commodity and the cost of extracting it at that site. Where deposits differ in richness, the owners of the better deposits collect differential rent. Where the commodity is scarce enough that price is set by the worst deposit in production, every owner collects. The state, as owner of the subsoil in most of Latin America's legal traditions, stands in the rentier's position by constitution, and a commodity boom is, from the treasury's window, a rent explosion.

Marx devoted the closing sections of the third volume of Capital to ground rent, and the category has had a long career in the region's own scholarship, where the analysis of rent runs through the dependency literature and the debates over whether Latin American economies generated surplus through production or appropriated it through circulation and land. The wider argument about rent's relation to profit, whether rent is a deduction from surplus value or an independent form of revenue, is taken up in the companion treatment of the [rent versus profit debate](/2021/02/15/rent-versus-profit-debate/), and only its consequences are needed here. Three consequences matter. First, rent is revenue without a corresponding domestic productive act, which is why it can be spent without organising anything. Second, rent accrues to whoever holds the title, which is why struggles over rent are struggles over law and sovereignty, over royalty schedules and concession terms, rather than over the labour process. Third, rent depends on world prices set elsewhere, which is why a rent-financed state is prosperous and exposed in the same gesture.

### How does rent differ from the revenue a state raises by taxing production?

Taxation of production takes a share of value being created inside the economy, and its level is fought over by the classes who create and appropriate that value. Rent is a claim on value realised in world markets for a resource the economy did not make. It can rise tenfold while domestic production stagnates, and vanish while production continues, which no tax base behaves like.

The political sociology of rent follows from those three consequences, and it is the sociology the pink tide exhibited. A state living on rent does not need to bargain with its citizens over taxation to anything like the degree a tax state does, and the accountability that historically grew from that bargaining weakens accordingly. A state living on rent can buy social peace sector by sector, funding the pensioners one year, the transport subsidy the next, the public works programme after that, without ever assembling those constituencies into a coalition for transforming the economy itself. And a state living on rent experiences its own prosperity as a fact about the world rather than as an achievement of its society, which quietly reshapes the self-understanding of governments and governed alike. None of this is a moral judgement; rentier states exist across the ideological spectrum, and some of the period's governments fought the rentier logic from inside it, building sovereign funds or preaching diversification. It is a structural description, and the pink tide is unintelligible without it, because the wave's central drama, redistribution without transformation, is precisely what rent makes possible and what rent makes tempting.

There is a further subtlety that the best of the period's analysts insisted on. The rent that flooded in was not, strictly, a product of the region's economies at all. In the terms of the dependency tradition, a large part of it represented value transferred through world prices, the same mechanism that in other configurations had drained the periphery. For one exceptional decade the transfer ran in the periphery's favour, because Chinese demand met constrained supply at prices far above production costs. Governments of the left were thus redistributing, in considerable part, a surplus generated by industrial workers on another continent and paid over by the firms that employed them. The irony was noted at the time and deserves recording without relish: the material basis of Latin America's left turn was the most successful capitalist industrialisation of the age, proceeding at full speed on the far side of the Pacific. Nothing in that irony discredits the redistribution. It does underline that the basis lay outside the region's control, in decisions taken in Beijing's planning agencies and in the investment committees of funds in New York and London, and that a politics built on it was built on ground that others could move.

## The rent redistribution table

The model can now be set out in one view. The rent redistribution table below reads the pink tide the way an engineer reads a circuit: a source of revenue, a form of redistribution, a structure left unchanged, and two constituencies, one created and one alienated, at the output end. Each row of the table is developed in the sections around it, but the table itself is the article's findable artifact, the thing a reader can carry away and apply to any case, including cases outside the period and the region.

| Element of the model | How it operated across the wave |
| --- | --- |
| Source of revenue | World-market rent from oil, gas, minerals, and soy, captured through royalties, export taxes, renegotiated concessions, and state companies, swelling treasuries without new claims on domestic wealth |
| Form of redistribution | Conditional cash transfers, expanded pensions, subsidised fuel and food, rising minimum wages, public employment, and wider health and education provision, delivered as income support rather than as changes in ownership |
| What was left unchanged | The structure of property: concentrated landholding, private banking, foreign and domestic ownership of industry, and an export basket still dominated by a handful of primary commodities |
| Constituency created | Beneficiaries of transfers and services, public sector workers, and organised movements bound to the state as the distributor of the windfall, loyal while the distribution lasted |
| Constituency alienated | Indigenous and environmental movements confronting extraction on their territories, together with taxpayers and savers who bore the inflation and the eventual fiscal correction |

Read the table from left to right and the period's plot appears. Revenue arrived from the world market, which meant it arrived without a domestic class compromise having been struck. Redistribution proceeded by transfer, which meant it raised incomes without raising the recipients' power over production. Property stayed untouched, which meant the classes that might have destroyed the governments in their first years were left with their assets and their patience. The constituency created was real and numerous, and its loyalty was the wave's electoral engine. The constituency alienated was smaller but strategically placed: it included the very movements whose mobilisation had made the victories possible, now asked to accept drilling, mining, and road-building on their lands as the price of the transfers flowing to the cities. A model that creates its majority and alienates its founders, while leaving its enemies intact, has a biography written into its structure. The sections that follow simply read that biography out in prose.

### What does the table show that a narrative of leaders and speeches cannot?

It shows the mechanism's indifference to personnel. Replace every president with a rival from the same coalition and the rows still fill in the same way, because the rows describe revenue and property, not character. Narratives explain why a particular government fell in a particular year; the table explains why they all faced the same wall in the same decade.

One caution belongs with the artifact. A table is a compression, and compressions can bully the evidence. The rows describe the centre of gravity of the wave, not the biography of each government: the Venezuelan case pushed further against property than the row suggests, the Chilean case redistributed with a lighter rent component than the row implies, and the Bolivian case combined the model with a movement discipline that gave its transfers an organisational depth the table cannot show. The table's defence is that it never claimed otherwise. It is a model, offered so that the deviations become measurable: a reader who can say how far a given case sits from each row has understood that case better than a reader armed with either the table alone or the anecdotes alone.

## The rent model, stated as a claim

The namable claim of this article can now be given its name and its full form. The rent model holds that the pink tide governments redistributed resource rent rather than transforming production relations, and that this single fact explains the pattern of the whole period: why substantial reductions in poverty were achieved without any confrontation with domestic property, why dependence on primary exports deepened instead of shrinking, why the governing coalitions fractured along the extraction frontier, and why the fiscal basis of the settlement was exhausted when world prices fell. The claim is deliberately narrow. It does not say the governments were insincere, or that their achievements were illusions, or that nothing distinguished them from their predecessors. It says that the mechanism they rode determined the shape of what they could achieve, the way a river's course determines the shape of the valley whatever the intentions of the water.

Three implications follow, and each will be tested against the record in the sections ahead. The first concerns the character of the achievements. Redistribution through transfers changes the distribution of income; it does not change the distribution of assets, of workplaces, or of the power that flows from owning the means by which a society produces. A household lifted above a poverty line by a transfer is genuinely better off, and remains exactly as dependent on the continuation of the transfer as it was on the first day. The model's first implication is therefore that the achievements, however large, were reversible in a way that transformed production relations would not have been: what the budget gives, the budget crisis takes away. The second implication concerns the export structure. A government financed by commodity rent has no fiscal interest in diversifying away from commodities; on the contrary, its revenue rises with every expansion of the extractive frontier, and diversification, which pays in decades, competes for funds against redistribution, which pays in electoral cycles. The model predicts deepening dependence as the path of least resistance, and the period's critics named the result neo-extractivism. The third implication concerns time. Rent at boom prices is a wasting asset politically even before it wastes geologically, because the settlement built on it creates expectations indexed to the boom. When the price falls, the government must cut the settlement or borrow against a recovery, and either course dissolves the coalition the settlement created. The model thus contains its own ending, dated not by politics but by the world market.

### What would falsify the rent model as an account of the period?

Evidence that the governments used the windfall predominantly to change ownership and production, building diversified industry behind the rent shield, or evidence that their social settlements survived the price fall intact because they rested on organisation rather than expenditure. Either finding, in a major case, would break the model's spine; the record instead runs the other way.

It sharpens the model to say what it is not. It is not the claim that nothing changed. Constitutions were rewritten, courts and electoral systems reformed, foreign policy reoriented, and the political inclusion of peoples the old republics had excluded was a change in the structure of the state, not a transfer payment. The model governs the economy's commanding relations, land, capital, and the export structure, and it is silent, not negative, about changes elsewhere. Nor is the model the claim that the governments were merely lucky. Luck supplied the windfall; politics decided its use, and other imaginable governments of the same years, of the right, would have used it to cut taxes on capital and retire debt, as some neighbouring governments in fact did. The pink tide's choice to spend the rent downward, on the poor, was a real choice with real beneficiaries, and the model loses nothing by honouring it. What the model insists on is the distinction between the use of a windfall and the transformation of a structure, and the period is the great modern demonstration of how far the first can go without becoming the second.

## The achievements, weighed seriously

Any account that cannot state the achievements at full strength has no standing to state the limits, so they are stated here without discount. Across the region, during the boom decade, poverty fell substantially and extreme poverty fell faster, a movement recorded in the household survey literature and in the survey reporting of the United Nations' regional commission for the continent, and large enough to reverse the deterioration of the market decades. Conditional cash transfers reached tens of millions of households, paying mothers for children's school attendance and health checks, and whatever their design limits, they put a floor under incomes that had previously had none. Non-contributory pensions extended a measure of security to elderly people whom the contributory systems, built for a formal workforce that had shrunk, had abandoned. Minimum wages rose in real terms in most of the wave countries, public employment expanded, and in several cases labour law moved, against the current of the preceding era, toward formalisation rather than away from it. Health and education provision widened: clinics and hospitals built, universities founded in provinces that had never had them, literacy campaigns in the Venezuelan and Bolivian cases conducted as national mobilisations.

The achievements had a texture that aggregate descriptions miss. A transfer paid to a mother in her own name changed relations inside households. A pension arriving in a village changed the standing of the old. A university campus in a provincial town changed what a family could imagine for its children, and the first generation of students from poor and indigenous backgrounds changed the composition of the professions they entered. Political scientists who studied the period's electorates found what the model would predict and what cynicism would not: beneficiaries did not merely consume the transfers, they organised around them, defended them, and punished parties that threatened them, so that the social programmes became a durable fact of the political landscape even where the governments that founded them later fell. The settlement outlasted the settlement's authors in the only way available to it, as an expectation lodged in the electorate.

### Which achievement mattered most to the people who received it?

The testimony gathered by the period's ethnographers points less to any single programme than to predictability: a transfer that arrived on a known date, a pension that did not depend on a patron's favour, a wage floor that an employer had to respect. For households whose incomes had been a sequence of emergencies, the conversion of luck into schedule was itself the transformation they felt.

It is equally part of the record that the achievements were unevenly shared across the family's cases, and the unevenness is instructive. Where the rent stream was largest relative to the population, the spending could be most expansive and the institutional improvisation most ambitious. Where the coalition included strong unions and an organised party, the gains were more likely to be written into law and labour relations, and so more likely to survive. Where the programmes rested on presidential discretion, they lived and died with administrations. The model accommodates these differences without strain: rent explains the scale of what was possible, organisation explains how much of the possible was made durable, and the confusion of the two is the commonest error in the period's polemics. Admirers credited the governments' will for what the world market financed; detractors blamed the governments' character for what the world market withdrew. Both errors flatter the importance of virtue. The achievements were the joint product of a windfall and a choice, and they should be honoured as the choice and analysed as the windfall.

## Inclusion: the achievement that was structural

One achievement of the period resists the rent model's logic, and honesty requires isolating it. The political inclusion of indigenous and Afro-descendant peoples, and the advance of women into the region's legislatures and cabinets, changed who the state was, not merely what it distributed. In Bolivia, the constituent assembly that refounded the republic as a plurinational state seated delegates from peoples the old order had treated as a labour force and a folklore. In Ecuador, a new constitution wrote the rights of nature and the principle of sumak kawsay, living well, into the fundamental law. Across the region, indigenous deputies, ministers, and eventually heads of government occupied offices from which their grandparents had been excluded by law, custom, and the price of a suit. Afro-descendant movements won recognition, collective land titles in some jurisdictions, and a place in the official story of nations that had described themselves as white or mestizo and harmonious. These were changes in political structure. They cannot be reduced to transfers, and the model does not try: its claim concerns production relations, and a change in the composition and self-description of the state is a different order of fact.

The distinction matters for the argument's later stages, because the coalition fractures that mark the period's second half ran precisely through this achievement. The indigenous movements that broke with the governments were not an external opposition; they were, in the Andean cases, the governments' own founding constituency, the movements whose marches and blockades had destroyed the old parties and carried the new ones to power. Their grievance was not that inclusion had failed but that it had been outbid: the same state that seated indigenous ministers licensed the mine, the dam, and the highway through indigenous land, on the argument that the rent from extraction paid for the inclusion. A theory of the period that treats the movements' break as ingratitude or as manipulation by the right has understood nothing. The break was the model's internal contradiction arriving on schedule: redistribution required extraction, extraction required territory, and the territory belonged, by the new constitutions' own words, to the peoples the redistribution was supposed to emancipate.

### Why did inclusion not protect the governments from their own movements?

Because inclusion changed representation faster than it changed the material relation between the state and indigenous land. A minister's face in the cabinet did not stop the concession map from advancing, and movements that had won recognition discovered that recognition, without a veto over extraction, left the decisive question, what happens to the territory, answered by the treasury's needs.

There is a lesson here that reaches beyond the period. Political inclusion and economic transformation are often spoken of as stages of a single process, the first ripening into the second. The pink tide separated them in time and thereby tested the claim. Inclusion arrived, was real, and then stood still for a decade while the extractive structure deepened beneath it, and the stillness was not an accident of personnel but the rent model's signature: a state financed by subsoil wealth has every reason to widen the circle of those who share the proceeds and no reason at all to widen the circle of those who decide about the subsoil. Where the two circles were forced to coincide, in the consultations and conflicts over specific projects, the period's defining confrontations occurred. They are examined in their own section below; here it is enough to register that the inclusion achievement and the extractivism critique are not two stories but one, told from the palace and from the territory respectively.

## What redistribution left untouched

Against the achievements stands the inventory of the unchanged, and it is a long inventory. Landholding, the oldest structure of Latin American inequality, emerged from the period essentially intact: the great estates of the Brazilian interior, the soy latifundia of the Southern Cone, the cattle frontiers of Central America expanded or held their ground, and agrarian reform, where it proceeded at all, proceeded at the margins, settling families on peripheral land without disturbing the productive core. The financial system was untouched and handsomely rewarded: banks in the wave's largest economies recorded some of their most profitable years in financial history during governments elected to discipline them, because high interest rates, public debt service, and a consumption boom financed by transfers are a banker's ideal environment. Industrial ownership changed little; the manufacturing that existed at the wave's opening existed, somewhat shrunken as a share of output, at its close, and the commanding positions in mining, energy, and agribusiness remained with the same mixture of foreign corporations and domestic conglomerates, now paying higher royalties into the treasury. Tax structures, the quiet skeleton of every social order, shifted least of all: the region's reliance on consumption taxes paid by the poor, and its gentle treatment of wealth, property, and inheritance, survived governments of every stripe within the family.

The point of the inventory is not the cheap one, that the governments changed nothing, which the previous sections have already refuted. The point is the precise location of the boundary between what changed and what did not. Income changed; services changed; representation changed; the constitution of several republics changed. Property did not. The boundary is exactly where the rent model draws it, and it is worth asking why the boundary held so uniformly across governments that differed in rhetoric, courage, and base. The answer the model gives is that the boundary was the condition of the financing. Rent could be raised by renegotiating the state's share with foreign extractors, a negotiation conducted across a table. Property at home could be touched only by confronting the classes who staffed the courts, owned the media, commanded the officer corps' sympathies, and could move their capital abroad in an afternoon. Every government in the family made the same calculation, including the ones whose speeches promised otherwise, and the uniformity of the outcome across such different political cultures is the strongest single evidence that the constraint was structural rather than a coincidence of timidity.

### If the governments avoided confronting property, was anything they did genuinely of the left?

The question assumes that redistribution is not a left achievement, which the history of the labour movement refutes: pensions, transfers, wage floors, and public services were the left's concrete programme for a century. What the period lacked was not left policy but the left's further horizon, the transformation of production, and confusing the two judgements produces both the adulation and the contempt the period attracts.

A subtler item in the inventory of the unchanged deserves its own paragraph, because it governed the period's ending: the organisation of the beneficiaries. The classical left, whatever its failures, built institutions designed to outlast governments, unions, cooperatives, party schools, peasant leagues, and it understood those institutions as the real deposit of its victories. The pink tide, with honourable exceptions in the movement-governments of the Andes, built programmes instead: registries of beneficiaries, payment systems, delivery bureaucracies. Programmes are revocable by signature; institutions must be dismantled, and dismantling has a political price. When the fiscal squeeze came, the difference announced itself. Constituencies that existed as entries in a registry could be cut by adjusting the registry; constituencies organised in bodies of their own could and did fight. The model's third implication, that the settlement was reversible, thus had an organisational as well as a fiscal meaning, and the governments that had come to power through movements discovered, at the end, how much of the movement they had converted into an audience.

## Dependence deepened: the export structure under the boom

The dependency tradition, whose full treatment belongs to the companion article on [dependency theory in Latin America](/2019/01/15/dependency-theory-latin-america/), taught that the region's insertion into the world economy as a supplier of primary products was the master fact of its history. The pink tide, which included parties formed by that tradition, might have been expected to fight the insertion. The record runs the other way. During the boom decade the export baskets of the wave's economies grew more concentrated in primary products, not less; the share of manufactures in exports fell across most of the region; and the phenomenon acquired a name in the Spanish-language literature, reprimarisation, the return to primary specialisation under governments rhetorically committed to escaping it. The mechanism was not mysterious. High commodity prices raised the return on every hectare and every well relative to every factory, pulled investment and credit toward the extractive sectors, appreciated currencies against the manufactures that had to compete with imports, and filled the treasury in proportion to the concentration itself. A finance minister did not need to choose reprimarisation. It was the default setting of the boom, and choosing against it would have required spending the windfall on the slow, uncertain work of industrial transformation while taxing or restraining the sectors that generated the windfall. No government in the family sustained that choice.

The Chinese connection tightened the structure. The region's trade reoriented across the Pacific within a decade: raw materials flowed east, manufactured goods flowed back, and the bilateral pattern reproduced, with a new partner, the classic centre and periphery geometry that the dependency writers had mapped for the age of British and then North American predominance. South American governments of the left were not passive in this; they sought the relationship, competed for its investment, and celebrated its arrival as a diversification away from the old hegemon. In one register it was: a second buyer disciplines the first. In the register that matters to this article it was a deepening of the same specialisation with a hungrier customer, and the hungrier the customer, the stronger the pull. The period thus offers a paradox that only a materialist analysis states cleanly: the governments that most sincerely intended to loosen dependence presided over its intensification, because the instrument of their social programme, rent, was dependence in fiscal form.

### Why did diversification fail even where governments announced it as a goal?

Announcing diversification costs nothing and building it costs the windfall twice: once in the industries subsidised before they can compete, and again in the extractive output forgone or taxed to pay for them. Electoral time punished both costs and rewarded neither, since the factories of a successful diversification mature after the government that planted them has faced the voters.

The deepening had a geography, and the geography explains the period's conflicts. Extraction is not an abstraction; it happens in specific watersheds, forests, and territories, and the boom's arithmetic pushed the frontier outward into places that previous price levels had left uneconomic: deeper Amazonian oil blocks, higher Andean mines, the soy frontier's advance into the Chaco and the cerrado, mega-dams on tributary rivers, open pits where underground workings had sufficed. Each advance converted a fiscal abstraction into a local fact, a road crew, a tailings pond, a flooded valley, a concession map laid over a community's titled or untitled land. The literature calls the result the extractive frontier, and the frontier is where the pink tide's second history was written, in the consultations refused, the blockades raised, and the marches that walked from the territories to the capitals of governments the marchers had elected. That second history is the subject of the next three sections, and it begins with the concept the period's internal critics forged to name the whole pattern.

## Neo-extractivism: the charge from within the left

The most damaging criticism of the pink tide did not come from the right, which mostly repeated its older vocabulary of populism and fiscal sin, but from intellectuals and movements inside the left itself, and its key term was neo-extractivism. The Uruguayan scholar Eduardo Gudynas gave the concept its systematic form: extractivism, the large-scale removal of natural resources, largely unprocessed, for export, had persisted across the ideological spectrum of the region's governments, and its progressive variant, neo-extractivism, differed from the older kind in the state's larger take of the rent and in the legitimation that take provided, since extraction could now be defended as the financier of social justice. The Ecuadorian economist Alberto Acosta, who had served in the first government of his country's citizens' movement before breaking with it, pressed a parallel argument from inside the experience: the abundance of rent, he argued, reproduced the resource curse under a progressive administration, discouraging the productive transformation that alone could free the economy from the subsoil. The Argentine sociologist Maristella Svampa supplied the period's other durable formula, the commodities consensus, examined in the next section. What united these critics was their location: they wrote as participants in the broad left the wave had carried, several of them as former officials or allies of the governments they criticised, and their charge was not that the governments had betrayed a blueprint but that the model itself, rent-financed redistribution through deepened extraction, carried its limits inside it.

The charge deserves statement in its strongest form, because the article's own argument stands close to it and the differences must be visible. In its strongest form, the neo-extractivism critique holds that the progressive governments did not merely fail to transform the extractive structure but actively modernised and legitimised it, expanding the frontier, criminalising or marginalising the movements that resisted particular projects, and converting the language of social justice into the extraction industry's most effective public argument. Where the old extractivism of the market decades had at least faced a hostile left, the new extractivism disarmed opposition by identifying the mine with the pension and the well with the clinic. The critique further holds that the environmental and territorial costs were not incidental damage but a transfer, in which the incomes of the urban poor were raised partly at the expense of the rural and indigenous poor, whose land, water, and health absorbed the difference. A redistribution that moves costs onto the least powerful while moving income toward the more numerous is, on this account, not a stage toward transformation but a substitute for it, and a corrosive one.

### How does neo-extractivism differ from the extractivism of the earlier market governments?

In the destination of the rent and in the story told about it. The earlier model remitted the surplus to companies and creditors and called the arrangement modernisation; the progressive variant captured a larger share for the state, spent it visibly on the poor, and called the arrangement sovereignty. The critics' point is that the hole in the ground, and the frontier's advance, were common to both.

Two qualifications keep the critique honest, and its best authors made both. The first is that the critique is about a structure, not about a uniquely progressive hypocrisy: the extractive model predated the wave, survived it in the region's other governments, and would have expanded under administrations of the right with a smaller state share and a harsher treatment of protest. The second is that the critique's authors were not demanding the immediate closure of the extractive economy, which would have collapsed the fiscal basis of every social programme they also valued; they were demanding that the rent be treated as a bridge, taxed and invested toward a post-extractive economy, with the frontier's expansion restrained and the affected peoples holding real rights of refusal. The argument between the governments and these critics was therefore not extraction or no extraction. It was whether the windfall years were being used to build the exit from extraction or to furnish a more comfortable room inside it. On the evidence of the export structure examined above, the critics had the better of that argument, and the governments' own diversification announcements, repeated annually and unfulfilled, stand as the record's quiet agreement.

## The commodities consensus

Svampa's formula names the settlement that succeeded the market era's own. The Washington Consensus, in her periodisation, had organised the region's economies around privatisation, openness, and the retreat of the state, and had been legitimated by a promise of eventual prosperity through integration. The commodities consensus organised them around the export of nature, was legitimated by the visible redistribution of the rent, and rested on a broader social agreement: governments of left and right, companies, and much of public opinion converged on the proposition that extraction was the engine and argument about it concerned only the division of its proceeds. The formula's bite lies in the word consensus. The market era's order had been imposed against resistance and never ceased to be contested; the commodities order, at its height, was hegemonic in the strict sense, structuring the assumptions even of its critics, who found themselves arguing for a larger state share of the mine's rent, a cleaner mine, a mine with consultation, rarely for a political economy in which the mine's centrality itself was the question.

The consensus had an institutional machinery. Environmental licensing was streamlined in the name of national development; prior consultation of indigenous peoples, guaranteed in international conventions the region's states had ratified, was implemented, where it was implemented, as a procedure to be completed rather than a decision to be shared; criminal and administrative law was turned, in case after case documented by the period's human rights literature, against the movements that blockaded roads and occupied installations. None of this required a conspiracy. It required only that the treasury's arithmetic be allowed to answer every question put to it, and the arithmetic was eloquent: each delayed project was a school unbuilt, each refused licence a transfer unpaid. Governments that had arrived denouncing the subordination of politics to economics found themselves administering a subordination of their own, with the difference that the economics now wore the colours of the poor. The consensus is therefore best understood not as a betrayal narrative, in which villains sell a cause, but as the period's deepest structural fact: a revenue model generating its own common sense, inside which decent people administering real improvements could not hear the objection that the model was the problem.

### What made the commodities consensus harder to fight than the market consensus had been?

The market consensus had promised future prosperity and delivered present pain, so experience itself recruited for the opposition. The commodities consensus delivered present income to majorities while exporting its costs to territorial minorities, so experience recruited for the government, and the opposition was reduced to those who lived on the frontier and those who listened to them.

Beneath the consensus lay a genuine intellectual difficulty, and the article's fairness requires naming it. The critics of extractivism never fully answered the question of sequence: with what revenue, in the short run that governments inhabit, were the transfers and services to be financed while a post-extractive economy was built, and who would vote for the austerity of the bridge? The governments asked the question constantly and it was not a trick. The region's tax takes were low, its wealthy practised evasion as a national sport, its industrial base was narrow, and the one revenue that arrived without a fight was the rent. The consensus was thus overdetermined, held in place by arithmetic as well as by ideology, which is why the strongest form of the critique, as the answer section below will show, finally concedes the constraint and argues about the use of the time the constraint purchased. The windfall bought a decade. The indictment is not that the governments accepted the purchase, which no plausible alternative government would have refused, but that the decade was spent furnishing the room.

## Coalitions break on the territory

The fracture of the governing coalitions followed the frontier's map. In Bolivia, the government of the Movement for Socialism, itself the creation of cocalero, peasant, and indigenous organisations, collided with the lowland indigenous peoples over a highway planned through the Isiboro Sécure Indigenous Territory and National Park, the TIPNIS: the road promised integration and served the expanding agricultural and coca frontiers, the territory's peoples marched on the capital in defence of their land and their constitutional rights, and the repression of that march in 2011, followed by a consultation process the movements rejected as managed, broke something in the alliance that speeches could not repair. In Ecuador, the citizens' movement government and the Confederation of Indigenous Nationalities, whose uprisings had cleared the road to power for a generation of politicians, moved from alliance to open antagonism over mining law, water law, and oil expansion in Amazonian territories, until the confederation stood in opposition to a president it had once helped make possible. In Peru, whose government after 2011 belongs to the wave's penumbra, the conflicts at Conga and Bagua wrote the same story in a harsher hand. In Brazil, the great dam at Belo Monte on the Xingu River set the Workers' Party government against the riverine and indigenous peoples of the Amazon in a conflict that divided the party's own founders. In Argentina, the soy frontier's advance and the fumigation of peasant and indigenous communities in the northern provinces proceeded under a government whose human rights discourse was celebrated abroad.

The pattern is more instructive than any single episode. In each case the government experienced the conflict as a misunderstanding to be managed: the project was national, the benefits general, the opponents a minority, sometimes described as manipulated by foreign organisations or by the right. In each case the movements experienced the conflict as the revelation of a hierarchy: when the treasury's needs met the territory's rights, the rights yielded, and the inclusion celebrated in the constitutions was revealed as conditional on the subsoil's availability. Both experiences were accurate reports of the same structure. The rent model predicts the collision's location with precision, at the point where the revenue is physically produced, and it predicts the government's rhetoric too, because a state that finances justice from extraction must describe extraction as justice's instrument and its opponents as justice's enemies. The tragedy of the period, a word this article uses sparingly and means here, is that both sides of these confrontations were the left: the movements that had made the governments, and the governments the movements had made, divided by a hole in the ground that the world market had priced.

### Why did the conflicts concentrate on roads, dams, and mines rather than on taxation or wages?

Because the model's costs were territorial while its benefits were fiscal. A tax dispute is argued in a capital among organised interests; an extraction dispute is lived in a watershed by a community that cannot move. The frontier made the abstract structure visible as bulldozers, which is why the period's iconic images are blockades and marches rather than strikes.

A second fracture ran through the coalitions in the opposite direction, and completeness requires it. The urban middle strata, whose votes had been necessary to every victory and whose incomes rose with the consumption boom, developed grievances of their own as the period lengthened: inflation eroding salaries in the cases where monetary discipline slipped, insecurity, congested and decaying public services used by those too prosperous for the targeted programmes and too poor for the private alternatives, and a growing sense, cultivated assiduously by opposition media, that the transfers they funded through prices and taxes flowed to others. The pink tide's electoral majorities were coalitions of the poor who received and the middle strata who acquiesced, and acquiescence is a wasting asset. When the boom ended and governments reached for the middle strata's pockets, through subsidy cuts, tax adjustments, or inflation, the acquiescence was withdrawn, and the electoral collapses of the period's end were typically larger than the fiscal collapses that triggered them, because the coalition had already thinned from within. The model, which explains the founding bargain, explains this too: a settlement financed by a third party, the foreign consumer of commodities, holds its domestic parties together only while the third party pays.

## The defence of the model, stated at full strength

The governments and their intellectuals were not silent under the criticism, and their defence, assembled from the period's speeches, essays, and theoretical statements, deserves to be heard as its proponents made it, at full strength. Its most systematic voice was Álvaro García Linera, the Bolivian sociologist who served as his country's vice president through the movement government's years and who wrote, in essays and addresses, a theory of the process he was administering. Emir Sader, the Brazilian sociologist, gave the defence its continental form in his writings on post-neoliberalism. Between them and a wider circle of allied intellectuals, the defence rests on five pillars, and each pillar is load-bearing.

The first pillar is the constraint itself. A left government in a dependent economy, the defence begins, does not choose its circumstances: it inherits a narrow tax base, a wealthy class practised in evasion and flight, a judiciary and a media owned by its opponents, an officer corps of uncertain loyalty, and a world market that punishes heterodoxy through the exchange rate before any parliament votes. Within those circumstances, the demand that such a government transform production relations is a demand that it first survive a confrontation it would probably lose, and the graveyard of the region's hopes, from Guatemala in the nineteen fifties to Chile in the nineteen seventies, is filled with governments that accepted the confrontation on the critics' schedule. The second pillar is the reality of the gains. The reductions in poverty and the extensions of provision were not accounting tricks: they were meals, medicines, pensions, and school places, delivered to millions, and a criticism that treats them as the model's alibi rather than its achievement has lost contact with the people in whose name it speaks. The third pillar is sequence. García Linera's formulation cast extraction as a transitional necessity: the rent from gas and minerals would finance education, infrastructure, and an industrial base over a horizon of decades, and the post-extractive economy was a destination to be approached by stages, not a door to be walked through in a single term. On this account the critics mistook a bridge for a residence because they refused to read the plans. The fourth pillar is the comparison. Every alternative actually on offer in the period's elections, the defence notes, was worse for the poor: the right proposed to spend the same rent on tax cuts and debt service, and the ultra-left proposed programmes without a path through a single congress. Judged against feasible rivals rather than against utopia, the governments were the best available instrument of their own electorates. The fifth pillar is democratic. The governments won elections, repeatedly, under observation, with the beneficiaries of redistribution voting as free citizens; a model that majorities keep choosing imposes a burden of proof on critics who would override the choice in the name of a transformation the majorities did not request.

### What is the strongest single sentence of the defence?

That the critics never produced a budget. Every alternative to rent-financed redistribution had to name the revenue that would replace the rent in the first year of the transition, at the same scale, without collapsing the currency or the coalition, and in the whole literature of the internal critique that sentence, with its figures and its politics, was never written.

Stated that way, the defence is formidable, and it is important to the integrity of this article that the reader feel its force. It draws on the region's real history of crushed experiments. It speaks for beneficiaries whose gains were the largest of their lifetimes. It exposes, in the budget challenge, a genuine evasion in the critical literature, which is eloquent about destinations and reticent about the first fiscal year. And it rests on a reading of the constraint, the dependence of a small open economy on world prices and on the forbearance of its own propertied classes, that this article's own analysis has repeatedly confirmed rather than refuted. The rent model and the defence agree about the structure of the situation. They disagree about what the governments did inside it, and about what the structure, correctly read, predicted. The answer must therefore be precise: not a denial of the constraint, but an audit of the decade the constraint purchased.

## Answering the defence, with the concession on the record

The answer begins with the concession, stated without varnish, because the checkpoint of honest criticism is to concede what the other side has proved. The defence correctly identifies the constraint. A left government inheriting a dependent economy, a narrow fiscal base, and hostile concentrations of private power faced a situation in which rent-financed redistribution was, in the short run, the available policy, and the critics of the model, in the whole body of their work, did not specify an alternative that was available: no worked-out sequence by which a government of the period could have financed equivalent social provision from domestic taxation while surviving the capital flight, the congressional arithmetic, and the media war that the attempt would have triggered. That concession is owed and is made. A criticism that cannot name the alternative budget has identified a tragedy, not a betrayal, and the vocabulary of betrayal, which parts of the internal critique could not resist, should be retired from the subject.

What the concession does not settle is the audit of the purchased time, and the audit is where the defence weakens. The sequence argument, the third pillar, is an empirical claim disguised as a patience claim: it asserts that the windfall was being converted, behind the consumption figures, into the assets of a different economy. That assertion can be checked, and the record checks it harshly. Export concentration rose. Manufacturing's share fell. The diversification funds and industrial strategies announced across the family remained, in case after case, small relative to the transfers they were supposed to outlast, and where they were large they tended toward infrastructure for extraction itself, the roads and ports of the frontier. Research, technical education, and the patient construction of supplier industries received the rhetoric of priority and the budget of an afterthought. A bridge is not refuted by being crossed slowly; it is refuted by the discovery, at the far end of the decade, that the traffic has been building a larger bridgehead on the same bank. The decade was real, the constraint was real, and the conversion did not happen. That is the answer's core, and it is an answer about use, not about permission.

### Does the concession to the defence dissolve the criticism entirely?

No, because the criticism has two objects and the concession covers only one. It covers the choice to redistribute rent, which was justified. It does not cover the failure to convert the windfall into transformation during the years when conversion was affordable, nor the treatment of the movements that pointed at the failure while there was still time to answer them differently.

The remaining pillars can be dealt with more briefly. The reality of the gains is not in dispute in this article and never was; the argument concerns their reversibility, and the defence's silence about the registry problem, about beneficiaries organised as audiences rather than as institutions, is the silence of the pillar that matters most for the period's ending. The comparison pillar is true and insufficient: that the right would have spent the rent worse is a reason to have voted for these governments, which the article's analysis supports, and not a reason to exempt their economic structure from judgement, unless the standard for a left government is merely to be better than its opponents, a standard the period's own founders would have rejected with contempt. The democratic pillar, finally, cuts both ways. Majorities chose the governments, and majorities also, in the territories, chose against the projects, in consultations and assemblies whose democratic credentials were at least as direct as any parliament's. A defence that rests on the people's verdict cannot consistently discount the verdicts the people delivered at the frontier. The defence, taken whole, establishes that the governments did what was available at the start. It does not establish that they did what was available by the middle, when the treasuries were full, the oppositions were weak, and the price plateau looked permanent. The rent model's verdict rests on that interval, the fat years, and on what was not built in them.

## The turn in prices and the fiscal squeeze

The boom's end arrived the way booms usually end, from outside and all at once. Chinese demand growth slowed as that economy's own investment cycle matured; supply, summoned by a decade of high prices, arrived in volume just as the appetite faltered; financial positions unwound. Between the middle of the twenty tens' first years and the middle of the decade, the prices that had financed the wave fell back, in the emblematic case of petroleum by a magnitude that halved and then halved again the revenue assumptions in the budgets of the oil states, and across the complex of metals and agricultural goods by declines that no treasury had provisioned for. The precise figures are, by this article's protocol, left to the statistical literature. The direction is not in doubt, and neither is the mechanism by which the direction became a political crisis, because the mechanism was the model running in reverse.

A rent-financed settlement has a characteristic pathology in a downturn, and each element of it appeared on schedule. Revenues fell faster than economies contracted, because rent is the most volatile of all fiscal bases, geared to world prices rather than to domestic activity. Expenditures, meanwhile, had hardened: transfers indexed by law or by expectation, subsidies whose removal is a political explosion, public payrolls, debt service on borrowing undertaken in the fat years. The gap opened immediately and could be closed only by some combination of borrowing, monetary emission, reserve drawdown, and cuts, each with its own constituency of victims. Governments that had accumulated reserves and run cautious fiscal policies in the boom, and several in the family had, bought themselves time and a softer landing; governments that had spent the windfall as it arrived, and expanded the state's commitments on the assumption of permanence, met the wall at speed. The variation in outcomes, often attributed to virtue, tracks the variation in exposure and prudence during the plateau, which is to say it tracks the model again: the same windfall, banked or spent, decided the manner of the ending years before the ending arrived.

### Why does a rent settlement crack faster than a tax settlement when conditions turn?

A tax base falls with the domestic economy, gradually and partially, and the state's creditors and citizens share an interest in its recovery. A rent base falls with a price set in markets the state does not influence, and it can fall while the domestic economy still stands, leaving the government insolvent inside a society that does not yet feel poor.

The squeeze had a distributional sequence that the literature on the period records with grim consistency. The first adjustments fell on investment and maintenance, invisible in the short run. The second fell on the subsidies, fuel above all, whose reform united the transport sector, the informal economy, and the urban poor in the streets. The third fell on the transfers themselves, by erosion where indexation lagged inflation, by tightening of registries, by arrears. Inflation, where governments chose the printing press over the cuts, taxed the poor through the price of food with an efficiency no finance ministry would have dared to legislate. By the time formal austerity programmes were announced, much of the settlement had already been withdrawn by quieter means, and the political fact that matters for the history is that the withdrawal was experienced by the beneficiaries as a broken promise rather than as a shared sacrifice, because the settlement had been delivered as a gift of the government rather than built as a right administered by the beneficiaries' own institutions. The registry problem, flagged when the settlement was described, collected its debt.

## Why the wave receded

The recession of the pink tide is usually narrated as a sequence of political events: elections lost, a parliamentary removal in Brazil, a referendum defeat in Bolivia, the long Venezuelan emergency, a rightward succession in Argentina and Ecuador. The events are real, and a purely political narration of them is the mirror image of the error this article corrected at the start. Just as the victories were not a conversion experience, the defeats were not a change of heart. The electorate that had rewarded incumbents for a decade punished them for the same reason, the state of its income, and the incomes turned because the financing turned. The rent model's fourth implication, that the settlement's political half-life was set by the world market, is the recession's first explanation, and everything else is a modifier.

The modifiers matter, and three deserve their weight. The first is the prior thinning of the coalitions, examined above: the movements alienated at the frontier did not return for the defence of the governments in their bad years, and the middle strata whose acquiescence had been rented with stability withdrew it when stability ended, so the electoral collapses exceeded what income loss alone would predict. The second is the opposition's education. A decade out of power taught the region's right to contest the social settlement rather than promise its abolition, to speak the language of the transfers while promising to administer them honestly and without the incumbent's corruption or chaos; the right that returned was, in its presentation, the pink tide's own pupil, and its victories are partly the settlement's posthumous compliment. The third is the role of scandal and institutional conflict, which varied by case and which this article, by its protocol, treats only in general terms: corruption investigations, clashes between executives and courts or legislatures, and constitutional hardball supplied the occasions for several of the period's endings, but occasions are not causes, and the same scandals in the fat years would have been survived, as similar ones were.

### Did the wave end because voters turned against the left's ideas?

The evidence points the other way. The social programmes remained popular after the governments fell; successor administrations that promised to abolish them retreated; and the electorates that punished the incumbents punished, in the same years, incumbents of other colours whose financing had also failed. The verdict fell on a material settlement, not on a doctrine, which is why the doctrine survived its governments.

A word should be said about the Venezuelan case, which dominates outside commentary on the recession and distorts it. There the rent dependence was the family's most extreme, the confrontation with the old order the most direct, the institutional improvisation the deepest, and the collapse of the oil price met an economy already weakened by distortions accumulated in the boom years themselves. The result was a crisis of a different order from the electoral rotations elsewhere, a social catastrophe whose dimensions the statistical literature continues to contest and which this article, by its figures protocol, does not quantify. For the analysis, the case functions as the model's limiting instance rather than its typical one: it shows where the road of maximum rent dependence and minimum conversion leads when the price turns, and it should be read as the end of a spectrum whose other points are the negotiated successions and ordinary defeats of the less exposed governments. Commentary that treats the limiting instance as the whole story repeats, in reverse, the error of the wave's own propaganda, which had treated the same case as the model's vanguard. The rent model is vindicated by the spectrum, not by either of its ends.

### What separates the cases that rotated power quietly from the cases that broke?

Three things, in the record's consistent pattern: the depth of rent dependence, the reserves and fiscal room banked during the plateau, and whether the social settlement had been lodged in law and organisation or in discretionary programmes. Where dependence was shallower and the settlement institutional, the ending was an election. Where all three ran the wrong way, the ending was a crisis of the state itself.

With the recession described, the two-lefts schema can be given its final examination. The schema had predicted that the modern, market-compatible left would prove durable and the nationalist, redistributive left would collapse with its finances. The record is more interesting. Governments of the supposedly modern camp lost office in the same years and for recognisably similar reasons, while at least one government of the supposedly populist camp, the Bolivian, sustained its position longest of all, on the strength of fiscal prudence during the boom and a movement organisation that survived its conversion into a government. Durability tracked the treasury and the organisation, not the temperament. That is the rent model's cleanest external victory over its rival, and it is worth dwelling on, because it shows what the materialist method is for. The schema sorted the governments by their speeches and was surprised by their fates. The model sorted them by their revenue and was not.

## Misreadings this article exists to correct

Five errors recur in the coverage of the period, and each has been answered in place; gathering them exposes the pattern they share. The first is that the pink tide was one project, a coordinated continental design. It was a family of national coalitions united by a revenue environment, and its members quarrelled, diverged, and at times worked against one another's candidates and projects. The second is that the wave was socialist in the sense of transforming ownership. With partial exceptions at the family's Venezuelan edge, the commanding property relations of every economy in the wave were intact at the end, which is the rent model's central observation and not a debating point. The third is the mirror error, that the achievements were illusory, a statist mirage dissolved on inspection. The poverty reductions and extensions of provision were measured by hostile as well as friendly instruments, were felt in the daily arithmetic of millions of households, and survive in the expectations of electorates that still punish their withdrawal. The fourth is that the extractivism critique came from the right, as a green gloss on the old opposition. It came from the left's own intellectuals and movements, several of them founders of the governing coalitions, and its bitterness is the bitterness of a family argument. The fifth is that the wave receded for political reasons alone, a failure of messaging or of succession planning. The recession tracks the price of what the region sells with a fidelity that no theory of messaging can match.

The five errors share a root: each substitutes a story about intentions for an account of mechanisms. Projects, socialism, illusion, betrayal, and messaging are all vocabularies of intention. Revenue, property, rent, and coalition are vocabularies of mechanism, and the period yields its secrets only to the second set. That is the methodological claim of this article, and it is also, not incidentally, the claim of the Marxist tradition the series surveys: that the first question to ask of any government is not what it believes but how it is financed, and the second is not what it promises but what it owns.

### Why do intention-based accounts of the period keep failing their readers?

Because the intentions were genuinely various and the outcomes were stubbornly similar. Any explanation that begins from what the governments wanted must explain why governments that wanted opposite things converged on the same structure, and it cannot. An explanation that begins from the revenue begins exactly where the convergence begins, and the intentions return to their proper place, as the explanation of the variations.

## Grading the cases against the model

A model that cannot be applied case by case is a slogan, so the family's principal cases are graded here, briefly and in turn, against the rent model's rows. Venezuela is the maximum case: the largest rent relative to the economy, the deepest confrontation with the old property order in the boom's first years, the most ambitious parallel institutions, and, because the conversion of rent into diversified production was not achieved there either, the most catastrophic exposure when the price collapsed. The model fits it as a limit fits a curve. Bolivia is the disciplined case: a movement government that raised the state's share of gas rent dramatically, spent it on transfers and inclusion with genuine effect, and simultaneously ran the treasury with a prudence that stored reserves and stabilised the currency, so that its settlement survived the price turn longer than any comparable case; the model's rows fit, with the organisation row, the one the table compresses, doing the explanatory work for the survival. Ecuador is the exposed case: dollarised, oil-dependent, its government used the rent for infrastructure and provision on a large scale while breaking early and thoroughly with the indigenous movement that had been its road to power, and its settlement passed, at the period's close, to a successor from within its own movement who governed in a different register. Brazil is the coalition case: the continent's most diversified economy in the family, where rent mattered through the general boom rather than through a single treasury stream, where the workers' party's alliance with finance and agribusiness set the boundary of the possible, and where the achievements in transfers and wages coexisted with record bank profits in the purest demonstration of the model's thesis that redistribution and property can be kept in separate rooms. Argentina is the recovery case: a government formed from a collapse, financed by the soy rent through export taxes and by the space opened by default and devaluation, which fought its own agrarian bourgeoisie over the export tax schedule in the period's most direct confrontation between a pink tide government and a propertied class, and lost enough of that fight to confirm the rule it had challenged. Chile is the moderated case: copper rent channelled through a stabilisation framework inherited from before the wave, social spending expanded within fiscal rules, the structure of the model administered by the coalition that had built it, so that Chile shows the rent logic operating at the lowest temperature in the family, and shows thereby that the logic does not require a refoundational rhetoric to operate. Uruguay is the institutional case: the family's most party-structured government, where the gains were lodged in wage councils and law to an unusual degree, and where the settlement's durability after the wave has been correspondingly high.

### Which case is the model's hardest test?

Bolivia, because it combines the model's mechanism with the two variables the model treats as modifiers, movement organisation and fiscal prudence, at their strongest. If the rent model were the whole story, Bolivia's settlement should have cracked on the regional schedule. That it bent instead is the record's instruction that mechanisms set the constraints and organisation decides how much room the constraints leave.

The grading exercise has a further use. It separates the questions that the period's polemics habitually fuse. Whether a government was admirable is one question, on which readers will divide by their values. Whether it was financed by rent is a second, answerable from the revenue structure. Whether it converted the rent is a third, answerable from the export and ownership data. Whether its ending was necessary is a fourth, answerable only by the comparison of cases. The polemics of the period, friendly and hostile, run the four together into a single verdict, and the verdicts are worthless precisely in proportion to the fusion. The model's discipline is to keep the questions apart, and a reader who finishes this article able to grade a new case on the four questions separately has acquired the article's real content, of which the pink tide itself is only the worked example.

## Labour, wages, and the transfer: the working class under the wave

A Marxist account owes the period a question that its own polemics often skipped: what happened to the working class, as a class, while its parties governed? The answer is double, and both halves belong to the model. On the side of income, the period was the best in a generation: real minimum wages rose across most of the family, formal employment expanded as growth and policy pulled workers out of informality, collective bargaining revived where the state encouraged it, and in the Uruguayan case the restoration of wage councils rebuilt an institution of class negotiation that the dictatorship and the market decades had dismantled. On the side of organisation and structure, the record is thinner. Union density recovered modestly from a low base; the informal sector, though reduced, remained the condition of a vast share of the workforce; and the fastest-growing employment sat in services, construction, commerce, and the extractive enclaves themselves, sectors whose organisation is difficult and whose relation to the export model made them hostages to it. The transfer, meanwhile, addressed the worker as a citizen and a mother rather than as a producer: the period's emblematic social relationship ran from the treasury to the household, not from the union to the employer. A class can grow richer and less organised in the same decade, and the pink tide's working class did, which is one reason the settlement's defence, when the bad years came, was conducted more by voting than by the older repertoire of strikes and occupations.

The point connects to the oldest argument inside Marxism about such periods. A government that raises wages and transfers while leaving the structure of production untouched improves the terms on which labour is exploited without altering the fact, and the tradition has a vocabulary for the result: the gains are real, they are worth defending, and they are not socialism. The pink tide's own rhetoric sometimes honoured the distinction and sometimes dissolved it, claiming the name of socialism for the redistribution itself, in the Venezuelan case as an explicit doctrine of a national road. The rent model restores the distinction without rancour. What the wave achieved belongs to the honourable history of reform, the history of the welfare provisions and labour rights that workers' movements have won inside capitalism on every continent. What it did not attempt, with the partial and instructive exception already noted, belongs to a different history. Confusing the two histories flatters the period and insults the reforms, which need no borrowed glory.

### Did the period strengthen or weaken the left as an organised force?

Both, in sequence and by level. It strengthened the left as an electoral brand and a governing class, staffing states and normalising its presence in office. It weakened, in several cases, the left as an autonomous movement, as organisations were absorbed into administrations, funded by programmes, or demobilised by loyalty, a weakening the recession exposed when the movements were needed and found thinned.

## Constitutions, courts, and the state the wave built

The wave's institutional ambitions varied with its edges. At the refoundational pole, three countries rewrote their constitutions through elected assemblies, and the texts repay attention as the period's most durable artifacts. They expanded catalogues of rights, recognised plurinationality and the rights of nature, created mechanisms of recall and participation, and in the Bolivian and Ecuadorian cases attempted to constitutionalise the development model itself, including the state's command of natural resources. Whatever their later fortunes, the assemblies were real exercises of popular constitution-making, and the contrast with the market era's method, reform by decree and treaty, is a point in the wave's favour that its critics on the right rarely concede. At the family's other pole, governments worked entirely within inherited constitutions, amending at the margins, and their restraint was itself a doctrine: stability as the precondition of the social programme.

Between the poles, the period's institutional record holds a tension that the rent model illuminates from an unexpected angle. Rent-financed governments are tempted toward the concentration of authority, not from ideology alone but from the mechanics of distribution: a settlement administered as a programme rewards a strong executive, a disciplined legislature, and a state apparatus oriented to delivery, while the checks and plural centres of a constitutional order appear, from the delivery desk, as friction. The period shows the temptation at work in several cases, in pressures on courts, electoral bodies, and media that the human rights literature of the time documented and that this article records in general terms. It also shows the counter-cases, governments that accepted the friction and governed through it. The analytical point is that the temptation is structural before it is personal: where the state's legitimacy rests on distributing a windfall, the distributor's impatience with interruption is a predictable product of the model, and the constitutions that survived the period best were those that had priced the temptation in.

### Why do distribution-centred governments chafe against constitutional friction?

Because their claim to rule is performance, the visible delivery of goods, and every veto point between the treasury and the beneficiary reads as an obstacle to the performance that legitimises them. Governments legitimated by procedure tolerate procedure; governments legitimated by the cheque experience the cheque's delay as a political cost, and reach for the shortcut.

## The region to the world: integration projects and the northern neighbour

The wave had a foreign policy, and its shape followed the revenue. Freed by the boom from dependence on the old lending channels, the governments built or deepened regional institutions of their own: a South American union of nations, a bank of the south that remained more project than bank, a regional defence council, and, at the family's more ambitious edge, an alliance organised around subsidised oil and a rhetoric of continental unity. A larger diplomatic architecture, the community of Latin American and Caribbean states, gathered the whole region without the northern neighbour for the first time. Trade and investment ties with China expanded from a modest base to a structural fact. The United States, absorbed in wars elsewhere during the wave's first decade, watched its traditional predominance diluted, and the period's diplomacy is unintelligible without that distraction.

For this article's argument, the integration projects matter chiefly as a test of the model at the regional scale. A serious integration, of the kind the period's rhetoric promised, would have meant building regional production chains, coordinating industrial policy, and pooling the rent to finance a shared transformation, in short, applying the conversion strategy collectively that no government applied nationally. What was built instead was principally diplomatic and financial plumbing: summits, councils, a development bank capitalised modestly, and bilateral energy arrangements. The rent was distributed nationally, because the coalitions that lived on it were national, and the integration remained a diplomacy of governments rather than an economy of the region. The pattern is the model's signature at a larger scale: the windfall financed solidarity as a sentiment and a foreign policy, and left the productive map, each country exporting its nature to the world and importing its manufactures, exactly where the boom had found it.

## An earlier electoral road, and the difference it teaches

The pink tide was not the region's first attempt to reach socialism, or something like it, through elections, and the comparison with the Chilean experience of the early nineteen seventies is the comparison the period's own participants kept making. That earlier road, treated fully in the account of [Allende's Chile and the democratic road](/2018/12/01/allende-chile-democratic-road/), attempted the transformation the pink tide declined: expropriation of the commanding heights, agrarian reform at depth, a confrontation with property conducted through law until law and property together destroyed the government. The pink tide's founders had studied that ending. Several of the wave's governments said explicitly, in their founding documents and speeches, that they would not repeat it: they would respect the constitutional frame, reassure the owners they did not intend to expropriate, and seek in the ballot box a durable majority rather than a heroic minority. The rent made the strategy viable. Where the earlier road had to seize the means of payment, the wave received payment from the world market, and could purchase social peace from the propertied instead of confiscating their property.

The comparison cuts in both directions, and both cuts belong in the record. Against the earlier road, the wave's caution delivered what the heroic strategy could not: a decade and more of government, provision extended to millions, and endings that were, in most cases, elections rather than massacres. Against the wave, the earlier road's fate poses the question the rent model cannot dissolve: whether a transformation postponed until the financing is painless is a transformation postponed forever, since the financing of a dependent economy is never painless, and the painless decade, when it miraculously arrived, was spent on everything except the transformation. The two roads frame the region's enduring dilemma, and the series' strategic comparison of armed and electoral paths, in the article on guerrilla and electoral strategy, takes the dilemma up at the level of method. Here it is enough to fix the pink tide's place in the sequence: it was the electoral road rebuilt for an age of rent, and its limits are the limits of that rebuilding.

### What did the pink tide learn from the Chilean catastrophe, and what did the lesson cost?

It learned to survive: to keep the constitution, the currency, and the officer corps inside the settlement, and to buy with rent the time that the earlier road had tried to seize. The cost was the postponement of every structural ambition to a future that the revenue model never scheduled, so that survival became the achievement and the transformation became the rhetoric that survival wore.

## The ecological charge in theoretical form

The extractivism critique has a theoretical root system, and the companion treatment of [ecological Marxism](/2017/07/01/ecological-marxism-complete-guide/) maps it fully; the connection needs only to be drawn here. In that framework, capitalism's relation to nature is extractive by constitution: it treats the soil, the forest, and the subsoil as free gifts, draws them down, and exports the damage to the places and peoples least able to refuse it. A government of the left that finances itself from extraction does not escape the framework by distributing the proceeds justly; it operates the framework with a kindlier budget. The Andean constitutions of the period attempted, on paper, to break the frame itself, granting rights to nature and enthroning the indigenous principle of living well as a civilisational alternative to development. The attempt is one of the period's most interesting documents, precisely because its fate demonstrates the model's power: within a few years, the same governments were explaining that living well required the mine, that the rights of nature were compatible with the expanded concession map, and that the alternative to extraction was the poverty of the people the constitution existed to serve. The argument was not even false, given the constraint. It was the constraint speaking through the constitution's own authors, and the spectacle of a founding text being reinterpreted by its founders under fiscal pressure is the period's clearest lesson in the relative weight of law and revenue.

The ecological reading adds one further element that the fiscal analysis alone would miss: irreversibility. A fiscal settlement can be rebuilt when prices recover, and parts of the period's settlement were. A flooded valley, a poisoned watershed, a displaced people, and a species extinguished are not rebuilt at any price, which is why the movements at the frontier spoke a language of absolutes that the governments, thinking in budget cycles, persistently misheard as bargaining. The two sides were not disagreeing about a price. They were keeping different kinds of accounts, one in a currency that recovers and one in a currency that does not, and the tragedy of the confrontations is partly a tragedy of incommensurable ledgers.

## The literature and its languages: a note on sources

The critical literature on which this article's argument rests was written principally in Spanish and Portuguese, and an English-language reader deserves a plain statement of what can and cannot be reached. The two-lefts article that organised so much commentary was published in English in a journal of foreign affairs and is fully accessible. The major works of the internal critique are another matter. Gudynas has published essays in English in development and environmental journals, and selections of the neo-extractivism debate circulate in translation in academic collections, but his systematic books remain in Spanish. Svampa's central works on the commodities consensus and on the region's development debates are available in Spanish, with individual essays translated in journals; Acosta's writings on the resource curse and on living well exist in Spanish, with shorter pieces in English in the periodical literature of the development left. García Linera's theoretical essays have appeared in English in a collected volume from a left publisher, which is the exception that proves the rule, and Sader's continental syntheses circulate in Portuguese and Spanish with partial English renderings. The documentary record of the coalition conflicts, the consultations, marches, and legal struggles, lives overwhelmingly in Spanish-language journalism, movement publications, and the reports of regional human rights organisations, of which only a fraction is translated.

The consequence is a distortion in the Anglophone debate that readers should correct for. In English, the period is disproportionately narrated by its friends and enemies in the United States and Europe, for whom the wave was a proxy in other arguments, while the argument that mattered most, the internal one about rent and extraction, arrives late, in fragments, and often stripped of its fiscal sophistication. This article has tried to work from the internal debate's own categories, the rent, the consensus, the frontier, because those categories were forged by people arguing about their own governments in their own languages, and they fit the record better than the imported filing systems. A reader who follows up one thread from this article should follow that one, in translation where translation exists, aware that the map of the debate in English is a map of its coastline and that the interior is Spanish and Portuguese.

## What a transformation would have required

The answer to the defence promised an identification, and this section pays the debt. A strategy that used the windfall to transform production relations, rather than to redistribute its proceeds, would have had to do five things, in an order that the politics of the period made brutal. It would have had to tax the windfall at the maximum the extractors would bear and place the surplus, by law, beyond the reach of the annual budget, in funds dedicated to conversion. It would have had to spend that surplus on the unglamorous assets of a different economy: technical education at scale, research and extension for agriculture and industry, supplier development that forced the extractive enclaves to buy domestically, energy and transport built for internal integration rather than for export corridors. It would have had to reform the tax structure during the fat years, when the pain could be afforded, so that the state emerged from the boom with a domestic revenue base capable of sustaining the social settlement from production rather than from rent. It would have had to carry out the agrarian reform that alone could break the oldest property structure and create an internal market of producers. And it would have had to build, from the beneficiaries of the transition, organisations capable of defending it when the price turned, which means organisations with their own resources and their own authority, not registries administered by a ministry.

Whether any government of the period possessed the conditions for such a strategy is a question with a case-by-case answer, and the honest answer is that none possessed all of them. The strategy requires a coalition willing to be taxed and restrained in consumption during its own victory, a state apparatus of unusual competence, a legislature that will bind itself, and an electorate that rewards investment it cannot eat. The movement governments of the Andes came closest in coalition discipline and furthest in state capacity. The large economies had the capacity and lacked the coalition. The oil state had the revenue and lacked everything else. But the audit of the period is not thereby dissolved into impossibility, because the five requirements admit of degree. Every government could have banked more, taxed more progressively, built more conversion assets, and organised its beneficiaries more durably than it did, and the distance between what each did and what its own conditions allowed is the measure of its responsibility. The rent model fixes the wall. It does not fix where, between the wall and the starting point, each government chose to stop, and the choices differed enough, as the grading section showed, to acquit the analysis of determinism.

### Is the transformation standard a counsel of perfection of the same kind the defence denounces?

It would be, if it were applied as a threshold that the governments either met or betrayed. Applied as a gradient, as an audit of how much of the available conversion each government achieved, it is the ordinary standard of historical judgement, the same standard by which the period's admirers praise the governments that banked reserves and organised their bases over those that did neither.

## The Marxist criticism, gathered in one place

The criticism of the pink tide from within the Marxist tradition can now be stated whole, because its elements have been built up through the article. It holds that the wave's governments mistook a favourable conjuncture for a transformation of the mode of production's local form; that they redistributed the revenue of dependence while deepening the dependence; that they substituted the state as distributor for the working class and the peasantry as producers organised in their own right; that they demobilised, absorbed, or confronted the movements whose pressure was the only force capable of pushing beyond redistribution; and that they therefore left the class structure of their societies, land, capital, and the export apparatus, intact to reclaim the political field when the conjuncture turned. In the tradition's own terms, the wave belongs to the history of reformism, and specifically to the variant the tradition calls social democracy in dependent economies, with all the honour and all the limits the classification carries; the wider comparison is pursued in the article on [Marxism versus social democracy](/2019/11/15/marxism-vs-social-democracy/).

The criticism should be stated with its own internal division visible, because the tradition argued with itself about the wave and the argument is part of the record. One wing, strongest in the movements and in the parties that stayed outside the governments, held the position just stated in full severity and treated the period as a lesson in the impossibility of administering capitalism into its opposite. Another wing, strongest among the governments' own intellectuals, replied with the defence already examined, and added a theoretical claim of its own: that in economies where the working class is a minority of the labouring population and the informal and peasant sectors predominate, the classical sequence, organise production, then transform it, must be reordered, and a state that first universalises income, education, and political membership is building the subject of a later transformation rather than evading it. The reply is the period's most serious contribution to Marxist theory, and it cannot be refuted by pointing at the export statistics, because it is a claim about sequence across generations. It can only be assessed by the test its own logic proposes: whether the universalised citizens, educated and enfranchised, became in fact a force pressing beyond the rent settlement, or whether the settlement's comforts reconciled them to it. The evidence of the recession years, when the defence of the settlement fell largely to the ballot and the settlement's own institutions proved thin, is not encouraging for the reply. But the question outlives the period, and any future left that governs a dependent economy will have to answer it again, which is the deepest sense in which the pink tide remains a live chapter of the tradition rather than a closed one. The period is closed; the problem is not.

## Women, care, and the design of the transfer

The transfer state of the pink tide had a gender, and the fact is central to both its achievements and its limits. The flagship conditional transfers were paid, by deliberate design, to mothers, on the reasoning, supported by a generation of development research, that income in a mother's hands reached children more reliably. The design worked on its own terms and was celebrated for it. It also built the period's social policy around women's unpaid labour: the conditions attached to the payments, school attendance, health checks, nutritional sessions, were administered in practice by mothers' time, so that the state's new presence in poor households arrived as an expansion of women's obligations as well as of their income. Feminist scholarship within the region, much of it sympathetic to the governments, pressed the point throughout the period: a settlement that relieves poverty by intensifying the care burden of poor women has redistributed income and left the sexual division of labour, one of the oldest production relations of all, exactly where it found it. The rent model absorbs the point without strain, because the point is the model stated in the household's register: distribution adjusted, relations untouched.

Against that structural observation stands a record of political change that the same scholarship records. The period saw women's presence in the region's legislatures and executives rise to levels that led the world by regional comparison, quota laws strengthened, and in several countries the presidencies themselves held by women elected within the wave's coalitions. Reproductive rights advanced in a few jurisdictions and were blocked in others by the alliances the governments maintained with religious authorities, a reminder that the coalitions' breadth was purchased in this register too. The honest summary is the one this article has given for the period as a whole: representation and income moved, relations moved least, and the distance between the two movements is the measure of the transformation that did not occur. A future account of the period written from the care economy outward would reach the rent model's conclusions by another road, and the convergence of the two accounts, fiscal and feminist, on the same structure is among the strongest reasons to trust the structure is really there.

### Why were the transfers paid to mothers, and what did the choice reveal?

The payment recognised, correctly, who in poor households converted income into children's welfare, and it revealed the settlement's method in miniature: it worked through the existing relations of the household, empowering women inside them, rather than reorganising the relations themselves, which would have required public care provision on a scale the budgets never attempted.

## The agrarian question, postponed again

No structure in Latin America is older than the land, and no item in the inventory of the unchanged carries more history. The pink tide inherited agrarian questions of legendary depth: the Brazilian latifundio and the landless movement that had made occupation a national politics, the Andean minifundio and the communal lands of indigenous peoples, the soy frontier transforming the Argentine pampas and the Paraguayan east into a single mechanised sea, the coca economy of the Bolivian and Peruvian highlands. The period's record on this ground is the rent model's most melancholy confirmation. Land reform proceeded where it proceeded at all as settlement and titling at the margins. The great export estates, far from being expropriated, were the fiscal partners of the redistribution, their soy and beef and sugar generating the foreign exchange and the export taxes the treasury spent, and a government does not expropriate its own revenue. The landless movement in Brazil, whose encampments had been the moral scenery of the workers' party's rise, found its occupations answered with a slowing reform and a governing alliance that included the agribusiness caucus itself. In the Andes, the agrarian question fused with the territorial one, as communal land became the frontier's target, and the movements' defence of it became the coalition fracture already examined.

The agrarian record matters beyond its acreage because land is where the transformation question is least evadable. A government can plausibly describe a transfer programme as a stage; it cannot describe the untouched latifundio as anything but a decision, renewed annually, to leave the founding property relation of the continent in place. The dependency tradition had always insisted that the external structure and the internal one were a single fabric, that the export economy abroad and the estate at home were woven together. The pink tide's agrarian record is that insistence verified from the governing side: the rent from the world market and the rent from the land arrived in the same treasury, and the government that lived on both disturbed neither.

### Why did agrarian reform stall even under governments born from peasant movements?

Because the export estate had become a fiscal asset of the redistribution itself, generating the foreign exchange and the tax flow the settlement spent, while the reform's beneficiaries, dispersed and poor, could not match the estate's organised weight in the coalition's councils. The movement governments faced the same arithmetic with better intentions, and the arithmetic won in both registers.

## A fiscal anatomy of the spending

Where the money went is as diagnostic as where it came from, and the period's budgets, read as a class of documents, show a consistent anatomy. The largest single flows were the transfers and pensions already described, the human face of the model. Behind them sat a less celebrated and often larger item: subsidies, above all to energy. Cheap fuel, cheap electricity, and cheap transport were the boom's universal dividend, paid to every citizen who bought a litre or a ticket, and in the oil and gas states the fuel subsidy alone could rival the entire targeted transfer system in cost. Its politics were the model's politics in concentrated form: it was redistribution without a registry, available to the car-owning middle class in larger absolute amounts than to the poor, defended ferociously whenever reform was attempted, and justified as the people's share of the national subsoil. A government that could not reform its fuel subsidy, and several tried and retreated in the face of the streets, had located the exact boundary of its power: it could give, and it could not take away, which is the definition of a distributor rather than a transformer.

Public investment formed the third great flow, and its composition tells the conversion story in budgetary form. Roads, ports, stadiums, and energy projects dominated; the roads ran disproportionately to the ports, serving the export structure; the energy projects served the frontier; and the investments that a conversion strategy required, technical institutes, research systems, industrial extension, the unglamorous plumbing of a different economy, appear in the budgets as small lines with large speeches attached. The fourth flow was the public payroll, expanded across the family as the state resumed its role as employer, a real contribution to the formalisation of work and simultaneously a commitment that the downturn would expose. Read whole, the anatomy confirms the model's central distinction with unusual clarity. The spending was overwhelmingly spending on consumption, distribution, and the infrastructure of extraction. The spending on the transformation of production was the smallest part of it, in the fat years, when it was most affordable. Budgets are the autobiographies that governments cannot edit afterwards, and this period's autobiography is written in that proportion.

### What does the fuel subsidy reveal that the transfer programmes conceal?

The transfers were targeted, conditional, and proud of their design; the subsidy was universal, unconditional, and regressive in its incidence, yet politically untouchable. Together they show a state whose redistributive legitimacy rested on delivering the subsoil's dividend to everyone visibly, and whose reforming capacity stopped exactly where the dividend began.

## The city: informality, housing, and the urban majority

The pink tide was elected, in the main, by cities, and its urban record completes the picture of the settlement. The region's great metropolises entered the period with majorities housed informally, in settlements built by their residents on occupied or subdivided land, served irregularly by water, sewerage, and transport, and policed as problems. The wave's governments invested in the settlements on a scale the market decades had not attempted: regularisation of titles, water and paving programmes, cable cars and bus corridors climbing into the hillside districts, housing programmes that in the largest case delivered subsidised homes by the million across the following years. The urban poor's experience of the period is inseparable from these works, and oral histories of the era return to them as insistently as to the transfers: the year the water came, the year the street was paved, the year the title arrived. A materialist account should honour the testimony. Infrastructure is not a transfer; a sewer does not vanish when a budget is cut, and the urban works are among the settlement's most durable deposits.

The limits in the city were the model's limits in concrete. The works serviced the informal city without transforming the land market that produced it: urban land remained a speculative asset, the formal housing market remained priced beyond the beneficiaries of the programmes, and each new serviced settlement was followed, at the city's edge, by the founding of the next unserviced one. Transport investment moved the poor to their jobs faster without moving the jobs, or the ownership of the firms that offered them, at all. And the urban middle strata, squeezed between the celebrated programmes below them and the private services above, nursed the grievances of status and congestion that the coalition analysis recorded. The city thus exhibits the whole argument in one landscape: a decade of genuine improvement in the conditions of labour's reproduction, financed from outside production, leaving the relations of production, in the factory, the office, the land market, and the bank, serenely intact. The reproduction of labour power was socialised and subsidised; its exploitation was not touched. Marxists have a long name for that configuration, and the period's governments, whatever their intentions, built it thoroughly.

## The wave in the region's longer century

The pink tide was not the first time Latin American governments of a redistributive temper rode a commodity windfall, and the comparison with the mid-twentieth century is the surest way to see both its novelty and its recurrence. The classical populisms of that earlier age, in Argentina, Brazil, and Mexico above all, had also converted export income into urban wages, social rights, and national industry, and had also built their political majorities on the conversion. They differed from the pink tide in one decisive respect: the world of the nineteen forties and fifties permitted, and the balance of class forces encouraged, a measure of industrial transformation behind tariff walls, so that the earlier redistributions were accompanied by a real, if dependent and incomplete, building of manufacturing capacity. The pink tide governed in a world where that road was blocked by open trade regimes, by the mobility of capital, and by the manufacturing supremacy of East Asia, and it therefore redistributed with one hand tied: the windfall could be spent, but the industrial deepening that might have given the spending a future had become far harder than it was for the earlier generation. The comparison acquits the wave's governments of a simple failure of will and convicts the structure more deeply. They faced the populist temptation with fewer exits than their predecessors, and they took the temptation.

The Venezuelan memory holds the pattern's emblem. In the oil boom of the nineteen seventies, the country's most famous man of letters, Arturo Uslar Pietri, had already coined the injunction to sow the oil, to convert the subsoil's rent into the productive wealth of the surface before the rent ran out. The phrase became the standing reproach of Venezuelan public life, repeated in every boom and mourned in every bust, and the pink tide's Venezuelan chapter heard it as insistently as any. That the sowing again did not happen, in the country where the rent was largest and the rhetoric of transformation most ambitious, is the pattern's hardest datum. It suggests that the obstacle to conversion is not the absence of the idea, which every generation of the region's intellectuals has supplied, nor the absence of revenue, which the booms supplied in flood. The obstacle is the political economy of the boom itself: the windfall relaxes every constraint that might force conversion, rewards every constituency that prefers distribution, and makes the long work of transformation feel, to each actor in turn, like somebody else's assignment for a later term. Booms do not merely fail to cause transformation. They actively anaesthetise the coalition that might have attempted it, and the pink tide, across all its national variations of virtue and competence, exhibits the anaesthesia at work.

### What distinguishes the pink tide from the classical populisms it resembles?

Its world. The earlier redistributions operated behind protective walls in an age when industrialisation by substitution was the common sense of development, and they built factories along with majorities. The pink tide operated in open economies facing East Asian manufacturing supremacy, and built majorities whose factories were, in the main, the mines and fields the world market already wanted.

## How the period should be studied: evidence and its traps

A closing methodological section is owed, because the pink tide is a case where the sources conspire against the student. The first trap is the memoir and the speech: the period's protagonists were voluble, several of its governments maintained formidable communication apparatuses, and the archive of self-description is vast, seductive, and organised to prove that intentions were outcomes. The second trap is the hostile mirror archive, the opposition and exile literatures in which every programme was a fraud and every statistic a lie; between the two archives, the student can assemble any verdict desired, which is the sign that the archives are being used as ammunition rather than as evidence. The third trap is the figure detached from its method, the poverty rate or the growth rate quoted without the survey, the basket, or the base year, in a literature where the choice of measure is itself a political act. The defences against all three traps are the ones this article has practised: prefer budgets and trade structures to speeches, since they record what was done rather than what was meant; prefer the internal critics to the external ones, since they shared the governments' premises and cannot be dismissed as enemies; state directions rather than digits where the digits are contested; and test every general claim against the variation among cases, because a truth about the wave should survive contact with both its Bolivian discipline and its Venezuelan excess.

There is a fourth trap, subtler than the others, and it is the trap of the ending. Because the wave receded, its history is easily written backwards, as a failure unfolding toward a foreknown collapse, in which every achievement was a loan and every speech a delusion. The record resists the backward reading. For a decade, the settlement worked on its own terms: incomes rose, provision widened, elections were won, and the people who lived the period experienced it, in their majority, as an improvement in their lives, a fact no analysis of its ending is licensed to confiscate. The rent model's discipline is precisely to hold the two truths in one frame: the settlement was real, and it was built on a wasting asset; the governments were neither the heroes of their own archive nor the frauds of the opposing one, but the administrators of a windfall whose terms they did not set and whose conversion they did not achieve. A student who can hold that sentence steady, against the pull of both propagandas, has learned the period's transferable skill, which is the analysis of any government, anywhere, whose prosperity arrives from outside its own productive powers.

### Why is the period's history so often written backwards from its ending?

Because endings organise archives: the fallen government's papers are read by its enemies, its programmes are audited by its successors, and the decade in which the settlement functioned leaves fewer dramatic documents than the crisis that closed it. The historian's correction is to reconstruct the expectations of the fat years, when permanence looked reasonable, before judging the men and women who shared them.

## Debt, reserves, and the creditor's quiet return

The boom years rewrote the region's relation to its creditors, and the rewriting was one of the wave's proudest claims. Argentina repudiated and restructured; Ecuador audited its debt and defaulted selectively; several governments repaid the International Monetary Fund early and announced the fact as a recovery of sovereignty; reserves accumulated across the family to levels that made the old crisis script, the empty treasury begging at the fund's window, look like a document from another age. The pink tide's financial nationalism was real, and its psychological effect on the region's politics, the sense that the external tutelage of the market decades had been thrown off, should not be understated. Beneath the emancipation, however, the structure of dependence migrated rather than vanished. Governments that would not borrow from the fund borrowed from the bond markets at the boom's generous prices, from their own central banks, and, in the emblematic arrangement of the period, from Chinese state banks against future oil deliveries, loans for oil, in which the subsoil was mortgaged forward to finance the present. The creditor changed; the collateral, the commodity stream, remained.

The downturn revealed the migration's meaning. A debt owed to bondholders cannot be renegotiated by speech; a loan collateralised by oil falls due in oil, at prices the debtor does not set; a reserve accumulated in the boom is spent in the bust at the speed of the panic it exists to calm. The governments that had banked the most and borrowed the least, the family's prudent cases, crossed the downturn as a hard passage. The governments that had converted the boom into obligations discovered that the rent had been spent twice, once when it arrived and once when it was pledged. The fiscal anatomy section described the spending; this is its balance-sheet twin, and together they state the model's financial form: a rent settlement in the fat years decides, by its borrowing and its saving, the distribution of pain in the lean ones, and the choices are made, characteristically, at the moment when they feel least necessary.

### Why did liberation from the old creditors not free the wave's finances?

Because the dependence was never primarily on a creditor but on a price. The fund, the bond market, and the oil-collateralised loan are three doors into the same room, in which the state's solvency waits on the world value of what it digs and grows, and changing doors does not change the room.

## The old powers: elites, militaries, and media under the wave

A settlement that leaves property intact must live with the owners, and the period's daily politics was the negotiation of that coexistence. The region's great conglomerates adapted to the wave with the suppleness of long practice: they supplied the governments' growth, staffed the public-private partnerships, accepted the higher royalties and taxes the boom made painless, and kept their powder, their media, and their foreign accounts for a change of weather. The armed forces, whose political weight the transitions had reduced without abolishing, were courted with budgets, missions, and, in several cases, roles in the distribution itself, administering programmes and infrastructure in ways that bound the barracks to the settlement. The great media groups, concentrated and partisan, fought the governments through the period in a war of framing that both sides experienced as existential, and governments answered with media laws, state outlets, and advertising budgets deployed as discipline. Each of these accommodations is a chapter of the rent model's political sociology: the windfall purchased not only the poor's loyalty but the old powers' neutrality, and the purchase, like all the period's purchases, ran for exactly as long as the money did.

The point should not be overstated into conspiracy. The old powers were not a committee, and they quarrelled among themselves, between exporters who prospered under the wave and importers and financiers squeezed by its exchange rates, between media owners who fought and conglomerates that collaborated. Nor were the governments passive purchasers: several used the fat years to build counterweights, public media, regulatory agencies, judicial appointments, that outlasted the boom and complicated the restoration. What the model claims is narrower and stronger than a story of deals. It claims that a transformation of property would have required defeating these powers, that redistribution required only renting their tolerance, and that the period's politics, its daily texture of negotiation, pressure, and truce, is the visible form of the rent. When the rent failed, the tolerance expired, and the old powers resumed their historical role with an ease that surprised only those who had mistaken the truce for a conversion.

## Health and education: systems, not programmes

The social sectors repay a closer look, because they show the settlement's best self and its boundary in the same institutions. In health, the period's governments expanded coverage toward universality in law and substantially in fact: primary care networks extended into districts the market decades had left to private improvisation, public hospitals built and restaffed, essential medicines programmes, and in the Brazilian case the defence and deepening of a universal public system that predated the wave and that the wave's governments funded rather than dismantled. In education, enrolments rose at every level, the secondary completion of poor cohorts improved, and the university map of several countries was redrawn, with new public institutions in the interior and the first mass generation of working-class and indigenous students. These were not transfers. They were the construction and staffing of public systems, and systems, like sewers, persist: the clinics and campuses of the boom years did not evaporate in the downturn, and the professionals they trained remained. If the article's verdict on the period has a place where it softens most, it is here, in the sectors where distribution took the form of durable public capacity.

The boundary appears in the financing and in the staffing's terms. Systems built in a boom are staffed and supplied at boom prices, and their recurrent costs, salaries, medicines, maintenance, are precisely the expenditures a rent downturn squeezes first and forgives last. The period's health systems therefore entered the lean years carrying structures their budgets could no longer feed, and the queue lengthened where the clinic stood. In education, the expansion's quantity outran its quality in ways the region's own assessments documented: buildings faster than teacher formation, enrolments faster than completion, universities faster than the research systems that make a university more than a credential. None of this cancels the construction. It locates it in the model: even where the wave built durably, it built on a recurrent-cost base indexed to the windfall, so that the durability of the walls exceeded the durability of the budgets meant to animate them. The lesson generalises beyond the period. Capital spending is the easy part of a boom; the conversion question is always whether the recurrent life of the thing built can be carried by the economy that remains when the boom ends, and it is the question the fat years are for.

### What separates the period's durable gains from its reversible ones?

Whether the gain was a stock or a flow. Buildings, trained professionals, legal entitlements, and serviced land persisted through the downturn and raised the floor beneath the poor. Income supplements, subsidies, and discretionary programmes were flows, and they thinned or vanished with the revenue, leaving the memory of a standard the economy could no longer fund.

## Succession: the settlement's personalisation problem

Every government of the family faced, at the height of its success, the question of what would follow its founder, and the answers illuminate the settlement's institutional thinness from another side. The wave's coalitions were, in several cases, built around a founding figure whose authority fused the movement, the party, and the state; the transfers and programmes were branded, in the public mind and often in their official presentation, as the founder's gift. Succession in such a structure is not a procedure but a crisis of the model's own making, because the settlement's legitimacy has been personalised at exactly the point where its durability required impersonality. Where parties with internal lives of their own managed the succession, the settlement crossed the transition damaged but standing. Where the founder's authority had consumed the party, the succession became a contest over the inheritance conducted amid the downturn, with the treasury shrinking as the claimants multiplied. The pattern is not a judgement on any individual; it is the registry problem at the level of the state. A settlement lodged in institutions survives its authors. A settlement lodged in a biography is renegotiated at every succession, and the pink tide, which had a decade to institutionalise, too often spent the decade personalising instead, because personalisation, like distribution, is what a windfall rewards in the short run.

## The enclave and the territory: extraction's economic geography

Extraction has a geography that the fiscal aggregates conceal, and recovering it completes the model's map. The mine, the well, and the plantation port form an enclave economy: capital-intensive, importing its machinery and much of its skilled labour, exporting its product unprocessed, and touching the surrounding society mainly through the treasury and through its damage. The enclave's employment is small relative to its revenue, which is the fiscal secret of the whole period: the sector that financed the state employed a fraction of its people, so the state's prosperity and the people's employment ran on different tracks, the first on the world price of copper or oil, the second on the domestic commerce and services that the spending of the rent set spinning. The celebrated employment gains of the boom were thus, in their majority, the rent at one remove, jobs in construction, retail, transport, and public service that existed because the windfall was being spent nearby. When the windfall stopped, the enclave kept producing, its few thousand workers kept working, and the employment built on the spending collapsed around it. The geography explains a paradox that puzzled outside observers of the downturn, that export volumes held while societies convulsed: the hole in the ground was never the economy, only its paymaster.

The enclave's other export was its cost, and the cost's geography is the frontier examined earlier. Between the enclave and the treasury stands the territory, the watershed, the forest, the communal land, which absorbs the water use, the tailings, the roads, and the in-migration, and which received, of the rent, the smallest share of any party to the arrangement. The fiscal flows ran from the territory to the capital and out again as transfers to the cities; the physical flows ran the other way. A map of the period drawn in money and a map drawn in damage are mirror images, and the coalition fractures occurred where the two maps were laid over one another in a single community's experience. This is why the territorial movements' slogan, in its many national variants, so often took the form of a refusal of the exchange itself, water against gold, land against rent: they were rejecting not a price but a structure, the structure in which their home was the cost column of somebody else's social policy.

### Why did booming exports coexist with fragile employment through the whole period?

Because the export sector and the employment sector were different economies joined by a treasury. The enclave generated the revenue with few hands; the spending of the revenue generated the jobs with many. Observers who watched the ports saw strength and observers who watched the neighbourhoods, after the turn, saw collapse, and both were reporting the same model at different points of its circuit.

## The young: a generation formed by the settlement

A decade is a political generation, and the pink tide raised one. The children of the transfers entered the new universities; the beneficiaries of the housing programmes formed households under roofs their parents had not owned; the young workers of the boom years knew formal contracts, rising wage floors, and expanding cities as the normal weather of economic life. Political scientists who surveyed the region's youth through the period found the settlement's deepest deposit in this cohort's expectations: the belief that the state owed its citizens a floor, that education was a right in practice and not only in text, and that a government which let the floor collapse had broken something more binding than a promise. Those expectations outlasted the fiscal basis that created them, and they are the period's most consequential bequest to whatever comes next in the region's politics, because a generation that has held a standard does not unhold it when the treasury empties. It becomes, instead, the standing plaintiff in every subsequent settlement's trial.

The same generation also inherited the model's unfinished business in its most personal form. The universities expanded faster than the economies that were to employ their graduates in kind; the credentials multiplied against a productive structure that still exported nature and imported manufactures, and the graduate underemployed in services, driving, delivering, selling, became a figure of the period's end as emblematic as the beneficiary mother had been of its beginning. The frustration of educated expectation is among the most combustible materials in politics, and the region's subsequent turbulence, across the ideological spectrum, cannot be read without it. The rent model does not predict the direction such frustration takes, and honesty requires saying so: a disappointed generation can power a renovation of the left, a turn to order, or an exit from politics altogether, and the period's bequest includes all three possibilities in solution. What the model does predict is that the frustration would arrive, because a settlement that educates a generation for an economy it does not build has scheduled the disappointment in its own budget.

## Corridors and ports: the infrastructure of the model

If a visitor from the dependency era had returned during the boom to ask what the region was building, the honest answer would have been written on the infrastructure map. The great projects of the period, financed by national treasuries, by the regional development banks, and increasingly by Chinese credit, were corridors: highway and waterway systems designed to carry the interior's commodities to the ports, interoceanic routes binding the Brazilian and Andean production zones to Pacific terminals, port expansions, dredged rivers, and the energy interconnections that served the extractive grid. A continental initiative for the integration of regional infrastructure, launched at the century's turn and absorbed into the wave's own institutions, supplied the project lists, and governments of the left administered them with the same enthusiasm as their predecessors had, because the corridors served the revenue on which everything else depended. Infrastructure is the most durable form of policy, outliving the governments that pour its concrete, and the corridors are the rent model's most literal monument: a continent re-engineered, at the height of its left turn, to export its nature faster.

The corridors also concentrated the period's conflicts in space, because a corridor is a line drawn across other people's land. The highway through the indigenous territory in Bolivia, the dams on the Amazonian tributaries, the mining roads of the Andes, were corridor projects, and their opponents understood them as such: not isolated developments but segments of a single geometry whose destination was a port and whose origin was a treasury. The movements' counter-proposal, where they developed one, was an infrastructure of a different geometry, internal markets, food sovereignty, the connection of the interior to itself rather than to the sea. The contrast deserves recording as the period's suppressed planning debate, the argument between two maps that was never held at the level of the state because the revenue had already chosen between them. A government financed by exports builds toward the ports as a plant grows toward the light, without deliberation and without malice, and the map of the boom is the proof that the choice of revenue is the choice of geography, made before any minister opens an atlas.

### Why did governments rhetorically committed to integration build so little of it between their own regions?

Because integration between regions pays in decades and integration with the world market pays this quarter. The corridor to the port raised the rent immediately; the railway between two poor provinces raised only costs within the budget's horizon, and the budget's horizon, set by the electoral cycle and the price plateau's false permanence, governed the map.

## The language of the period: sovereignty, dignity, and the people

The wave changed the region's political language, and a history that ignores the words will miss part of what the period was. Sovereignty, a term the market decades had retired as an embarrassment, returned to the centre of public speech, applied to resources, to debt, to foreign policy, and it carried the rent argument inside it: the subsoil was the nation's, its income was the people's, and the government's distribution of it was the recovery of a patrimony rather than an act of policy. Dignity did similar work at the level of the person: the transfer was not charity but a right, the pension not a favour but a restitution, and the language mattered to the recipients, who distinguished, in interview after interview recorded by the period's researchers, between the humiliation of the old clientelism and the entitlement of the new programmes. The people, finally, was the period's sovereign word, the name in which constitutions were refounded and opponents were cast as the people's enemies, a usage whose democratic energy and whose dangers grew together, since a politics conducted in the people's singular name has a standing temptation to hear disagreement as betrayal.

A materialist reading of the language neither dismisses it as decoration nor mistakes it for the structure. The words did real work: they converted a fiscal flow into a story of recovered nationhood, and the story was a political asset of the first order, the form in which the settlement was understood and defended by its beneficiaries. But the language also performed the model's characteristic concealment. Sovereignty over the subsoil is a legal fact; sovereignty over the price of what the subsoil yields is a fiction, and the rhetoric of the first continually borrowed the authority of the second, so that the dependence the model deepened was experienced, in the fat years, as its opposite. When the price fell, the language had no word for what had happened, since a sovereign people cannot be bankrupted by a market it was said to command, and the bewilderment of the period's end, the sense of a theft without a thief, is partly a linguistic event: the vocabulary of emancipation had spent a decade describing a revenue stream, and the stream's failure arrived as a metaphysical shock rather than as the ordinary behaviour of prices. The lesson for any successor politics is written there. Language that flatters a structure will be billed for the flattery when the structure turns.

## Food, land prices, and the outer limits of the model

The commodity boom cut through the region's dinner tables in both directions, and the contradiction belongs in the account. Export agriculture's prosperity raised the price of land and of the food crops that competed with export crops for it, while the same governments that celebrated the soy and beef revenues confronted food inflation as their most corrosive political enemy, since the poor spend the largest share of income on eating. Several governments answered with the instruments at hand: export taxes and quotas to hold domestic food prices below world levels, subsidised staples, price agreements with retailers, and in the Argentine case a running war with the agricultural exporters over exactly this margin, the share of the world price the domestic consumer would be allowed to keep. The war's terms reveal the model's geometry with unusual clarity. The treasury needed the export price high; the urban electorate needed the food price low; the difference had to be taken from the exporter, who was also the treasury's partner and the countryside's power. Every government of the family lived inside that triangle, and the compromises it forced, export taxes calibrated to the edge of the producers' revolt, subsidies calibrated to the edge of the budget, explain much of the period's otherwise puzzling agrarian politics.

Beyond the triangle stood a longer question that the period raised and left open: food sovereignty, the demand, carried by the peasant movements of the continent and written into two of the refounded constitutions, that a nation feed itself from its own land under its own control before it feeds the world market. As policy, sovereignty collided with the model at its root, since the export estate was the treasury's engine and the smallholder sector its poor relation in every budget. As an idea, it survived the period intact and gathered force from the frontier conflicts, and it stands in the record as another of the wave's unbuilt alternatives, the road of internal provisioning that the rent made unnecessary in the fat years and unaffordable in the lean ones. The pattern by now is familiar enough to state as the article's refrain: the windfall did not merely postpone the alternatives; it consumed the years in which they were buildable, and left them to be admired, afterwards, as the period's conscience.

## The balance sheet, in the tradition's own terms

The article's argument can now be drawn together into the balance sheet the Marxist tradition would draw, asset by asset against liability, with the rent model as the ledger's ruling. On the asset side stand the poverty reductions and the extensions of provision, real and large; the entry into the state's institutions of peoples the old republics had kept outside them, a change in the composition of political power itself; the recovery of public language about sovereignty and dignity, and of the state's presence in territories and districts it had abandoned; the construction, in health, education, and urban infrastructure, of public capacity that outlasted the financing; and the demonstration, valuable beyond the region, that the market orthodoxy of the preceding decades was a choice and not a fate. On the liability side stand the deepening of the primary export structure and the reprimarisation of economies whose escape from dependence was the tradition's oldest promise; the conversion of movements into constituencies and of beneficiaries into audiences; the territorial costs exported to the peoples least able to refuse them; the fiscal settlements built on a wasting asset and therefore reversed, in whole or in part, when the asset wasted; and the survival, intact and enriched, of the property structure the wave had been elected, in its own self-understanding, to transcend.

The tradition's verdict on such a ledger is not a sum but a classification, and the classification has been given: a reform era of the first importance, which changed the distribution of income and the composition of the state while leaving the relations of production where it found them, and which is therefore to be honoured as reform and refused the name of transformation. The refusal is not a sneer. In the tradition's own history, the reforms are what most people's lives are actually made of, and the generations that won pensions, schools, and clinics inside an unchanged structure did more for the poor than the generations that proclaimed transformation and delivered catastrophe. But the tradition also exists to keep the distinction bright, because a left that forgets the difference between distributing the surplus and reorganising its production will repeat the pink tide's biography in the next boom, with the next commodity, under the next colour. The period's final lesson is addressed to that future left, and it is the lesson the rent model was built to carry: when the windfall comes, and it will come, the question is never whether to accept it. The question is what will still be standing when it goes.

## The exchange rate: the model's transmission belt

Between the world price of a commodity and the daily life of a citizen stands the exchange rate, and the boom worked through it as through a belt. As export earnings and capital inflows flooded in, the currencies of the wave's economies strengthened, in several cases to levels their own central banks fought with reserves and controls. A strong currency is a distributional event disguised as a number. It cheapens imports, which delights consumers, travellers, and every household whose basket holds foreign goods; it cheapens foreign travel and foreign education for the middle strata; and it quietly taxes every domestic producer who competes with imports or exports anything other than the booming commodities, since their costs are in the strong currency and their prices are set abroad. Manufacturing lived inside that tax for a decade. The industrialists of the family's largest economy named the result deindustrialisation and blamed the exchange rate, and the governments, which needed the strong currency for the cheap imports that kept urban prices and tempers down, answered with credit lines and sectoral plans that treated the symptom. The Dutch disease, the old name for the mechanism in the resource literature, operated under governments that had read the literature and cited it in opposition. Reading, it turns out, is not an antidote; the belt moves whoever stands on it.

The exchange rate also governed the settlement's emotional register, which is not a small thing in politics. Cheap imports filled the shops with goods the poor had never been able to buy, appliances, electronics, clothing, and the consumption boom was experienced, correctly, as a rise in the standard of life. That the same exchange rate was hollowing the industrial employment that might have sustained the standard after the boom was a fact legible in statistics and invisible in the shops. Governments are not rewarded for explaining that the abundance and the fragility are one fact, and none explained it. When the currencies finally fell with the prices, the imports vanished from the shops in the same movement that was supposed, in the textbooks, to rescue manufacturing, and the public experienced the correction as pure loss, which in the short run it was. The belt had run in both directions through the same households, and the decade's consumption and the downturn's shock were a single mechanism photographed at two moments.

### Why did cheap imports prove politically stronger than the factories they displaced?

Imports are consumed by everyone immediately and their cheapness is felt in every basket, while a factory's closure is suffered by its workers and its town, and its survival benefits a public that never sees the counterfactual. The exchange rate thus assembled its own majority, consumers against producers, and the majority held for exactly as long as the inflow that fed it.

## The neighbours who did not turn: the control cases

The family's story sharpens against the neighbours who shared the boom without sharing the politics. Peru and Colombia, governed through the period from the centre and the right, received the same windfall in minerals and energy, grew on the same prices, reduced poverty on the same tide of revenue and remittances, and did so while explicitly repudiating the pink tide's rhetoric and much of its programme. Mexico, integrated with the northern economy and governed from the right and then the centre, had its own, weaker version of the windfall in oil, and its social policy expanded in the same transfer idiom, a conditional cash programme that became the world literature's model case, under governments no one filed with the left. The control cases discipline the argument in both directions. Against the wave's admirers, they show that poverty reduction in a commodity boom was not the left's unique achievement: the revenue reduced poverty under every flag, because the revenue, not the flag, was the active ingredient, and the neighbours' floors rose on the same tide. Against the wave's detractors, the comparison shows what the flag did add: the scale and speed of the inclusion, the constitutional and representational changes, the willingness to raise the state's share of the rent against the extractors, and the conversion of the windfall into public systems rather than into tax relief for the already comfortable. The neighbours spent the boom as income; the family, at its best, spent part of it as citizenship. The rent model holds both halves without strain, because it never claimed the left invented the windfall, only that the left's use of it had a signature, and that the signature, like the neighbours' restraint, stopped at the boundary of property.

## Order, security, and the settlement's hard edge

The period's social contract had a coercive margin, and the account is incomplete without it. The same years that expanded provision saw the region's homicide and incarceration burdens remain among the world's heaviest, concentrated in the same districts the transfers reached, and the governments of the wave, whatever their founding rhetoric about the social roots of violence, governed security largely through the inherited instruments: police forces unreformed in structure, prisons filled beyond every standard, and periodic mano dura campaigns whose popularity crossed the class lines the rest of the settlement divided. The point is not that the left invented the region's punitive politics; it is that the settlement did not transform it, and the reason belongs to the model. Security, like property, sat on the far side of the boundary the rent drew: transforming it would have required rebuilding the police and judicial institutions against their own entrenched interests, a confrontation with no rent to pay for it and no constituency organised to demand it, while the transfers and subsidies that the rent did pay for purchased a governability in which the hard edge could be left as found. The districts that received the period's generosity and its policing in the same week learned to hold both facts together, and their testimony, gathered by the period's ethnographers, is the settlement's most honest portrait: gratitude for the pension and the clinic, and no illusion that the state which delivered them had become theirs.

## The delivery machine: bureaucrats, registries, and the new statecraft

Between the treasury and the beneficiary stood an apparatus the period built almost from nothing, and its construction is one of the wave's most consequential and least sung achievements. The targeted state required knowledge the old states had never possessed: registries of the poor by household and by need, payment systems that reached villages without banks, conditionalities monitored across millions of families, and cadres of social workers, health agents, and teachers to staff the interface. Across the family, ministries of social development rose from insignificance to become the state's largest civilian presence, and a generation of technocrats learned a new statecraft, the administration of inclusion. The apparatus outlasted the wave in every country, because no successor government, of any colour, could govern the poor without it, and its persistence is the clearest case of the period depositing an institution rather than a programme.

Yet the apparatus also embodied the model's ambiguity in organisational form. A registry is a way of knowing citizens as cases, and the delivery state's knowledge ran downward, from the ministry to the household, far more fluently than the accountability ran upward. Beneficiaries were legible to the state in unprecedented detail while the state's own decisions about the registry, who entered, who was audited, who was removed, remained administrative mysteries to the people they governed. The movements that had once organised the poor as protagonists watched the poor reorganised as recipients, and the friction between the two knowledges, the movement's and the ministry's, runs through the period's internal history like a second, quieter version of the frontier conflicts. In both, the question was the same: whether the people in whose name the settlement ruled would be its authors or its objects. The delivery machine, for all its competence, answered that question by design, and the answer is the organisational form of the rent model's limit.

## Remittances: the other windfall

The rent had a private twin, and no anatomy of the period's incomes is complete without it. Across the region, and decisively in its northern tier, money sent home by emigrants working abroad rivalled or exceeded the public transfers as a support of poor households, arriving monthly, denominated in foreign currency, and immune to the domestic budget's politics. The two windfalls behaved alike in the household and differently in the state. Alike, because both were income unearned by domestic production, spent overwhelmingly on consumption, housing improvement, and schooling, and both therefore raised the floor without altering the structure beneath it. Differently, because remittances bypassed the treasury entirely: no government could tax them at the source, claim them as its gift, or lose them in a fiscal crisis, and they flowed, if anything, counter-cyclically, rising when the home economy faltered and the emigrant's sacrifice deepened. The comparison sharpens the article's central distinction one final time. A society can be lifted, for a generation, by income it does not produce, whether the income arrives as a state's rent or as a family's remittance, and the lifting is real in both cases. What neither windfall does, by itself, is build the productive capacity that would let the society lift itself, and the pink tide's countries, like its emigrants' households, face the same question when the flow thins: what was built while the money came.

## The heterodox moment: the period's economists

The wave had an economics profession of its own, and its internal argument mirrors the article's. In the finance ministries and central banks of the family sat a generation of economists formed against the market orthodoxy, several of them veterans of the region's heterodox traditions, who administered the boom with a mixture of pride and unease that their memoirs and papers preserve. Their achievement, on their own account, was to prove that the sky did not fall: that a state could raise its take of the rent, spend it downward, run the occasional deficit, and still be rewarded by the bond markets while prices held. Their unease, recorded in the period's own journals, was the conversion question in technical dress: the fear that the real exchange rate was eating the industry the future would need, that the fiscal rules were being bent by the windfall's flattery, that the development banks' lending was building corridors rather than capabilities. The profession's argument never resolved, because the boom resolved it by ending, and the post-mortems divided along the line this article has drawn throughout: those who judged the administration of the windfall, which was often skilful, and those who judged its use, which was the older and harder question. The distinction between administering a boom well and using it well is the technocratic form of the rent model, and the period's economists, in their honest moments, stated it themselves.

### What did the boom prove about the orthodox economics the wave had opposed?

That its crisis predictions were wrong and its structural warnings were not. Heterodox administration did not produce the immediate catastrophes the orthodoxy forecast, and governments that spent the windfall survived and were re-elected. But the orthodoxy's older insistence, that an economy cannot consume its way out of dependence, survived the boom intact, waiting at the end of it with the export statistics in its hand.

## Consultation and the law: the frontier's legal history

The frontier conflicts generated a jurisprudence, and the jurisprudence may outlast every other artifact of the period. The legal instruments predated the wave: the International Labour Organization's Convention 169, ratified across the region, guaranteed indigenous peoples consultation on measures affecting them, and the refounded constitutions of the period strengthened the guarantee into rights over territory and, in the boldest texts, toward consent. The conflicts forced the question the instruments had left open: whether consultation is a procedure the state performs before deciding, or a decision the peoples share. Governments of the wave, pressed by the treasury's timetable, legislated and litigated for the first reading; the movements, backed by the regional human rights system and by the period's own constitutional courts in several landmark rulings, pressed for the second. The case law that accumulated, on the TIPNIS consultation, on Amazonian oil rounds, on mining concessions granted over titled land without process, is a genuinely new body of doctrine, and it constrains every successor government regardless of colour, which is the definition of a structural change. Here, exceptionally, the period did transform a relation: not the ownership of the subsoil, which the states retained, but the legal cost of reaching it, which rose permanently. The movements that lost most of the period's battles may have won its longest war, in the courts and in the texts, and a balance sheet that omits the jurisprudence understates what the resistance deposited.

The qualification writes itself, and honesty requires it. A right that must be litigated project by project, against a state that controls the timetable and the police, is a right whose enjoyment is rationed by the movements' stamina and the courts' courage, and the period's record contains consultations performed as theatre and rulings honoured in the breach alongside the genuine precedents. Law, like the settlement itself, divides into the stock and the flow: the stock of doctrine grew impressively, the flow of its enforcement remained at the mercy of the revenue's needs. Even so, the stock is real, and it is the one major asset on the period's balance sheet that the downturn could not devalue, because it is denominated in precedent rather than in rent.

## The wave as the world left's mirror

For a decade, the pink tide served as the international left's favourite evidence and its favourite argument. European and North American movements, emerging from their own long defeats, studied the region as proof that another politics was administratively possible: that a government could raise the minimum wage, defy the creditors, rewrite a constitution, and be re-elected for it. Delegations made the pilgrimage; the period's texts were translated, belatedly, for the new audiences; and at least one European movement of the following decade designed itself consciously on the Latin American example, down to the vocabulary of the people against the caste. The mirror showed the admirers what they wished to see, and the rent model is the correction the mirror required. The conditions that made the wave governable, a commodity supercycle financing a decade of painless redistribution, were not exportable, and the movements that imported the rhetoric without the revenue discovered the difference in office or in opposition. The traffic was not one-way. The region's internal critics, the theorists of neo-extractivism and the commodities consensus, became in the same years the teachers of a worldwide argument about the left and nature, and their categories now organise debates on continents that have never seen a pink tide government. The period's most durable export, in the end, was not its model of government but its critique of that model, written by its own participants while the model was still in power. There are worse legacies for a political era than to have produced, from inside itself, the analysis by which it will be judged.

## The rent at one remove: the Caribbean and Central American edges

The wave had a penumbra beyond the family's electoral map, and it confirms the model from an unexpected direction. Through the boom years, Venezuela's oil diplomacy supplied Caribbean and Central American states with petroleum on concessional terms, financing part of the bill over long horizons, and the recipient governments, of several political colours, spent the subsidy as every recipient of rent spends it: on the visible costs of governing, fuel, transport, and social programmes hurriedly branded with the scheme's name. None of these states had a pink tide movement of its own; none underwent an ideological conversion; yet the fiscal behaviour replicated the family's in miniature, because the behaviour follows the revenue and not the rhetoric. When the supplying state's own crisis cut the flow, the penumbra's settlements contracted on the same schedule as the family's, a continent away from the politics that supposedly explained them. The episode is as close as political economy comes to a controlled experiment, and its result is the article's thesis in miniature: give a government rent, and it will redistribute and postpone; take the rent away, and the postponement ends, whatever the government believes.

## What the model instructs, the next time the prices rise

The article closes its analysis with the use to which the model is meant to be put, because commodity prices will rise again, some future left will govern somewhere on the proceeds, and the pink tide is the most complete record of what that government will face. The model's instructions, drawn from the period's successes as much as from its failures, can be stated plainly. Bank the windfall as if it were already ending, because the only fact known about a boom is that it ends. Convert while converting is cheap: the tax reform, the industrial capability, and the agrarian settlement that are impossible in the lean years are merely difficult in the fat ones. Lodge the social settlement in law and in organisations with their own resources, so that its defence does not depend on the distributor's survival. Treat the frontier's peoples as the settlement's authors rather than its cost, because the coalition that fractures at the mine will not be reassembled at the treasury. And keep the distinction between distribution and transformation bright in the movement's own language, so that the fat years are experienced as borrowed time rather than as the destination. None of these instructions is technically obscure; every one of them was stated, in the period itself, by its internal critics. Their difficulty is political, and the difficulty has a name the model supplies: in a boom, each instruction asks a government to spend its scarcest asset, popularity, on benefits that mature after the spending is forgotten. The pink tide's governments, with exceptions of degree that this article has recorded, declined the exchange. The record of their decade, its real gains and its scheduled ending, is the price list for the next refusal, set out in advance.

## Four subsoils, four politics: the commodity as a variable

The rent model treats the windfall as one thing, and at the level of the treasury it is one thing, money. At the level of politics the commodity matters, because each of the region's great exports organised a different relationship among the state, the producers, and the people who lived on top of the deposit. Petroleum is the purest rent: it is extracted by few hands, sold by the state or its company, and arrives in the budget with no domestic constituency of producers to bargain with, which is why the oil states could be at once the most generous and the most volatile members of the family, their politics a direct conversation between the palace and the price. Natural gas, in the Bolivian case, travelled by pipeline to two giant neighbours under long contracts, and its rent arrived steadier and more negotiable, one reason the treasury that lived on it could plan with a calmer hand. Copper, in Chile and Peru, was mined substantially by private and foreign capital under a stable legal frame, so the state's share came through taxation and a state company's surplus, and the politics of copper was a politics of the tax schedule rather than of the wellhead. Soy was the outlier, and the most instructive: grown by private farmers and great agro-industrial firms on private land, it generated no subsoil rent at all, and the Argentine state reached its income only through export taxes levied at the port, which meant taxing identifiable, organised, and politically armed producers, the one case in the family where the windfall had a domestic owner who could fight for it in the open. The fight came, in the agrarian conflict of 2008, when the countryside's organisations blockaded the roads against a sliding scale of export duties and the government discovered that a rent with owners is a different substance from a rent without them.

The comparison does explanatory work the aggregate model cannot. It explains why the family's most radical fiscal nationalism flowered over oil and gas, where the state's title to the subsoil was constitutionally ancient and the extractor could be cast as a foreign tenant, while its most grinding political defeats were suffered over soy, where the supposed rentier turned out to be a neighbour with a tractor, a cooperative, and a vote. It explains why the copper states argued about royalty percentages in committee while the oil states argued about sovereignty in the streets. And it refines the conversion question: the subsoil rents, arriving without a producing class attached, were in principle the easiest to bank and invest, since no domestic producer's income depended on spending them as they came, while the agricultural windfall was entangled from the first day with the incomes of the countryside and could be redirected only through a political war. That the easiest rents were, in the largest case, the least converted is the comparison's uncomfortable residue, and it points the explanation away from structure alone and back toward the choices made inside it, which is where this article has always located the final judgement.

### Why did the state capture the oil rent more easily than the soy rent?

Title and visibility. The subsoil belonged to the nation by law, its extractor was often a foreign firm, and raising the public share could be framed as recovering a patrimony. The soybean belonged to a named farmer on named land, and the export tax that reached its value was experienced, and fought, as a levy on a neighbour's harvest rather than as a royalty on geology.

## Taxation: the road not taken, examined closely

Every item in the conversion programme listed earlier has a fiscal twin, and the twin's history is the period's quietest and most damning record. A state that intends to outlive its windfall must learn to tax its own society, above all its rich, and the pink tide's decade was the moment to learn, when growth made the lesson painless and the governments' majorities made it possible. The record, with variations, is one of avoidance. Income tax schedules were adjusted at the edges; a few governments introduced or raised taxes on financial transactions, on large fortunes in modest and temporary forms, on the windfall profits of the extractors themselves; evasion was prosecuted with publicity in some capitals. But the structure, a low overall take resting disproportionately on consumption taxes paid by the poor and the informal, emerged from the decade recognisably itself in country after country. The region's fiscal pacts, the settlements between states and their wealthy citizens, were not renegotiated under governments elected to renegotiate everything else.

The reasons repay attention, because they are the rent model's reasons operating inside the finance ministry. Rent made taxation unnecessary in the short run, and taxation is never popular in any run. A finance minister choosing between a royalty renegotiation, concluded across a table with a handful of firms and celebrated as sovereignty, and a wealth tax, fought through congress, the courts, the press, and the capital markets for a yield that arrives slowly, faces an incentive gradient that no ideology fully escapes. There was also a genuine doctrine in play, and it should be recorded fairly: several of the period's governments held that taxing the middle strata and the domestic market would strangle the consumption that drove their growth model, and that the rent was, in effect, the progressive tax, levied on foreign consumers and foreign firms. The doctrine was coherent. Its flaw was temporal. A tax on foreigners' appetite lasts exactly as long as the appetite, and a state that has declined to build the domestic alternative discovers, when the appetite fades, that the machinery of taxation, the cadastres, the inspectorates, the legal precedents, the political habit, cannot be improvised in a crisis. The downturn therefore found the family's treasuries reaching, in emergency, for the instruments they had neglected in prosperity, and the reaching is among the period's most consistent scenes.


## The protest cycle of the plateau's end

The settlement's first mass challenges from below did not wait for the price collapse, and their timing is evidence in the article's argument. In Chile in 2011, secondary and university students mounted the largest demonstrations since the dictatorship's end against an education system whose market architecture the long-governing centre-left had administered rather than dismantled, demanding free public education as a right; the movement's leaders were explicit that their target was the model, not a ministry. In Brazil in June 2013, a protest against a bus fare rise in São Paulo ignited weeks of nationwide demonstrations whose demands widened from transport to the quality of public services, the cost of the stadiums rising for the football World Cup, and the distance between the state's spectacle and the citizen's clinic. In both cases the crowds were drawn heavily from the very strata the boom had benefited, students headed for the expanded universities, families riding the buses the settlement subsidised, and their grievance was not the settlement's absence but its shallowness: the income had arrived, the services had not kept pace, and the promises of the fat years were being audited in the streets before the treasuries failed.

The protest cycle matters to the rent model because it dates the coalition's thinning from inside the boom rather than from the bust. A purely fiscal account would expect contentment until the money stopped; the record shows the beneficiaries distinguishing, earlier than the analysts, between the parts of the settlement that were flows and the parts that were stocks, and protesting the flow-heavy balance while the flows still ran. The Chilean students were, in effect, demanding the conversion the model says was skipped: that the copper income be turned into a public system rather than into a subsidy for a private one. The Brazilian crowds made the same demand in the vernacular of the fare and the hospital queue. Governments read the protests as ingratitude or as the work of agitators, and the misreading was expensive, because the movements of 2011 and 2013 were the settlement's own children presenting its unpaid bills, and a politics capable of hearing them might have begun the conversion under pressure from below while the revenue to fund it still existed.

### What did the plateau protests reveal that the fiscal data had not yet shown?

That the settlement's legitimacy was thinner than its income figures, because legitimacy attaches to systems, schools, hospitals, transport, that people judge daily, while the boom's statistics measured transfers that arrive monthly. The crowds were auditing the conversion gap in person, several years before the price turn made the same audit unavoidable in the budgets.

## Spectacle and the mega-event state

Brazil's turn as host to the football World Cup of 2014 and the Olympic Games of 2016 gave the period its most visible theatre, and the theatre is worth a section because it concentrated the settlement's tensions into a single image. The governing party had won the events as certificates of arrival, proof that the country of the boom belonged among the organisers of the world's festivals, and it built for them at the boom's prices and the boom's confidence. The stadiums rose in cities whose hospitals queued out the door; the contracts flowed through the great construction conglomerates whose intimacy with the state the period's later investigations would anatomise; and the evictions and clearances around the venues wrote the frontier's logic into the city, territory cleared for a project whose benefits were announced as everyone's. When the crowds of June 2013 adopted the demand for schools and hospitals of the same standard as the stadiums, they had found the period's perfect slogan, because it measured the state against its own spectacle and found the spectacle better funded.

The mega-events also mark the exact altitude of the boom's self-confidence, and their chronology is the wave's in miniature: bid for in triumph, built in argument, and delivered, in the Olympic case, into a country already in recession and political crisis, the fireworks photographed against a darkening sky. A history of the period that confined itself to treasuries would miss what the spectacle reveals about the settlement's imagination. The governments of the wave wanted, intensely, to be seen arriving, and the desire was not vanity alone; for countries whose elites had apologised for them for a century, arrival was a political programme. But arrival purchased as spectacle is the consumption form of sovereignty, and it obeys the model's law like everything else in the period: it was financed by the windfall, it transformed nothing, and it ended on schedule.

## The party as an institution: the wave's scarcest resource

Running through the case gradings is a variable the model treats as a modifier and the record treats as a fate: the quality of the governing party as an institution. The family contained parties of genuinely different kinds. At one pole stood organisations with decades of continuous life, internal elections, affiliated unions and movements with their own leaderships, and a memory longer than any leader's career; the Uruguayan coalition and the Brazilian workers' party, for all their differences, belonged to this world, and the Chilean coalition's parties were older still. At the other pole stood vehicles assembled around a founder and a moment, whose internal life was the founder's authority and whose cadres were appointed rather than formed. The difference decided, in case after case, questions the rent could not: whether a succession could be managed as a procedure, whether a minister could be corrected by the organisation that had sent him, whether the base experienced the government as its own instrument or as a benefactor to be petitioned. A party with a life of its own could convert loyalty into organisation; a vehicle could only convert it into applause, and applause is the one currency that a downturn demonetises completely.

The parties also faced, in government, a solvent that opposition had never applied to them: the state itself as a career. A movement that wins office acquires ministries, municipalities, agencies, and contracts to distribute among its own, and its best organisers are drawn upward into administration, where their skills serve the delivery machine and their absence thins the base. The period's literature on the governing left returns to this absorption again and again, in the language of cadres lost to the apparatus. The rent intensified the solvent, because a rich state has more posts to fill and more programmes to staff, and because the party that administers a distribution acquires an interest in the distribution's continuation that can quietly replace its interest in the transformation the distribution was meant to prepare. By the period's close, several of the family's parties were better understood as federations of officeholders than as movements, and their behaviour in the downturn, defending positions rather than mobilising constituencies, followed from the change. The exception proves the rule's shape: where the party had grown out of movements that kept their own organisations and their own capacity to march against the government itself, as in the Bolivian case, the absorption was contested from inside throughout the period, painfully and to the coalition's ultimate benefit.

### Why did governing dissolve some parties and merely strain others?

Because the solvent acts on what is soluble. Organisations whose cohesion came from shared institutions, dues, internal argument, and a life outside office could lose members to the state and remain themselves. Organisations whose cohesion was a leader's authority and a treasury's favour had nothing to lose members from; the state did not absorb them, it revealed what they had always been.

## Post-neoliberalism: the period names itself

The wave's own theorists reached for a name for what they were building, and the name they settled on is a document in itself. Post-neoliberalism, in the usage Emir Sader gave it and a generation of allied writers adopted, announced a sequence: the market order had been a period, it had ended, and something else, defined by the restored primacy of politics, the state, and the social, had begun. The name had the virtues of its moment. It marked the real rupture with the preceding decades' common sense, it refused the older vocabulary of socialism that the governments mostly did not claim, and it gathered a family of unlike experiments under a banner of direction rather than of destination. Its weakness was the weakness of every negative name: it said what the period was after, and what it was against, and nothing about what it was. A politics defined as the successor of its enemy inherits the enemy's agenda as its own table of contents, and the pink tide's agenda, redistribution, inclusion, sovereignty, was in large part the market era's list of damages read backwards. The rent model can be stated as the missing positive name: what the period was, in its economic substance, was a rentier redistribution, and the failure of its own vocabulary to say so is one measure of how completely the windfall's beneficiaries, theorists included, preferred the flattering description to the structural one.

The naming question is not pedantry, because names set the test by which a period is judged. Judged as post-neoliberalism, the wave succeeded for a decade on its own terms and then was interrupted, and its history becomes a story of an experiment cut short. Judged as rentier redistribution, the same decade contains its ending inside its mechanism, and the history becomes a story of a structure completing itself. The second judgement is the sterner and the more useful, because it alone generates instructions for a successor. A movement that believes it was interrupted will wait for a fairer wind and repeat the design; a movement that understands it completed a structure will redesign before the wind returns. The argument over the period's name, conducted in the journals of the region's left throughout the downturn, is therefore not an epitaph but a fork, and this article's allegiance in it has been declared from its first page.


## Mobility: the continent on the move

The boom years moved people as well as money, and the movements belong to the period's material history. Within South America, labour migrated toward the booming economies and their construction sites, farms, and households: Paraguayans and Bolivians to Argentina, Peruvians to Chile, Colombians and later others toward the oil economy at the continent's north, in flows that the receiving states alternately welcomed and policed. The older current toward the United States and Spain continued, driven now less by desperation than by networks the earlier exodus had built, and its remittances, treated in their own section above, underwrote the consumption of whole provinces. The wave's governments, several of them founded by men who had known exile, spoke a language of the migrant's dignity and in places regularised status and eased regional residence through the common market's agreements, one of the period's quieter and more durable liberalisations. The migrant, however, occupied an ambiguous place in the settlement's imagination: celebrated as a citizen of the wider homeland, employed in practice in the informal margins of the booming cities, and absent from the registries through which the period defined its beneficiaries, since the transfer state knew its poor by household and address, and the newcomer had neither that the state recognised.

The mobility matters to the argument for a reason beyond completeness. A political economy that exports its commodities and imports its manufactures also, in the region's pattern, exports its people and imports their earnings, and the two circuits are one: the same structure that could not employ its graduates at home collected a share of their wages abroad. The pink tide moderated the human cost of that structure, regularising, receiving, and spending, and it did not alter the structure, which resumed its operation at full strength when the boom ended and the flows reversed with a speed that filled the period's closing years with new migrations and new border politics. The migrant's biography, departure in the bad years, remittance in the good ones, return or onward flight in the bad ones again, is the rent model's circuit drawn at the scale of a single life.

## The armed forces as an economic actor

No account of the region's states is complete without the institution that owns, in several of them, an economy of its own. The armed forces of the wave's countries entered the period with their prerogatives reduced by the transitions but their material bases substantially intact: military industries and banks, pension funds, land, and, in the Andean and Venezuelan cases, expanding roles in the administration of the boom itself, from infrastructure battalions to the management of distribution programmes and state enterprises. The governments of the left, whose founding generations had been the barracks' prisoners and exiles, governed this institution with a caution that the record shows in budgets honoured, promotions respected, and missions multiplied. The caution was purchased, like everything else in the period, and its price rose with the rent: an officer corps administering ports, programmes, and petroleum logistics is an officer corps with a stake in the settlement and a knowledge of its weaknesses, and the period bequeathed to its successors, in more than one country, a military more deeply woven into the economy than the one the wave inherited. The transformation question, asked of land and of banks throughout this article, applies with equal force to the barracks, and the answer has the same shape. The institution was accommodated, funded, and flattered; it was not rebuilt; and the accommodation, like the tolerance of the old economic powers, was a tenancy whose term the revenue set.

## The warning from Honduras: the coup inside the period

The wave's confidence that the age of coups had ended was broken inside the period itself, and the break belongs in any honest anatomy. In Honduras in 2009, a president elected from the traditional Liberal Party who had moved toward the wave's orbit, raising the minimum wage, joining the Venezuelan-led alliance, and proposing a consultation on a constituent assembly, was seized by soldiers, flown out of the country in his nightclothes, and replaced by an interim regime that the hemisphere's governments refused to recognise and could not dislodge. The episode's lessons were absorbed by every chancellery in the family. The old powers, military, oligarchic, and judicial, retained the capacity for the classic move where the settlement was shallowest and the rent thinnest; international condemnation, however unanimous, had a price ceiling; and the electoral road's safety depended, in the last instance, on the same calculations of force the wave had hoped to retire. Honduras was the family's smallest and poorest theatre, which is precisely why it functioned as a demonstration: what was done there at low cost was noted everywhere as available, at higher cost, elsewhere.

The Honduran case also disciplines the article's own model, and the discipline should be acknowledged. The rent explains the settlement's economics; it does not explain why one country's old order reached for the barracks while another's reached for the courts or the ballot, and the variation runs through histories, of militaries, of party systems, of the northern neighbour's attention, that no revenue table captures. A model that knows its own boundary is stronger than one that denies having one. The pink tide's governments drew from Honduras the conclusion that survival required the management of the armed forces and the courts as a first-order task, and several of the period's subsequent accommodations, criticised in their time as excessive caution or worse, were priced by men and women who had watched a president expelled at dawn for proposing to ask his people a question.

### What did the Honduran coup demonstrate that the wave's theory had discounted?

That the constitutional road's guarantees were conventions resting on the old powers' calculation of costs, not on the parchment's authority. Where the settlement had neither rent enough to purchase tolerance nor organisation enough to raise the price of rupture, the convention could be cancelled in a morning, and the hemisphere's opinion, however united, would not by itself restore what the calculation had removed.

## The informal economy: the settlement's foundation and its limit

Beneath every statistic of the period stands the informal economy, the street markets, the unregistered workshops, the domestic service, the day labour, in which roughly half the region's workers earned their lives at the wave's opening and a still-vast share earned them at its close. The settlement addressed this population as its principal beneficiary and its principal puzzle. As beneficiaries, informal workers and their families received the transfers, the clinics, the schools, and the pensions of old age, and their incomes rose with the general tide of consumption the spending generated. As a puzzle, they stood outside the classical instruments of both the old left and the old state: no employer to bargain with, no payroll to tax, no union card to count, their working lives governed by the price of a pitch, the weather, the police, and the bus fare. The period's social policy, to its credit, invented forms that reached them, the registry, the conditional transfer, the microcredit, the simplified tax regime for the smallest firms, and several governments coaxed measurable shares of the informal mass into formality while the boom made formality worth having.

The limit appeared when the boom ended, because informality is the economy's shock absorber and its trap at once. Workers expelled from formal jobs in the downturn were absorbed by the street and the stall, which prevented destitution and registered as resilience in the surveys, while locking a generation back into the low-productivity, unprotected work the settlement had promised to retire. A transformation of production relations, had one been attempted, would have had to be measured precisely here, in the share of the population working with capital, protection, and a future, and by that measure the period's advance was real, partial, and reversible, the three adjectives that the rent model predicts for every achievement the windfall financed. The informal vendor who received the transfer, sent her children to the new school, and returned to the same corner when the prices turned is the period's most complete citizen, and any verdict that cannot hold her whole story, the gain and the limit in one life, has not understood the thing it judges.

## Climate and the commodity model: the outer account

The period's ledger has a final column that its participants were only beginning to read. The commodity model the wave deepened was, in its physical substance, a carbon model: hydrocarbons burned abroad, forests cleared for the soy and cattle frontiers, minerals torn out with water and energy, and the region's own territories exposed, as the frontier advanced, to the droughts, floods, and shifting seasons of a warming continent. The governments of the family spoke the language of climate justice in the world's forums with real authority, since their countries' historical responsibility was small and their vulnerability large, and two of the refounded constitutions wrote the rights of nature into law. At home, the fiscal machine ran on the expansion of the very activities the speeches indicted, and the contradiction was not hypocrisy in the simple sense: it was the rent model's arithmetic asserting itself over the period's own best texts, as it had asserted itself over the consultation rights and the agrarian promises. The atmosphere, like the territory, received the costs that the treasury's beneficiaries did not, and the accounting was deferred to a ledger no election audits.

For the tradition this article writes within, the climate column converts the extractivism critique from a regional argument into the period's most general lesson. A left that finances emancipation by accelerating the drawdown of nature has not escaped the old economy's logic; it has socialised the proceeds of the logic for a decade, and the decade's end, fiscal and climatic alike, arrives on a schedule set elsewhere. The pink tide did not create that dilemma, which confronts every government of every colour on a finite planet, and its defenders are entitled to note that the region's contribution to the accumulated stock of emissions is a fraction of the great industrial powers'. What the period supplies is the dilemma's clearest modern demonstration, run at continental scale with the best intentions available: the windfall that could have financed the exit financed the deepening instead, because the deepening was what paid. Any future politics that proposes to do otherwise will have to begin where this article ends, with the revenue, and will have to answer, in its first budget rather than its last speech, the question the pink tide spent a decade not answering.

## The battle for the story: media, communication, and the settlement

No government of the family governed without a war over its own description, and the war's intensity is itself a datum about the period. The region's dominant media were concentrated in a small number of family and corporate groups, editorially hostile to the wave in most countries, and possessed of the agenda-setting power that concentrated ownership confers. The governments answered on three levels. They built or expanded public media, state broadcasters and news agencies intended to contest the private groups' framing. They legislated, in several countries, communications laws that redistributed frequencies, limited concentration, and recognised community media, measures whose democratic content and whose convenience to the incumbents were argued then and are argued still. And they fought, in speeches, in advertising budgets, and in the courts, a running battle in which each side described the other as the enemy of free expression itself. The period's press freedom indices and its governments' counter-indices survive as rival archives of the same war.

The rent model reads the media war as a surface disturbance of a deeper settlement. A government that leaves the commanding property of the economy untouched leaves, among that property, the commanding heights of communication, and then experiences the consequence as a permanent campaign against its legitimacy conducted from assets it chose not to expropriate. The choice was consistent with the whole design: confrontation with media property would have been a confrontation with property as such, the very thing the financing existed to avoid. What the governments built instead, public media dependent on the incumbent's budget, reproduced the model's ambiguity in the register of information: a counterweight that existed at the distributor's pleasure, credible exactly to the extent that it could criticise its funder, and therefore structurally tempted to the loyalty that destroyed its credibility. The community media sector, licensed and multiplied in several countries, was the partial exception, an archipelago of small voices with their own roots, and its survival beyond the period, where it survived, followed the article's familiar law: what had its own organisation outlasted what had only a budget.

### Why did the media war burn hotter under governments that had left media property untouched?

Because the settlement's restraint was read by the owners as a truce rather than a settlement, and truces are tested. The governments, having declined the expropriation that would have ended the war, had to fight it daily with the weapons of the state, law, advertising, and the bully pulpit, and a war fought daily by other means looks, to each side, like proof of the other's bad faith.

## The settlement at street level: mayors, municipalities, and the local state

The pink tide is usually written from the presidential palace, and the view from the municipality corrects it in ways the model welcomes. The settlement was delivered, where it touched daily life, by local states: the municipality that paved and lit the settlement's streets, the local clinic and school, the mayor who became, for most citizens, the face of the era. Several of the family's parties had been formed in municipal government during the market decades, governing great cities as showcases of an alternative, participatory budgeting in the Brazilian case having taught a generation of the world's urban left its methods before the national victories came. In power nationally, the parties discovered the municipality as both instrument and trap. As instrument, the local state was the delivery machine's last mile, and its performance decided the settlement's reputation in the only court that sits daily. As trap, the fiscal structure of the region left municipalities dependent on transfers from the centre, so that the local state experienced the boom as a grant and the downturn as an abandonment, and mayors of the governing parties found themselves administering the cuts their own national governments had promised the model made unnecessary.

The local record also preserves the period's most interesting institutional experiments in embryo. Participatory budgeting, water and waste cooperatives, community health councils, and the communal organisations that one case in the family promoted as a parallel structure of popular power all attempted, at street scale, the thing the national settlement declined: the conversion of beneficiaries into governors of the resources they received. Their fortunes varied and their scale was small against the treasuries above them, but they matter to the balance sheet because they demonstrate that the alternative organisational form was not a theorist's fantasy. It existed, inside the period, built by the same movements, and it was starved of exactly the resource the centre had in flood. The history of the pink tide written from its municipalities would be a history of that starvation and of what survived it, and it would reach this article's conclusions by the shortest road of all: follow the money downward, and watch where it was allowed to become power and where it was required to remain a gift.

## The savings question: funds, reserves, and the roads not banked

Between spending the windfall and wasting it stood a third possibility that several governments in the family genuinely attempted, and the attempt's uneven record is the conversion debate in its most concrete form. Chile entered the period with the machinery already built: a structural balance rule and sovereign funds, created in the copper sector's legislation, which banked the boom's extraordinary copper income abroad and released it by formula, so that the Chilean treasury crossed the price collapse of the following decade with a cushion no other case possessed in the same legal form. The machinery predated the wave and was administered, to their credit, by the wave's Chilean governments rather than dismantled, a fact the grading section's portrait of the moderated case rests on. Bolivia, without the inherited machinery, improvised an equivalent discipline: the central bank's reserves were accumulated to levels that astonished the country's own history, the currency held steady, and the government preached its prudence as a refutation of the spendthrift stereotype, with justice. At the other pole, Venezuela's off-budget development fund received oil income on a scale that dwarfed the formal budget's social ministries and spent it under discretionary authority with minimal published accounting, the purest instance in the family of the windfall consumed as it arrived and of the opacity that discretionary rent invites. Ecuador and Argentina occupied the middle ground, saving less than their rhetoric suggested and borrowing against the future in the forms the debt section described.

The comparison carries the article's fairest lesson, because it separates the model's necessity from the governments' choices with unusual clarity. The rent did not dictate the savings rate; politics did, and the politics tracked the strength of the institutions and movements that could punish a government for consuming the future. Where a fiscal rule existed with legal force and a technocracy to defend it, the windfall was partly banked. Where a movement government had built its legitimacy on prudence as a national virtue, reserves accumulated as a point of honour. Where legitimacy rested on the founder's generosity, the fund became the founder's purse. The downturn then collected the differences with interest, in the literal sense: the banked cases bought time, adjustment, and in one case a continuity of the settlement's core, while the consumed cases met the wall. A reader who wants the rent model's limits stated in one exhibit should take this one. The mechanism set the menu; it did not place the order.

### What distinguished the governments that banked the windfall from those that consumed it?

The presence of a constituency for the future with institutional teeth: a fiscal rule a minister could hide behind, a movement that counted reserves as honour, a public that had been taught to read the fund's balance as its own savings. Where the only constituency was for the present distribution, the present won every vote, and the fund, if it existed, was a pocket rather than a vault.

## Culture in the boom years: the fiesta and its discontents

The period had a cultural weather, and no social history of it is complete without a glance at the arts the windfall floated. State cultural funding expanded across the family: film institutes and production funds that underwrote a renaissance of the region's cinema, book programmes and libraries, restored theatres, national orchestras for the young on the Venezuelan model that had predated the wave and was now lavishly extended, and a calendar of bicentennials, summits, and festivals at which the new states presented themselves to themselves. The cultural boom was real in its outputs, films that travelled the world's festivals, a generation of musicians trained in the youth orchestra system, publishing that reached readers the market had never served, and it was continuous with the settlement in its structure: financed from the same revenue, distributed as a good whose receipt required no transformation of its recipient, and vulnerable, when the revenue failed, to the same withdrawal, as cultural budgets are always the first cut and the last restored. Artists and intellectuals divided as the society divided, between gratitude for the patronage and unease at its terms, and the period's cultural polemics, conducted in every capital, rehearsed the whole argument of this article in the register of the studio and the stage: whether a renaissance funded by rent is a foundation or a decoration, and what remains standing when the patron's income falls. What remained, in the event, was what the model predicts: the trained people and the built institutions persisted, the flows that had animated them thinned, and the argument outlasted both.

## The countryside that did not export

Behind the export estate and the extractive frontier stood a third countryside, the one that fed the region's cities from small and medium farms, and its experience of the period complicates every simple version of the story, including, in places, this article's. The boom's urban demand, fattened by transfers and rising wages, was a market for the smallholder as well as a threat: families that had survived the market decades by retreating into subsistence found buyers for their produce at prices the import competition of the earlier era had destroyed, and several governments supported the sector with credit, extension, and procurement programmes that bought the small farm's output for the school meals and food reserves of the state itself. In the family's most ambitious case, a food sovereignty law and a ministry for the peasant economy attempted to build the internal provisioning circuit that the export model had bypassed for a century. The attempts were real, and their scale was the period's scale in miniature: always the smaller line in the budget, always the second priority of the ministry, always the first casualty when the treasury tightened, because the export sector paid the state while the food sector asked to be paid by it. The asymmetry is the rent model's deepest root system. A state financed from the frontier will govern for the frontier, whatever its manifestos, and the countryside that feeds the nation will be honoured in its speeches and rationed in its budgets, until a financing exists that reverses the direction of dependence. The peasant movements understood this with a clarity the capitals lacked, which is why their demands, across the whole period and every national variation, converged on the same point: not a larger share of the rent, but a different economy, in which the land that feeds the people is the foundation and the hole in the ground is the supplement. The demand outlived the wave, as the movements did, and it stands in the record as the alternative the fat years were not used to build.

## The regional market that was not built

The integration section above recorded the diplomacy; the economics deserves its own paragraph of regret. At the boom's height, the region possessed, simultaneously, the revenue to finance integration, governments rhetorically committed to it, and a world market whose prices made every national treasury feel self-sufficient. The third condition devoured the other two. Integration is a discipline for the poor: countries pool markets and build shared infrastructure when none can prosper alone, and they endure the negotiations because the alternative is insignificance. A continent of treasuries swollen with rent felt no such compulsion. Each capital could finance its own ports, negotiate its own contracts with the new Asian buyers, and treat the regional project as a summit photograph rather than as an economy, and the intra-regional trade that did grow rode the general boom rather than any deepening of production shared across borders. Energy told the story in its purest form: pipelines and interconnections were announced across the map, a great gas pipeline of the south was projected with ceremony, and the wires and tubes that actually arrived were a fraction of the drawings, because a gas exporter with full coffers prefers a bilateral price to a regional system. When the prices fell, the integration that might have cushioned the fall, internal demand, shared reserves, a regional lender of weight, existed as letterheads. The pink tide's historians will record this as the conversion failure at continental scale, the same choice made by the same mechanism in a larger room: the windfall was consumed nationally because the coalitions were national, and the region, which the period's rhetoric invoked in every speech, remained a geography rather than becoming an economy.

## Altars and ballots: religion inside the period

The wave governed societies in religious motion, and the motion interacted with the settlement in ways the fiscal analysis alone would miss. The Catholic Church entered the period as the continent's oldest institution of the poor, its parishes and base communities woven into the same neighbourhoods the transfers reached, and its hierarchy divided, as it had been for a generation, between a pastorate formed by the preferential option for the poor and an episcopate appointed for its distance from that tradition. The governments of the family contained Catholics of both formations, courted the Church's blessing for their social programmes, fought it over the moral legislation of abortion, marriage, and education, and in the Andean cases wrote constitutions whose invocations of indigenous spirituality sat beside the Church's prerogatives in an negotiated pluralism. Simultaneously, and largely beneath the notice of the period's political commentary, evangelical and Pentecostal churches grew at a pace that was remaking the religious map from below, congregations of the poor built without any state's patronage, funded by the tithes of the informal economy, and preaching a gospel of personal discipline and prosperity that spoke directly to the stallholder's and the driver's experience of risk. The pink tide's settlement and the Pentecostal congregation were, in an exact sense, competitors: two institutions offering the poor a floor, one fiscal and one spiritual, one delivered by the registry and the other by the congregation, and the second required no treasury and feared no downturn. Political scientists who mapped the period's end noted the evangelical constituency's availability to new political offers, including offers from the settlement's enemies, and the observation belongs to this article's organisational theme. The movements and churches that the poor built for themselves endured on their own resources; the settlement that the state built for them endured on the rent. In the competition for the loyalty of the same streets, the institution with the independent revenue base was not the state.

The religious field also supplied the period with one of its few genuinely new political languages. The theology of the option for the poor, which the series treats in its own article on liberation theology, had formed a generation of the family's cadres in the base communities and the Christian left parties, and its vocabulary of dignity, of the poor as subjects of history, passed into the constitutions and the speeches of the wave even where its church had retreated from it. That a doctrine formed in seminaries and slum chapels should supply the moral grammar of a fiscal era is a reminder that the rent model, for all its explanatory power, describes the period's engine and not its soul, and that the people who lived the settlement understood it in languages, of faith, of dignity, of the recovered nation, that the treasury's arithmetic cannot translate. The historian's task is to hold both, the engine and the soul, without letting either confiscate the other, which is, in the end, only the article's founding discipline restated at the level of belief.

## The statistical wars: a short guide to the period's contested numbers

A reader who goes from this article to the wider literature will meet, within pages, numbers brandished like weapons, and a short guide to their manufacture is a genuine service. The period's poverty figures descend from household surveys whose questionnaires, income definitions, and poverty lines differ by country and were revised, in several countries, mid-period, so that a series can break at exactly the point where a government claims its triumph. Inflation, the period's most politically charged statistic, was in one major case the object of an open war between the state statistical institute and private estimators, a war whose existence is a fact and whose figures this article, by protocol, does not reproduce. Growth figures were revised by international agencies in both directions after the fact. Electoral results, the hardest numbers in principle, were contested in the family's most polarised case to the point where rival counts circulated as rival realities. None of this means the period is unknowable. It means its magnitudes live inside institutions, and the institutions were parties to the conflict. The directions, by contrast, are established by the convergence of hostile and friendly instruments alike: no serious source, of any alignment, disputes that poverty fell in the boom, that revenues rose and then fell, that export concentration deepened, or that the fiscal settlements cracked when prices turned. This article has built on the directions and declined the digits, and it recommends the same discipline to every reader of the period, not as a counsel of despair about measurement but as the only honest response to a literature in which the choice of a base year can be an act of war. The mechanisms, fortunately, do not need the digits. Rent rose, was distributed, and fell, under every plausible revision of every series, and a history that can be overturned by a rebasing was never a history of mechanisms at all.

## Envoi: reading any boom after this one

The pink tide's final use is as a training set for the reader's eye. Booms financed from outside production recur in every region and every century, in oil states and mining states, in the capitals that receive a financial flood and the provinces that receive a tourist one, and they share a physiology that this period displays in its purest modern form. The revenue arrives without a domestic bargain. The government that receives it becomes generous, then indispensable, then convinced of its permanence. The opposition is disarmed by prosperity and rearmed by the first bad quarter. The constituencies organise themselves around the distribution and neglect to organise around anything else. The structures that would outlast the boom, the tax base, the industry, the institution with its own resources, are postponed in the name of the emergency of spending, and the emergency lasts exactly as long as the money. A reader who has worked through the Latin American case with the rent model in hand owns a diagnostic that transfers: ask first how the prosperity is financed, and the rest of the politics, the rhetoric, the coalition, the ending, will arrange itself around the answer. That is the article's bequest, and it is worth more than its verdict on any single government, because governments end and the physiology does not.

The discipline cuts against sympathy as readily as against hostility, and that is its credential. It honoured the period's achievements by explaining the revenue that made them possible, and it honoured the period's critics by conceding the constraint they could not budget around. What it refused, from the first page to this one, was the consolation of intentions: the belief that a government is what it says, that a boom is what it promises, or that a wave is weather. The pink tide was none of those things. It was a mechanism, run by serious people under real constraints, and it is remembered most usefully by readers who can name the mechanism and still see the people.

## The view from the north: the hemisphere's hegemon and the FTAA's grave

The wave's external history has a founding scene, and it belongs in the record before the envoi. At the Summit of the Americas held at Mar del Plata in Argentina in 2005, the host government and its allies refused to place the Free Trade Area of the Americas, the hemisphere-wide free trade project that the United States had promoted for a decade, on the summit's declaration, and the project, already ailing, was buried in public by the presidents of the region's largest economies while the northern president looked on. The scene condensed the period's external meaning. The market era's central institutional project for the hemisphere died at a table of elected governments of the left and centre, in the country whose collapse four years earlier had announced the era's end, and it died not by denunciation but by agenda, the quietest and most final of diplomatic deaths. What replaced it was not a counter-system but a pluralisation: the regional bodies described earlier, the bilateral courtship of Asian buyers, and a generation of diplomacy conducted in the language of sovereignty the period had restored. The northern power, absorbed in its wars in the Middle East through the wave's first decade, acquiesced in the dilution of its predominance with a pragmatism that its own later assessments recorded as neglect. The rent model adds its usual footnote to the diplomatic history: the defiance was affordable because the treasuries were full, the alternative markets were bidding, and the cost of the northern displeasure, in credit and in access, had fallen toward zero while prices held. Sovereignty, like everything else in the period, was partly a revenue effect, and the diplomats who exercised it most boldly were those whose finance ministries could show them the reserves. The scene at Mar del Plata therefore belongs in this article not as a flourish but as a measurement: the altitude of a region's independence, taken at the moment of its greatest height, in the full knowledge of what the altitude was standing on.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The rent model teaches the distinction between redistribution and transformation more clearly than any abstract treatment, because the period supplies the distinction with a decade of evidence and a built-in ending. A productive seminar sequence begins with the two-lefts schema, lets students file the governments by temperament, and then confronts the filing with the revenue data's directions, so that the class discovers the materialist objection in its own work. The rent redistribution table serves as the second session's worksheet: each group takes one case, grades it against the rows, and defends the grade. The third session belongs to the internal critics, Gudynas, Svampa, and Acosta in translation where available, set against the defence's strongest form, with the class required to state the concession each side owes the other before any verdict is allowed. Assessment should reward the separation of questions: students who can hold the achievements and the limits in one frame, without letting either confiscate the other, have learned the transferable skill the topic exists to teach.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier on this period runs through the internal critique's archive, much of it in Spanish and Portuguese, and the first professional task is linguistic: the Anglophone debate's dependence on translated fragments distorts its proportions, overweighting the governments' friends and enemies abroad and underweighting the fiscal and territorial argument conducted at home. Budgets, royalty schedules, export structures, and the jurisprudence of consultation are the underexploited sources, and each supports work that the memoir literature cannot. Historians should note the period's documentary asymmetry, the abundance of its self-description against the thinness of its internal administrative record, and price their claims accordingly. Journalists covering any successor politics in the region will find the rent model a working instrument rather than a retrospect: ask of any government, in any year, how it is financed, what the financing leaves untouched, and what is being built that will stand when the price turns. The pink tide is the worked example that teaches those questions, and its archive, handled with the protocols this article has used, remains open.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the Pink Tide?

Journalists coined the name, in Spanish the marea rosa, for the run of elections won by parties of the left and centre-left across the continent between the end of the nineteen nineties and the middle of the twenty tens. The governments were a family rather than a project: a refoundational movement in Venezuela, a workers' party in Brazil, movement governments in the Andes, cautious coalitions in the Southern Cone. What united them was a shared inheritance of grievance from the market reform decades, a shared electorate, and a shared windfall, the commodity price boom that filled their treasuries. They widened provision and widened political membership, they kept the commanding property relations of their economies in place, and they lost their fiscal footing together when world prices reversed.

### Q: Were there two different lefts?

The distinction comes from a widely discussed article of 2006 by the Mexican writer Jorge Castañeda, which separated a modern, social-democratic, market-compatible left from a nationalist, statist, redistributive one. The schema captures real differences of rhetoric, diplomacy, and macroeconomic temperament. Its critics answer on three grounds: the supposedly irresponsible camp included fiscally cautious governments, the supposedly responsible camp redistributed modestly, the difference tracks each country's revenue from commodities more closely than its doctrine, and the partition flattens the variety inside each camp into a morality tale. Used as a sketch of tendencies the map helps; used as an explanation of the period it misleads, because the financing explains the variation better than the temperament does.

### Q: What financed the Pink Tide governments?

A sustained rise in the world prices of primary commodities, running from roughly 2003 to roughly 2013, which filled state treasuries with resource rent. The money arrived through royalties and reformed hydrocarbon and mining laws, through export taxes on agricultural shipments, through the surpluses of state companies, and through renegotiated concessions in which private extractors conceded a larger public share of prices far above their planning assumptions. Because the revenue came from foreign buyers of oil, gas, copper, soy, and iron ore rather than from taxing domestic wealth, the governments could spend on a large scale without first defeating the propertied classes at home, and that single fact shaped everything they did and declined to do.

### Q: What did the Pink Tide governments achieve?

The household surveys assembled by the United Nations regional commission and by national statistical offices agree on the direction, whatever their disputes about magnitude: hardship retreated across the continent during the boom decade, and its severest forms retreated fastest. Cash transfers conditioned on schooling and health visits put an income floor under tens of millions of families; pensions detached from contribution records reached old people whom the formal labour market had never covered; wage floors were raised year after year in most of the family's countries; and clinics, hospitals, schools, and universities were built or expanded in districts the state had previously visited mainly as a tax collector or a policeman. Peoples written out of the old republics entered their institutions as deputies, ministers, and constitution-makers. Every item on that list was paid for, directly or at one remove, by the commodity windfall, which is why the list's survival was hostage to the windfall's.

### Q: What is neo-extractivism?

Eduardo Gudynas, the Uruguayan scholar who systematised the term, used it for the form extractivism took under governments of the left. The older pattern had shipped the surplus abroad to companies and creditors; the newer pattern kept a far larger share at home, in the treasury, and spent it where voters could see it, on transfers, pensions, and services. Svampa in Argentina and Acosta in Ecuador developed the charge in parallel: the digging itself, the widening frontier of mines, wells, and plantations, and the subordination of every other economic ambition to the hole in the ground, continued unchanged beneath the kindlier budget. Extraction defended as the price of the pension and the clinic is harder to oppose than extraction defended as modernisation, and that defensive strength, the critics argued, was precisely the problem.

### Q: Why did the commodity boom matter so much?

Because duration converted luck into structure. A short price spike would have padded a budget or two; a plateau lasting roughly a decade, from 2003 toward 2013, underwrote multi-year programmes, justified new ministries and registries, and taught electorates to treat expanded provision as the ordinary duty of a state. Incumbents across the family won re-election or installed successors in conditions that would have destroyed the region's governments in earlier decades, and the reason sat in the treasury rather than in any party's charisma. The boom also chose the politics available: money arriving from foreign consumers spared the governments any early reckoning with domestic wealth, so the redistributive era could be generous and unthreatening at once. Its ending set the wave's ending, which is the final proof of how much it mattered.

### Q: Did these governments transform production relations?

In the main, no, and the precision of the answer matters. The governments transformed the distribution of income, the composition and self-description of several states, the legal position of indigenous peoples, and the stock of public infrastructure and services. They did not transform the ownership of land, of the financial system, of industry, or of the extractive apparatus, and the export structures of their economies grew more concentrated in primary products during their tenure rather than less. The Venezuelan case pushed furthest against property and remains the family's partial exception. The distinction between what changed and what did not is the rent model's central observation: redistribution financed by rent can proceed for a decade without touching the relations that generate the income being redistributed.

### Q: Why did indigenous movements break with these governments?

Because the redistribution they supported was financed from their territories. The expanded extractive frontier, the mines, oil blocks, dams, and highways that generated the rent, ran across indigenous land and water, and the governments, whose treasuries depended on the frontier's advance, treated consultation as a procedure to complete rather than a decision to share. In Bolivia the highway planned through the Isiboro Sécure territory and the repression of the march against it in 2011 broke the alliance between the movement government and the lowland peoples; in Ecuador the indigenous confederation moved from ally to opponent over mining, water, and oil. The movements' charge was structural, not personal: inclusion in the state had not included a veto over the subsoil, and when the two claims collided, the treasury's claim prevailed.

### Q: What is the Marxist criticism of the Pink Tide?

In the tradition's judgement the wave's error was categorical: it treated a revenue windfall as if spending it downward amounted to changing how the society produced. The income of dependence was shared more justly while the dependence grew deeper; the ministries of distribution flourished while unions, cooperatives, and peasant organisations, the bodies that might have carried a deeper change, were absorbed into administrations, starved of autonomy, or fought at the frontier; and when prices turned, the estates, the banks, and the export houses stood exactly where the wave had found them, ready to resume command of a field the governments were vacating. The classification that follows is reformism under dependent conditions, granted its real conquests and denied the title of transformation. The same tradition, at its most honest, adds the concession recorded in this article: no critic produced the alternative budget, at equivalent scale, that the first years allowed.

### Q: Why did the wave recede?

Start with the treasury. When world prices reversed in the middle of the twenty tens, the income that had paid for everything fell away while the commitments entered in the fat years, transfers, subsidies, payrolls, debt service, stayed on the books, and each government was pushed toward the same narrowing set of choices among borrowing, inflation, and cuts. Prudence divided the endings: treasuries that had saved crossed as hard passages, treasuries that had spent and pledged the windfall faced emergencies of the state. Politics then amplified economics. Coalitions had already thinned, at the frontier and among the urban middle strata; oppositions had learned to bid for the settlement's beneficiaries instead of against the settlement; scandals supplied sparks. Voters punished insolvency and disorder, not ideas, and the proof is that the programmes outlived the administrations in public esteem long after the money for them had gone.

### Q: How does the rent model grade a pink tide government's record?

By four separate questions, kept apart because the period's polemics fuse them. How was the government financed, and how deep was its dependence on rent? How much of the windfall did it convert, into diversified production, a domestic tax base, durable public systems, and organised constituencies, against how much it distributed as current consumption? What did it leave untouched, in land, finance, and the export structure? And how did its ending compare with the endings the model predicts for its level of exposure and prudence? Graded this way, Bolivia scores highest on prudence and organisation, Venezuela stands as the limiting case of dependence without conversion, and the moderated Chilean case shows the same logic operating at the lowest temperature.

### Q: What should a student separate between redistributing rent and transforming production relations?

Four things. The object: redistribution moves income between households through the budget, while transformation moves ownership and control of the means of production between classes. The politics: redistribution from rent can proceed without defeating the propertied, while transformation cannot, which is why the first was chosen everywhere and the second almost nowhere. The timescale: a transfer can be delivered in a month and revoked in a budget, while transformed relations, once built into law, organisation, and a different export structure, survive the government that built them. And the evidence: redistribution is measured in poverty and provision, transformation in landholding, industrial composition, and the tax base. An answer that grades a government on the first set while claiming the second has committed the period's characteristic error.

### Q: Why did the wave recede when commodity conditions turned?

Rent is the most cyclical revenue a state can live on, because it is geared to prices quoted in markets abroad rather than to the taxable activity of the home economy, and it can halve while factories still run and shops still open. The pink tide's budgets had been built upward to the plateau: subsidy bills, transfer rolls, and public payrolls all assumed the plateau's income. Once the income dropped, each month of delay widened a gap that only debt, reserves, the printing press, or retrenchment could close, and every one of those remedies withdrew support from a different limb of the governing coalition. Nor did the coalition reassemble for the emergency. The territorial movements had been fought too bitterly, and too late in the day, to return as rescuers, and the beneficiaries, enrolled as names on a payment registry, had no organisation of their own through which to defend what they received. A settlement financed as a gift was withdrawn as an accounting decision, and the wave went out with the price that had floated it in.

### Q: How does the rent redistribution table order the cases?

The table's five rows, source of revenue, form of redistribution, what was left unchanged, the constituency created, and the constituency alienated, supply a grid on which each case takes a position. Venezuela fills the revenue row at its maximum and the unchanged row with its sharpest exception; Bolivia fills the constituency rows most strongly on both sides, gaining the deepest loyalty and the most consequential alienation; Brazil shows redistribution operating through a general boom rather than a single rent stream; Chile shows every row at reduced intensity inside inherited fiscal rules. A case is understood, in this scheme, not by its label but by its distances from the rows, and the comparison of distances is the model's working method.

### Q: What separates a closed historical period from a judgement on a present government?

A closed period has stated dates, identified actors holding offices that have ended, and outcomes that can be assessed against the alternatives available at the time. A judgement on a present government is a political act directed at actors who still hold power and can still answer, bargain, and retaliate. The two genres obey different ethics: history owes the dead accuracy about their constraints, while commentary owes the living candour about their choices, and confusing them corrupts both. This article therefore treats the wave strictly within its dates, names no serving official, evaluates no administration outside the period, and leaves the reader to draw any parallel unassisted, which is where parallels belong.
