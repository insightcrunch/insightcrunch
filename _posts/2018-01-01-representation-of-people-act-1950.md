The Representation of the People Act, 1950 decides a question that sounds administrative and is in fact constitutional in its consequences: whose name stands on the electoral roll of a constituency, and whose does not. Every other part of Indian election law, the nominations, the campaign, the polling, the counting and the disputes, operates on a population of voters that this Act has already defined. A citizen who satisfies every condition the Constitution sets for the franchise and whose name is missing from the roll on polling day has, for that election, no vote at all. A name wrongly present on the roll carries a vote that the law never granted. The Act is therefore best read not as a register-keeping statute but as the working definition of the Indian electorate, written in the form of conditions, officers and procedures.

![The Representation of the People Act 1950 and the electoral roll, showing how registration conditions decide who may vote - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-61.webp)

The One Test for this statute can be stated in a sentence. The right to vote in India is a statutory entitlement conditional on registration: a person may vote only if entitled to be registered, actually registered, and still registered when the election comes. Whether a person is entitled turns on four things, citizenship, age on a qualifying date, ordinary residence in the constituency, and the absence of a disqualification for registration. Whether the entitlement becomes a vote turns on the work of a single officer, the Electoral Registration Officer, who prepares and revises the roll, corrects entries, includes omitted names and deletes entries that should not stand. Each of those acts can be challenged, and the appeal the Act provides against a wrongful deletion or refusal is, in practical terms, the most consequential remedy in election law and the one least known to the people it protects. This article works through the whole design: the allocation of seats with which the Act opens, the delimitation provisions, the election machinery, the registration conditions in their full statutory detail, the meaning of ordinarily resident, the special provisions for service voters and for citizens living abroad, the Aadhaar provision of 2021 and the protection written into it, the revision machinery and the controversy that intensive revision attracts, and the judicial position on what kind of right the vote is.

## The constitutional design the Act completes

The Act was among the first statutes of the Republic, and it exists because the Constitution deliberately left the composition of the electorate to legislation. Part XV of the Constitution, Articles 324 to 329, builds the frame. Article 324 vests the superintendence, direction and control of the preparation of electoral rolls and the conduct of elections in the Election Commission. Article 325 forbids separate electorates and requires one general electoral roll for every territorial constituency, with no person ineligible for inclusion on grounds only of religion, race, caste or sex. Article 326 states the franchise itself: elections to the House of the People and to the Legislative Assembly of every State are held on the basis of adult suffrage, and every citizen of India who is not less than eighteen years of age on the qualifying date and is not otherwise disqualified under the Constitution or under any law made by the appropriate legislature, on the ground of non-residence, unsoundness of mind, crime or corrupt or illegal practice, is entitled to be registered as a voter. Article 327 empowers Parliament to legislate on all matters relating to elections to Parliament and the state legislatures, including the preparation of electoral rolls and the delimitation of constituencies. Article 329 then closes the courts out of the process except through an election petition after the election, a bar whose interaction with the roll is examined later in this article.

Two features of that frame matter for everything in the Act. The first is that Article 326 grants an entitlement to be registered, not an unmediated right to vote. The entitlement is expressly subject to disqualification under law, and the Constitution itself names the permissible grounds, which include non-residence and unsoundness of mind. The second is that the qualifying date is built into the constitutional text: age is measured not on polling day but on a date the registration law fixes. The Act of 1950 is the instrument that converts those constitutional choices into a working register. It fixes the qualifying dates, states the conditions of registration, defines ordinary residence, creates the officers, and prescribes how the roll is made, revised, corrected and appealed against.

The scale of the original undertaking deserves a pause. The first general election of 1951 to 1952 required a roll of a population that had never voted in a national election on universal franchise, prepared under a statute enacted in May 1950 by the provisional Parliament. Adult suffrage itself was the gamble of the founding settlement: the Constitution adopted it at a stroke for a largely illiterate electorate, rejecting the property, tax and education qualifications that had limited the franchise under the Government of India Act, 1935 to a small fraction of adults. The 1950 Act was the administrative price of that gamble. A universal franchise is only as real as the register that records it, and the founders knew that the register would be built constituency by constituency, name by name, by officers most citizens would never meet. The statute they wrote for that task has been amended many times, most significantly for the purposes of this article in 2010 and in 2021, but its architecture, allocation first, machinery second, rolls third, remains the architecture of 1950.

## Two Acts, one number: the 1950 Act and the 1951 Act

### What separates the Representation of the People Act 1950 from the Act of 1951?

The 1950 Act governs everything before an election is called: allocation of seats, delimitation of constituencies, the officers who prepare the rolls, and the conditions of registration as a voter. The 1951 Act governs the election itself: nominations, polling, counting, corrupt practices and election petitions. Registration questions belong to the Act of 1950; candidate questions to the Act of 1951.

The division is clean in design and persistently confused in citation, because of an accident of numbering that this article draws expressly, as every careful writer on Indian election law must. The Representation of the People Act, 1950 is Act 43 of 1950. The Representation of the People Act, 1951 is Act 43 of 1951. Both statutes carry the number 43, each in its own year. A reference to Act 43 without the year is therefore ambiguous between the two most cited election statutes in the country, and the error is not harmless, because the two Acts govern different subjects: a lawyer who cites the registration conditions of the 1950 Act as provisions of the 1951 Act, or the corrupt practices of the 1951 Act as provisions of the 1950 Act, has misdirected the reader entirely. The discipline this article follows, and the discipline the series follows, is to give the year with the number every time the number is used.

Within its own field the 1950 Act is organised in five parts and a set of schedules. Part I contains the short title and definitions. Part II, sections 3 to 13, deals with the allocation of seats in the House of the People, the Council of States and the state legislatures, and with the delimitation of constituencies. Part IIA, sections 13A to 13CC, creates the officers connected with the preparation of rolls, the Chief Electoral Officer, the District Election Officer, the Electoral Registration Officer and the Assistant Electoral Registration Officer. Part IIB contains section 13D, which provides the electoral roll for Parliamentary constituencies. Part III, sections 14 to 25A, is the heart of the Act: electoral rolls for Assembly constituencies, the disqualifications and conditions for registration, the meaning of ordinarily resident, the overseas elector provision, and the machinery for preparation, revision, correction, inclusion and appeal. Part IV, section 27, covers electoral rolls for Council constituencies, and Part IVA the manner of filling seats in the Council of States allotted to Union territories. Part V, sections 28 to 32, contains the general provisions: the rule-making power, the availability of local authority staff, the bar on civil court jurisdiction, and the offences of making false declarations and breach of official duty. The First Schedule records the allocation of seats in the House of the People among the states and Union territories, the Second the total number of seats in the Legislative Assemblies, the Third the allocation of seats in the Legislative Councils, and the Fourth the local authorities for the purposes of elections to the Legislative Councils. The later schedules of the original Act stand repealed.

The long title repays reading, because it is the Act's own table of contents. The Act provides for the allocation of seats in, and the delimitation of constituencies for the purpose of election to, the House of the People and the Legislatures of States, the qualifications of voters at such elections, the preparation of electoral rolls, the manner of filling seats in the Council of States to be filled by representatives of Union territories, and matters connected with those subjects. Everything the statute does sits inside those five purposes, and nothing in it touches the conduct of the poll itself. That boundary explains the statute's durability. The conduct of elections has been transformed since 1950 by technology, by the growth of the electorate and by repeated amendment of the 1951 Act. The register has been transformed far less, because the underlying administrative problem, identifying who is entitled and recording the entitlement in the right constituency, has not changed its shape.

## The allocation of seats

The Act opens with arithmetic, because representation begins with the question of how many seats each state sends to each House. Section 3 states the allocation of seats in the House of the People among the states and Union territories, in the terms recorded in the First Schedule, subject to the constitutional ceilings in Article 81. Section 4 deals with the filling of seats in the Council of States, whose members are elected by the elected members of the state Legislative Assemblies in accordance with the system of proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote, as Article 80 provides. Section 7 performs the corresponding function for the state legislatures, fixing the total number of seats in each Legislative Assembly as recorded in the Second Schedule and the seats in each Legislative Council as recorded in the Third Schedule. Sections 5 and 6 state the qualifications for membership of the House of the People and of the Council of States respectively, which is the point at which the Act touches candidates, briefly and only to state the qualification threshold, before returning to its true subject.

Allocation is not a frozen founding fact. The Constitution, in Articles 82 and 170, originally contemplated readjustment of the allocation and of constituency boundaries after each census, so that the weight of a vote would remain broadly equal as populations shifted. The political difficulty of that design became plain within a generation: states that succeeded in slowing population growth stood to lose seats to states that did not, and a readjustment on raw population figures would have punished demographic success in the allocation of political power. Parliament answered with the Constitution (Forty-second Amendment) Act, 1976, which froze the allocation of seats and the division of states into constituencies on the basis of the 1971 census until the first census taken after the year 2000. The Constitution (Eighty-fourth Amendment) Act, 2001 extended the freeze until the first census taken after the year 2026, while permitting, and the Constitution (Eighty-seventh Amendment) Act, 2003 then enabled, a readjustment and rationalisation of constituency boundaries within each state on the basis of the 2001 census, without altering the number of seats allotted to any state. The schedules to the 1950 Act therefore record a distribution whose state totals descend from 1971 population figures, applied through constituency boundaries that have been redrawn within states on 2001 figures. The constituency map through which the freeze operates is drawn under the delimitation provisions examined in the next section, and the dedicated treatment of the delimitation statutes is the companion article on [delimitation Acts in India explained](/2018/04/01/delimitation-acts-india-explained/), which owns the detailed history of the four Delimitation Commissions.

The allocation provisions matter to the registration story in one practical way. Because the number of seats per state is frozen while populations move, the size of constituencies has diverged sharply across the country, and the roll for a single Parliamentary constituency in a high-growth urban area may carry several times the electors of a constituency elsewhere. The Registration Officer in each place performs the same statutory task on radically different scales, and the quality of the roll, its accuracy at the margin where names are added and deleted, is where that scale difference is felt by individual voters.

## Delimitation of constituencies

Sections 8 to 11 of the Act contain its delimitation code. Section 8 provides for the delimitation of Parliamentary and Assembly constituencies by order of the Election Commission, published in the Gazette, dividing each state into territorial constituencies on the principles the Constitution and the applicable Delimitation Act supply. Section 9 gives the Commission the powers it needs for the work, including the power of a civil court for specified purposes and the association of members of Parliament and of the state legislature as associate members without a vote on the final proposals. Section 10 addresses the allocation of seats in the Legislative Assemblies and the procedure for it, and section 11 fixes the date from which a delimitation order takes effect, providing that an order does not affect representation in an existing House until its dissolution, so that a boundary change never unseats a sitting member mid-term.

Two characteristics of this code explain its quiet importance. The first is finality. A delimitation order published under the Act has the force of law, and Article 329(a) of the Constitution bars any court from calling in question a law relating to the delimitation of constituencies made or purporting to be made under that article. The Supreme Court gave that bar full effect in Meghraj Kothari v Delimitation Commission, holding that a delimitation order could not be challenged on the ground that the statutory procedure had not been followed, since the order, once published, stood as law. The consequence is that the map is settled by the delimitation process itself and not by litigation, which places the entire weight of fairness on the consultation the process conducts before publication. The second characteristic is that the Commission under the 1950 Act acts alongside, and at times in the shadow of, the Delimitation Commissions constituted under separate Delimitation Acts for the great post-census exercises. The readjustment conducted on the 2001 census within the freeze was performed by the Delimitation Commission constituted under the Delimitation Act, 2002, chaired by Justice Kuldip Singh, a retired judge of the Supreme Court, and its orders recast almost every constituency boundary in the country while leaving state seat totals untouched. The 1950 Act's sections supply the standing machinery; the Delimitation Acts supply the periodic commissions. A reader who keeps that division in mind will not mistake the one for the other.

## The officers who make the roll

### Which officers prepare and revise the electoral roll?

The Chief Electoral Officer of each state supervises roll work across the state. Below that officer, the District Election Officer coordinates the district, and the Electoral Registration Officer appointed for each constituency prepares, revises, corrects and maintains its roll, assisted by Assistant Electoral Registration Officers. Every operative decision on a name is the Registration Officer's decision.

Part IIA builds this hierarchy in four short sections. Section 13A provides for the Chief Electoral Officer for each state, designated or nominated by the Election Commission in consultation with the state government, subject to the Commission's general supervision and control, and charged with supervising the preparation, revision and correction of all electoral rolls in the state under the superintendence of the Commission. Section 13AA provides for District Election Officers, one or more for each district, designated by the Commission in consultation with the state government, who coordinate and supervise the work within the district, subject to the Chief Electoral Officer's direction. Section 13B is the operative provision: for every constituency, the Election Commission, in consultation with the government of the state in which the constituency falls, designates or nominates an officer of government or of a local authority as the Electoral Registration Officer. Section 13C permits the appointment of Assistant Electoral Registration Officers to assist the Registration Officer, who may perform any of the Officer's functions subject to the Officer's control. Section 13CC then deems the staff employed on roll work to be on deputation to the Election Commission for the period of that employment, subject to the Commission's control and discipline, a provision that loosens the state government's administrative grip over officers while they do election work and mirrors the deputation design of Article 324(6) of the Constitution.

Beneath this statutory hierarchy sits a field layer the Act does not name and practice cannot do without: the Booth Level Officer, drawn usually from teachers, revenue officials and other local staff, who knows the households of a polling station area, distributes and collects forms, verifies entries at the door and reports deaths and shifts of residence. The Booth Level Officer has no statutory power to add or delete a name, and that distinction is worth fixing firmly. Verification reports may inform a decision, but the decision to include, correct or delete an entry is the Registration Officer's alone, taken under sections 22 and 23 after the inquiry the Act and the Registration of Electors Rules, 1960 prescribe. When deletions go wrong at scale, the legal failure, if there is one, is located in the Officer's order, not in the field report that preceded it, and it is the Officer's order that the appeal under section 24 attacks.

The machinery design carries a structural tension that runs through this entire article. The officers are state government servants working under the Commission's superintendence, on a register whose accuracy determines the effective franchise of every resident. Their incentives are administrative: complete the revision on schedule, process the claims, publish the roll. The individual's incentive is singular: to be on the roll, correctly, in the constituency of ordinary residence. Most of the time those incentives coincide. The cases that reach courts, commissions of inquiry and newspaper front pages are the cases where they diverge, where completing the exercise on schedule and deciding each name correctly come apart. The safeguards the Act builds, notice, hearing, appeal, exist for exactly that divergence.

## The roll for every constituency

Section 15 is the foundation section of Part III: for every constituency there shall be an electoral roll prepared in accordance with the provisions of the Act under the superintendence, direction and control of the Election Commission. Section 14 supplies the definitions Part III works with, of which two carry the system. The first is the qualifying date, defined in section 14(b) in relation to the preparation or revision of a roll, a definition whose content was transformed by the amendment of 2021, as the next section explains. The second is the constituency framework itself, since the roll is constituency-wise: there is no single national register of voters in India, and no entry exists except as an entry on the roll of a particular constituency. Section 13D completes the arrangement for Parliamentary constituencies: the electoral roll for a Parliamentary constituency consists of the rolls of the Assembly constituencies comprised within it, so the Assembly constituency roll is the single building block of the whole system, maintained once and used for elections to both the Assembly and the House of the People. That is why one registration serves every election a voter will ever face in that constituency, and why one wrongful deletion excludes the voter from all of them.

## The conditions of registration

Section 19 states the conditions of entitlement in terms that track Article 326. Every person who is not less than eighteen years of age on the qualifying date, and is ordinarily resident in a constituency, shall be entitled to be registered in the electoral roll for that constituency, unless disqualified under the Act. Three elements do the work, and each repays separate attention.

Age is measured against the qualifying date, not against the election. Until the amendment of 2021, section 14(b) recognised a single qualifying date, the first day of January of the year in which the roll is prepared or revised. A person who turned eighteen on the second of January waited, in strict law, a full year for the next annual revision anchored to the following January, and in practice often longer, since inclusion between revisions depended on the continuous updating machinery. The Election Laws (Amendment) Act, 2021, Act 49 of 2021, which received the assent of the President on 29 December 2021 and was brought into force on 1 August 2022, substituted four qualifying dates: the first day of January, the first day of April, the first day of July and the first day of October in a calendar year. The practical meaning is that entitlement ripens four times a year, and a person attaining eighteen may be registered with reference to whichever of the four dates next follows the birthday. The reform is modest in text and large in effect for the youngest cohort of voters, the cohort most likely to be studying or working away from home and least likely to chase a registration that the calendar has postponed by a year.

Ordinary residence is the second element, and it is important enough to carry its own section of this article below. Citizenship is the third threshold fact, though section 19 does not state it in terms: it enters through section 16, which disqualifies a person who is not a citizen of India from registration altogether. The registration conditions are therefore four in substance, citizenship, eighteen years on a qualifying date, ordinary residence in the constituency, and freedom from the disqualifications in section 16, and the Act distributes them across two sections in a way that summary accounts frequently mangle. A person may satisfy section 19 completely and still be barred by section 16; a person clear of section 16 may still fail section 19 by living in the wrong constituency or by being seventeen on every qualifying date that the revision recognises.

Registration is also, it must be stressed, an act that has to be applied for and effected. India has no automatic enrolment: turning eighteen adds nobody to any roll, and residence in a constituency adds nobody either. The entitlement in section 19 is a right to be registered on application and proof, processed through the forms and inquiries that Part III and the Registration of Electors Rules, 1960 establish, and summarised in the findable artifact later in this article. The distance between entitlement and entry, between satisfying section 19 and standing on the roll, is the space in which this statute does its real work and in which its failures occur.

## The disqualifications for registration

Section 16 states who shall not be entitled to registration in the electoral roll for any constituency. A person is disqualified if the person is not a citizen of India; if the person is of unsound mind and stands so declared by a competent court; or if the person is for the time being disqualified from voting under the provisions of any law relating to corrupt practices and other offences in connection with elections. Each limb is narrow, and the narrowness is deliberate.

The citizenship limb is absolute and admits no exception for long residence, property, taxation or family connection. A foreign citizen who has lived in a constituency for decades is not registrable, and the overseas elector provision considered below exists precisely because it addresses citizens, not residents generally. The unsoundness limb requires a declaration by a competent court; unsoundness of mind as a social fact, a family opinion or a medical certificate does not disqualify, because the disqualification attaches to the judicial declaration, not to the condition. The third limb imports disqualifications imposed under election law itself, principally the disqualification for corrupt practices imposed under the 1951 Act upon a finding recorded in an election petition or by the President on the Election Commission's opinion, which runs for the period that law specifies. A person under that disqualification loses registration, not merely the seat or the candidature.

Two bars complete the registration code, and both are about the integrity of the register rather than the character of the voter. Section 17 provides that no person shall be entitled to be registered in the electoral roll for more than one constituency. Section 18 provides that no person shall be entitled to be registered in the electoral roll for a constituency more than once. The two sections are the statutory root of every de-duplication exercise the Commission runs: a second registration is not a surplus copy of a lawful entry but an entry the Act forbids, and when it is found, the machinery of section 22 is used to strike off the entry that should not stand, ordinarily the earlier entry where the person has genuinely moved and registered afresh, with the person entitled to be heard before deletion where the deletion is adverse. Sections 17 and 18 also frame the offence end of the system, since a declaration that conceals an existing registration is a false declaration within section 31, examined with the general provisions below.

## Ordinarily resident: the meaning that decides where India votes

Section 20 gives the expression ordinarily resident the meaning on which the constituency of registration depends, and it is drafted largely in negatives and deeming provisions, because the draftsman knew the expression would be litigated at the margins of Indian mobility: the student in a hostel, the worker on a two year contract in another state, the soldier, the diplomat, the family that owns a house in the village and rents a flat in the city.

The section begins by removing the easiest mistake. Section 20(1) provides that a person shall not be deemed to be ordinarily resident in a constituency on the ground only that the person owns, or is in possession of, a dwelling house in it. Ownership is not residence. A house held as an investment, an ancestral property visited on festivals, a flat bought for a child's future, none of these makes the owner an elector of that constituency. Section 20(1A) then supplies the counterweight for absence: a person who is absenting from the place of ordinary residence, temporarily, shall not by reason only of that absence cease to be ordinarily resident there. Residence under the Act is a matter of settled presence with the character of a home, judged on the facts of how a life is actually lived, and the case law on the expression, of which the Gauhati High Court's discussion in the Manmohan Singh case is the most cited, insists on a degree of permanence of intention rather than continuous physical presence. A person working in another city for a season remains ordinarily resident at home; a person who has shifted the centre of life, family, belongings and intention to the new place has become ordinarily resident there, and sections 17 and 19 then combine to require the registration to move too.

The deeming provisions handle the cases where the state itself has displaced the person. Section 20(3) deems specified classes to be ordinarily resident in the constituency in which they would have been ordinarily resident but for their service: persons holding an office declared by the President, in consultation with the Election Commission, to be an office to which the provision applies; members of the armed forces of the Union; members of an armed police force of a state serving outside that state; and persons employed under the Government of India in a post outside India. These are the persons the election law calls service voters when the roll records them in that capacity, and the roll for their home constituency carries their names with the facility, under the 1951 Act, of voting by postal ballot or by proxy rather than in person. The spouse provision completes the family picture: where a person within the service classes is deemed ordinarily resident in a constituency, the spouse of that person, if ordinarily resident in India, is likewise deemed ordinarily resident there if the spouse so chooses in the manner the rules prescribe. The Election Laws (Amendment) Act, 2021 made that provision gender neutral, substituting the word spouse for the word wife in section 20 of this Act and in the corresponding provision of the 1951 Act, so that the husband of a woman in the armed forces or on a posting outside India stands in the same deemed position that the wife of a serviceman always occupied. The change is one word long and it corrected an assumption, written into the statute book in 1950, about whose service displaces a household.

For the far larger class of internal migrants who hold no service qualification, the Act offers no deeming provision at all, and the ordinarily resident test applies in its raw factual form. A worker who has lived for years in a city constituency, returns to the village for festivals and intends at some point to retire there presents the test in its hardest form, and Registration Officers decide such cases on evidence of where life is centred: where the family lives, where the ration and banking and schooling arrangements sit, how long the presence has lasted and whether it carries the intention of remaining. The political sensitivity of these decisions at scale, when a revision exercise asks lakhs of such voters to document their position, is the subject of the revision sections below. The legal position itself is stable and can be stated plainly: ordinary residence is neither birthplace, nor property, nor the address on any single document; it is the place a person lives as home, and temporary absence does not break it while a genuine relocation does.

## The overseas elector: registration without a remote vote

### Why was the voting age reduced from twenty-one to eighteen?

The Constitution (Sixty-first Amendment) Act, 1988 reduced the age in Article 326 from twenty-one years to eighteen years, bringing the franchise into line with the age of majority. The change took effect on 28 March 1989. Section 19 of the 1950 Act works with the constitutional figure, measured on a qualifying date.

The citizen living outside India stood, for sixty years, in a statutory trap. Section 16 required citizenship, which the citizen abroad retained; section 19 required ordinary residence in a constituency, which absence on employment or education abroad was liable to defeat, and the practice under the old law treated a citizen absent from India for an extended period as no longer ordinarily resident, so names were removed or never entered. The Representation of the People (Amendment) Act, 2010, Act 36 of 2010, cut through the trap by inserting section 20A, which came into force on 10 February 2011. Section 20A provides that every citizen of India whose name is not included in the electoral roll, who has not acquired the citizenship of any other country, and who is absenting from the place of ordinary residence in India owing to employment, education or otherwise outside India, whether temporarily or not, shall be entitled to have the name registered in the electoral roll of the constituency in which the place of residence in India as mentioned in the passport is located. The passport address became the statutory anchor: the overseas elector is registered where the passport says home is, regardless of how long the absence has lasted, and the application route is the dedicated form under the Registration of Electors Rules, 1960.

The limitation is the one that disappoints, and the brief for this article is right to insist on it, because a very large number of registered overseas electors discover it late. Registration under section 20A confers no remote vote. Section 20A(3) allows the registered overseas elector to vote at an election in the constituency if otherwise eligible, and the manner of voting is the ordinary manner: in person, at the polling station for the part of the roll in which the name stands, on production of the original passport. No postal ballot, no proxy and no embassy voting attaches to the overseas elector's registration. The named figures tell the story of the resulting gap between registration and participation: registrations by overseas electors rose from a little under twelve thousand at the 2014 general election to a little under one lakh at the 2019 general election, while the votes actually cast by that class in 2019 were a fraction of the registrations, since each vote required a journey to the home constituency. The figures are stated here as orders of magnitude from the Commission's published statistical reports, and the pattern, not the precise count, is the point: a right that can be exercised only by intercontinental travel is exercised rarely.

Parliament has considered closing the gap once. The Representation of the People (Amendment) Bill, 2017 proposed to amend the 1951 Act so that an overseas elector could vote by proxy, appointing a person to cast the vote in the constituency, broadly on the lines long available to service voters. The Lok Sabha passed the Bill in August 2018. It was not taken up in the Rajya Sabha, and it lapsed on the dissolution of the Sixteenth Lok Sabha in 2019. The Election Commission separately proposed, in 2020, that the electronically transmitted postal ballot system already used for service voters be extended to overseas electors, a change achievable substantially through the election rules rather than a fresh statute; the proposal was placed before the Law Ministry and was not acted upon. The durable legal position is therefore the one section 20A states: the citizen abroad may register at the passport address, and must be physically present in the constituency to vote. Any account that says overseas electors can vote from abroad misstates the law, and any account that says they cannot register understates it.

## The Aadhaar provision and its protection

The same amending Act of 2021 that multiplied the qualifying dates inserted the Aadhaar provisions into section 23, as sub-sections (4), (5) and (6), with effect from 1 August 2022. Their exact terms matter more than any summary of them, because the public argument about the provision is conducted largely about a version of it that Parliament did not enact.

Section 23(4) empowers the Electoral Registration Officer, for the purpose of establishing the identity of a person who applies for inclusion of a name in the roll, to require that person to furnish the Aadhaar number, the unique identification number issued under the Aadhaar statute whose own architecture is treated in the article on [the Aadhaar Act 2016 explained](/2019/05/01/aadhaar-act-2016-explained/). The officer may likewise require a person whose name already stands on the roll to furnish or intimate the Aadhaar number for the authentication of entries and for identifying a registration of the same person in more than one constituency or more than once in the same constituency. Section 23(5) provides that every person whose name is included in the roll may intimate the Aadhaar number to the prescribed authority in the prescribed form and manner, on or before a date notified by the Central Government in the Official Gazette; the date notified for the exercise was 1 April 2023, and the form prescribed for the intimation by an existing elector is Form 6B under the Registration of Electors Rules, 1960. Form 6, the application for first registration, carries a field for the Aadhaar number in the same design.

Section 23(6) is the protection, and it is written in prohibitory terms. No application for inclusion of a name in the electoral roll shall be denied, and no entry in the electoral roll shall be deleted, for inability of an individual to furnish or intimate the Aadhaar number due to such sufficient cause as may be prescribed; and the proviso adds that such an individual may be allowed to furnish other alternate documents as may be prescribed. The linkage the statute creates is therefore, on its face, a power to require and an invitation to furnish, fenced by an express bar on the two consequences a voter fears: refusal at the door and deletion from the roll. The furnishing of Aadhaar is not a condition of registration in the way citizenship, age and ordinary residence are conditions under sections 16 and 19, and an officer who treated it as one would act against the text.

The controversy lives in the gap between that text and administrative practice. The challenge to the amendment, brought before the Supreme Court by petitioners including the Congress leader Jairam Ramesh, argued that a power to require, exercised through forms and field staff across a billion electors, would operate as compulsion in fact whatever the text said, and that the linkage exposed the roll to the privacy defects identified in the Aadhaar litigation itself. The Union's answer, given in Parliament by the Law Minister during the passage of the Bill and repeated in the proceedings, rested on the text: the protection in section 23(6) is statutory, the alternate documents are prescribed, and no deletion may rest on the absence of Aadhaar. Both positions are honestly stateable, and the honest summary is the one the brief for this article directs: the concern raised is about practice rather than about the text. A reader testing any individual case should therefore ask two separate questions. What does the officer demand, as a matter of the form and the field instruction? And what does section 23(6) permit to happen if the elector cannot furnish the number? The first question is administrative and varies; the second is legal and does not.

## Preparation and revision of the roll

Section 21 governs how the roll is prepared and kept current. The roll for each constituency is prepared in accordance with the Act and revised in the prescribed manner, and the section obliges revision at the points where the register would otherwise drift from reality: the roll must be revised before a general election to the House of the People or the Legislative Assembly, and before a bye-election to fill a casual vacancy, unless the Commission directs otherwise, and it may be revised in any year in the manner the Commission directs. In the intervals, the roll is maintained by continuous updating under sections 22 and 23, as deaths, shifts, corrections and new attainments of eighteen are reported and processed through the year.

Revision practice has settled into recognised forms. A summary revision is the routine annual exercise: the existing roll is published in draft, claims and objections are invited over a fixed window, house level verification is conducted at a level of intensity the programme prescribes, the claims are decided, and the final roll is published, all anchored to a qualifying date. An intensive revision rebuilds the roll from the ground up, with enumerators visiting households and preparing the register afresh rather than amending the old one, the method used for the first roll and periodically since. Between the two stands the power in section 21(3): the Election Commission may at any time, for reasons to be recorded, direct a special revision of the electoral roll for any constituency or part of a constituency, in such manner as it thinks fit. The special intensive revision is the exercise that power produces when the Commission directs that entries be verified afresh against documentation rather than carried forward on the strength of the existing roll. Section 21(3) is short, and nearly everything contested about intensive revision is an argument about what those few words authorise and what fairness requires of their exercise.

One discipline governs all revision outcomes, and it is statutory, not merely administrative. Before an entry is deleted, the person concerned must be given a reasonable opportunity of being heard in respect of the action proposed, after proper verification of facts in the prescribed manner; that safeguard, expressed in section 22 for corrections and deletions directed by the Registration Officer and embedded in the rules governing claims and objections during a revision, is the legal spine of every deletion exercise. The Supreme Court applied the underlying principle with force in Lal Babu Hussein v Electoral Registration Officer, a case arising out of deletions in a Bombay revision in which names had been marked for removal on the basis of police reports doubting citizenship. The Court held that electors already on the roll could not be struck off without an inquiry in which the individual was told the case against the entry and heard on it, that a roll entry carries a presumption of regularity earned by the inquiry that put it there, and that citizenship, where it is the ground, must be determined on evidence through a proper inquiry and not on suspicion generated at scale. The judgment predates the intensive revision controversies of later decades and remains the controlling statement of what a deletion must look like when it is challenged.

## Correction, inclusion and the freeze at the election

Between revisions, two sections do the daily work of the register. Section 22 empowers the Electoral Registration Officer, on application or on the officer's own motion after inquiry, to correct an entry that is erroneous or defective, to transpose an entry within the constituency where the elector has moved residence inside it, and to delete an entry where the person has died, has ceased to be ordinarily resident in the constituency, or is otherwise not entitled to stand on the roll, in every adverse case subject to the hearing safeguard described above. Section 23 empowers the officer, on application by a person whose name has been omitted or who has become entitled, to direct that the name be included, after verification, where satisfied that the applicant is entitled to be registered; and where the applicant is registered in another constituency, the officer informs that constituency's officer, who strikes the name off the earlier roll, which is how the single registration rule of sections 17 and 18 is enforced in practice when a voter moves and registers at the new address.

Section 23(3) imposes the freeze that every candidate's agent knows and many voters discover too late. No amendment, transposition or deletion of an entry shall be made under section 22, and no direction for inclusion shall be given under section 23, after the last date for making nominations for an election in the constituency and before the completion of that election. The freeze is the Act's answer to a structural danger: a roll that could change between nomination and polling could be changed strategically, and a roll that changed after the election was called would deny parties and candidates a fixed electorate to address. Its price is paid by the individual who turns eighteen, moves house or discovers an omission in the window between the last date for nominations and polling day, and whose remedy is postponed until the election completes. The freeze is constituency specific and election specific; it binds the constituency in which the election is being held, and it lifts when that election is complete.

## The appeal against the Registration Officer

### What must happen before a name can lawfully be deleted from the roll?

Three things, in sequence. The Registration Officer must verify the facts in the prescribed manner rather than act on a bare report; the elector must be told of the proposed deletion and given a reasonable opportunity of being heard on it; and the deletion must be made by the Officer's own order, the order the section 24 appeal can attack.

A deletion made without the hearing is unlawful however accurate the underlying information turns out to be.

Section 24, inserted by the amending Act of 1961 and given a second tier by the amending Act of 2009 with effect from 1 February 2010, provides the appeal. An appeal lies, within the time and in the manner prescribed, to the District Magistrate or Additional District Magistrate or Executive Magistrate or District Collector or an officer of equivalent rank, from any order of the Electoral Registration Officer under section 22 or section 23; and a further appeal lies to the Chief Electoral Officer from the order of the District Magistrate or Additional District Magistrate in the first appeal. The Registration of Electors Rules, 1960 prescribe the procedure, and for the cognate decisions on claims and objections in a revision the rules require the appeal to be presented within fifteen days from the date of the announcement of the decision, a short fuse that reflects the tempo of a revision programme in which the final roll cannot wait on leisurely litigation. The appeal is a creature of the register, decided by senior election officers on the record of the inquiry, and its outcomes are given effect by correcting the roll where the appeal succeeds.

The appeal deserves the emphasis this article gives it, because it corrects the precise injury the rest of election law cannot reach. An election petition under the 1951 Act, the route Article 329 reserves for election disputes and the subject of the companion treatment of [election petitions and disqualification in India](/2018/03/15/election-petitions-disqualification-india/), attacks an election result; it is not a vehicle for restoring one wrongly deleted name, and no court will entertain a challenge to the roll itself while an election process is on foot, a position the Supreme Court settled in N.P. Ponnuswami v Returning Officer in the first year of the Republic and has maintained since. The citizen whose name has been deleted wrongly therefore has, in practice, one remedy designed for the injury: the section 24 appeal, taken promptly, on the record of the officer's own inquiry, supplemented where the appeal window has closed by an application for inclusion under section 23 and, where a public law wrong of substance is alleged, by the writ jurisdiction of the High Court outside the protected window of an ongoing election. The general position described in this article is legal information about how that machinery is built; it is not advice on any individual case, and the limitation periods and appeal windows in this field are short enough that an individual facing a deletion should act on advice taken in time rather than on a general account.

## Intensive revision and the argument it always starts

### What does the Election Commission actually order in a special intensive revision?

A special intensive revision, directed under section 21(3) for reasons the Commission records, requires the roll to be verified afresh rather than carried forward. Existing entries are tested against enumeration forms and documents, names that cannot be verified are left out of the draft roll, and the claims and appeal routes decide who returns to the final roll.

The case for periodic intensive revision is administrative and, on its own terms, strong. A roll maintained only by continuous updating decays in known ways. Deaths are reported imperfectly, especially where no family member pursues the paperwork. Electors who move register at the new address and, despite sections 17 and 23, the old entry frequently survives because the information chain between officers breaks. Young electors are added; old duplicates accumulate; urbanisation redraws the human map of a constituency faster than summary revisions track it. The Election Commission's constitutional charge under Article 324 is to prepare rolls worthy of the elections they serve, and a roll carrying a large residue of the dead, the departed and the doubled is a standing invitation to impersonation and a standing slur on every result it produces. On this view, an exercise that verifies entries afresh at intervals is not an innovation but the discharge of the original duty, and section 21(3), with its recorded reasons and its freedom as to manner, was written into the Act for exactly such occasions.

The case made against a particular intensive exercise, in the form in which the Association for Democratic Reforms and other petitioners put it to the Supreme Court in the Bihar litigation, is about burden and timing rather than about the existence of the power. Verification that requires the elector to produce documents reverses the ordinary direction of the register: instead of the state proving a ground of deletion after inquiry, as Lal Babu Hussein requires, the citizen is asked to prove the entitlement afresh, and the citizens least able to produce documents are those whose documentary lives are thinnest, the poor, the migrant, the woman married not long before, whose records sit in another district, the elderly elector whose papers predate the formats demanded. Conducted close to an election, the argument runs, the exercise compresses the claims and appeal stages into weeks, and a name wrongly left out of the final roll loses the vote for that election in a way no later correction can restore, since the franchise for a completed election cannot be rerun for one voter. The safeguards the statute supplies, notice, hearing and the section 24 appeal, are real but are designed for individual grievances processed at ordinary tempo, and the critics' question is whether they retain substance when the grievances number in lakhs and the calendar is fixed by an approaching poll.

The Bihar exercise of 2025 is the worked example, and its record can be stated without characterising any actor's motive. Bihar's rolls had last undergone an intensive revision in 2003. By an order dated 24 June 2025, recording reasons that included rapid urbanisation, frequent migration for education and livelihood, the growth of duplicate entries and the presence of deceased persons on the rolls, the Commission directed a special intensive revision across the state's Assembly constituencies ahead of the Legislative Assembly elections due in November 2025, exercising the power in section 21(3) read with Article 324. Electors on the existing roll received pre-filled enumeration forms, to be returned by 25 July 2025; the 2003 roll was treated as probative evidence of eligibility for those who stood on it, while persons not on the 2003 roll were required to establish eligibility through one or more documents from an indicative list of eleven, which included birth certificates, passports, educational certificates and government identity and service documents, and which did not, in the order as issued, include the Aadhaar card, the elector's photo identity card or the ration card. The draft roll published on 1 August 2025 left out about sixty-five lakh names of the roughly seven crore eighty-nine lakh electors on the pre-exercise roll, recorded in the main as dead, permanently shifted, untraceable or duplicated, and the final roll published on 30 September 2025 carried about seven crore forty-two lakh electors. The elections were held in November 2025 on that roll.

The Supreme Court's engagement came in stages, and each stage is part of the durable record. In July 2025, in Association for Democratic Reforms v Election Commission of India, the Court declined to stay the exercise, while urging the Commission to consider accepting the Aadhaar card, the elector's photo identity card and the ration card as documents for the purpose of identification in the verification. In August 2025 the Court directed the Commission to publish the names of the electors left out of the draft roll with the reasons for non-inclusion, and to accept the Aadhaar card from a non-included person who came forward to challenge the omission. On the petitions being finally heard, the Court upheld the exercise: it held that the power to direct a special revision is traceable to section 21(3) of this Act read with Article 324 of the Constitution, that the Commission is entitled to satisfy itself that persons on the roll are citizens, citizenship being a condition of registration under section 16, that the reasons recorded for the Bihar exercise were germane to the statutory purpose, and that the procedure, enumeration, draft publication, claims and objections, and the transparency directions given along the way, met the fairness the registration law requires. The petitions stood dismissed. The position the case law states is therefore twofold, and both halves bind the future: the Commission may verify the roll afresh, including on citizenship, under section 21(3); and an elector whose name is left out holds statutory and appellate routes, reinforced in the Bihar case by court-ordered publication of names and reasons, that must be real rather than nominal if the exercise is to survive scrutiny.

A parallel argument ran on the Aadhaar question inside the same litigation landscape. The petitioners' complaint about the indicative list was, in substance, that the most widely held identity documents in the state had been excluded while rarer documents were demanded. The Commission's defence was that the listed documents went to eligibility, citizenship and residence and date of birth, whereas the Aadhaar card by its own statute is proof of identity and not of citizenship, and the ration card is a household entitlement document of a different character. The Court's interim course, pressing the Commission toward accepting Aadhaar for identification while the eligibility inquiry proceeded on the listed documents, reflects the distinction this article drew in the Aadhaar section: a document that establishes who a person is does not, without more, establish that the person satisfies sections 16 and 19, and a document that goes to eligibility does not cease to matter because identification is easy. The two questions, identity and entitlement, run through every part of this Act, and confusing them is the commonest error in argument about it.

## What kind of right the vote is

The judicial anchor of this article is a distinction that public argument persistently collapses. In the line of authority that begins with N.P. Ponnuswami in 1952 and runs through Jyoti Basu v Debi Ghosal and the Constitution Bench in Kuldip Nayar v Union of India, the Supreme Court has held that the right to vote, like the right to be elected, is a statutory right: it is created by the election statutes, it is exercisable in the manner and subject to the conditions those statutes impose, and it is not, in itself, a fundamental right. Article 326 constitutionalises the entitlement to be registered on adult suffrage, but the exercise of the franchise takes place inside the statutory scheme, of which this Act's register is the gate. That holding is the jurisprudential form of this article's namable claim: because the right is statutory and registration is its statutory precondition, the clerical act of the Registration Officer stands between the citizen and the Constitution's promise, and the safeguards around that act are constitutional safeguards in substance whatever their form.

The second half of the distinction is equally settled and equally often missed. The act of voting, once the voter stands at the poll, is an act of expression protected by the freedom of speech and expression in Article 19(1)(a). The Supreme Court so held in People's Union for Civil Liberties v Union of India in 2013, the judgment that required the voting machine and the ballot to carry the option of none of the above, since a voter who rejects every candidate expresses a political opinion in the rejecting, and in the companion ruling on the secrecy of the ballot read with the voter's right to know the antecedents of candidates. The two holdings coexist without tension once the stages are separated. Registration is statutory; expression is fundamental. A person excluded from the roll has lost a statutory entitlement and must fight in the statutory forums, the appeal under section 24 and, outside the election window, the writ court. A person on the roll who is prevented from recording a choice, including the choice of none, has a fundamental right in play. Arguments that describe the vote simply as a fundamental right, or simply as a statutory privilege with no constitutional dimension, each take half the doctrine for the whole.

The candidate side of the same design, qualifications, nominations, corrupt practices and the disqualifications that follow an election, belongs to the companion pillar on [the Representation of the People Act 1951](/2017/12/15/representation-of-people-act-1951/), and the two articles are written to be read together: this Act decides who may vote, that Act decides who may be voted for and what follows when the rules of the contest are broken. The field as a whole, the constitutional text, both statutes, the delimitation law and the subordinate rules, is mapped in the thematic guide to [election law in India](/2017/12/01/election-law-india-complete-guide/), to which readers who arrive here from a syllabus or a syllabus-shaped question are directed for the connective account.

## Council constituencies and the general provisions

Part IV, section 27, extends the roll system to the Legislative Councils, and its design shows the Act adapting one machinery to electorates of very different kinds. Council constituencies are of distinct types under Article 171: constituencies of local authorities, of graduates, of teachers, and the constituencies from which members are elected by the members of the Legislative Assembly. Section 27 provides for the preparation of electoral rolls for the graduates' and teachers' constituencies and the other Council constituencies, on qualifications the Constitution itself fixes, a graduate of three years' standing for the graduates' constituency, a teacher of three years' standing in an institution not lower in standard than a secondary school for the teachers' constituency, with entitlement turning on residence within the constituency and on the professional qualification rather than on the ordinary register. The Fourth Schedule lists the local authorities whose members form the electorate of the local authorities' constituencies. Section 25A, a special provision for the Sangha constituency in Sikkim, completes the picture of a statute willing to bend its general scheme to a constitutionally recognised peculiarity, providing for registration in that constituency of the Sanghas of monasteries recognised for the purpose.

Part V holds the general provisions, of which four matter daily. Section 28 vests the rule-making power in the Central Government, to be exercised after consultation with the Election Commission, and the Registration of Electors Rules, 1960 are its principal product: the forms, the manner of preparation and publication of rolls, the claims and objections procedure, the hearing discipline and the appeal procedure all live in those rules, which is why the Act reads as a frame and the rules as the operating manual, and why the forms revision of 2022 was accomplished by amending the rules under the parentage of the 2021 Act. Section 29 obliges local authorities to make their staff available for roll work when the Commission requires it, the statutory root of the Booth Level Officer's secondment. Section 30 bars the jurisdiction of civil courts both on the question whether a person is entitled to be registered, which is for the Registration Officer and the appellate officers alone, and on the validity of anything done under the Act's roll provisions; the bar channels every registration grievance into the section 24 structure and, beyond it, to the constitutional courts. Section 31 punishes the making of a false declaration in connection with the preparation, revision or correction of a roll, or in an application for inclusion or duplication of a name, with imprisonment that may extend to one year, or with fine, or with both, the provision that gives the declaration printed on every registration form its legal edge. Section 32 addresses the other side of the desk: an officer who commits a breach of official duty in connection with the preparation or revision of a roll, without reasonable cause, is punishable on conviction, a provision invoked rarely and significant chiefly as a statement that the integrity of the register is a duty owed by the administration and not merely a burden carried by the applicant.

## The first roll and the displaced persons

The Act was written for an electorate that included millions of people whom Partition had uprooted, and its earliest special provisions faced that fact directly. The Statement of Objects and Reasons recorded that the Bill provided for the registration of electors for Parliamentary and Assembly and Council constituencies, for the qualifications and disqualifications for registration, and for a relaxation of the residence qualification in the case of displaced persons who had migrated to India from the territory of Pakistan before the twenty-fifth day of July, 1949. The relaxation was not a detail. A displaced person who had arrived with nothing could not prove ordinary residence of any settled duration in a constituency that had become home by allotment and camp rather than by choice of years, and a registration law that insisted on the ordinary incidents of residence would have excluded the very population whose political membership the new Republic most needed to record. The young Act therefore bent its central test at the first application, by declaration and relaxed proof, and the precedent matters beyond its period: the ordinarily resident test has carried, from birth, an instruction to read it against the reality of displacement rather than against an ideal of settled documented life.

The preparation of the first roll on that basis was an enumeration exercise without a template. Draft rolls were prepared constituency by constituency from household enumeration, published for inspection, and opened to claims and objections in the manner the Act and the rules under it prescribed; the machinery of draft, claim, decision and final publication that a voter meets in every revision since descends in a direct line from that first exercise. The first general election was held on the roll so prepared, across the winter of 1951 to 1952, and the register's imperfections, omissions in remote areas, entries for the dead, households missed entire, were absorbed by a political culture that treated the first election as a founding act rather than a finished product. The lesson the early record teaches, and which every later controversy about revision rehearses, is that the roll has never been a perfect mirror of the entitled population. It is an administrative approximation, built under time pressure, and the law's real design question is not how to make the approximation perfect but how to distribute the cost of its imperfection: on the administration, through verification duties and hearing obligations, or on the citizen, through documentary demands. The sections of this Act distribute that cost in both directions, and the litigation examined in this article is, at bottom, argument about the distribution.

## How an application is decided

The statute states entitlement; the Registration of Electors Rules, 1960 govern the encounter in which entitlement is tested. An application for inclusion, whether on the printed form at a Booth Level Officer or through the Commission's online portal, is an application to the Electoral Registration Officer, and the officer's duty on receiving it is inquiry, not mere receipt. The rules provide for the application to be checked, for field verification of the particulars through the Booth Level Officer where the officer requires it, for the claims received in a revision to be published by being displayed and listed, for objections to be received and heard, and for each claim and objection to be decided after a summary inquiry in which the claimant and any objector may be heard. A decision is an order, and it is from that order that the statutory appeal runs.

Three features of that encounter deserve notice because they decide real cases. The first is the declaration. Every application form carries a declaration by the applicant of citizenship, of age, of ordinary residence, and that the applicant's name is not included in the roll of any other constituency or twice in the same constituency; the declaration is not ceremonial, because section 31 makes a declaration known or believed to be false, or not believed to be true, in a material particular, an offence. The second is the standard of proof, which is administrative and practical rather than forensic: the officer must be satisfied of entitlement on the material produced and verified, and the documents called for in the process, age proof, residence proof and a photograph, are evidence toward that satisfaction, not statutory conditions in themselves. A case that fails is therefore usually a case of unsatisfied inquiry rather than disqualification, and the distinction matters for the remedy, since an unsatisfied inquiry can be reopened with better material while a section 16 disqualification cannot. The third feature is the artefact the process issues: the elector's photo identity card, introduced across the country from 1993 as the Commission's answer to impersonation at the poll. The card is evidence of the entry, convenient and widely used as an identity document far beyond elections, but it is not the franchise: the roll is the franchise's record, a card without an entry does not entitle its holder to vote, and an entry without a card does not extinguish the vote, since the polling rules have long permitted identity to be established by other specified documents where the card is absent. Confusion between the card and the roll is the single most common error in popular discussion of this Act, ahead even of the error about the right to vote examined earlier.

Between revisions, the encounter is continuous. The Commission maintains a standing facility for applications through the year, the forms for inclusion, correction, shifting and objection being receivable outside any revision window, and the officer deciding them as they come, with the results carried into the roll by supplements at the next publication. The revision window concentrates the traffic and the publicity; the continuous route carries the steady flow of eighteen year olds, movers and the omitted. Both routes end at the same desk and are governed by the same sections, and the freeze in section 23(3) is the only point at which the continuous route closes, for the constituency and the duration of the election there.

## The roll in the age of databases

The register the Act describes is a paper conception, a roll for each constituency prepared, revised and published; the register the Commission maintains is a database, unified in a single national application, photo-bearing since the general move to photo electoral rolls completed around the 2009 general election, and worked on by software that searches it for duplicates across constituencies and states. The statute accommodates the transformation without strain, because sections 15 and 21 speak of preparation and revision in the prescribed manner and leave the manner to rules and Commission directions, and the prescribed manner has absorbed enumeration software, online application, digital publication of draft rolls and searchable final rolls without any amendment to the Act's operative words. The transformation still changes where error lives. In a paper register, the characteristic errors were local: a missed household, a careless enumerator, a name copied wrong. In a database, the characteristic errors are systematic: a de-duplication match that treats two electors as one person, a transliteration that splits one elector into two entries, a field migration that drops a column of entries. De-duplication is commanded by sections 17 and 18 and is indispensable at the scale of a register approaching a billion entries; it is also the operation most capable of deleting a lawful entry without any officer ever forming an intention about that entry, which is why the Act's procedural promises, verification, notice, hearing, appeal, attach to outcomes and not to intentions, and why the deleted elector's case in law does not depend on proving that anyone decided wrongly about that elector in particular.

The absent, shifted and dead lists maintained during revisions are the administrative face of the same transformation. Booth Level Officers report electors found absent from the recorded address, shifted from it or dead, and the reports feed the inquiry that section 22 requires before deletion. A report of absence is not a finding of non-residence, since section 20(1A) protects the temporarily absent in terms, and the distinction between a locked house on the day of the visit and a household that has ceased to live there is the distinction on which wrongful deletions in intensive exercises most often turn. The Delhi litigation of 2026, in which electors deleted after an intensive revision because they had shifted residence sought restoration through the correction route and were directed by the High Court to the inclusion route of Form 6, illustrates the enduring practical question beneath the database: whether a moved elector's entry is to be transposed, carrying its history with it, or extinguished and recreated. The Court accepted the Commission's position that a person whose name stands deleted and who seeks to be on the roll again applies for inclusion, and the episode records, in miniature, the article's theme: at every step the register's categories, inclusion, correction, transposition, deletion, are legal acts with distinct consequences, and choosing the wrong category is not a technicality but a change in what the citizen must prove and what the citizen keeps.

## Where the roll meets the election petition

Section 30's bar on civil courts and Article 329's bar on judicial interference with an election in progress together draw the boundary between registration grievances and election disputes, and the Supreme Court has policed that boundary in both directions. In Lakshmi Charan Sen v A.K.M. Hassan Uzzaman, arising out of challenges to the preparation of rolls for the West Bengal Assembly, the Court held that the preparation and revision of electoral rolls is a continuous process governed by the Act, that the courts would not interdict a revision or an ensuing election for alleged defects in the roll, and that a person aggrieved by an entry or an omission was remitted to the remedies the Act itself provides. The holding is the judicial counterpart of the freeze in section 23(3): the electoral calendar cannot be held hostage to the register's imperfections, because a register of this size is never perfected, only improved, and an election postponed until the roll is beyond criticism is an election postponed indefinitely. In the same movement, the Court treated the statutory remedies as the real protection and expected them to be used, which is the assumption the section 24 appeal exists to satisfy.

The converse boundary is equally firm. A defect in the roll is not, without more, a ground to undo an election. The election petition under the 1951 Act attacks the result on the grounds that statute specifies, and the petitioner who says the roll was defective must bring the case within the statutory grounds, typically by showing that the result was materially affected by the reception of votes that were void or the refusal of votes that were lawful, rather than by demonstrating imperfection in the register at large. The two positions compose into a coherent settlement. The roll is challengeable name by name, through the Act's own machinery, at the time the machinery runs; the election is challengeable result by result, through the petition, on the grounds the 1951 Act allows; and neither challenge may be converted into the other. The citizen who understands the settlement understands why the claims and objections window of a revision, unglamorous and poorly attended, is legally the main event, and why the appeal that follows it is the remedy on which the effective franchise depends.

## Service voters on the roll

The service voter deserves a fuller account than the deeming provision alone gives, because the category tests the Act's residence logic at its most artificial and its most justified. The member of the armed forces posted to a field station, the member of a state armed police force serving outside the state, the official posted to an embassy abroad, each lives, in the factual sense, somewhere else entirely, often for years. Ordinary residence applied without help would register them where they serve, in constituencies with which they may have no durable connection, and would scatter a unit's votes across every station of a career. Section 20 instead deems them ordinarily resident where they would have been resident but for the service, and the roll of the home constituency carries the entry, recorded as a service entry in the last part of the roll, separate from the general electors of the part. The spouse who so elects is deemed resident alongside, on the gender neutral terms the 2021 amendment settled.

The voting consequence of the service entry is delivered by the 1951 Act and the Conduct of Elections Rules, 1961: the service voter votes by postal ballot, or by a proxy appointed in the prescribed manner, and the electronically transmitted postal ballot system, under which the blank ballot is sent electronically to the service voter and returned by post for counting, has modernised the postal limb since its adoption for the general election of 2019. The contrast with the overseas elector is instructive and deliberate. The service voter's absence is the state's own posting, and the law answers it with a remote vote; the overseas elector's absence is a private choice of employment or education, and the law, having twice considered the matter through the lapsed Bill of 2017 and the unacted proposal of 2020, has so far answered it with registration alone. Whether that asymmetry is principled, a judgment about whose absence the Republic should subsidise with machinery, or merely historical, a product of which reform reached the statute book first, is a question this article leaves where the record leaves it: the asymmetry is real, its rationale is contestable, and its correction is a legislative act that has been drafted once and not yet been delivered.

## The calendar of a revision, walked through

A summary revision, the form a voter is most likely to meet, runs on a published programme that the Act's sections and the 1960 Rules together generate. The programme opens with the publication of the existing roll in draft, part by part for every polling station area of the constituency, at the offices of the Registration Officer and the Assistant Registration Officers, at designated locations including polling stations, and in searchable form on the Chief Electoral Officer's website. Publication is not a formality: it is the act that starts the statutory clock, because claims and objections run from it. The claims and objections window follows, a period fixed in the programme, ordinarily running for several weeks, during which Form 6 applications for inclusion, Form 7 objections, Form 8 corrections and shiftings, and Form 6B intimations of Aadhaar may be filed on paper with the Booth Level Officer or the officers above, or online through the Commission's voter services portal and application. House level verification proceeds alongside, the Booth Level Officer visiting the area, confirming the living and the dead, reporting the shifted and the absent.

Disposal follows the window. Every claim and objection is decided by the Registration Officer after the summary inquiry the Rules prescribe, the lists of claims and objections are shared with the recognised political parties as the programme directs, and the decisions feed a series of supplements to the draft roll. The final roll is then published, incorporating the supplements, and stands as the roll of the constituency until the next revision or the continuous updating alters it. Two features of the calendar repay attention. The qualifying date logic runs through it: the revision is anchored to the qualifying dates in section 14(b), and since the amendment of 2021 the programme accommodates applicants who attain eighteen on any of the four dates, so that a revision concluding in one quarter carries forward applications maturing in the next. The allied reform of the Registration of Electors (Amendment) Rules, 2022 permits a person above seventeen years of age to apply in advance, the application to be taken up when the applicant attains eighteen against the relevant qualifying date, a change that attacks the commonest single cause of first time omission, the application filed too late because the birthday fell in the wrong part of the year. Neither reform altered a word of sections 16 or 19; both altered the probability that an entitled person becomes an entered person, which, on this article's analysis, is where the Act succeeds or fails.

The intensive revision follows the same legal skeleton with the flesh reversed: instead of a draft roll carried forward and amended, the enumeration builds the register household by household, and the publication, claims and final stages then test what the enumeration built. The special revision under section 21(3) borrows from both, as the Bihar exercise showed: an enumeration form placed in every existing elector's hands, a documentary test for those the earlier anchor roll did not cover, a draft that excluded the unverified, and then the claims, objections and appellate stages carrying the burden of restoration. The legal questions such an exercise raises are therefore not about the stages, which are common to every revision, but about the weight placed on each: how much verification the enumeration stage may demand, how wide the draft exclusion may run, and how real the restoration stages must be for the exercise as a whole to remain the preparation of a roll rather than its replacement by a new and narrower one.

## Who decides citizenship, unsoundness and the corrupt practice bar

The three disqualifications in section 16 are stated in a sentence each, but each conceals a distinct decision maker, and confusing them produces bad argument. Citizenship for the purposes of registration is decided, in the first instance, by the Electoral Registration Officer in the inquiry on the application or the entry. The officer is not conducting a trial of nationality under the Citizenship Act, 1955; the officer is deciding whether the registration condition is satisfied on the material available, and the decision operates for the roll unless displaced within the election law structure or by a competent authority on the citizenship question itself. Where a doubt arises in the course of a revision about a person already entered, Lal Babu Hussein fixes the discipline: the entry stands on a presumption earned by the original inquiry, the person must be told the ground of doubt and heard, and suspicion, including suspicion generated by a police report or by the scale of an exercise, is not evidence. In Assam, the interaction between roll revision and the separate citizenship determination machinery built around the National Register of Citizens and the Foreigners Tribunals has generated its own line of controversy, and the distinction of functions is the point to hold: a tribunal's determination of citizenship status and a Registration Officer's determination of registration entitlement are different acts by different authorities, and the roll decision must still be the officer's own, made after the hearing the Act requires.

Unsoundness of mind, the second limb, is decided by a court before the Registration Officer is involved at all: the disqualification requires that the person stands declared by a competent court to be of unsound mind, and in the absence of such a declaration the officer has nothing to apply. The narrowness is a deliberate protection, inserted in an era when unsoundness was loosely alleged, and its practical consequence is that the limb operates in a very small class of cases where a court has in fact made the declaration in proceedings directed to the question.

The third limb, disqualification from voting under a law relating to corrupt practices and other offences in connection with elections, is fed principally by the 1951 Act. A person found guilty of a corrupt practice, whether by the High Court trying an election petition or on the determination made under that Act, is liable to disqualification for a period of six years under section 8A of the 1951 Act, the question of disqualification and its period being decided by the President on the opinion of the Election Commission. While that disqualification runs, the person is not entitled to registration under the third limb of section 16 of this Act, and an existing entry is liable to deletion under section 22. The registration limb thus gives the corrupt practice regime its continuing bite between elections: the disgraced candidate does not merely sit out a Parliament but drops off the register of voters for the period, a consequence rarely noticed in accounts of the 1951 Act and a good example of the two statutes interlocking, the one supplying the finding, the other the registration consequence.

## The electors the register strains to hold

Three classes of elector expose the register's design limits in ways the general account can pass over. The first is the elector of the third gender. The Commission's forms and rolls recognised electors in the others category from 2009, recording gender outside the male and female columns for electors who so identified, an administrative recognition that preceded the Supreme Court's judgment in National Legal Services Authority v Union of India in 2014 and the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019. The registration questions that arise, of name, of gender entry, of the documentary trail a transition disrupts, are decided by the same sections as every other correction, through Form 8 and the officer's inquiry, but they illustrate the register's dependence on documents that describe a person consistently over time, a dependence that bears hardest on citizens whose lives have involved a change the documents record imperfectly.

The second class is the internal migrant, the largest strained class in the register by an order of magnitude. The Act's answer to migration is transposition: the migrant is entitled, and on the logic of sections 17 and 19 required, to move the registration to the constituency of ordinary residence as life relocates. The strain is that the entitlement must be exercised affirmatively, at a distance from the institutions that know the migrant, against documents that often still point home, and the result is the register's best documented pathology: crores of electors registered in constituencies they have left, unregistered in the constituencies they inhabit, and voting, if they vote, by travelling to the place the register says they live. The Election Commission's exploration of remote voting for internal migrants, including the development and demonstration in 2023 of a multi-constituency remote electronic voting prototype that would let a migrant vote for the home constituency from a remote station in the city of work, and the reservations expressed by political parties about its integrity and secrecy, belong to the conduct side of election law rather than to registration, since the migrant in that design remains registered at home. The registration question underneath it remains untouched by any machine: whether ordinary residence should follow the migrant faster than the migrant's own applications make it follow, and the Act, as written, answers that question with the application, the inquiry and nothing else.

The third class is the homeless and the institutionalised: persons without a fixed dwelling whose ordinary residence is real but unaddressed, residents of institutions, undertrials in custody whose registration is not the same question as their right to vote, which the 1951 Act separately restricts for persons confined in prison or in lawful police custody. For the homeless elector, the Registration Officer's inquiry accepts the place where the person habitually sleeps as the residence to be recorded, attested in the manner the Commission's instructions provide, and the entry is made without a house to attach it to. The cases are few beside the migrant millions, but they mark the outer edge of the ordinarily resident idea: residence under this Act is a fact about a life, not a fact about a property, which is precisely what section 20(1) said at the outset by denying that a dwelling house, without more, makes the resident.

## The scale of the register

Figures for the size of the electorate are among the few statistics in election writing that are both stable and published, and they convey what the Act administers better than any adjective. The Commission's statistical reports record an electorate of about seventeen crore at the first general election of 1951 to 1952. The figure for the general election of 2019 was about ninety-one crore, and for the general election of 2024 about ninety-seven crore, the largest electorate ever enrolled for a single national election anywhere. Behind those totals the register's unit of account is not the nation but the part: each Assembly constituency roll is divided into parts corresponding to polling station areas, each part carrying, in the ordinary case, in the region of a thousand to fifteen hundred electors grouped by neighbourhood, so that the national register is in truth several lakh of small registers bound by common rules. The Registration Officer administers the constituency; the Booth Level Officer knows the part; the elector's lived experience of this Act is an experience of the part, the short list of names of one street or one village segment in which the elector's own name either stands or does not.

Scale explains the statute's procedural economy. A register of ninety-seven crore entries cannot be maintained by adjudication of a judicial kind in each case; it is maintained by forms, field reports, summary inquiries and sample checks, with full hearing discipline reserved for the adverse individual decision, the deletion and the refusal, where the Act insists on it. Every serious proposal to reform the register is, whether it says so or not, a proposal about where in that economy to spend the scarce resource of official attention: on the front end, verifying every entry as if it were suspect, or on the back end, deciding the contested entries with care while the uncontested mass flows through. The Bihar controversy, stripped of its politics, was that argument conducted in public, and the Supreme Court's resolution of it, accepting a demanding front end for one exercise while reinforcing the back end duties of publication, reasons and access to appeal, is the closest the law has come to stating a general doctrine of the register's economy. It will be cited in every future revision dispute for exactly that reason.

## Checking a name: the verification habit the Act presupposes

The Act presupposes a citizen who can discover the state of the entry, and the modern register makes that discovery a matter of minutes for any elector with a phone. The final roll of every constituency is published in searchable form; an elector may search by name and family particulars, or by the number of the elector's photo identity card, and retrieve the entry with its part number and serial number, the two coordinates that identify the polling station and the position on its list. The application portals carry the status of a pending Form 6, 7 or 8, so the applicant can see whether the inquiry has been completed, the field verification recorded and the order made. None of this machinery appears in the Act, whose operative words are satisfied by publication and inspection in the offices the sections name, and all of it is the prescribed manner of the Act's own sections working through rules and Commission directions, which is why the account given here describes the habit rather than the interface: interfaces change with each revision cycle, the habit, check the draft, claim within the window, keep the acknowledgement, appeal within the period, is permanent.

The habit matters most in the draft stage of a revision, because the claims and objections window is the one period in the register's life when correction is cheap. An omission discovered in the draft is cured by a claim decided in the ordinary course before final publication; the same omission discovered on polling day is incurable for that election, since the freeze of section 23(3) and the bar of Article 329 stand between the voter and any remedy that operates in time. Political parties understand this and staff the window with booth level agents authorised to file claims and objections in bulk on printed lists; the individual elector's equivalent is the few minutes the search takes. A disproportionate share of the register's real quality is determined in that window, by whether the people whose names are wrong take the cheap remedy while it is open, and the Commission's publicity for the window, whatever its volume, chronically reaches the already registered more easily than the omitted, which is one more reason the omissions concentrate where they do.

## The superintendence above the officers

Above the hierarchy of Part IIA stands the Election Commission itself, and the relation between the Commission and the roll deserves a paragraph of its own, because it is the constitutional guarantee behind the statutory scheme. Article 324 vests the superintendence, direction and control of the preparation of electoral rolls in the Commission, in terms the Supreme Court, in Mohinder Singh Gill v Chief Election Commissioner, described as a reservoir of power intended to let the Commission act where the enacted law is silent, so that the election process is not defeated by a gap in the rules. The roll provisions of this Act are the enacted law for their subject, and within them the Commission acts as the directing authority: it fixes revision programmes, issues the manual for electoral rolls that standardises the officers' practice, orders the special revisions that section 21(3) authorises, and answers for the register's integrity to Parliament and to the courts. The state governments supply the officers and the staff, who work on deputation to the Commission for this purpose under section 13CC, a design that separates the hand that prepares the roll from the political executive whose members the roll will elect. When the design fails, it fails at the joints of that separation, in a state administration slow to release staff, in a district where the Commission's programme meets an unreceptive local calendar, and the Commission's remedies are supervisory rather than executive: it can direct, it can report, it can deploy observers, and in the last resort it can say, publicly, that a roll is not ready. The authority is real but it is the authority of superintendence, and the Act's distribution of the operative decisions to named local officers is the necessary complement: somebody must decide each name, and the Commission, three persons in New Delhi, cannot.

## One general roll: the founding refusal

Section 15's direction that there be an electoral roll for every constituency operates under Article 325, and the pairing is a founding decision that the machinery sections can make a reader forget. The colonial franchise had been built on separate electorates, constituencies in which only members of a designated community voted for the community's reserved seats, a device introduced for Muslims in 1909, extended to other communities under the Government of India Act, 1935, and regarded by the nationalist movement, and by the Constituent Assembly after the experience of Partition, as an engine of political division. Article 325 abolished the device in terms: there shall be one general electoral roll for every territorial constituency, and no person shall be ineligible for inclusion in it, or claim to be included in a special roll for it, on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex or any of them. The roll the 1950 Act builds is therefore, by constitutional command, a roll of neighbours: the electors of a constituency stand on one list in the order of their houses, without communal sorting, and the only qualifications the register may test are the secular qualifications of citizenship, age, residence and freedom from disqualification that sections 16 and 19 state. Reservation of seats for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes under Articles 330 and 332 operates at the level of the constituency and the candidature, deciding who may stand, and never at the level of the roll, deciding who may vote, and the distinction between a reserved constituency and a separate electorate, routinely blurred in public argument, is exactly the distinction between the 1951 Act's candidate rules and this Act's single register.

The roll's internal organisation carries the same principle into the small print. Entries are arranged by part and within the part by house and household, so a family's names stand together and a street reads as a street. The supplements that revisions generate are integrated into the roll at publication, with deletions carried out and additions merged, rather than left as accretions of separate lists through which an elector must hunt. These are administrative choices, not statutory commands, but they are the choices that make the single general roll a usable civic document rather than a warehouse of names, and they explain a small ritual of Indian elections that visitors remark on: the voter at the polling station door finding a name by part and serial number in seconds, because the roll was built, from the first edition, to be searched by the people it lists.

## The amendment arc in outline

Read in sequence, the amendments to the roll provisions describe the Republic's changing anxieties about its register. The earliest amendments were territorial and institutional, adapting the Act to the reorganisation of states and to the filling of Council of States seats for Union territories, the work of the amending Act 73 of 1950 and the adaptation orders of the nineteen fifties. The amending Act 40 of 1961 inserted the appeal in section 24, the point at which Parliament decided that the Registration Officer's orders were too consequential to rest without an internal appeal, and the amending Act 47 of 1966 recast section 23 and pruned the fee and procedural apparatus around applications and appeals. The amending Act 41 of 2009, effective from 1 February 2010, completed the appellate structure by adding the second appeal to the Chief Electoral Officer, and in the same period the continuous revision practice and the photo roll were transforming the register's physical form beneath an unamended text.

The two amendments that changed the substance of entitlement came later and have been treated at length above: the Act 36 of 2010, which inserted section 20A and admitted the citizen abroad to the register, and the Act 49 of 2021, which multiplied the qualifying dates, admitted the Aadhaar number to section 23 behind the protection of section 23(6), and made the service spouse provision gender neutral. Between them sits the constitutional change that altered section 19's world without touching its words, the Sixty-first Amendment's reduction of the voting age in 1988, effective in 1989, which added a cohort of young electors larger than the entire electorate of the first general election and quietly made the qualifying date, the least discussed definition in the Act, into a provision of mass consequence. The arc as a whole moves in one direction: each generation's amendment widens the class the register can hold, or lowers the friction of entering it, while the conditions themselves, citizenship, age, residence, clean record, have not moved since 1950. The disputes have migrated accordingly, from the conditions, which are settled, to the administration of the conditions, which is not, and a reader who sees that migration sees the Act whole.

## What an entry is worth beyond the vote

An entry on the roll does work far beyond the polling station, and the secondary uses explain some of the pressure the register carries. The elector's photo identity card is accepted across the administrative system as proof of identity and of address: banks take it for account opening, it supports applications for passports, ration cards and driving licences, and it is produced in employment and housing transactions as a standard credential. The entry itself is treated in many inquiries as evidence of residence and, more debatably, as an indicium of citizenship, since section 16 confines registration to citizens. Both uses are understandable and both are, strictly, misconceived in part. The card certifies that an entry stood on a roll after an administrative inquiry at a point in time; it is not a citizenship certificate, and neither the card nor the entry decides nationality, which is governed by the Citizenship Act, 1955 and determined by the authorities that Act and the Foreigners Act, 1946 empower. The Commission itself cautions that the card is an identity document for electoral purposes. The distinction is the one the Bihar litigation pressed from the other side: if the roll is not proof of citizenship for the citizen who waves the card, neither is the absence of an entry proof of non-citizenship against the citizen who lacks one, and an exercise that slides between the two propositions in either direction has left the statute behind.

The secondary uses also explain why deletion hurts beyond the lost vote, and why the politics of revision are so charged. A household struck off a roll in a documentary exercise fears, rationally or not, a cascade: the credential weakens, the address proof thins, the standing of the family in every office that asks for the card is diminished. The Act knows nothing of that cascade; its deletion is a registration act with registration consequences, and the section 24 appeal restores the entry, not the documents the entry supported. But the citizen experiences the register as a single thread through the state's records, and reform writing that ignores the cascade will always misjudge the temperature of a revision dispute. It is a further reason to insist on the modesty of the statutory propositions: the roll decides who may vote in a constituency, it evidences residence at a standard of administrative satisfaction, and it does not decide, confer or extinguish citizenship. Keeping those three propositions separate, in drafting, in administration and in argument, is the intellectual hygiene this statute most requires and most often misses.

## Documents in the registration inquiry

Because entitlement is tested on documents in most real cases, the documentary practice of the register deserves its own statement. The Act itself prescribes no list of documents for an ordinary application; the list lives in the Commission's instructions and the forms: proof of age, drawn from a range that includes the birth certificate, the school leaving or matriculation certificate, the passport, the Aadhaar card and other specified records; proof of residence, drawn from utility records, the passport, bank and post office records, rent instruments and like material; and a photograph for the photo roll. The officer's duty is satisfaction, not tallying, and the instructions have always contemplated that cases will arise in which the papers are thin and the facts are strong, a lifelong resident of a village whose birth predates registration of births, a tenant without a written lease in a city of informal housing. In such cases the field verification through the Booth Level Officer, the testimony of neighbours taken in the summary inquiry, and the officer's own inspection carry the weight the paper cannot. The system's documentary failures run in both directions: papers demanded that the law does not require, which is the grievance in the Bihar argument, and papers accepted without the verification the rules prescribe, which is the grievance in every allegation of bogus enrolment. Both failures are failures of the same duty, the officer's duty to be satisfied after proper verification, and the remedy structure of the Act, objection during the window, appeal after the order, addresses each without distinction. A registration law for a billion electors cannot specify its evidence in the statute; it can only specify the standard, satisfaction after inquiry, and hold the deciding officer to it. Sections 22 and 23 hold the officer to it in terms, and the whole documentary apparatus of forms and lists is subordinate to those words, a hierarchy that argument about this Act forgets at its peril.

## The roll and the census: two counts of the people

The register is one of two great counts of the Indian population, and the comparison with the other, the decennial census, sharpens what kind of instrument it is. The census counts everyone present, citizen and foreigner, adult and child, and its figures drive the allocation of seats and the delimitation of constituencies that Part II of this Act administers. The roll counts a subset defined by law, citizens of eighteen and above who are ordinarily resident in each constituency and free of disqualification, and its figures drive nothing but the election itself. The two counts diverge by design and the divergence is diagnostic: where the roll for a constituency falls far short of the census-derived estimate of its adult citizen population, the gap is a measure of under-registration, and the Commission's revision planning uses such comparisons to target areas for special attention. Where the roll exceeds the plausible adult population, the excess signals duplication or the survival of entries for the departed, the classic pathology of high out-migration districts. Neither count corrects the other; the census cannot put a name on the roll, since it records no entitlement, and the roll cannot amend the census. But each audits the other at the level of totals, and a Registration Officer whose constituency shows an implausible ratio in either direction knows where the next revision's work lies. The Act, characteristically, says none of this: it builds the register and leaves the demography to practice. The register's credibility also rests in part on these silent comparisons, and the political argument about a revision often begins when a constituency's roll and its population visibly part company. The gender ratio of the roll is the comparison watched most closely in the Commission's own reporting: a constituency whose roll records markedly fewer women per thousand men than its population contains is a constituency whose registration machinery is failing women in particular, through the marriage migration that moves a woman's ordinary residence across constituency lines, through documentary trails that sit in a parental home, and through simple household gatekeeping of forms. Successive revisions have run special enrolment drives aimed at that gap, and the slow narrowing of the national roll's gender deficit across recent decades is one of the register's measurable achievements, obtained entirely inside sections 19 to 23, by treating an application gap as what it is, a registration failure and not a franchise fact.

## The honest assessment

The strengths of the design are genuine and should be recorded before its failures. The Act unifies the register: one roll per constituency, built once, serving Assembly and Parliament alike, maintained by a permanent hierarchy of named officers under a constitutional commission, with entitlement defined by four intelligible conditions and contested decisions channelled into a two-tier appeal inside the election administration itself. The ordinarily resident test, for all its marginal difficulty, has proved flexible enough to absorb the soldier, the diplomat, the student and the seasonal worker without statutory amendment to its core, and the two great modern extensions of the register, the overseas elector in 2010 and the four qualifying dates and Aadhaar authentication in 2021, were each fitted into the existing sections rather than bolted on as parallel systems. The statute's text has worn well because it states conditions and powers plainly and leaves the volatile detail, forms, documents, calendars, to rules that can be remade without reopening the Act.

The weaknesses are structural, and they are the obverse of the strengths. A franchise administered through applications will systematically under-register the citizens with the least administrative capacity: the internal migrant whose ordinary residence is arguable, the young voter who assumes enrolment is automatic, the woman whose name and address change on marriage across district lines, the elderly elector in a household that no enumerator reaches. A register maintained by officers measured on the completion of exercises will, at the margin, prefer a defensible deletion to a laborious verification, and the hearing and appeal safeguards, though real, must be invoked by the person least equipped to invoke them, within windows measured in days. And the overseas elector's position, registration without a practicable vote, stands as the design's clearest unfinished business, left unfinished not by drafting failure but by legislative choice: the proxy Bill of 2017 was passed by one House and allowed to lapse, and the postal ballot proposal of 2020 was made and not taken up.

One further strength belongs in the balance, because it is easy to overlook in a statute built of officers and forms: the Act is short. Its operative roll provisions run from section 14 to section 25A, a dozen sections that a diligent reader can hold entire, and the whole law of who may vote in the world's largest democracy can be read in an afternoon and argued about for a career. That brevity is a democratic asset. The conditions of the franchise should be stateable by the citizen they govern without professional translation, and these can be: citizen, eighteen on a qualifying date, ordinarily resident, not disqualified, entered on the roll of the constituency, with a hearing before deletion and an appeal after it. A voter who can recite those terms owns the statute in the only sense that finally matters, and the administrative controversies of each decade are, in the end, arguments about whether the practice of a given year honoured terms the citizen could recite in 1950.

The verdict this article defends follows from the namable claim with which it began. The vote in India is conditional on a clerical act, and that is not a defect to be lamented in the abstract; every mass franchise in history has run on a register, and a register without conditions would be a register without meaning. The defect appears only when the clerical act is not matched by clerical accountability, when a name can be deleted more easily than it can be restored, when the burden of the administration's information failures is placed on the citizen's documents, and when the appeal the Act provides remains unknown to the people whose names disappear. The law as written holds the balance better than the law as sometimes administered: sections 16 and 19 state who is entitled, section 22 forbids deletion without a hearing, section 23(6) forbids the Aadhaar shortcut, section 24 provides the appeal, and the Supreme Court, from Lal Babu Hussein to the Bihar judgment, has insisted that the entitlement of a person already on the roll is not to be displaced by suspicion, by scale or by schedule. A reader who finishes this article knowing those five propositions, and knowing that the appeal against a wrongful deletion exists and where it lies, holds the working core of the Representation of the People Act, 1950, and the practical franchise of the Republic that runs on it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Representation of the People Act 1950 cover?

The Act covers the groundwork of Indian elections rather than the elections themselves. It provides for the allocation of seats in the House of the People and in the state Legislative Assemblies and Councils, recorded in its schedules; the delimitation of constituencies; the qualifications of voters; the officers who prepare the rolls, from the Chief Electoral Officer to the Electoral Registration Officer; and the preparation, revision, correction and appeal machinery of the electoral rolls. It also contains the rolls for Council constituencies and the manner of filling Council of States seats for Union territories. The conduct of elections, candidates, polling and election petitions belong to the Representation of the People Act, 1951, which is, by coincidence, also Act 43 of its year.

### Q: Who can be registered as a voter?

A person can be registered in the electoral roll of a constituency if the person is a citizen of India, is not less than eighteen years of age on the qualifying date for the roll, is ordinarily resident in that constituency, and is not disqualified under section 16 of the Act. Section 16 disqualifies a person who is not a citizen, a person of unsound mind who stands so declared by a competent court, and a person disqualified from voting for the time being under a law relating to corrupt practices and other election offences. Registration is not automatic: the entitled person must apply, ordinarily on Form 6, and the Electoral Registration Officer must be satisfied after inquiry that the conditions are met.

### Q: How is ordinarily resident decided for voter registration?

Ordinary residence under section 20 of the Act is decided on the facts of how a person's life is centred, not on property or on any single document. Owning or possessing a house in a constituency does not by itself make a person ordinarily resident there, and temporary absence from the home constituency does not end ordinary residence there. The question is where the person lives as home, judged from the duration and character of the presence, the location of the family and belongings, and the intention to remain or to return. Special deeming rules protect service voters, including members of the armed forces and officials posted outside India, whose spouses may also be registered at the home constituency. The Electoral Registration Officer decides borderline cases on the material produced and verified in the inquiry.

### Q: Can an overseas elector vote from abroad?

No. Section 20A of the Act, inserted by the amending Act of 2010 and in force from 10 February 2011, entitles a citizen of India who is absent from the place of ordinary residence owing to employment, education or otherwise outside India, and who has not acquired another citizenship, to be registered in the constituency where the place of residence recorded in the passport is located. Registration, however, carries no remote vote: the overseas elector must attend in person at the polling station in that constituency and vote on production of the original passport. A Bill passed by the Lok Sabha in 2018 would have allowed overseas electors to vote by proxy, but it lapsed without passing the Rajya Sabha, and a later proposal to extend the postal ballot system used by service voters was not acted upon.

### Q: Which qualifying dates apply for voter registration?

Four qualifying dates apply: the first day of January, the first day of April, the first day of July and the first day of October in a calendar year. Age for registration is measured against these dates under section 14(b) of the Act, so a person who has turned eighteen on or before the relevant qualifying date may be registered with reference to that date. Before the Election Laws (Amendment) Act, 2021 there was a single qualifying date, the first of January, which made an applicant whose eighteenth birthday fell just after it wait close to a year. The four dates mean entitlement ripens quarterly, and the rules also permit a person above seventeen to apply in advance so the application matures on attaining eighteen.

### Q: Is linking Aadhaar with the electoral roll compulsory?

No, not on the text of the statute. Sections 23(4) and 23(5) of the Act, inserted by the Election Laws (Amendment) Act, 2021, allow the Electoral Registration Officer to require an applicant's Aadhaar number for establishing identity, and allow an existing elector to intimate the Aadhaar number for authentication of the entry and detection of duplicate registrations. Section 23(6) then states the protection expressly: no application for inclusion shall be denied and no entry shall be deleted for inability of an individual to furnish or intimate the Aadhaar number due to sufficient cause as prescribed, and such an individual may furnish other alternate documents as prescribed. The debate about the provision concerns administrative practice; the statute itself does not make registration depend on Aadhaar.

### Q: How is an electoral roll revised?

The roll for each constituency is revised under section 21 of the Act in the manner the Election Commission directs. In a summary revision the existing roll is published in draft, claims and objections are invited over a fixed window, verification is carried out through the Booth Level Officers, every claim and objection is decided by the Electoral Registration Officer after summary inquiry, and the final roll is published incorporating the decisions. An intensive revision rebuilds the roll by fresh household enumeration instead of amending the old roll. Under section 21(3) the Commission may at any time, for reasons recorded, direct a special revision of the roll of any constituency in the manner it thinks fit, which is the power behind special intensive revision exercises.

### Q: How can a name be added to or removed from the electoral roll?

A name is added on application, ordinarily Form 6 for a new elector and Form 6A for an overseas elector, made to the Electoral Registration Officer, who includes the name under section 23 after verification if satisfied of entitlement. A name is removed by the officer under section 22, on the officer's own motion after inquiry or on an objection, ordinarily on Form 7, where the person has died, has ceased to be ordinarily resident in the constituency, or is otherwise not entitled to stand on the roll. Before a deletion the person concerned must be given a reasonable opportunity of being heard. Both inclusion refusals and deletions can be appealed under section 24, first to the District Magistrate and then to the Chief Electoral Officer.

### Q: Does turning eighteen automatically put a person's name on the electoral roll?

No. Indian registration law has no automatic enrolment. Attaining eighteen creates the entitlement to be registered under section 19, provided the person is a citizen, ordinarily resident in the constituency and not disqualified, but the name reaches the roll only when an application is made and allowed, or when an enumeration exercise takes the person up. A person above seventeen may apply in advance under the rules so that the application matures against the next qualifying date. The gap between entitlement and entry is the commonest reason young voters discover at an election that they cannot vote: nothing in the Act moves a name onto the roll by the passage of a birthday alone, and the application, with its declaration, is the act that sets the registration machinery in motion.

### Q: What is the difference between the electoral roll and the elector's photo identity card?

The electoral roll is the legal record of who may vote in a constituency; the photo identity card, the EPIC, is a credential issued to a person whose name stands on the roll. The distinction decides real cases. A person holding a card whose name has been deleted from the roll cannot vote on the strength of the card, because the franchise follows the entry. A person whose name stands on the roll but who has lost the card can still vote, establishing identity at the polling station by one of the other documents the Commission specifies. The card was introduced across the country from 1993 as a protection against impersonation and has become a general identity document, but under the Act it evidences the entry rather than creating the right.

### Q: When do additions and deletions to the roll stop before an election?

They stop, for the constituency concerned, on the last date for making nominations for that election, and the stop lasts until the election is complete. Section 23(3) provides that no amendment, transposition or deletion of an entry shall be made under section 22, and no direction for inclusion shall be given under section 23, after that date and before the completion of the election. The freeze guarantees a fixed electorate for the contest and prevents strategic alteration of the register while it is being fought over. Its cost falls on the individual who turns eighteen, shifts residence or discovers an omission inside the frozen window: the remedy is real but postponed until the election ends.

### Q: What is a draft electoral roll and what happens during the claims and objections period?

The draft roll is the roll as proposed, published at the start of a revision for public inspection in the offices of the registration officers, at designated places including polling stations, and in searchable form online. Publication opens the claims and objections period, a window fixed in the revision programme during which any person may file a claim for inclusion on Form 6, an objection to an entry on Form 7, or a correction or shifting application on Form 8. Lists of claims and objections are displayed and shared with recognised political parties, the Booth Level Officer verifies in the field, and the Electoral Registration Officer decides each matter after summary inquiry before the final roll is published.

### Q: Who is a Booth Level Officer and what work does the officer do in a revision?

The Booth Level Officer is the field level official, usually a teacher, revenue official or other local government employee made available for election work, who covers the electors of one polling station area, the part of the roll for that area. In a revision the officer distributes and collects forms, verifies the particulars of applicants at the household, reports deaths, shifts of residence and absences, assists electors in filing claims and corrections, and displays the draft roll for the part. The officer has no power to add, correct or delete an entry: those decisions belong to the Electoral Registration Officer alone, taken on the officer's own inquiry, of which the Booth Level Officer's report is evidence rather than the decision.

### Q: How are electoral rolls prepared for Legislative Council constituencies?

Section 27 of the Act provides for the rolls of Council constituencies, which are built on electorates defined by the Constitution rather than by general residence alone. For graduates' constituencies the elector must be a graduate of at least three years' standing resident in the constituency; for teachers' constituencies, a person engaged in teaching for at least three years in an institution not lower in standard than a secondary school within the constituency. The rolls are prepared and revised under the Commission's superintendence on applications establishing the professional qualification and residence, while the electorate of the local authorities' constituencies consists of the members of the local authorities listed for the state in the Fourth Schedule to the Act.

### Q: Why does one electoral roll serve both Parliamentary and Assembly elections?

Because section 13D of the Act builds the Parliamentary constituency roll out of the Assembly constituency rolls. A Parliamentary constituency consists of a set of Assembly constituencies, and its electoral roll is simply the rolls of those Assembly constituencies taken together, so a single registration in the Assembly constituency places the elector on the roll for both legislatures. Article 325 of the Constitution reinforces the design by requiring one general electoral roll for every territorial constituency and forbidding separate rolls on grounds of religion, race, caste or sex. The practical consequence is that registration, correction, deletion and the section 24 appeal are single acts with system wide effect: one entry serves every election, and one wrongful deletion excludes the elector from all of them.

### Q: How is a service voter registered, and how does that differ from ordinary registration?

A service voter is registered at the home constituency, not at the place of posting. Section 20 of the Act deems persons holding declared offices, members of the armed forces of the Union, members of a state armed police force serving outside the state, and persons employed under the Government of India in posts outside India to be ordinarily resident in the constituency where they would have been resident but for the service, and the spouse of such a person is deemed ordinarily resident there too if the spouse so elects. The entry is recorded in the service part of the roll. Ordinary registration, by contrast, follows the factual centre of a person's life. The service voter's difference continues at the poll: the 1951 Act allows voting by postal ballot or by proxy, facilities the ordinary elector does not have.

### Q: What happens if a name is registered in two constituencies?

The second registration is one the Act forbids. Section 17 provides that no person shall be entitled to be registered in the electoral roll for more than one constituency, and section 18 bars registration more than once in the same constituency. When the duplication is found, whether through the Commission's de-duplication of the database, an objection or the officer's own inquiry, the entry that should not stand is struck off under section 22 after the hearing the Act requires, ordinarily leaving the entry for the constituency of actual ordinary residence. A person who procures the second registration on a declaration concealing the first also risks prosecution under section 31 for a false declaration, punishable with imprisonment up to one year, or fine, or both.

### Q: Can a civil court correct a wrong entry in the electoral roll?

No. Section 30 of the Act bars the jurisdiction of civil courts over the question whether a person is entitled to be registered in a roll and over anything done under the roll provisions. The correction must be sought inside the Act's own structure: correction or inclusion by the Electoral Registration Officer under sections 22 and 23, and appeal under section 24 to the District Magistrate and then to the Chief Electoral Officer. Article 329 of the Constitution separately bars court interference with an election in progress except through an election petition after the election. Outside the election window, the constitutional courts can be moved under their writ jurisdiction for a substantial public law wrong, but the ordinary wrong entry is for the registration machinery alone.

### Q: What punishment follows a false declaration in a registration application?

Section 31 of the Act makes it an offence, in connection with the preparation, revision or correction of an electoral roll or the inclusion or exclusion of an entry, to make a statement or declaration in writing that is false and that the maker knows or believes to be false or does not believe to be true. The punishment is imprisonment for a term that may extend to one year, or fine, or both. The declaration on every registration form, of citizenship, age, ordinary residence and the absence of another registration, is the statement this section guards, which is why an application that conceals an existing registration elsewhere, or claims a residence the applicant does not have, is a criminal act and not merely a defective form.

### Q: What is the difference between shifting an entry through Form 8 and applying afresh through Form 6?

The two forms answer different legal situations. Form 6 is the application of a new elector, a person whose name stands on no roll and who seeks first inclusion under section 23, with the declaration that the application is a first registration. Form 8 is the application of a person already on a roll who seeks the shifting of residence within or across constituencies, the correction of particulars in an existing entry, the replacement of the identity card, or the marking of disability status; the entry travels and is amended rather than created. Since the forms revision of 1 August 2022, shifting belongs to Form 8 and the older Form 8A for transposition within a constituency stands discontinued. Using the wrong form changes what must be proved and whether the entry's history is carried forward.
