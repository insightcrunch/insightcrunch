One test separates a passing familiarity with American trade law from a working command of the subject. The test asks whether a statute known almost entirely for a national security tariff provision can be recognized as a statute enacted to authorize a multilateral tariff cutting round. The Trade Expansion Act of 1962 passes that test in reverse. Later litigation and later presidential action attached the act to section 232, the provision under which a president may adjust imports that threaten to impair the national security. The Congress that enacted the statute had a different central purpose in view. The House of Representatives passed the bill on June 28, 1962, by a vote of 298 to 125, the Senate passed the bill on September 19, 1962, by a vote of 78 to 8, and President John F. Kennedy signed the act on October 11, 1962. The enacted text is Public Law 87-794, printed at 76 Stat. 872, carried through the 87th Congress as H.R. 11970, and codified at 19 U.S.C. ch. 7.

The occasion was a change in the organization of the European market. The European Economic Community, created by the Treaty of Rome in 1957, was forming a customs union with a common external tariff, and the administration argued that the United States needed authority of a new scale to negotiate with the bloc and keep American goods from being shut out of the European market. The prior authority descended from the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of 1934, renewed 11 times, under which bargaining proceeded item by item. The Dillon Round of 1960 to 1961, conducted under the old method, produced limited results. The long title stated the combination directly, describing a measure to promote the general welfare, foreign policy, and security of the United States through international trade agreements and through adjustment assistance to domestic industry, agriculture, and labor.

![Trade Expansion Act of 1962 and the Instruments of American Trade Policy - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-89.webp)

The first instrument was the negotiating authority at section 201. For five years, running to June 30, 1967, the president could enter trade agreements and proclaim reductions of up to 50 percent of the duty rates in effect on July 1, 1962. No limit applied to reductions of duties already at or below 5 percent ad valorem. A special authority allowed elimination of duties entirely on categories of articles in which the United States and the European Economic Community together accounted for at least 80 percent of world trade, with agricultural products excepted from that rule. Across the board, or linear, percentage reductions replaced product by product bargaining as the negotiating design. The act also created the office of Special Representative for Trade Negotiations, the predecessor of the modern United States Trade Representative. The Kennedy Round, the negotiation the authority enabled, opened at Geneva in May 1964 under the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, drew about 50 participating countries, and concluded with agreements signed on June 30, 1967, the last day of the authority. American linear cuts averaged about 35 percent and covered about 64 percent of non-agricultural dutiable imports, foreign reductions averaged about 34 percent. Agriculture produced limited results, and the round also produced the GATT Antidumping Code of 1967 and a grains arrangement with a food aid program.

The second instrument was section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, codified at 19 U.S.C. 1862. The provision traced to the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1955, was reenacted in 1958 and again in 1962, and was amended by the Trade Act of 1974 without being created by that act. The text authorized the president to adjust imports of an article imported in such quantities or under such circumstances as to threaten to impair the national security, and listed factors concerning domestic production for national defense, capacity, manpower and resources, and the effect of foreign competition on domestic industries, including the economic welfare of the nation. Under the procedure as codified, the Secretary of Commerce investigated within 270 days, the president decided concurrence and remedy within 90 days, and action followed within a further 15 days. The Congressional Research Service counted 24 investigations from 1962 to 1995, and duties on steel and aluminum in 2018 returned the provision to prominence.

The third instrument was adjustment assistance, created at section 301 and following as the first federal program of that kind, and the namable claim of the statute attaches to that instrument. Compensation promised, compensation withheld. The program offered readjustment allowances, retraining, and relocation allowances for workers, loans, loan guarantees, and technical assistance for firms, and assistance for industries and communities, as part of the liberalization bargain. Eligibility required a Tariff Commission determination that increased imports had resulted in major part from concessions granted under trade agreements and had been the major cause of the unemployment, underemployment, or injury. The Commission never found the concessions test satisfied during the first years, no petitions were certified from 1962 to 1969, and through 1974 only 100 petitions covering about 54,000 workers were certified. The Trade Act of 1974 dropped the concessions test, lowered the standard to imports contributing importantly, and moved worker petitions to the Department of Labor.

Litigation completed the preview. In Federal Energy Administration v. Algonquin SNG, Inc., decided on June 17, 1976, the Supreme Court held that section 232 authorized a license fee program on imported oil and that the standards the statute supplied were sufficient against a delegation attack. In American Institute for International Steel v. United States, a three judge panel of the Court of International Trade rejected a facial challenge on March 25, 2019, the Federal Circuit affirmed in February 2020, and the Supreme Court denied certiorari on June 22, 2020. Against the charge that the security provision operated as a loophole, the counter reading carried in the sections that follow holds that Congress enacted the breadth deliberately, in 1955 and again in 1962 and 1974, that the courts declined to narrow the breadth, and that the objection belongs to the delegation Congress made rather than to the use of the delegation. The sections that follow take each instrument in order, through the vantage of August 1, 2020.

## The Occasion: A Customs Union in Europe

### Why did Europe force the question?

Europe forced the question because the European Economic Community was replacing separate national tariffs with one common external tariff, and the United States, bargaining product by product under authority dating from 1934, lacked a method equal to negotiating with a single bloc organized on that scale.

The authority under which the United States conducted trade negotiations in 1962 had a long legislative history. Its foundation was the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of 1934. That statute had moved the making of tariff bargains toward the executive branch by authorizing the president to enter reciprocal agreements with foreign governments and to proclaim the tariff changes those agreements required. Congress retained control through the terms of the authorization itself. The authorization was limited in duration, limited in the reductions it permitted, and dependent on renewal when its period expired. Between the original enactment in 1934 and the early 1960s, Congress renewed the authority 11 times. Each renewal extended a basic design that had been created for a different trading world. The design rested on reciprocity. One government offered a reduction on a product, another government offered a reduction in return, and the resulting bargain was recorded product by product.

The place of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 in [the sequence of trade statutes since 1950](/2020/10/15/us-trade-legislation-since-1950/) therefore cannot be understood apart from that inheritance. The 1962 act was not the first congressional decision to delegate negotiating authority, and it was not a repudiation of the principle established in 1934. It was a judgment that the scale and form of the authority granted in earlier renewals no longer matched the structure of the negotiation the United States faced. Continuity and change were present in the same measure. The continuity lay in the reciprocal premise, in congressional authorization as the source of presidential power, and in the expectation that tariff reductions would be exchanged for reductions by trading partners. The change lay in the unit of negotiation. Under the older design, the product was the unit, tariff lines were taken up one at a time, and concessions were assembled from separate bargains. Its limits appeared when the counterpart across the table was organizing an entire market under one tariff and when the negotiation required movement across broad categories rather than adjustment of isolated lines.

An item by item method also shaped the politics and the arithmetic of a negotiation. A concession on a single product had an identifiable domestic producer, an identifiable foreign beneficiary, and an identifiable effect on the duty collected at the border. That precision narrowed the field of possible agreement. A bargain constructed line by line moved at the speed of the most difficult lines, and an offer that might have been acceptable as part of a broad settlement could fail when isolated, because the gain on one side of the ledger was not placed against gains distributed across many products. The method rewarded caution, and it allowed progress on less sensitive items to stand in place of a general reduction. For bilateral bargaining between national tariff systems, that structure had produced agreements over nearly three decades. For a negotiation with a customs union, the structure presented a different problem.

The change in Europe was institutional before it was commercial. The Treaty of Rome in 1957 created the European Economic Community. The Community was forming a customs union, and a customs union carries a consequence that matters directly to outsiders. Its members align the duties they charge on goods entering from outside, so that an exporter selling into the Community faces a common external tariff rather than a set of separate national tariffs that can be played against one another. Decisions about the height and structure of that external tariff are decisions of the bloc. A government seeking better access for its exporters therefore has to negotiate with the bloc as a bloc. An agreement reached with one member on one product does not settle access to the market as a whole, because the rate that governs entry is the common rate. The negotiation has to engage the common tariff across the range of goods the exporter sells, and it has to do so with an authority broad enough to offer reductions across a comparable range in return.

That institutional fact explains why the European development could not be treated as one more occasion for a routine renewal. A routine renewal would have extended the authority to bargain item by item for another period, and it would not have changed the mismatch between a product by product method at home and a common tariff abroad. The administration of President John F. Kennedy argued that the United States needed authority of a new scale to negotiate with the bloc and to keep American goods from being shut out of the European market. The argument joined a description of Europe to a description of risk. The description of Europe was that a large market was being organized under a single external tariff. The description of risk was that American goods, entering that market from outside, would face the common tariff while goods produced inside the Community moved within it, and that the resulting disadvantage would grow as the customs union took form. Access that had been negotiated country by country and product by product would have to be defended, and improved, in a negotiation with the Community itself.

The risk of exclusion was not a claim that European markets would close in a formal sense. American goods would continue to enter Europe on payment of the duties the common tariff imposed. The risk was comparative. A tariff is a charge on entry, and a charge borne by an outside supplier and not borne in the same way by a supplier inside a customs union alters the terms on which the two compete. If the common external tariff stood at a level set without a bargain with the United States, American exporters would sell under a handicap fixed by decisions in which American concessions had played no part, and the relative position of the outside supplier would deteriorate even where the nominal duty on a given product had not risen. That is why the formation of the customs union forced a question about authority rather than merely a question about rates. The rates that mattered were being set for a bloc, and they would be changed, if they were changed for American benefit, through a negotiation conducted at the scale of the bloc.

Evidence that the old method could not deliver a negotiation of that scale came from the most recent exercise conducted under it. The Dillon Round of 1960 to 1961 was a multilateral negotiation carried on under the inherited authority, and it produced limited results. The outcome carried weight in the debate of 1962 precisely because the round was recent. A negotiation concluded in 1961 under the existing method had reached only limited results at a moment when the European Community was already in formation and when its common external tariff was the central fact American negotiators faced. The inference drawn by the administration was not that negotiation itself had failed. The inference was that the authority under which the negotiation had been conducted had set the boundary of what the negotiation could achieve. Where authority is framed around individual products and limited reductions, the settlement that emerges will reflect those limits. A round that ends with limited results under a limited authority supplies, to a government seeking broader authority, an exhibit. It allows the request to be presented not as dissatisfaction with negotiators but as a conclusion about design.

The Dillon Round also clarified the difference between extending authority and enlarging it. Extension answers a question of time, keeping a power in existence beyond a date on which it would otherwise expire. Enlargement answers a question of capacity, changing what the holder of the power may offer, across how many products, and by how much. The Kennedy administration sought enlargement, on a scale framed for a negotiation with the European Economic Community and for the tariff structure that Community was adopting. In 1962, the question before Congress was therefore whether a method that had governed since 1934 should be replaced as the operating design for the largest negotiation then in prospect, and whether the domestic consequences of the reductions that might follow should be provided for in the same statute that authorized the reductions.

Congressional consideration of the request took place amid persistent unemployment, and skepticism about the proposal was expressed from industry and labor quarters. The record is stated here without characterization of that skepticism. Its presence in the debate is a fact of the legislative history, and its sources matter to an understanding of the statute that emerged. Industry had a direct interest in the duties that protected particular products from import competition, because a reduction negotiated across broad categories could reach products that an item by item process might have left untouched. Labor had a direct interest in the employment consequences of increased imports, because a tariff reduction that expanded imports in a sector could affect the jobs of workers in that sector. Persistent unemployment formed the background against which those interests were pressed, and in a period when unemployment persisted, displacement presented a harder problem than it would have presented under conditions of full employment. Members of Congress hearing industry and labor skepticism were hearing, in concrete form, the domestic side of a bargain whose benefits were stated in general terms. The benefits of access to the European market would be distributed across exporting industries and across the economy. The costs of concessions, where costs fell, would be concentrated on particular firms, particular sectors, and particular communities. That asymmetry was present in the consideration of the 1962 bill in an acute form because the authority requested was broader than the authority granted in earlier renewals.

The statute that emerged from that consideration joined the grant of negotiating authority to a program of assistance for those who bore the domestic costs. That junction was written into the long title of the act. The title described the measure as "An Act to promote the general welfare, foreign policy, and security of the United States through international trade agreements and through adjustment assistance to domestic industry, agriculture, and labor." The phrasing repays attention because it states the design in a single sentence. Three objects are named first, the general welfare, foreign policy, and security. Two instruments are then named, international trade agreements and adjustment assistance. The beneficiaries of the second instrument are specified as domestic industry, agriculture, and labor. A title of that form does not itself confer authority, but it announces the relationship the enacting Congress understood between the parts of the bill. Trade agreements were not presented as an end detached from domestic consequences. Adjustment assistance was not presented as an afterthought appended to a tariff bill. The two were named together as the means by which the stated objects were to be promoted, and the groups to whom assistance was directed were the groups on whom the effects of trade agreements could be expected to fall.

Passage of the bill moved through both chambers in the summer and early autumn of 1962. The House of Representatives passed the measure on June 28, 1962, by a vote of 298 to 125. The Senate passed the measure on September 19, 1962, by a vote of 78 to 8. President Kennedy signed the act on October 11, 1962. The margins record the outcome of the debate without resolving the disagreements the debate contained. A majority of the House voting, and a large majority of the Senate voting, accepted the proposition that authority of a new scale should be granted and that adjustment assistance should accompany the grant. The minority in the House, at 125 votes, showed that the skepticism expressed during consideration had a durable base in that chamber. Taken together, the two votes enacted a design that neither chamber had been asked to approve in that combined form before. Earlier renewals had continued an authority. The 1962 votes created an authority of a different order and attached a federal assistance program to it in the same enactment.

The enacted measure is cited as the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, Public Law 87-794, published at 76 Stat. 872. The bill that became the act was H.R. 11970 in the 87th Congress, and the act is codified at 19 U.S.C. ch. 7. Those identifiers fix the statute in the public record, and identification matters for a statute of this kind because its parts had different lives after enactment. A reader tracing the negotiating authority, a reader tracing the national security provision, and a reader tracing adjustment assistance are tracing three distinct provisions that share a single origin in Public Law 87-794. The shared origin is part of the meaning of each. None of the three was enacted as a freestanding measure. Each was enacted as one element of a settlement reached in 1962 about the terms on which the United States would expand trade.

That settlement had a foreign setting, a legislative setting, and a domestic setting, and the three settings explain one another. The foreign setting was the formation of the European Economic Community under the Treaty of Rome in 1957 and the construction of a common external tariff for a customs union. The legislative setting was the exhaustion of the item by item method inherited from the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of 1934, renewed 11 times and tested in the Dillon Round of 1960 to 1961, which produced limited results. The domestic setting was a debate conducted amid persistent unemployment, in which industry and labor expressed skepticism about broader authority and in which the case for assistance to those affected by imports was heard as part of the case for the bill itself. A statute written in those settings would be expected to do more than renew a power. It would be expected to define a new way of negotiating, to retain a safeguard for cases in which imports bore on national security, and to provide a mechanism for addressing injury at home. The Trade Expansion Act of 1962 did those three things in one text.

The preview can be stated plainly. As a negotiating instrument, the act replaced product by product bargaining as the operating design and substituted authority to make reductions across categories of goods, on a scale intended to match negotiation with a customs union. As a security instrument, the act carried a provision under which imports could be adjusted where they threatened to impair the national security. As a compensation instrument, the act created the first federal program of adjustment assistance, offering support to workers, firms, and industries affected by increased imports, under eligibility tests that determined how far the promise of assistance would in practice be redeemed. The three elements were one bargain. The negotiating authority was the element the administration had requested. The security provision preserved a presidential power to act on imports for reasons of national security within the same statutory scheme. The compensation program answered, in legislative form, the skepticism that industry and labor had expressed during the passage of the bill. To read any one element without the other two is to miss the structure of the settlement of October 11, 1962.

The occasion of the act, then, was a change in the organization of a market across the Atlantic, and its passage was a change in the organization of American trade law. The Treaty of Rome had set in motion the formation of a customs union, and the common external tariff required any outside government seeking improved access to negotiate with the Community as such, across the breadth of the tariff. The inherited American method, built over 11 renewals of a 1934 statute for bargaining item by item and confirmed in its limits by the limited results of the Dillon Round, could not conduct a negotiation on that breadth. Congress, after a debate in which the domestic costs of broader reductions were pressed by industry and labor amid persistent unemployment, granted the requested authority, joined it to adjustment assistance for domestic industry, agriculture, and labor, and enacted the combination as Public Law 87-794. The votes in the House on June 28, 1962, and in the Senate on September 19, 1962, and the signature of President Kennedy on October 11, 1962, closed the legislative process that Europe had opened. What the statute made possible, and what its provisions did in the years after enactment, are the subjects that follow.

## The Method Change: Bargaining by Formula

### What could the president actually do with the new authority?

The president could enter trade agreements for five years and proclaim reductions of up to 50 percent of the duty rates in effect on July 1, 1962, eliminate duties without limit where the rate stood at or below 5 percent ad valorem, and eliminate duties entirely in qualifying dominant supplier categories.

The Trade Expansion Act of 1962 changed the instrument through which the United States conducted tariff bargaining. Earlier authority, descending from the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of 1934 and renewed 11 times, had supported negotiation product by product. A concession on one article was identified, valued, and exchanged for a concession on another article, and the process repeated across the tariff schedule. The 1962 act retained the arrangement under which Congress set the terms of delegation and the president negotiated and proclaimed the results, but the scale and the method of the delegation altered what a negotiation could attempt. Section 201 authorized agreements that assumed a general reduction across broad ranges of trade rather than a sequence of isolated exchanges, and the design pointed negotiators toward a formula that could be applied to many duties at once. The European Economic Community, created by the Treaty of Rome in 1957, was forming a customs union with a common external tariff, and the administration argued that the United States needed authority of a new scale to negotiate with the bloc and keep American goods from being shut out of the European market. A method built for single products faced a partner that presented a single tariff for an entire market, and the mismatch between method and partner formed the practical case for the statute.

The authority at section 201 ran for five years, to June 30, 1967. Within that period the president could enter into trade agreements and proclaim reductions of up to 50 percent of the duty rates in effect on July 1, 1962. Each element of that formulation carried a limiting function as well as a granting function. The five year term fixed the window during which agreements could be concluded under the grant, and the terminal date gave Congress a point at which the delegation expired unless Congress acted again. The baseline date fixed the measure of the concession. A reduction was calculated against the rate in effect on July 1, 1962, rather than against a moving rate that negotiation itself might have altered, and that anchor made the extent of any proclaimed cut verifiable against a known schedule. The ceiling of 50 percent fixed the depth of the cut that the president could proclaim without returning to Congress for further authority. A duty could be halved under the grant, and a deeper reduction required a different legal basis. The combination of a fixed term, a fixed baseline, and a fixed ceiling described a delegation that was broad in coverage and bounded in depth and duration, and that combination shaped the bargaining that followed because foreign governments could read the same limits in the statute and calculate the maximum concession the American negotiator could deliver under the authority in hand.

A second provision removed the ceiling for the lowest duties. Section 201 placed no limit on reductions of duties already at or below 5 percent ad valorem. For that class of articles the president could reduce the duty without the 50 percent restriction and could eliminate the duty altogether. The practical significance lay in the character of low duties as obstacles to trade. A duty of 4 percent or 3 percent yielded limited revenue and limited protection when measured against the administrative cost of assessing and collecting the duty and against the friction the duty imposed on shipments that crossed borders repeatedly as components and materials. Authority to remove such duties entirely allowed negotiators to clear away a large number of small barriers in a single agreement without consuming bargaining credit that higher duties required. The provision also simplified the formula problem. A linear reduction applied to a very low rate produced a fraction that had little economic meaning and considerable administrative awkwardness, while outright elimination produced a clean result that customs administration could apply without rounding disputes. The distinction between duties above the threshold and duties at or below the threshold therefore gave the negotiator two tools within one grant, a proportional tool for the main body of the schedule and an eliminative tool for the margin where proportionality served no useful purpose.

The act added a special authority directed at the structure of transatlantic trade. The president could eliminate duties entirely on categories of articles in which the United States and the European Economic Community together accounted for at least 80 percent of world trade. The condition identified categories in which two parties to a negotiation dominated world commerce to such a degree that an agreement between those parties effectively settled the terms of trade for the category as a whole. Where the combined share reached the threshold, concessions exchanged between other parties could not undermine the bargain to a material degree, because the remaining share of world trade was by definition no more than 20 percent. Elimination, rather than reduction, was the authorized result in such categories, and the depth of the authorized result reflected the dominance condition that triggered the authority. Agricultural products were excepted from that 80 percent rule. The exception removed farm goods from the special eliminative authority even where the combined share condition might otherwise have been met, and the exception recorded a congressional judgment that agricultural trade presented distinct problems that the general dominant supplier design was not to govern. The special authority therefore operated as a targeted instrument within the broader grant. The general authority allowed halving, the low duty provision allowed elimination at the bottom of the schedule, and the dominant supplier provision allowed elimination in defined categories where transatlantic dominance made elimination a negotiable outcome between the two parties principally concerned.

The mechanics of the older method explain why a new grant of authority required a new method of bargaining. Item by item bargaining meant that each product generated a separate contest. Negotiators had to assemble requests and offers article by article, assess the value of a concession on a particular good against the domestic cost of admitting competing imports of that good, and defend or attack each line of the schedule on its own record. A tariff schedule contained thousands of distinct items, and a comprehensive negotiation conducted on that basis required thousands of separate product fights, each with its own producers, importers, and foreign suppliers attached to the outcome. The process consumed time in proportion to the breadth of the ambition. A narrow negotiation covering a limited range of goods could proceed item by item within a manageable period, while a negotiation intended to liberalize trade across the whole range of industrial commerce faced an administrative burden that grew with every additional article placed on the table. The 1960 to 1961 Dillon Round, conducted under the old method, produced limited results, and that record formed part of the background against which the case for a different method was made. Limited results under a method that required separate treatment of each item suggested a constraint in the method itself, apart from the willingness of the parties to liberalize, because the cost of negotiating each concession separately limited the number of concessions that a round of fixed duration could process.

Chief supplier rules compounded the burden and narrowed the reach of item by item bargaining. Under that practice a concession on an article was negotiated principally with the country that supplied the largest share of imports of that article, on the reasoning that the chief supplier captured most of the benefit of the concession and should therefore pay for the concession with a corresponding concession in return. The rule gave each product fight a designated counterpart and excluded other suppliers from the central exchange for that article, even though a concession once proclaimed generally extended to other trading partners through the structure of the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade. The result was a negotiation fragmented not only by product but also by bilateral pairing, with each article assigned to a particular supplier relationship and each exchange balanced within that pairing. Such a design made broad tradeoffs difficult to construct. A concession that cost one domestic industry heavily could not readily be balanced against a gain in an unrelated sector negotiated with a different partner, because the accounting of give and take proceeded within narrow channels. Negotiators who sought a general improvement in market access across many sectors found the chief supplier framework resistant to the kind of package settlement in which losses in one area were accepted for gains in another and the package as a whole was judged on its aggregate balance.

A linear formula cut addressed both problems by changing the unit of negotiation from the individual article to the rate structure as a whole. Under a linear approach the parties agreed on a percentage reduction to be applied across the board to a defined range of duties, subject to exceptions that each party identified and defended. The negotiation then centered on the size of the general cut, the range of products the cut covered, and the list of exceptions claimed from the cut, rather than on the rate for each article taken in isolation. The administrative gain was direct. One agreement on a formula settled the treatment of thousands of items at once, and the remaining work concentrated on the exceptions, which were the items for which a government contended that the general rule should not apply. That inversion of the default carried negotiating significance beyond administrative convenience. Under item by item bargaining the default was the existing rate, and liberalization occurred only where negotiators affirmatively constructed a concession. Under a formula approach the default was reduction, and protection continued only where a government affirmatively constructed an exception and sustained the claim against the pressure of partners who sought a short exceptions list. The burden of justification moved from the party seeking a cut to the party seeking to avoid a cut, and the movement of that burden altered the dynamics of a round even before any particular rate was proclaimed.

The formula method also made a broad multilateral round administratively feasible in a sense that item by item bargaining could not match. A round that included about 50 participating countries, as the Kennedy Round later did, multiplied the bilateral pairings that a product by product method required, because each article in each pairing presented a separate exchange to be valued and balanced. A formula applied multilaterally avoided that multiplication. Participating governments negotiated a common pattern of reduction and then applied the pattern within a single framework, with exceptions and special treatments negotiated inside the framework rather than through a separate bargain for every line. The Kennedy Round, the multilateral round the authority enabled, opened at Geneva in May 1964 under the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade and concluded with agreements signed on June 30, 1967, the last day of the authority. American linear cuts in that round averaged about 35 percent and covered about 64 percent of non-agricultural dutiable imports, while foreign reductions averaged about 34 percent. Those figures illustrate the method in operation. An average reduction in the range of one third, applied across nearly two thirds of dutiable non-agricultural trade, represented a settlement expressed as a general movement of rates rather than as a catalogue of individual bargains, and concessions in the round were staged in five annual installments beginning January 1, 1968. Staging spread the adjustment over time.

Treatment of the European Economic Community as a single partner followed from the same logic and from the institutional fact of the common external tariff. The Community negotiated on the basis of a common tariff for its member states, and a negotiation that attempted to proceed member by member or product by product against that common structure would have fragmented a partner that presented itself as a unit. Linear authority allowed the American negotiator to address a general reduction to the Community as a whole and to receive a general reduction from the Community as a whole, matching the form of the authority to the form of the counterpart. The dominant supplier provision reinforced that treatment in the categories where the combined American and Community share of world trade reached at least 80 percent, because in those categories the two parties together constituted the market to a degree that made a bilateral settlement between the bloc and the United States the effective multilateral settlement.

The limits Congress wrote into the delegation deserve attention equal to the grant, because the limits defined the authority as a congressional instrument rather than an open transfer of the tariff power. The percentage ceiling meant that the president entered negotiation with a known maximum concession and no capacity under the act to promise a deeper general cut, however advantageous a deeper cut might have appeared in the course of bargaining. The fixed expiry meant that the authority carried its own deadline into the negotiation. Agreements had to be concluded by June 30, 1967, and the Kennedy Round in fact concluded on that date, a coincidence of statutory deadline and diplomatic settlement that showed the expiry operating as a real constraint on timing rather than as a formal notation. The baseline date performed a similar disciplining function for measurement. Because reductions were calculated from rates in effect on July 1, 1962, neither an intervening administrative change nor the negotiation itself could shift the starting point from which the authorized percentage was computed. Congress also retained distinctions among classes of trade within the grant itself, as the agricultural exception to the 80 percent rule demonstrated. A delegation that distinguished farm products from industrial categories, low duties from higher duties, and dominant supplier categories from the general schedule was a delegation drafted with care at the level of design, even though the negotiation it authorized proceeded by formula at the level of execution. Broad but bounded captures the structure accurately. The breadth lay in coverage, in the proportion of the schedule a formula could reach in one agreement. The bounds lay in depth, duration, and category, and each bound remained in the text for a foreign negotiator, a domestic producer, or a reviewing reader to measure a proclaimed result against.

Congress paired the delegation with a new negotiating office designed for the scale of the enterprise. The act created the office of Special Representative for Trade Negotiations as the president's negotiator, and a presidential proclamation issued for the fiftieth anniversary of the act in 2012 confirmed that establishment as one of the act's institutional legacies. A dedicated representative recognized that formula bargaining across the whole schedule, conducted with a customs union and within a multilateral round, required a negotiator whose responsibility centered on trade negotiation as a continuing function rather than as an occasional assignment distributed among departments with other primary missions. The Special Representative stood as the predecessor of the modern United States Trade Representative. A single negotiator also gave Congress and foreign governments an identifiable counterpart, and formula bargaining over a package required a negotiator positioned to hold the package together.

Reporting and consultation duties owed to Congress completed the structure of control that accompanied the delegation. A grant of authority to halve duties across broad ranges of trade, exercised over five years, placed Congress in the position of authorizing results that Congress did not itself negotiate line by line, and reporting and consultation supplied the channel through which the legislative branch followed the exercise of the authority and conveyed congressional concerns while negotiation remained in progress. Consultation placed congressional views before the negotiator during the formation of positions, when those views could affect the size of an exceptions list or the treatment of a sensitive category, and reporting placed the record of the exercise before Congress for assessment of whether the delegation had been used within its terms. Congress set the ceiling, the term, the baseline, and the categories in the statute, the president exercised the authority through the Special Representative, and reporting and consultation maintained a flow of information between the two branches proportionate to the breadth of the grant.

The act retained a safeguard for cases in which liberalization produced injury that the general settlement had not absorbed. Under the retained escape clause, duties could be raised again for an injured industry, and the president could also raise duties by up to 50 percent of the 1934 rates in the escape clause setting. The clause functioned as a pressure release within the liberalizing design, since a government that could raise a duty again in a defined case of injury could accept a general reduction with greater confidence. The escape clause addressed the consequences of concessions already made, while section 201 created the capacity to make concessions on a new scale.

The relationship between the authority and a later generation of negotiating grants appears in [the history of trade promotion authority](/2020/08/15/trade-promotion-authority-explained/), where the recurring congressional problem of delegating negotiating power while retaining control over the result received repeated statutory treatment. The 1962 act supplied an early and influential answer to that problem. Congress delegated by formula and by ceiling rather than by schedule of individual rates, accepted a dedicated negotiator as the instrument of the delegation, and relied on expiry, reporting, and consultation to keep the delegation accountable.

The methodological innovation that shaped the following decades of negotiation was the across the board authority itself. Product by product bargaining had treated the tariff as a list, and each concession as an amendment to one entry on the list negotiated with one principal supplier. Formula authority treated the tariff as a level, and a negotiation as a movement of that level across a defined range, with exceptions argued as departures from a general movement. That shift in conception outlasted the particular percentages and dates of the 1962 grant. Negotiations after the Kennedy Round continued to confront the same administrative reality that thousands of individual bargains could not liberalize trade at the scale modern commerce presented, and continued to rely on general formulas, average reductions, and coverage measures to describe settlements. The five year term of section 201 expired on June 30, 1967, and the specific ceilings and baselines of the grant expired with the term, but the method the grant embodied, bargaining by formula under a bounded congressional delegation administered by a dedicated trade negotiator, remained the template against which later authority was drafted and debated. As a grant, the act belonged to a defined period that closed in 1967. As a method, the act established the form of negotiating authority that the period after 1962 repeatedly returned to.

## The Round the Authority Bought

The multilateral round that the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 made possible opened at Geneva in May 1964 under the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade. Geneva placed the bargaining inside an established multilateral framework rather than inside a series of separate bilateral exchanges, and the General Agreement supplied the institutional home within which concessions offered by one participant could be framed as part of a single general settlement. The American authority had been written for that kind of negotiation. The five year authority in the 1962 act ran to June 30, 1967, and permitted reductions of up to 50 percent of the duty rates in effect on July 1, 1962. A delegation of that breadth had little purpose if bargaining remained confined to narrow exchanges over isolated products. Its purpose appeared when the delegation was carried into a room in which a general formula could be proposed, tested against exceptions, and pressed toward a closing date fixed by statute.

The round took the name of the president whose administration had won the authority and who had died in November 1963. The name carried a factual connection rather than a merely commemorative one. The Kennedy administration had sought the new negotiating authority, had argued that the older item by item method no longer matched the structure of trade facing the United States, and had obtained from Congress a delegation framed for across the board bargaining. The administration that followed inherited both the authority and the term within which the authority had to be used. By the time the round opened at Geneva in May 1964, the negotiation rested on a statute associated with a president who could no longer direct it, on a deadline that the statute had fixed in advance, and on a method that departed in a deliberate way from practice under the authority descended from the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of 1934.

The occasion for that authority lay in Europe as well as in method. The European Economic Community, created by the Treaty of Rome in 1957, was forming a customs union with a common external tariff, and the Kennedy administration argued that the United States needed authority of a new scale to negotiate with the bloc and to keep American goods from being shut out of the European market. A customs union changed the counterpart in a negotiation. Individual European rates mattered less as a common external tariff took shape, and American bargaining addressed to single products in single countries fit that counterpart poorly. The prior authority had been renewed 11 times, and bargaining under it proceeded item by item. The Dillon Round of 1960 to 1961, conducted under the old method, produced limited results. The lesson drawn by the sponsors of the 1962 act was that procedure itself constrained outcome. The linear method answered that constraint by changing the unit of negotiation from the individual product to the tariff as a whole, subject to exceptions that each participant would name.

### What did the Kennedy Round actually deliver?

The Kennedy Round delivered broad formula tariff cuts rather than item by item bargains, with American linear reductions averaging about 35 percent, foreign reductions averaging about 34 percent, concessions staged from January 1, 1968, a limited agricultural result, an antidumping code, and a grains arrangement with food aid.

The linear method in practice began with a formula offer of equal percentage cuts, subject to exceptions lists. The sequence deserves attention because the order of the steps shaped the bargaining. A participant did not open by naming the products on which concessions might be granted and then invite requests on each one. A participant opened with a general offer expressed as a percentage, applicable across dutiable trade as a starting proposition, and then defended the products placed on an exceptions list. That reversal changed the burden inside the negotiation. Under item by item bargaining, a product remained untouched unless a concession on that product was affirmatively proposed and agreed. Under the linear method, a product fell inside the general cut unless an exception for that product was sustained. Protection therefore had to be justified line by line against a general rule, while liberalization proceeded from the general rule outward.

Exceptions lists were the necessary counterpart to a formula, and also the point at which the formula could be weakened. No participant treated the general offer as an unconditional surrender of every sensitive rate, and the 1962 act itself recognized differentiated treatment in parts of its design, including authority without limit for reductions of duties already at or below 5 percent ad valorem and a special authority, with agriculture excepted, to eliminate duties entirely on categories in which the United States and the European Economic Community together accounted for at least 80 percent of world trade. Exceptions also created a second negotiation inside the first. Each list had to be examined for its size, for the trade covered by the listed products, and for the effect of the list on the value of the offer that remained after the listed products were removed. A large percentage cut attached to a long exceptions list could be worth less than a smaller percentage cut attached to a short one. Participants therefore bargained over the formula and over the boundaries of the formula at the same time, and the measured results at the close of the round can only be understood as the product of both contests together.

The dispute over disparities exposed the deepest difficulty inside an apparently neutral formula. The European Community argued that equal percentage cuts left the protective effect of the higher American tariffs substantially intact while reducing the Community's own lower tariffs by the same percentage. American duties then averaged about 18 percent and the Community's about 12 percent. The round record presents the point as a contention between participants about the meaning of equal treatment rather than as a proposition that the percentages alone can resolve. An equal percentage applied to rates of different heights produces reductions that are equal in proportion and unequal in other respects that negotiators had reason to weigh. A high rate reduced by a given percentage retains a substantial absolute level after the reduction, and a low rate reduced by the same percentage approaches the point at which the remaining duty offers little margin at all. The European Community contention treated that difference as central to equivalence. On that contention, a formula that looked identical on paper imposed different practical consequences on schedules that began at different heights, and genuine reciprocity required attention to the height of rates and to the protective effect that remained, not only to the percentage written into the offer.

The scale of participation widened every one of those difficulties and also supplied the reason for accepting them. About 50 countries participated in the round. A negotiation among that number of participants could not reproduce the sequence of a bilateral bargain, and the linear method was in part an administrative answer to scale. A formula allowed dozens of schedules to be addressed within one general proceeding, while exceptions lists gave each participant a defined place in which particular sensitivities could be recorded and contested. That breadth helps account for both the achievement and the limits recorded at the close. Agreement had to remain acceptable to a large number of governments with different schedules, different sensitive products, and different readings of equivalence, including the disparities reading advanced by the European Community. A settlement that could hold together across about 50 participants had to rely on a general formula flexible enough to admit exceptions, and that same flexibility set bounds on uniformity.

Time pressed on the negotiation from the statute outward. The American authority expired on June 30, 1967, and a negotiator bargaining under the 1962 act could not promise that an unfinished settlement would be completed under the same delegation after that date. The closing phase reflected that pressure. The round moved through marathon closing sessions in May 1967, in which the remaining questions had to be reduced to terms that governments could accept before the American authority ran out. Marathon sessions in a multilateral negotiation serve a function distinct from ordinary deliberation. Remaining exceptions, contested equivalences, and unsettled products accumulate during the long middle of a round because each delegation has reason to hold a concession in reserve against a later need. A fixed end changes that calculation. A reserve held past the closing moment loses value if the settlement itself fails for want of agreement, and a delegation facing a deadline had to decide which reserves to spend and which objections to abandon while a settlement remained reachable. The May 1967 sessions therefore tested whether the formula, the exceptions, and the disputed treatment of disparities could be brought into a package in time.

The agreements were signed on June 30, 1967, the last day of the statutory authority. The date was not an incidental detail in the history of the round. Signature on the final day meant that the negotiation had consumed the full term Congress had granted, and that the settlement arrived at the boundary of the delegation rather than comfortably inside it. A delegation with no terminal date would have changed the incentives in the closing weeks, because delay would have carried a lower cost for a participant that preferred a contested point to the settlement available in May and June 1967. Under the 1962 act, delay carried the risk that the American authority underpinning the central offer would lapse. Signature on June 30, 1967 closed the negotiation at the exact point at which that risk became immediate, and the sequence supplies the clearest evidence in the round for the disciplining effect of a dated delegation.

The measured industrial result was substantial in breadth and close in balance between the American offer and the offers received. American linear cuts averaged about 35 percent and covered about 64 percent of non-agricultural dutiable imports. Foreign reductions averaged about 34 percent. Each figure requires the qualifiers attached to it. The American figure is an average of linear cuts, not a claim that every rate fell by 35 percent, and exceptions lists explain part of the distance between a formula offer framed around a higher authorized ceiling and an average result below that ceiling. The foreign average of about 34 percent stood close to the American average, and that proximity allowed the settlement to be presented as broadly reciprocal in percentage terms. The disparities dispute gives that proximity a necessary caution. Close averages established closeness on the measure the formula used, and the European Community argument had denied that the formula measure alone captured equivalence between schedules of different heights. Both statements belong in an account of the result, the averages as the recorded outcome and the dispute as the recorded objection to reading the averages as a complete account of balance.

The concessions were staged in five annual installments beginning January 1, 1968. Staging separated the legal conclusion of the round from the full economic arrival of its concessions, and that separation had practical consequences for firms, workers, and administering authorities. A duty reduction divided into five annual steps gave traders a known schedule on which to plan purchases, contracts, and production, rather than a single reduction on one date for which every adjustment had to be made at once. The first installment on January 1, 1968 placed the opening effect of the round about six months after signature, and the remaining installments carried the settlement forward in annual stages. The design fitted the character of the bargain. A formula negotiation that had taken years to conclude produced reductions that entered into force gradually, and the gradual entry moderated the abruptness of a general cut while preserving the certainty that the full agreed reduction would arrive on dates named in advance.

Agriculture stood apart from the industrial achievement, and the record states the outcome plainly as limited. The thin agricultural result contrasted with the breadth of the linear cuts on non-agricultural trade, and the contrast was built into the negotiation from several directions. The 1962 act had excepted agricultural products from the special 80 percent authority described earlier in this section, a statutory signal that agriculture occupied a distinct place in the delegation itself. Farm trade also raised questions that a tariff formula addressed only in part, because arrangements outside a simple duty rate shaped access to agricultural markets in many participating countries. The round record therefore supports a divided judgment on coverage. On industrial goods the formula produced averaged cuts of about 35 percent and about 34 percent across a large share of dutiable trade. On agriculture the same conference produced limited results, and any summary that merged the two fields into a single claim of general success would misstate the settlement. The Kennedy Round was a major tariff negotiation whose principal measured achievement lay outside agriculture, and the agreements reflected that division.

Two further products of the round stood outside the tariff averages and deserve separate statement. The round produced the GATT Antidumping Code of 1967, an agreement addressed to the rules governing antidumping action rather than to the height of ordinary customs duties. The round also produced a grains arrangement with a food aid program. The grains arrangement belonged to the agricultural side of the conference, where tariff results had been limited, and the food aid program attached a commitment concerning food assistance to an arrangement about a basic commodity. Neither product can be folded into the percentages that summarize the industrial bargain. The antidumping code concerned rules, the grains arrangement concerned a specific sector, and the tariff averages concerned linear reductions in duties. A complete account of delivery has to hold the three apart while recognizing that all three issued from the same closing settlement signed on June 30, 1967.

The structural lesson of the round concerns the deadline written into a delegation. Congress granted authority for a defined term, the term ended on June 30, 1967, and the negotiation concluded with signature on that final day after marathon closing sessions in May 1967. A deadline of that kind disciplines a negotiation in ways that a general exhortation to conclude cannot reproduce. A deadline fixes the moment at which reserves lose value, concentrates the settlement of exceptions and disputed equivalences into a closing period, and gives every participant the same knowledge that delay risks the lapse of the authority on which the central offers depend. The Trade Expansion Act of 1962 therefore bought more than tariff reductions with its five year term. The term bought a closing. The Kennedy Round delivered averaged industrial cuts of about 35 percent from the United States and about 34 percent from foreign participants, coverage of about 64 percent of American non-agricultural dutiable imports, staged concessions from January 1, 1968, a limited agricultural result, an antidumping code, and a grains arrangement with food aid, and the delivery arrived on the last day the statute allowed.

## The Security Provision in Text and Procedure

### Who decides a case under the security provision, and by what steps?

The Secretary of Commerce investigates, consulting the Secretary of Defense, and reports within two hundred seventy days. The president then has ninety days to concur or not and to determine the nature and duration of any action, and action follows within fifteen days.

The provision is section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, codified at 19 U.S.C. 1862. Codification placed the authority in the part of the United States Code devoted to customs duties and trade, and the placement described the reach of the power. The section addressed an article in import trade, and action under the section ran against the importation of that article. The text did not direct domestic production, allocate output, or command the operation of a plant. A domestic industry entered a case through the effect that imports had on production, capacity, and welfare inside the United States, rather than as the direct object of a command. The investigator examined an imported article and the conditions surrounding importation. The president, after the required finding, adjusted the importation itself.

The statutory trigger combined quantity, circumstance, and risk in a single standard. The provision authorized the president to adjust imports of an article imported "in such quantities or under such circumstances as to threaten to impair the national security". Quantity and circumstance stood in the alternative, so a case could rest on the volume of a flow of goods or on the conditions that accompanied that flow. Threaten placed the inquiry at the stage of risk, because the text required a threat to impair security rather than completed impairment. Impair described a weakening of security rather than a total defeat of a defense purpose. A determination therefore rested on a judgment that importation, by scale or by surrounding conditions, created the stated threat. A large flow could support a case through displacement, as foreign supply took the place of domestic production and left capacity idle or allowed capacity to disappear. A smaller flow could raise a question of dependence on a source that might be interrupted, or of a relationship between an imported input and a defense need that volume alone did not capture. The text did not rank the two routes, and quantity and circumstance could combine in a single investigation.

Lineage clarified the origin of the standard and prevented an error of attribution. The provision traced to the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1955. It was reenacted in 1958. It was carried into the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 at section 232. The Trade Act of 1974 amended the provision at its section 127(d). The 1974 act amended the provision and did not create it, and attribution of section 232 to the Trade Act of 1974 as its source misstates the record by two decades. The authority exercised under the codified section after 1974 rested on a text first enacted in the middle of the 1950s, renewed before the end of that decade, and placed in the 1962 act as part of a larger settlement over negotiating power and domestic adjustment. The 1974 amendment belongs in an account of administration and timing rather than in an account of origin.

The factors that the statute required the investigator and the president to take into account gave the inquiry working content. Section 232(d) listed domestic production for national defense, capacity, manpower, resources, and the effect of foreign competition on domestic industries, including the economic welfare of the nation. Domestic production for national defense asked whether production inside the United States supplied the articles and materials that defense required, and whether importation displaced or endangered that production. Capacity asked a distinct question about the ability to produce, apart from the level of production at a given moment. An industry could retain plants, equipment, and processes during a period of reduced output, or could lose the ability to expand output while a reduced level of production continued. An investigation that looked only at output in a single period missed the capacity question, and an investigation that looked only at installed capacity missed the question whether that capacity was staffed, supplied, and maintained in a condition to serve a defense need.

Manpower and resources extended the examination beyond plants and output figures. Manpower directed attention to the labor force that production required, including the skills that a defense related industry depended on and the effect that contraction of the industry had on the availability of those skills for later production. A line of production that closed took with it experience held by workers and managers, and the loss registered through the manpower factor even where physical equipment remained in place. Resources directed attention to the materials, energy, and other inputs that production consumed, and to the relationship between imported articles and the domestic resource base that supported an industry. The two factors made the inquiry an examination of an industrial system rather than of a single transaction. Imports entered the account because importation affected the system, and the system mattered because defense production depended on facilities, people, and inputs in combination.

The effect of foreign competition on domestic industries and on the economic welfare of the nation widened the account further. Foreign competition was the mechanism that connected importation to domestic consequence, because imports competed with domestic articles, affected price and market share, and influenced decisions about investment, employment, and the continuation of production. The statute placed that competitive effect inside a security provision. Economic injury described in purely commercial terms was not the stated test, and economic effect remained a required part of the inquiry that led to a security judgment. The reference to the economic welfare of the nation carried the examination past the condition of a single firm or plant. It invited evidence about the place of an industry in the larger economy and about the consequences that a weakening of the industry had for income, employment, and the support of other production. The factors moved from defense production outward through capacity, labor, and resources to competition and general welfare, and the movement gave the security judgment an economic foundation stated openly in the text.

Against that list stood an absence that defined much of the provision. The statute did not define national security beyond the factors that the investigator and the president were required to take into account. No separate definition supplied a closed set of qualifying industries, a threshold percentage of import penetration, a measure of dependence that decided a case by arithmetic, or a boundary that separated a security article from every other article in trade. The factors guided the inquiry without exhausting the meaning of the central term. Guidance and definition performed different work. Guidance told the decision maker where to look and what evidence to weigh. Definition told the decision maker what a term meant and where its reach ended. Section 232 supplied guidance in the form of a factor list and withheld a definition that would have converted the security judgment into the application of a formula. The content of the standard in a particular case depended on the record assembled under the listed factors, applied to a particular article and a particular industry.

The absence of a definition also allocated interpretive authority. Because the text did not resolve the security question in advance, the investigation had to build the connection between an imported article and national security from evidence about production, capacity, labor, resources, and competition, and the president then judged that connection in deciding whether to concur and what action followed. A defined term would have shifted more of the decision to the words of the statute and less to the record of the case. An undefined term, bounded by a factor list and by a procedural sequence, placed the weight of a case on the investigation and on the stated reasons that the procedure required. That allocation explains the attention that the design gave to the identity of the investigating officer, to consultation inside the executive branch, and to fixed periods for report, decision, and action. Where the substantive term remained open, procedure supplied much of the discipline that the statute imposed.

The identity of the investigating officer changed across the life of the provision, and each change altered the institutional home of the inquiry without altering the location of the final decision. In the original design, the advisory responsibility sat with the Director of the Office of Emergency Preparedness. That placement associated the investigation with an office concerned with preparedness and mobilization, a setting in which questions about production for defense, capacity, and the readiness of industry occupied a central place. The president received an investigation conducted from that vantage, and the arrangement matched the earliest form of the provision, in which an advisory function preceded presidential action.

The Trade Act of 1974, at section 127(d), moved the investigating responsibility to the Secretary of the Treasury and added a one year investigation limit. The move was an amendment to an existing provision, and the distinction between creation and amendment governed the attribution of every later feature of the section. The Treasury phase placed the inquiry in the department that carried major responsibilities for financial and economic policy, and that phase ran from the 1974 amendment until the transfer in 1988. The one year limit changed the character of the investigation in a practical respect. An inquiry acquired a deadline, and a deadline imposed choices about the scope of evidence, the pace of consultation, and the point at which a record closed and a report went forward. A limit of one year remained generous in comparison with the period that followed, and the comparison shows the direction of later amendments toward a shorter and more tightly sequenced proceeding.

The Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988, at section 402, moved the investigation to the Secretary of Commerce, reduced the investigation period to 270 days, and created the 15 day implementation period. The Commerce phase placed the inquiry in the department associated in the executive branch with industry, trade administration, and the condition of domestic producers, and that placement aligned the investigating officer with the factor list in the statute. Questions about domestic production, industrial capacity, manpower, resources, and competitive effect called for information about firms, plants, markets, and supply relationships of the kind that the Commerce Department gathered in the ordinary course of trade administration. The transfer did not move the decision itself. The president retained the concurrence decision, the determination of the nature and duration of action, and the statement of reasons to Congress. The 1988 amendment changed the officer who built the record and shortened the time for building that record, while the office that acted on the record remained the presidency.

Procedure as codified after those amendments began with initiation, and the text supplied three routes into a case. An investigation could begin on application. An investigation could begin at the request of the head of an agency. An investigation could begin on the motion of the Secretary of Commerce. The three routes distributed the power to start a proceeding across actors outside the government, across other parts of the executive branch, and across the investigating department itself. An application allowed a party outside the investigating office to present a claim that importation threatened national security and to ask for the inquiry that the statute provided. A request from an agency head allowed another part of the executive branch to place a matter before the Secretary. A motion by the Secretary allowed the department to open a case without an outside application or an interagency request. No single gatekeeper controlled access to the proceeding, and no route by itself decided an outcome, because initiation opened an investigation rather than established the statutory finding.

Consultation with the Secretary of Defense stood near the center of the investigation. The Secretary of Commerce investigated, and the statute directed consultation with the Secretary of Defense and with other officials. Defense consultation supplied an assessment from the department charged with military requirements, and that assessment bore directly on domestic production for national defense and on related questions of capacity and readiness. Other officials brought information within the responsibilities of other agencies, so a case touching another field could draw on the agency that administered that field. Consultation did not transfer the investigation away from Commerce, and it did not give a consulting official a separate decision under the statute. Consultation structured the record so that the report to the president rested on more than the view of a single department, in a proceeding where the finding concerned security rather than trade advantage alone.

The report was due within 270 days of initiation. That period governed investigation, consultation, analysis under the factor list, and preparation of the report that went to the president. A fixed period forced a distinction between evidence that belonged in the record and inquiry that might continue without a defined end. The date of initiation and the length of the period together fixed the date by which the report was owed. Under the Treasury design the corresponding period had been one year, and the reduction to 270 days in 1988 shortened the interval between the opening of a case and the receipt of the completed investigation by the president. The shortening expressed a preference for a proceeding that moved from application or request through evidence to decision inside a bounded span, rather than a proceeding that remained open while market and industrial conditions changed around an unresolved claim.

After the report reached the president, a second clock governed the presidential stage. The president had 90 days to concur or not concur in the finding and to determine the nature and duration of any action. The concurrence requirement made the investigation advisory rather than self executing. A report that found a threat did not by itself adjust an import, impose a duty, or limit a quantity, because the statute required the president to decide whether the finding commanded agreement and, upon agreement, what response the case required. A decision against concurrence ended the proceeding without action under the section. A decision in favor of concurrence opened the remedy question, and the statute joined that question to the concurrence stage by requiring a determination of nature and duration within the same 90 day period. Nature addressed the kind of action. Duration addressed the length of time that the action remained in force, a matter that the statute left to the determination rather than fixed in advance for every case.

Action followed within a further 15 days. The implementation period, created by the 1988 amendment, separated decision from execution by a short and defined interval. That interval allowed the instrument that carried the action to be prepared and issued in orderly form, and it prevented a long gap between a presidential determination and the adjustment that the determination required. The sequence from report to concurrence to action ran on two presidential clocks in succession, 90 days for decision and 15 days for implementation, after the 270 days allowed for investigation. Read together, the three periods supplied the timetable of a case under the codified procedure, and each period attached to a different function. Commerce held the first period. The president held the second and third periods, with the second devoted to judgment and the third devoted to execution.

A statement to Congress closed the sequence. The president stated the reasons for the action to Congress within 30 days. The statement ran to the legislature after determination and action, rather than before either step as a request for permission. That ordering preserved the character of the authority as a delegated presidential power exercised through a statutory procedure, with a report to Congress as an element of accountability inside the procedure. The reasons statement gave Congress the basis on which the president had acted, in a form that allowed the legislature to judge the use of the authority, to consider amendment of the provision, or to pursue the specific check that a later statute supplied for one class of cases. A proceeding that omitted the statement left the codified sequence incomplete, because the statute wrote the statement into the procedure alongside investigation and decision.

The remedies available after a finding and a concurrence illustrated the breadth that the trigger created. The statute set the trigger and the procedure but left the finding and the choice of remedy to the president, and the menu of remedies included duties, quotas, license fees, and negotiated arrangements. A duty raised the price of the imported article by adding a charge at entry, and a duty could alter the competitive relationship between the imported article and the domestic article without fixing a numerical ceiling on importation. A quota limited quantity directly, by allowing importation up to a stated amount and restricting importation beyond that amount, so the adjustment operated on volume rather than on price. The two instruments reached the same statutory object through different mechanisms, and the choice between the two instruments carried consequences for revenue, for price, for the allocation of supply among importers and purchasers, and for the response of supplier countries.

License fees and negotiated arrangements completed the menu and showed that adjustment extended beyond duty and quota. A license fee attached a charge to permission to import, so importation continued under a licensing arrangement while the fee altered the cost of bringing the article into the United States. A negotiated arrangement took a different form, because adjustment followed from an arrangement reached with supplier countries rather than from a unilateral proclamation of a duty or quota alone. An arrangement of that character could limit or condition shipments at the source, and the statute treated a negotiated result as a form of action available after the finding, alongside instruments that the United States imposed directly. Details of [how the tariff remedies operate in administration](/2020/10/01/us-tariff-law-administration/) belong to the administration of the customs laws, while the place of those remedies in section 232 depended on the finding and procedure described in this section.

Congress wrote one check of a specific kind into the later history of the provision, and the check was confined to petroleum. The Crude Oil Windfall Profit Tax Act of 1980, Public Law 96-223, at section 402, created a joint resolution of disapproval route against presidential petroleum adjustments. Under that route, a presidential adjustment affecting petroleum could be met by a joint resolution through which Congress expressed disapproval under the procedure that the 1980 act supplied. The route applied to petroleum only. The route did not create a general disapproval procedure for adjustments affecting other articles, and it did not convert the reasons statement that followed every action into a vote on every action. The narrowness of the check was a feature of the design after 1980. For the generality of cases, accountability ran through the investigation record, the concurrence decision, the stated reasons, and the power of Congress to amend the underlying statute. For petroleum alone, an additional legislative instrument existed from 1980 onward.

The text and the procedure divided responsibility among three centers. The Secretary of Commerce, after 1988, built the record through investigation and consultation and delivered a report inside 270 days. The president judged the record through the concurrence decision, selected the nature and duration of any action from a menu that ran from duties to negotiated arrangements, acted inside the implementation period, and stated reasons to Congress inside 30 days. Congress enacted the standard in 1955, reenacted the standard in 1958, placed the standard in the 1962 act, amended the provision in 1974, transferred the investigation in 1988, and added a petroleum disapproval route in 1980. Each center acted at a different point in the sequence. The investigator did not impose the duty. The president did not conduct the investigation. Congress did not concur or decline to concur in a finding. The statute assigned each function to a named actor, attached a period to each stage after initiation, and left the substantive term at the center of the case to be applied through the factor list rather than through a definition.

## Dormancy and Revival of the Security Provision

### How often was the provision actually used across its first five decades?

The Congressional Research Service counted 24 investigations under section 232 between 1962 and 1995. Petroleum supplied the actions that produced restrictions or fees in that period, machine tools produced negotiated arrangements in 1986, and investigations in 1999 and 2001 ended without action before the steel and aluminum investigations of April 2017.

The record of section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 divides into distinct phases when the investigations and presidential decisions are arranged by administration and year. The Congressional Research Service count of 24 investigations from 1962 to 1995 supplies the starting measure. That count covers investigations, not a matching number of restrictions, because an investigation under the provision could end in several ways. The Secretary of Commerce investigated, consulted the Secretary of Defense and other officials, and reported within 270 days under the procedure as codified after the Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988 moved the responsibility to Commerce. The president then had 90 days to concur or not and to determine the nature and duration of any action, and any action was taken within a further 15 days. The president stated the reasons to Congress within 30 days. A file could therefore close with duties, quotas, license fees, negotiated arrangements, or with no adjustment at all, and the historical record contains examples of each outcome.

The design of the provision helps explain why the count of investigations and the count of restrictions differ so sharply across the decades covered in this section. The statute set the trigger and the procedure but left the finding and the choice of remedy to the president. The statutory standard reached an article imported in such quantities or under such circumstances as to threaten to impair the national security. The statute did not define national security beyond the listed factors in section 232(d). Those factors included domestic production for national defense, capacity, manpower and resources, and the effect of foreign competition on domestic industries, including the economic welfare of the nation. The investigator and the president were required to take those factors into account. Within that frame, a decision to act and a decision to decline action both completed the procedure. The phases that followed were therefore phases of presidential decision as much as phases of investigation, and the same statutory language supported intervention in one period, negotiated settlement in another, and inaction in a third.

Petroleum dominated the early record of action. The actions of the 1970s and the early 1980s that rested on the provision arose from petroleum, and the remedies took petroleum forms: license fees on imported oil under one administration, a license fee on crude oil and gasoline under the next, and an embargo on crude oil from a named source under the administration after that. No comparable sequence of actions on other products appears in the record for those years. The machine tools case of the 1980s broke the petroleum pattern in product terms, but it closed with negotiated arrangements rather than with a proclamation imposing restrictions under the section.

President Ford raised license fees on imported oil in 1975. The action was issued as Proclamation 4341. The license fee form mattered to the later legal history of the provision, because the fees became the subject of Federal Energy Administration v. Algonquin SNG, Inc. In that case the Supreme Court held that section 232(b) authorized the license fee program on imported oil, and that fees were a permissible method of adjusting imports rather than a method limited to quotas. The Court also held that the standards the statute supplied were sufficient to meet any delegation doctrine attack. The finding under review in Algonquin was the finding of the Secretary of the Treasury, made under the provision as amended by the Trade Act of 1974, which had moved the advisory responsibility to the Secretary of the Treasury and added a one year investigation limit. For the chronology of use, the Ford action stands as the first of the petroleum actions described in this section, taken thirteen years after the 1962 act carried the provision at section 232, and it established the fee as one of the remedy forms the provision could carry in practice as well as in the descriptions of presidential discretion.

President Carter proclaimed a license fee on crude oil and gasoline in 1980. The action was issued as Proclamation 4744. The Carter action repeated the fee instrument that the Ford administration had used on imported oil five years earlier, but it applied the fee to crude oil and gasoline in the terms stated in the proclamation record, and its fate differed from the fate of the Ford program. The Ford fees were sustained by the Supreme Court in Algonquin in 1976. The Carter fee did not survive the challenges that followed its announcement in 1980. The sequence also illustrates the petroleum specific character of the congressional check that Congress wrote into law in that period.

The Carter proclamation provoked a congressional disapproval fight. For petroleum only, the Crude Oil Windfall Profit Tax Act of 1980, Public Law 96-223, section 402, created a joint resolution of disapproval route against presidential petroleum adjustments. That route was a statutory feature confined to petroleum; no equivalent joint resolution mechanism was written for other products under the provision. The fight over the Carter fee therefore produced a durable procedural change in the petroleum corner of the statute even as the fee itself failed elsewhere. In May 1980 a federal district court held the Carter action unlawful. The court decision closed the episode: the fee proclaimed under Proclamation 4744 in 1980 was held unlawful in the same year it was announced, by a federal district court, in May 1980. The Carter episode was, in sum, an affirmative use of the provision by the Carter administration in 1980, it drew a congressional disapproval response tied to the petroleum specific mechanism enacted in the Crude Oil Windfall Profit Tax Act of 1980, and it ended with a judicial holding of unlawfulness in May 1980.

President Reagan embargoed Libyan crude oil imports in 1982. The action was issued as Proclamation 4907. The remedy differed from the remedies of 1975 and 1980. The Ford and Carter administrations had used license fees, a charge collected on importation under a licensing structure. The Reagan administration used an embargo directed at crude oil from Libya, a prohibition on the imports named in the proclamation. All three actions rested on the same provision and all three concerned petroleum, yet the three administrations selected different instruments from the range the statute left to presidential choice. The record supports that comparison at the level of instruments and dates: Proclamation 4341 and a license fee increase in 1975 under President Ford, Proclamation 4744 and a license fee on crude oil and gasoline in 1980 under President Carter, and Proclamation 4907 and an embargo of Libyan crude oil in 1982 under President Reagan. After the Reagan embargo, the petroleum sequence ended. The next affirmative outcome in the record concerned a manufactured product, and it arrived through negotiation rather than through a proclaimed restriction.

Machine tools supplied that next outcome. A section 232 investigation of machine tools was conducted in 1983. The investigation ended with the administration negotiating voluntary restraint arrangements with supplier countries in 1986 rather than proclaiming restrictions under the section. The interval between the 1983 investigation and the 1986 arrangements spans three calendar years, and the outcome belongs in the catalogue of uses because the investigation produced a trade result through the process the provision set in motion. At the same time, the form of the outcome marks a boundary. No proclamation imposing restrictions under the section answered the machine tools investigation. The negotiated arrangements were the answer, and they were reached with supplier countries as arrangements described as voluntary restraint arrangements in the record. In the taxonomy of outcomes under the provision, the machine tools case therefore sits between the petroleum proclamations and the investigations that ended without action. It involved an investigation, a period of negotiation, and an arrangement affecting the trade concerned, reached in 1986, three years after the investigation year of 1983.

The long quiet period followed the machine tools arrangements. Investigations in later decades frequently ended without action. The Congressional Research Service count of 24 investigations from 1962 to 1995 already signals the pattern, because the affirmative outcomes described in the record for that span are confined to the petroleum actions and, in outcome terms, the machine tools negotiation that closed in 1986. Investigations continued to be opened and completed within the 1962 to 1995 window and after it, but completion did not produce proclamations adjusting imports. Two late investigations mark the period in the record. The first was the petroleum investigation of 1999. It concerned the product that had produced every proclaimed action of the 1970s and the early 1980s, and it ended without action. The second was the investigation of iron ore and semifinished steel opened in the period that closed with the 2001 investigation recorded in Congressional Research Service tables. That investigation also ended without action. The product in the 2001 case, semifinished steel, stood close to the product that would return to the center of the provision in 2017 and 2018, but at the 2001 vantage the investigation produced no adjustment.

The quiet period requires careful description, because the absence of action was not an absence of the provision. Section 232 remained codified at 19 U.S.C. 1862. The procedure remained available to applicants, to agency heads requesting an investigation, and to the Secretary of Commerce acting on the Secretary's own motion, which were the three routes by which an investigation could begin under the codified procedure. Commerce consulted the Secretary of Defense and other officials in an investigation, reported within 270 days, and left the concurrence decision and the choice of any action to the president within the periods the statute set. During the quiet years that machinery produced reports and presidential decisions in some cases and produced no restrictions in the investigations the record names for 1999 and 2001. The provision was therefore present in the statute book and in investigative practice across the period, while its restrictive output was absent. That distinction between availability and output defines the dormancy phase in factual terms: investigations occurred, including the 1999 petroleum investigation and the 2001 investigation of iron ore and semifinished steel, and those investigations ended without action.

The analytical point that connects the quiet period to the revival is that the text did not change while its salience did. The operative standard remained imports of an article in such quantities or under such circumstances as to threaten to impair the national security. The listed factors remained the factors of section 232(d), covering domestic production for national defense, capacity, manpower and resources, and the effect of foreign competition on domestic industries, including the economic welfare of the nation. The procedural settlement reached in 1988, under which the Secretary of Commerce investigated within 270 days and the president decided within 90 days and acted within a further 15 days, governed the quiet period and the revival years alike. The words under which Commerce investigated steel and aluminum in 2017 were the words under which earlier investigations had ended without action in 1999 and 2001. The difference lay in the findings made, the presidential decisions taken, and the remedies selected.

The revival began in April 2017. The Commerce Department opened investigations of steel and aluminum in that month. The two investigations were opened together in April 2017, and they concerned the two metals that would receive duties in the proclamations of the following year. Under the codified procedure, Commerce investigated in consultation with the Secretary of Defense and other officials, and the reports went to the president for a decision on concurrence and on the nature and duration of any action. The April 2017 openings therefore started the statutory clock that the 1988 procedure had set: a report due within 270 days, a presidential decision period of 90 days, and a further 15 days for any action to be taken. The openings also returned the provision to the product area of the 2001 investigation, because steel had been investigated in 2001 without action, and aluminum joined steel as a subject of investigation in April 2017. From April 2017 the record moves from investigation to proclamation within eleven months.

On March 8, 2018 the president signed the proclamations that answered the April 2017 investigations. Proclamation 9704 imposed a 25 percent duty on steel. Proclamation 9705 imposed a 10 percent duty on aluminum. The duties were effective from March 23, 2018. The two proclamations were signed on the same day, March 8, 2018, and took effect on the same day, March 23, 2018, but they set different rates for the two metals: 25 percent for steel and 10 percent for aluminum. In the chronology of the provision, March 2018 produced the first duties described in this record since the petroleum fees and embargo of 1975 to 1982 and the negotiated machine tools arrangements of 1986. The proclamations were followed by exemption and product exclusion processes. Those processes formed part of the administration of the duties after March 23, 2018: exemptions applied at the level the processes provided, and product exclusions were handled through the exclusion process that followed the proclamations. The duties, the March 23, 2018 effective date, and the exemption and product exclusion processes together constitute the 2018 action on steel and aluminum as recorded at the August 1, 2020 vantage.

In January 2020 the duties were extended to certain derivative products. The extension was issued as Proclamation 9980. The derivative products extension carried the March 2018 steel and aluminum duties to products derived from those metals, in the terms of the January 2020 proclamation. The extension is the last change to the 2018 duties made by August 1, 2020, the vantage of this record: the duties were imposed on the dates given in March 2018, and the derivative extension of January 2020 was the one subsequent step. Between March 2018 and January 2020 the provision had therefore produced duties on two metals, a set of exemption and product exclusion processes administering those duties, and an extension of the duties to derivative products. That sequence, from the April 2017 openings through the January 2020 extension, is the revival phase in its affirmative form. The same years also produced a set of investigations that ended without restrictions, and those cases belong in the same chronology because they were opened under the same procedure and closed by presidential decisions declining or deferring action.

The automobiles and parts investigation was the largest of the cases that ended without restrictions. Commerce opened the investigation in May 2018, two months after the steel and aluminum proclamations were signed. The Commerce report was delivered in February 2019. On May 17, 2019 a presidential proclamation deferred a decision for up to 180 days while negotiations proceeded. No import restrictions followed. The record of the automobiles and parts case at the August 1, 2020 vantage is therefore a complete sequence of opening, report, deferral, and absence of restrictions: an investigation opened in May 2018, a report delivered in February 2019, a proclamation of May 17, 2019 deferring a decision for up to 180 days, and no restrictions imposed after that deferral. The case shows the deferral possibility inside the presidential stage of the procedure operating in a major product sector, and it closed without the duties or other restrictions that a different presidential decision could have selected from the remedial range the statute left open.

The uranium investigation followed a related path to a different procedural ending. The investigation dated to 2018. On July 12, 2019 a presidential memorandum declined import restrictions and established a working group instead. The memorandum was the terminal presidential act in the uranium case as recorded at the August 1, 2020 vantage. Where the automobiles case ended with a proclamation deferring a decision and no restrictions following, the uranium case ended with a memorandum that declined restrictions in terms and substituted a working group for an import adjustment. Both outcomes fall on the no restriction side of the ledger for the revival years, alongside the steel and aluminum duties on the action side. The uranium decision of July 12, 2019 also arrived in the same summer season as the deferral period in the automobiles case, so that the middle of 2019 contains two presidential decisions under the provision, in two product areas, neither of which imposed import restrictions.

Titanium sponge supplied a third no restriction outcome in the revival period. The investigation dated to 2019. In February 2020 a presidential decision declined restrictions. The titanium sponge decision came one month after the January 2020 extension of the steel and aluminum duties to derivative products, so the opening weeks of 2020 contain one affirmative extension of existing duties and one decision declining new restrictions, both under the same provision and both within the first two months of that year. Arranged by year, the revival period record reads in prose as a mixed sequence rather than a single direction of travel. In 2017 Commerce opened the steel and aluminum investigations. In 2018 the president imposed the steel and aluminum duties and Commerce opened the automobiles and parts investigation, while the uranium investigation also dated to that year. In 2019 the automobiles report was delivered, the automobiles decision was deferred, uranium restrictions were declined, and the titanium sponge investigation dated to that year. In 2020 the derivative products extension was proclaimed in January, titanium sponge restrictions were declined in February, and one further investigation was initiated in June.

That further investigation concerned vanadium. An investigation of vanadium was initiated in June 2020. At the August 1, 2020 vantage the vanadium investigation stood pending. Nothing further is stated about the vanadium case in this record: no report date, no presidential decision, and no outcome are recorded for it at the vantage date. Its place in the chronology is as an initiated and pending investigation in June 2020, the last investigative event in the record before the August 1, 2020 vantage closed the account. The vanadium initiation also extended the list of products investigated in the revival years beyond steel, aluminum, derivative products of those metals, automobiles and parts, uranium, and titanium sponge. With the vanadium investigation pending, the revival period closed its recorded span with an open file rather than with a decision, which returns the chronology to the procedural starting point that had begun each earlier phase, an investigation under way under the codified procedure.

Read across the whole span from 1962 to the August 1, 2020 vantage, the uses fall into the groups the administrations and years supply. The Congressional Research Service counted 24 investigations from 1962 to 1995. The petroleum actions of 1975, 1980, and 1982 under Presidents Ford, Carter, and Reagan used license fees, a license fee held unlawful in May 1980 after a congressional disapproval fight, and an embargo. The machine tools investigation of 1983 produced negotiated voluntary restraint arrangements in 1986. The investigations of 1999 and 2001 ended without action. The steel and aluminum investigations opened in April 2017 produced duties of 25 percent and 10 percent from March 23, 2018, exemption and product exclusion processes, and a derivative products extension in January 2020. Automobiles and parts produced a deferral on May 17, 2019 with no restrictions following, uranium restrictions were declined on July 12, 2019, titanium sponge restrictions were declined in February 2020, and the vanadium investigation initiated in June 2020 stood pending on August 1, 2020.

## The Courts and the Delegation

The litigation over section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 turned on a single structural question that recurred in different forms across four decades. Congress had written a provision that set a trigger and a procedure, named factors for consideration, and then left the finding and the choice of remedy to the president. The statute authorized the president to adjust imports of an article imported in such quantities or under such circumstances as to threaten to impair the national security. The statute did not define national security beyond the factors listed for the investigator and the president, which included domestic production for national defense, capacity, manpower and resources, and the effect of foreign competition on domestic industries, including the economic welfare of the nation. That design placed a large measure of judgment in executive hands, and it placed a correspondingly difficult question before any court asked to review the result. A challenge could attack the breadth of the delegation itself, on the ground that Congress had handed over legislative power without adequate standards. A challenge could attack the particular method chosen, or the process followed. The reported decisions addressed each form of attack, and the answers given in 1976 controlled the answers given in 2019 and 2020.

The central decision was Federal Energy Administration v. Algonquin SNG, Inc., 426 U.S. 548, decided on June 17, 1976. The case arose from the license fees imposed on imported oil under the program associated with President Ford, who had raised license fees on imported oil in 1975 by Proclamation 4341. The finding under review was the finding of the Secretary of the Treasury, made under the provision as amended by the Trade Act of 1974. The provision itself had a longer lineage. It traced to the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1955, and Congress had carried it forward in the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 at section 232. The Trade Act of 1974 amended the provision at section 127(d). The question in 1976 therefore concerned a provision that Congress had enacted, reenacted, and amended across two decades, and that history formed part of the material the Supreme Court considered in deciding how broadly the operative language should be read.

### Has any court struck the provision down?

No. As of August 1, 2020, no court had struck down section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962. The Supreme Court sustained the provision in 1976, the Court of International Trade rejected a facial challenge in 2019, the Federal Circuit affirmed in 2020, and certiorari was denied.

The challengers in Algonquin were eight states, ten utility companies, and a member of Congress. Together, the challengers presented the fee program as a burden imposed without adequate statutory authority. The government defended the program as an exercise of the authority that section 232(b) conferred, under which the president could adjust imports after the prescribed finding had been made. The litigation thus presented both a question of method, whether fees were a permissible adjustment at all, and a question of delegation, whether the statute supplied standards sufficient to sustain the authority it conferred.

The case moved through the lower courts in opposite directions. The district court upheld the program. The court of appeals reversed, on the ground that the provision authorized direct controls such as quotas and did not authorize a fee system. A quota operates by quantity, fixing the amount of an article that may enter and excluding the remainder. A fee operates by price, raising the cost of entry without fixing the quantity that may enter in advance. The court of appeals treated the difference as decisive, reading the statutory authority to adjust imports as an authority to impose direct quantitative controls and not as an authority to impose charges on importation. On that reading, the fee program failed at the threshold, regardless of the finding that supported it, because the instrument chosen was one that Congress had not granted.

The Supreme Court decided the case on June 17, 1976. The decision was unanimous, and Justice Brennan wrote the opinion of the Court. Argument had been heard on April 20, 1976. The Court held that section 232(b) authorized the license fee program on imported oil. Fees, the Court held, were a permissible method of adjusting imports, and the authority was not limited to quotas. The holding rejected the narrowing construction that the court of appeals had adopted, under which only direct controls would have counted as an adjustment. On the Court's reading, an adjustment of imports could operate through a charge as well as through a quantitative limit, and the president's selection of the fee method fell within the discretion that the statute conferred over the nature of the action to be taken. The judgment of the court of appeals was therefore reversed in substance, and the program that the district court had upheld was sustained.

The reasoning on method drew support from the legislative history of reenactment. The Court reasoned in part from the fact that Congress had reenacted the provision in 1958, in 1962, and in 1974, in the face of the broad understanding of its language. The lineage recounted in the case began with enactment in 1955 and continued through those successive reenactments. Reenactment mattered because it showed that the broad language had not slipped into the statute book unnoticed in a single enactment and then remained untouched. Congress had returned to the provision repeatedly, and had done so against a broad understanding of what the language allowed. A court asked to confine the language to quotas alone therefore faced a text that Congress had retained in broad form across successive trade acts. The Court treated that record as confirmation that the broad reading was the correct reading.

The second holding in Algonquin addressed the delegation attack directly. The Court held that the standards the statute supplies are clearly sufficient to meet any delegation doctrine attack. The adverb in that formulation carried weight. The Court did not sustain the statute by a narrow margin, and it did not describe the standards as barely adequate to the constitutional requirement. The standards were clearly sufficient. The statute set the condition for action, that imports threaten to impair the national security. The statute directed attention to specified factors, including domestic production for national defense, productive capacity, manpower, resources, and the effect of foreign competition on domestic industries and on the economic welfare of the nation. The statute prescribed a procedure of investigation, report, presidential determination, and statement of reasons to Congress. On the Court's analysis, a statute framed in those terms supplied guidance adequate to govern the delegated authority, and the challenge to the delegation failed. That holding became the fixed point for later litigation, because a later challenger could not obtain a different result without persuading a court either to distinguish the precedent or to set it aside.

The doctrine that Algonquin fixed extended beyond the two holdings in the case. Judicial review of the president's determination under the section is extremely limited. Later challenges have succeeded, where at all, on whether the statute's procedural requirements were followed, rather than on the merits of a security finding. The distinction is between the regularity of the path and the soundness of the destination. A court could ask whether an investigation had been conducted by the officer the statute designated, whether the report had been delivered within the period the statute allowed, whether the president had acted within the periods prescribed for concurrence and for implementation, and whether the reasons had been stated to Congress as the statute required. A court applying Algonquin did not undertake to decide whether imports in fact threatened to impair the national security, or whether a different remedy would have served the national security better than the remedy chosen. The merits of the finding belonged to the president under the delegation, and the office of the court was confined to the boundaries of the statute and the Constitution.

The procedural emphasis in later review followed from the structure of the codified process. Each step in that process carried a date or a duty that a court could verify from the record without assessing the security judgment itself, from the investigation and report through the periods prescribed for presidential concurrence, for implementation, and for the statement of reasons to Congress. A procedural challenge therefore depended on the version of the statute in force for the investigation under review.

One later episode illustrated the limits of the protection that Algonquin gave to presidential action. President Carter proclaimed a license fee on crude oil and gasoline in 1980 by Proclamation 4744, and a federal district court held that action unlawful in May 1980. The episode showed that sustainment of the provision in the abstract did not immunize every measure taken under it. Measures taken afterward stood or fell on their own conformity to the statute.

For more than four decades after Algonquin, the delegation holding rested largely undisturbed as governing precedent. The Congressional Research Service counted 24 section 232 investigations from 1962 to 1995, with petroleum dominating the early record. Later investigations frequently ended without action, including the 1999 petroleum investigation and the 2001 iron ore and semifinished steel investigation. The constitutional objection returned to litigation when the provision was used again at scale. The Commerce Department opened investigations of steel and aluminum in April 2017. On March 8, 2018, the president signed proclamations imposing a 25 percent duty on steel by Proclamation 9704 and a 10 percent duty on aluminum by Proclamation 9705, effective March 23, 2018, with exemptions and exclusions processes that followed. In January 2020 the duties were extended to certain derivative products by Proclamation 9980. The 2018 duties supplied the occasion for a direct attempt to reopen the question that Algonquin had closed.

The attempt came in American Institute for International Steel v. United States. The suit was filed in the Court of International Trade in June 2018 by a trade association and two member companies. The challenge was facial. The plaintiffs argued on the face of the statute that section 232 delegates legislative power without limit. A facial challenge of that kind did not depend on the details of the steel investigation or on the particular findings made in 2018. The objection was to the provision itself, as written, and it would have applied to any exercise of the authority if the objection had prevailed. That posture made the case a direct test of Algonquin. The contention was that Congress, in enacting and reenacting the provision, had transferred legislative power on terms that the Constitution did not permit, and that the duties imposed in 2018 rested on an unconstitutional delegation. The remedy implicit in the argument was the invalidation of the provision itself, notwithstanding the Supreme Court's decision sustaining the provision in 1976.

The Court of International Trade heard the case before a three judge panel, a composition that reflected the constitutional character of the challenge. On March 25, 2019, the panel rejected the challenge, in a decision reported at 376 F. Supp. 3d 1335. The panel held itself bound by Algonquin. That holding of constraint was the core of the decision. The panel did not purport to decide the delegation question afresh as a matter of first impression, and it did not hold that the arguments against the delegation lacked force in the abstract. The panel's position was that a governing Supreme Court precedent had answered the question, and that a lower court had no authority to disregard the answer because the composition of the Supreme Court or the climate of doctrine might have changed. Algonquin had held that the standards in the statute are clearly sufficient against a delegation attack, and the steel duties rested on the same provision sustained in that case. The panel therefore entered judgment against the challenge, and the provision survived the most direct attack mounted against it since 1976.

Judge Katzmann joined the judgment while writing separately, in an opinion described as dubitante, to question whether the delegation, as revealed by the use made of it in the period under review, could be squared with the Constitution, and inviting reconsideration by a court free to give it. The separate opinion and the panel opinion together expressed the two sides of the lower court position. The judgment rested on obedience to precedent. The separate opinion recorded doubt about the precedent's fit with the delegation as exercised, and it directed that doubt to the one court with authority to revisit the precedent. A judge writing dubitante concurs in the result that precedent compels while stating reasons to doubt that the result is right, and Judge Katzmann's opinion performed exactly that function. The opinion did not supply a basis for relief in the case at hand, since the judge joined the judgment rejecting the challenge. The opinion instead preserved the constitutional argument in a form that the Supreme Court itself could consider.

The plaintiffs then attempted to take the case directly to the Supreme Court, and that attempt was turned away in 2019. Its failure left the ordinary appellate path in place, and the case proceeded to the Federal Circuit. The sequence matters to an accurate account of the litigation, because summaries that compress the episode into a single appellate rejection in 2019 misstate the record. The rejection in March 2019 was the decision of the three judge panel of the Court of International Trade. The appellate affirmance came afterward, in February 2020, and the Supreme Court's disposition on certiorari came later in 2020.

The Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit affirmed in February 2020. The court held that Algonquin answers the question presented, and that the tariffs could not be distinguished from the license fees sustained in Algonquin. The second part of the holding addressed the most obvious factual difference between the two cases. Algonquin had concerned license fees on imported oil, imposed under a program in the energy sector in the 1970s. The later case concerned duties on steel and aluminum, imposed in 2018 at 25 percent and 10 percent respectively. The plaintiffs' argument required a distinction between those measures sufficient to take the later case outside the precedent. The Federal Circuit declined to draw that distinction. A duty and a license fee were, for delegation purposes, exercises of the same statutory authority to adjust imports, and the constitutional adequacy of the standards did not vary with the instrument selected or the article affected. If the standards were clearly sufficient for fees on oil in 1976, the same standards governed tariffs on steel and aluminum in 2018, and the precedent controlled the outcome.

The Federal Circuit also addressed the argument drawn from developments in delegation doctrine since 1976. The court noted that expressions of interest among Supreme Court justices in revisiting the delegation standard gave it no license to disregard governing precedent. That observation stated a rule of judicial hierarchy rather than a judgment on the merits of revision. Interest expressed by individual justices was not a decision of the Court, and it did not release a lower court from an existing decision that remained in force. The affirmance therefore rested on the same foundation as the panel decision in the Court of International Trade. Both courts treated Algonquin as dispositive, both courts acknowledged the argument for reconsideration, and both courts held that reconsideration was not theirs to undertake.

The Supreme Court denied certiorari on June 22, 2020. A denial of certiorari left the judgment of the Federal Circuit in place and left Algonquin undisturbed, without a merits ruling on the renewed challenge. The sequence that had begun with the filing in June 2018 thus closed in June 2020 with the provision intact and the 2018 duties sustained against the facial delegation attack. No court in the sequence held the provision unconstitutional, and no court narrowed the construction that Algonquin had placed on section 232(b). The litigation instead confirmed the arrangement that Algonquin had established, under which the breadth of the delegation was a question the Supreme Court had answered, and under which further judicial scrutiny of action under the provision concentrated on conformity to the statute's procedural requirements.

Against that record, a counter-reading of the litigation was carried at full strength by defenders of the provision, and the counter-reading rejected the characterization of section 232 as a loophole. On that reading, the breadth of the provision was not an accident of drafting, an oversight in committee, or a gap that presidents had discovered and exploited against the design of Congress. Congress enacted the breadth deliberately. The provision was enacted in 1955, carried forward in 1958, enacted again as section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, and reenacted as amended in 1974, each time in language that the Supreme Court in Algonquin described as broadly understood. A legislature that returns to a provision across four trade statutes, retains the operative standard of threat to impair the national security, retains the listed factors, and retains presidential choice over the nature and duration of the adjustment, has made a decision about the allocation of authority. The counter-reading treated that repeated decision as the central fact of the provision's history, more significant than the identity of any president who later acted under it or the article to which any particular action applied.

The counter-reading drew a second element from the case law itself. The courts have declined to narrow what Congress wrote. The court of appeals in Algonquin had attempted a narrowing construction, confining the authority to direct controls such as quotas, and the Supreme Court rejected that construction and sustained fees as a permissible method of adjustment. In the American Institute for International Steel litigation, two lower courts declined to distinguish tariffs from fees, declined to treat expressions of judicial interest in a revised delegation standard as authority to disregard precedent, and declined to hold the provision unconstitutional on its face. Each refusal left the statutory text at the width Congress had given it. On the counter-reading, that judicial restraint was the correct institutional response to a broad delegation. The breadth was legislative in origin, and the courts left it legislative in responsibility.

The conclusion of the counter-reading followed from those two elements. The objection is properly addressed to the breadth of the delegation rather than to its use by any president. A president acting under section 232 exercised authority that Congress had conferred in terms the Supreme Court had sustained, through a procedure that Congress had prescribed and repeatedly amended. Disagreement with a particular duty, fee, or quota was therefore, on that reading, disagreement with the scope of the grant itself, and the forum for that disagreement was Congress. Congress retained the instruments of correction. Congress could amend the standard, narrow the factors, shorten the periods, restrict the remedies, or repeal the provision, as Congress had shown the capacity to do when it created a joint resolution of disapproval route for petroleum adjustments in the Crude Oil Windfall Profit Tax Act of 1980 and when it reassigned the investigating responsibility in 1974 and again in 1988. The courts, by contrast, had performed the narrower office that Algonquin assigned to them. The courts had tested the delegation against the Constitution in 1976 and sustained it, had tested the fee method against the statutory text and sustained it, had tested particular actions for conformity to the statute, and had left the security finding itself to the president. As of August 1, 2020, that distribution of responsibility described the state of the law on section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, and no decision had displaced it.

## The Assistance Bargain and Its Gatekeepers

The Trade Expansion Act of 1962 joined two commitments that pointed in opposite directions, and the tension between them defined the adjustment assistance program from enactment onward. The first commitment expanded the authority of the president to negotiate reductions in American duties, on a scale and by a method that earlier authority under the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of 1934 had not supplied. The second commitment addressed the domestic cost that wider import competition could impose on particular workers, firms, industries, and communities. Adjustment assistance, created at section 301 and following of the act, belonged to the second commitment. The statute treated the two commitments as parts of a single settlement. Liberalization would proceed, and the federal government would provide a defined form of help to the people and places that liberalization displaced.

The assistance was offered explicitly to secure labor support for liberalization, and that origin matters to an understanding of the design that followed. Adjustment assistance was not an afterthought attached to a completed trade bill, and it was not a general welfare program that happened to sit inside a trade statute. The program formed part of the price of the negotiating authority itself. Labor had reason to weigh the gains from expanded trade against the risk of job separations in import sensitive industries, and the administration that sought the authority offered assistance as the answer to that risk. Delivery was therefore the measure of the promise. A program that existed in the statute book but certified no one would fail as consideration for support already given, and the record of the 1962 program has to be read against that standard.

The act created the first federal program of adjustment assistance. No earlier federal program had undertaken to compensate workers and firms specifically for injury traced to trade liberalization, as distinct from unemployment or industrial decline in general. The designers worked without an established administrative practice for separating trade caused displacement from displacement caused by other forces, and without an established body of claimants, precedents, or expectations. That novelty helps explain the reach of the program on paper. Congress legislated for workers, for firms, and for industries and communities, because displacement could move through all of those levels at once.

For workers, the statute provided readjustment allowances, retraining, and relocation allowances. The readjustment allowances served as income support, replacing part of the flow of wages that a separation had interrupted while the worker searched for other employment or entered training. Retraining addressed the possibility that the job lost to import competition would not return in its earlier form, and that a displaced worker would need different skills for a different occupation or industry. Relocation allowances addressed the further possibility that suitable work existed somewhere else, so that the cost of moving stood between the worker and reemployment. Taken together, the three forms of worker assistance described a sequence rather than a single payment. Income support held the household together during the interval after separation, training rebuilt the capacity to earn, and relocation assistance removed a geographic barrier when local opportunity had contracted beyond what training alone could repair.

For firms, the act provided loans, loan guarantees, and technical assistance. A firm with a viable plan for adjustment might lack access to capital on terms that allowed the plan to proceed, and a direct loan supplied that capital from the federal government. A firm able to borrow from private sources with federal backing could obtain credit through a loan guarantee, which placed the federal commitment behind the loan without the same direct outlay. Technical assistance addressed a different deficiency, the knowledge required to change products, processes, or markets in response to a changed competitive position. The three instruments assumed that an injured firm was not necessarily a failed firm. Injury from increased imports could strike an enterprise that retained productive capacity, a workforce, and a market, provided the enterprise obtained time, credit, and advice in combination.

The act also provided assistance for industries and for communities, recognizing that displacement did not stop at the gate of a single plant and that a community dependent on an injured industry faced losses no individual benefit could offset. Intention, as expressed in the statute, was broad. The failure lay elsewhere, in the door through which every claimant had to pass before any form of help became available.

### Why did no worker qualify under the original assistance program?

No worker qualified because eligibility required a Tariff Commission finding that increased imports had resulted in major part from trade agreement concessions and had been the major factor in the separations, and the Commission never found the concessions test satisfied during the program's first years.

The gatekeeper was the Tariff Commission, and the gatekeeping design stood between every claimant and every benefit in the program. A worker could have lost employment in an industry facing rising imports, a firm could have documented injury, and a community could have shown decline, yet none of those showings alone opened the program. Eligibility required a determination by the Tariff Commission, and the determination governed access across the categories of assistance. The structure concentrated the fate of the entire program in a single institutional decision, repeated case by case. Where a determination was affirmative, the path toward assistance opened. Where the required findings could not be made, the statutory promises remained text without effect for the claimants in that case. The design made the Commission less an administrator of benefits than an adjudicator of entitlement, and the standards the Commission had to apply mattered as much as the benefits the statute listed.

The Commission had to make three findings, and each finding narrowed the class of cases that could succeed. The first finding concerned import volume. Articles like or directly competitive with the articles produced by the affected workers or firms had to be imported in increased quantities. The second finding concerned causation by concessions. The increased imports had to have resulted in major part from concessions granted under trade agreements. The third finding concerned causation of injury. The increased imports had to be the major cause of the unemployment, underemployment, or injury in the case, expressed in the formulation recorded by the General Accounting Office as the requirement that increased imports be the major factor in causing job separations. A petition that satisfied two findings and failed the third failed altogether, because the findings operated as a cumulative test rather than as alternative routes to relief.

The second and third findings together formed a double causation test, and the double test did more work than either part performed alone. The third finding asked whether imports had caused the separations, a question that already required the Commission to separate the effect of imports from the effects of changing demand, changing technology, domestic competition, management decisions, and the general condition of the economy. The second finding then asked a further question. The imports themselves, whose increase had caused the injury, had to owe that increase in major part to concessions granted under trade agreements. A claimant therefore had to prove a chain with two causal links, running from a governmental act of concession, through an increase in imports, to a loss of employment or injury. A break at either link defeated the claim, even when the injury itself was undisputed and even when imports plainly formed part of the explanation for the injury.

The concessions link proved to be the decisive barrier, and the reasons lie in the character of the proof the test demanded. Tariff concessions formed one influence among many on the volume of imports of any article. Foreign productive capacity, American demand, transportation costs, domestic prices, and the decisions of importers and foreign producers all acted on import volume at the same time as a reduced duty. To find that increased imports resulted in major part from concessions required the Commission to assign the largest share of an observed increase to one cause inside a set of interacting causes, with enough confidence to support a formal determination. The statute offered no simple presumption to carry a claimant past that difficulty. Increased imports alone did not establish the concessions link, injury alone did not establish the concessions link, and the coexistence of a concession and an import increase did not establish that the concession accounted in major part for the increase.

The import causation link imposed a parallel difficulty at the level of the workplace. Separations in a plant or a firm seldom have a single cause that announces itself in the records. An employer facing import competition might also face a shrinking market, a shift in consumer preference, rising costs, or a decision to reorganize production, and workers separated in such a setting lost employment through a combination that resisted division into neat shares. The statutory standard did not ask whether imports had contributed to the separations, or whether imports had formed a substantial part of the explanation. The standard asked whether increased imports were the major cause, the principal factor standing ahead of every rival explanation. A case in which imports accounted for a large part of the injury, but not a part larger than the test required, fell outside the program. Severity of need did not cure a shortfall in causal share.

Even an affirmative Commission finding did not compel assistance in every form, because the president retained discretion over parts of the response. That reservation added a second layer of contingency beyond the gatekeeping determination itself. The claimant first required the Commission to make the statutory findings, and then, for parts of the program, required an executive decision to deploy the available response. The failure of the first years occurred before that second layer could be tested at scale. Petitions were not reaching the stage at which presidential discretion became the operative question. The gate closed earlier, at the determination stage, and the discretion held in reserve behind the gate remained largely theoretical while the gate itself admitted no one.

The record that the design produced was, for the first years of the program, an unbroken series of failures to certify. The Tariff Commission never found the concessions test satisfied in any case during the first years of the program. That single fact explains the emptiness of the rest of the record, because failure on the concessions finding ended a petition regardless of the strength of the showing on import volume or on injury. Workers could demonstrate separation, firms could demonstrate harm, and imports could be shown to have increased, and the petition failed if the increase could not be attributed in major part to concessions granted under trade agreements. The concessions test operated not as one element among three, balanced against the others, but as a veto embedded in the middle of the test, and the veto fired in every case that came before the Commission in the opening period.

An official admission of the result came in a report dated January 14, 1969, from the Special Representative for Trade Negotiations. The report stated that the criteria had proved too strict and that no petition had been approved. The admission carries weight beyond the numbers alone because of its source and its timing. The Special Representative stood at the center of the negotiating enterprise that the assistance program had been created to support, and a statement from that office that the criteria had proved too strict amounted to a recognition, from inside the trade policy establishment, that the compensation mechanism had not functioned as enacted. By January 1969 the negotiating authority granted in 1962 had run its course through June 30, 1967, and the Kennedy Round had concluded with agreements signed on June 30, 1967. The liberalization side of the settlement had operated and concessions had been granted. The assistance side, measured over the same span, had approved no petition.

The General Accounting Office supplied the longer count in a later review. The Office recorded that no petitions were certified from 1962 to 1969, and that through 1974 only 100 petitions covering about 54,000 workers were certified. Those figures define the scale of the program across its life under the 1962 criteria. Twelve years after enactment, the total number of workers reached through certified petitions stood at about 54,000. The first certifications arrived in 1969, seven years after enactment, so that the entire opening period of the program, the period in which the bargain was freshest and in which the first concessions under the new authority took effect, passed without a single certified petition. Program histories summarize the point in compressed form as no certification in the first seven years, and the compression is accurate to the record the Commission and the accounting office left behind.

Seven empty years altered the meaning of the assistance promise for the audiences to whom the promise had been made. A compensation commitment gathers credibility from early cases, because early cases show claimants, unions, firms, and members of Congress that the mechanism answers when called upon. The 1962 program produced the opposite demonstration. Each unsuccessful petition taught the same lesson to the next potential petitioner, namely that the statutory route led to a determination process whose central test could not be met. The lesson discouraged reliance on the program and weakened the standing of the program as evidence that liberalization and compensation traveled together. Support for trade liberalization that had been obtained partly on the strength of the assistance commitment rested, after 1969, on a commitment with a documented record of nonperformance, and negotiators seeking later authority inherited that record as part of the political setting in which requests for authority were judged.

The credibility damage extended beyond the immediate parties to the original bargain. Members of Congress who had defended the 1962 act on the ground that displaced workers would receive help faced a record in which no constituent had received help through certification during the first seven years. The gap between the benefit list and the certification count became the most memorable fact about the design, and that gap is the setting in which [the wider history of American labor legislation](/2015/05/15/us-labor-legislation-since-1950/) treats trade adjustment assistance as a promise whose operative content was fixed by the eligibility section rather than the benefits section. For seven years, the eligibility terms answered every case the same way, and the promise existed for no one.

The redesign came through [the successor statute that redesigned the assistance program](/2020/06/01/trade-act-1974-complete-guide/), the Trade Act of 1974, and the redesign amounted to a judgment on the 1962 criteria expressed in statutory form. The 1974 act liberalized the design in three connected ways. The concessions causation test was dropped, ending the requirement that a claimant trace increased imports in major part to concessions granted under trade agreements. The causation standard that remained was lowered, so that imports needed only to contribute importantly to the job separations, rather than to stand as the major factor in causing those separations. Administration of worker petitions moved from the Tariff Commission to the Department of Labor, placing the petitions in the department charged with employment and training programs rather than in the body charged with tariff determinations. Each change answered a feature of the earlier failure. Dropping the concessions test removed the veto that had fired in every early case. Lowering the injury causation standard admitted cases in which imports formed a substantial cause among several causes. Moving worker petitions changed the institutional home of the decision.

The distance between the phrase contribute importantly and the earlier phrase major factor measures the distance between the two statutes. The later standard treated causation as a question of significant contribution within a mixed reality, in which domestic conditions could also bear on a separation. The earlier standard had treated causation as a contest that imports and concessions each had to win outright, and the contest had proved unwinnable in case after case.

The results under the redesigned criteria supplied the clearest available measure of what the earlier criteria had been doing. Certified workers rose from about 59,000 in fiscal 1975 to nearly 600,000 in fiscal 1980. Import competition had grown across the interval, so the increase cannot be assigned to the criteria change alone, but the scale carries a plain meaning. A standard that certified about 54,000 workers in total through 1974 gave way to a standard under which nearly 600,000 workers held certification in a single fiscal year by 1980. The criteria had functioned as the binding constraint on the program, and the small numbers of the 1962 program had shown the throughput of a particular eligibility machine rather than the rarity of trade related displacement. The later figures, produced after the machine was rebuilt, measured what the earlier criteria had been excluding.

The durable lesson of the adjustment assistance program under the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 can be stated plainly. A compensation mechanism's eligibility criteria are the policy. The benefit list in a statute describes what a successful claimant receives, but the eligibility criteria decide whether any claimant succeeds, and a public judgment of the program that stops at the benefit list mistakes the advertisement for the institution. Criteria written to be unmeetable convert a bargain into an unkept promise, and the conversion happens quietly, through determinations in individual cases, without any legislator having to vote against compensation in the open. The 1962 act promised readjustment allowances, retraining, relocation help, credit, and technical assistance, and for seven years delivered none of that promise through certification, because the findings demanded at the gate could not be made. Compensation promised, compensation withheld, stands as the compressed verdict on that record, and the verdict rests not on the intentions of the bargain but on the arithmetic of certification between 1962 and 1969.

The lesson extends to the reading of any later compensation design attached to trade legislation. Attention belongs first on the causation standard, on the institution that applies the standard, and on the burden a claimant bears in connecting injury to imports and imports to governmental action. A standard that requires a single cause to outrank all others will exclude mixed cases, and mixed cases form the normal run of industrial displacement.

Read as one settlement, the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 turns on two drafting decisions made in the same text and in the same year, and the record through August 1, 2020 is largely the working out of those two decisions. The first decision concerned the breadth of a delegation. Congress authorized formula tariff reductions up to a stated ceiling for a stated term, and Congress also carried forward a security provision that set a trigger, listed factors, prescribed a procedure, and left the finding and the choice of remedy to the president. The second decision concerned the criteria of eligibility for compensation. Congress listed generous forms of assistance for workers, firms, industries, and communities, and Congress then required a claimant to pass through a Tariff Commission determination built on two causal tests, each framed at the strength of a major part or a major cause. Delegation breadth decided how far presidential action could reach when a president chose to act. Eligibility criteria decided how far a promise of assistance could reach when a displaced worker or firm asked for help. Between those two decisions, the behavior of the statute over six decades was substantially settled in 1962.

The negotiating authority showed the benign side of a bounded delegation. The grant at section 201 was broad in coverage and bounded in depth, duration, and baseline, and the Kennedy Round consumed the grant almost exactly as the bounds predicted. Agreements were signed on June 30, 1967, the last day of the authority, averaged cuts stood close to one third on both sides, and concessions entered into force in stages from January 1, 1968. Expiry disciplined the closing, and the method of bargaining by formula survived the expiry of the percentages and dates that had embodied the method in 1962.

The security provision showed the other side of breadth. Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 supplied a standard without a definition, a factor list without a threshold, and a remedy menu that ran from duties and quotas to license fees and negotiated arrangements. The same text supported license fees on imported oil in 1975, a license fee held unlawful on its own terms in 1980, an embargo on Libyan crude oil in 1982, negotiated arrangements on machine tools in 1986, investigations without action in 1999 and 2001, and duties on steel and aluminum in 2018 followed by decisions declining or deferring restrictions on automobiles, uranium, and titanium sponge. The breadth allowed each administration to locate a different result inside the same standard, and the procedural sequence supplied the only regular discipline on the choice.

The counter reading therefore deserves restatement at full strength at the close. Section 232 is not a loophole. Congress enacted the breadth deliberately, in 1955 and again in 1962 and 1974, the courts declined to narrow the breadth in Algonquin in 1976 and again in the American Institute for International Steel litigation that ended with denial of certiorari on June 22, 2020, and the objection is properly to the breadth of the delegation Congress made, not to its use. A court applying that record had no textual basis for substituting a narrower standard, and a president acting inside the procedure exercised authority in the form Congress had conferred the authority. Correction of the breadth, on that reading, belonged to Congress, through amendment of the kind enacted in 1974, in 1980 for petroleum, and in 1988.

Adjustment assistance showed the mirror image of the same drafting lesson. A narrow eligibility test, rather than a broad delegation, decided the outcome, and the outcome was non delivery. No petitions were certified from 1962 to 1969, about 54,000 workers stood behind the 100 petitions certified through 1974, and the redesign of 1974, dropping the concessions test and lowering the causation standard to contribution in important part, was followed by certification of nearly 600,000 workers in fiscal 1980 alone. The gate, rather than the benefit list, had changed. Compensation promised, compensation withheld describes the 1962 design because the withholding occurred at the gate, in determinations that applied criteria a claimant could not satisfy, rather than in any refusal of a benefit after eligibility had been established.

For a reader keeping the sequence of enactment, amendment, investigation, and litigation in order across a statute of that span, the practical discipline is documentary, and a reader can [keep your statute notes, citations, and case chronologies together free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html) so that the date of a proclamation, the period in a procedure, and the standard in a determination remain attached to the provision that produced each result. The series point extends beyond a single act. In trade legislation, the operative policy often sits in the provisions that appear secondary in a summary, in the breadth of a delegation to act and in the criteria that govern access to a benefit. The Trade Expansion Act of 1962 placed a broad delegation and a narrow gate inside one settlement, delivered a multilateral round through the first, sustained a security power through the second half of the delegation, and withheld compensation through the gate, and each result was legible in the text that President Kennedy signed on October 11, 1962.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the Trade Expansion Act authorize?

The act authorized the president, for five years running to June 30, 1967, to enter trade agreements and proclaim reductions of up to 50 percent of the duty rates in effect on July 1, 1962. The act also carried the national security import provision at section 232, created the first federal program of adjustment assistance for workers, firms, industries, and communities, and created the office of Special Representative for Trade Negotiations. President John F. Kennedy signed the act on October 11, 1962, as Public Law 87-794, after passage by the House of Representatives by 298 to 125 and by the Senate by 78 to 8.

### Q: What is section 232 in the Trade Expansion Act?

Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, codified at 19 U.S.C. 1862, authorizes the president to adjust imports of an article imported in such quantities or under such circumstances as to threaten to impair the national security. The provision traces to the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1955, was reenacted in 1958 and again in 1962, and was amended by the Trade Act of 1974 without being created by that act. The statute lists factors concerning domestic production for national defense, capacity, manpower and resources, and the effect of foreign competition on domestic industries, including the economic welfare of the nation, and leaves the finding and the remedy to the president.

### Q: Who decides a section 232 case under the Trade Expansion Act?

Under the procedure as codified, the Secretary of Commerce investigates, consulting the Secretary of Defense and other officials, and delivers a report within 270 days. An investigation may begin on application, at the request of the head of an agency, or on the motion of the Secretary of Commerce. The president then has 90 days to concur or not concur in the finding and to determine the nature and duration of any action, and any action is taken within a further 15 days. The president states the reasons for the action to Congress within 30 days. The investigator therefore builds the record, and the president makes the decision on concurrence and remedy.

### Q: Did the Trade Expansion Act create trade adjustment assistance?

The act created the first federal program of adjustment assistance, at section 301 and following. For workers, the program provided readjustment allowances as income support, retraining, and relocation allowances. For firms, the program provided loans, loan guarantees, and technical assistance. Assistance also extended to industries and communities affected by increased imports. The program formed part of the liberalization bargain, offered explicitly to secure support for the negotiating authority, and the long title of the act named adjustment assistance to domestic industry, agriculture, and labor alongside international trade agreements as the means by which the act promoted the general welfare, foreign policy, and security of the United States.

### Q: Why did no worker qualify for early Trade Expansion Act assistance?

Eligibility required a Tariff Commission determination with three findings. Articles like or directly competitive had to be imported in increased quantities, the increased imports had to have resulted in major part from concessions granted under trade agreements, and the increased imports had to be the major cause of the unemployment, underemployment, or injury. The Commission never found the concessions test satisfied in any case during the first years of the program. No petitions were certified from 1962 to 1969, and the Special Representative for Trade Negotiations acknowledged in a report dated January 14, 1969 that the criteria had proved too strict and that no petition had been approved.

### Q: Did the Trade Expansion Act allow across the board tariff cuts?

The act changed the negotiating design from product by product bargaining to across the board, or linear, percentage reductions. Section 201 authorized reductions of up to 50 percent of the duty rates in effect on July 1, 1962, placed no limit on reductions of duties already at or below 5 percent ad valorem, and allowed elimination of duties entirely on categories in which the United States and the European Economic Community together accounted for at least 80 percent of world trade, with agricultural products excepted. The Kennedy Round applied the method in practice, with American linear cuts averaging about 35 percent and foreign reductions averaging about 34 percent.

### Q: Has a court ever struck down section 232 under the Trade Expansion Act?

No court had struck down the provision as of August 1, 2020. In Federal Energy Administration v. Algonquin SNG, Inc., decided on June 17, 1976, the Supreme Court sustained the provision, held that license fees were a permissible method of adjusting imports, and held that the standards the statute supplied were sufficient against a delegation attack. In American Institute for International Steel v. United States, a three judge panel of the Court of International Trade rejected a facial challenge on March 25, 2019, the Federal Circuit affirmed in February 2020, and the Supreme Court denied certiorari on June 22, 2020. Review of a presidential determination remained extremely limited, extending at most to procedural conformity.

### Q: Which president signed the Trade Expansion Act?

President John F. Kennedy signed the act on October 11, 1962. The measure had passed the House of Representatives on June 28, 1962, by a vote of 298 to 125, and the Senate on September 19, 1962, by a vote of 78 to 8. The bill was H.R. 11970 in the 87th Congress, and the enacted statute is Public Law 87-794, printed at 76 Stat. 872 and codified at 19 U.S.C. ch. 7. A presidential proclamation issued for the fiftieth anniversary of the act in 2012 confirmed the signing date and recorded that the act established the Special Representative for Trade Negotiations.

### Q: How long did the negotiating authority last?

The negotiating authority at section 201 ran for five years, to June 30, 1967. Within that term the president could enter trade agreements and proclaim the authorized reductions, measured against the duty rates in effect on July 1, 1962. The terminal date operated as a real constraint on the negotiation the authority enabled. The Kennedy Round, opened at Geneva in May 1964, concluded with agreements signed on June 30, 1967, the last day of the authority, after marathon closing sessions in May 1967. After the term expired, the specific ceilings and baselines of the grant expired with the term, and further authority required a new grant from Congress.

### Q: What office did the act create for trade negotiations?

The act created the office of Special Representative for Trade Negotiations, the predecessor of the modern United States Trade Representative. A dedicated representative recognized that formula bargaining across the whole schedule, conducted with a customs union and inside a multilateral round, required a negotiator whose responsibility centered on trade negotiation as a continuing function. The office also gave Congress and foreign governments an identifiable counterpart for the conduct of the negotiation. A presidential proclamation issued for the fiftieth anniversary of the act in 2012 confirmed the establishment of the office as one of the institutional legacies of the statute enacted on October 11, 1962.

### Q: How were the Kennedy Round concessions put into effect?

Concessions in the Kennedy Round were staged in five annual installments beginning January 1, 1968. Staging separated the legal conclusion of the round, marked by signature of the agreements on June 30, 1967, from the full economic arrival of the reductions. A duty reduction divided into five annual steps gave traders a known schedule for purchases, contracts, and production rather than a single reduction on one date. The settlement itself combined averaged industrial cuts of about 35 percent from the United States and about 34 percent from foreign participants, coverage of about 64 percent of American non-agricultural dutiable imports, a limited agricultural result, an antidumping code, and a grains arrangement with a food aid program.

### Q: What did the Kennedy Round produce besides tariff cuts?

The round produced the GATT Antidumping Code of 1967 and a grains arrangement with a food aid program, in addition to the linear tariff reductions. The antidumping code addressed the rules governing antidumping action rather than the height of ordinary customs duties, and the grains arrangement belonged to the agricultural side of the conference, where tariff results had been limited. About 50 countries participated in the round, which opened at Geneva in May 1964 under the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade and concluded with agreements signed on June 30, 1967. The non tariff products formed part of the same closing settlement as the averaged industrial cuts.

### Q: Why did petroleum dominate the early uses of the security provision?

Petroleum supplied the actions that produced restrictions or fees in the early record under section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962. President Ford raised license fees on imported oil in 1975 by Proclamation 4341, President Carter proclaimed a license fee on crude oil and gasoline in 1980 by Proclamation 4744, and President Reagan embargoed Libyan crude oil imports in 1982 by Proclamation 4907. A federal district court held the Carter action unlawful in May 1980. For petroleum only, the Crude Oil Windfall Profit Tax Act of 1980 created a joint resolution of disapproval route against presidential petroleum adjustments. No comparable sequence of actions on other products appears in the record for those years.

### Q: What happened in the automobiles investigation?

The Commerce Department opened an investigation of automobiles and parts in May 2018, and the Commerce report was delivered in February 2019. A presidential proclamation dated May 17, 2019 deferred a decision for up to 180 days while negotiations proceeded, and no import restrictions followed. The record at the August 1, 2020 vantage therefore consists of an opening, a report, a deferral, and an absence of restrictions. The case stands alongside other investigations of the same period that ended without restrictions, including the uranium investigation, in which a presidential memorandum dated July 12, 2019 declined restrictions and established a working group, and the titanium sponge investigation, in which restrictions were declined in February 2020.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court hold in Algonquin?

In Federal Energy Administration v. Algonquin SNG, Inc., 426 U.S. 548, argued on April 20, 1976 and decided on June 17, 1976, a unanimous Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice Brennan, held that section 232(b) authorized the license fee program on imported oil. Fees were a permissible method of adjusting imports and the authority was not limited to quotas, contrary to the narrower reading adopted by the court of appeals. The Court also held that the standards the statute supplied were clearly sufficient to meet any delegation doctrine attack. The finding under review was the finding of the Secretary of the Treasury, made under the provision as amended by the Trade Act of 1974.

### Q: What happened in the American Institute for International Steel litigation?

A trade association and two member companies filed suit in the Court of International Trade in June 2018, arguing on the face of the statute that section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 delegates legislative power without limit. A three judge panel rejected the challenge on March 25, 2019, holding itself bound by Algonquin, and Judge Katzmann joined the judgment while writing separately, dubitante, to question whether the delegation could be squared with the Constitution and to invite reconsideration by a court free to give reconsideration. An attempt to take the case directly to the Supreme Court was turned away in 2019, the Federal Circuit affirmed in February 2020, and the Supreme Court denied certiorari on June 22, 2020.

### Q: What assistance did the act offer workers?

For workers, the act provided readjustment allowances, retraining, and relocation allowances. Readjustment allowances served as income support during the interval after a separation, retraining addressed the possibility that a displaced worker required different skills for a different occupation or industry, and relocation allowances addressed the cost of moving where suitable work existed elsewhere. Access to each form of help depended on a Tariff Commission determination that the statutory findings had been satisfied, including the finding that increased imports had resulted in major part from concessions granted under trade agreements. That gatekeeping structure meant that the forms of assistance described in the statute became available only after certification of a petition.

### Q: What assistance did the act offer firms and communities?

For firms, the act provided loans, loan guarantees, and technical assistance. A direct loan supplied capital from the federal government, a loan guarantee placed a federal commitment behind borrowing from private sources, and technical assistance addressed the knowledge required to change products, processes, or markets in response to a changed competitive position. The act also provided assistance for industries and for communities, recognizing that displacement extended beyond a single plant to the community dependent on an injured industry. Each form of assistance stood behind the same gate, a Tariff Commission determination applying the import volume, concessions causation, and injury causation findings that the statute required before eligibility attached.

### Q: How did the successor statute change adjustment assistance?

The Trade Act of 1974 liberalized the design in three connected ways. The concessions causation test was dropped, ending the requirement that increased imports be traced in major part to concessions granted under trade agreements. The causation standard that remained was lowered, so that imports needed only to contribute importantly to the job separations rather than to stand as the major factor in causing the separations. Administration of worker petitions moved from the Tariff Commission to the Department of Labor. Certified workers rose from about 59,000 in fiscal 1975 to nearly 600,000 in fiscal 1980, a scale that measured the effect of the criteria change on access to the program.

### Q: What is the counter-reading of the security provision?

The counter reading holds that section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 is not a loophole. Congress enacted the breadth deliberately, in 1955 and again in 1962 and 1974, retaining the standard of threat to impair the national security, the listed factors, and presidential choice over the nature and duration of the adjustment. The courts declined to narrow the breadth, sustaining the fee method in Algonquin and refusing in the American Institute for International Steel litigation to distinguish tariffs from the fees sustained in that earlier case. On that reading, the objection is properly to the breadth of the delegation Congress made, not to its use, and correction belongs to Congress through amendment.
