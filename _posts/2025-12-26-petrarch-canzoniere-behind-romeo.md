Romeo's first love speeches sound, to an ear trained on anthologies of romantic quotation, like the spontaneous overflow of a young heart. They are nothing of the kind, and the play knows it. Behind nearly every figure the young Montague aims at the absent Rosaline stands a book: the Canzoniere of Francesco Petrarca, the 366 lyrics that Petrarch spent a lifetime assembling around the figure of Laura, and that fixed for three centuries the grammar in which Europe talked about desire. The verdict argued here is double, and the play itself draws the line. Petrarch the poet built a self-indicting, theologically troubled, formally restless book whose lover knows his worship is a beautiful error and says so in its first sonnet. Petrarchism, the convention that descended from that book through commentaries, courts, and print, flattened the error into a kit of portable conceits: the freezing and burning lover, the worshipped and refusing lady, the siege, the ship, the saint. Shakespeare's Romeo speaks the kit, not the book. The tragedy then does something no Elizabethan sonnet sequence dared to do at length: it lets the kit be answered, first by Mercutio's open mockery, which names Petrarch in the text, and then by a beloved who steps inside the sonnet, takes half its lines, and breaks the system's founding condition, which is her silence.

That distinction, between the poet and the ism, governs everything that follows. Critics who treat Romeo's early manner as a failure of sincerity are measuring him against the wrong standard, because the speeches were written to be heard as quotation, as the performance of a reader. The first audiences of the mid-1590s had spent five years buying printed sonnet sequences and could recognize a borrowed conceit the way a later audience recognizes a sampled hook. What they were offered in Act 1 was a boy ventriloquizing a tradition, and the comedy of the performance depends on the gap between the grandeur of the borrowed idiom and the vacancy of the occasion: Rosaline never appears, never speaks, never even receives a report of the speeches that worship her. Petrarch's Laura is scarcely less absent, but the Canzoniere makes that absence its explicit subject, worrying at it for 366 poems. Romeo's speeches make absence a convenience. Restoring the source restores the satire's target, and it also restores the play's surprising tenderness toward the source itself, because the broken, dialogic love language that replaces the kit is closer in spirit to Petrarch's own divided book than anything in the sequences that claimed his mantle.

## What Is the Canzoniere? The Book Behind the Borrowed Voice

The Canzoniere is not a pile of love poems that happened to accumulate. It is one of the most deliberately constructed books of the European Middle Ages, assembled, revised, reordered, and recopied by its author across roughly four decades, from the 1330s until Petrarch's death in 1374. Its Latin title, Rerum vulgarium fragmenta, fragments of vernacular things, performs a famous double gesture: the humanist who wrote Latin epics and was crowned with laurel on the Capitoline in Rome in 1341 pretends to apologize for his Italian lyrics as scattered trifles, while lavishing on them a care he gave to nothing else he wrote. The surviving working manuscript, Vatican Library MS Vat. Lat. 3195, partly in Petrarch's own hand and partly in the hand of his scribe Giovanni Malpaghini under the poet's correction, shows the book being built poem by poem, with lyrics drafted, cancelled, transposed, and polished across years. No English sonneteer of the 1590s worked at anything like this scale of self-editing. The sequences that flooded London bookstalls were assembled in seasons; the Canzoniere was assembled in a lifetime, and its architecture is part of its meaning.

The architecture is numerical before it is narrative. The book contains 366 poems, a count that readers since the Renaissance have connected to the days of the year with one day over, as though the lover's devotion filled a calendar and then exceeded it. Thomas P. Roche Jr., in his studies of the sequence's calendrical structure, argued that the numbering is devotional design rather than accident: a poem for every day, with the surplus poem, the closing canzone to the Virgin, standing outside the count of days as the soul's appeal stands outside the machinery of desire. Whether or not every joint of the scheme persuades, the broad fact is plain in the artifact: the lover first sees Laura on 6 April 1327, and she dies on 6 April 1348, twenty-one years later to the day, and the poems keep returning to that anniversary the way a liturgy returns to a feast. Petrarch recorded both dates in his famous note written in his manuscript of Virgil, now in the Biblioteca Ambrosiana in Milan, a private memorandum that turns biography into bookkeeping: Laura, "illustrious by her own virtues, long celebrated in my verses," first met his eyes on that April morning in the church of Sainte-Claire in Avignon, and on the same day and hour twenty-one years on, she was taken from the world during the plague. A tradition that would later make a style out of this poet began by ignoring how severely the poems are dated, placed, and counted.

### Of the 366 poems, how many are sonnets, and what are the rest?

The Canzoniere contains 317 sonnets among its 366 poems; the remainder are 29 canzoni, nine sestinas, seven ballate, and four madrigals, larger and smaller forms placed at the book's structural joints like columns among brickwork, with the canzoni carrying the narrative and penitential weight the sonnets cannot hold.

The distribution matters because the English tradition took the sonnet and left the rest behind. When Wyatt and Surrey translated Petrarch, they translated sonnets; when the 1590s sequences imitated him, they imitated the sonnet almost exclusively. Yet the Canzoniere's own weight-bearing members are often the canzoni, the long multi-stanza songs that interrupt the sonnet flow at moments of maximum pressure: the metamorphosis canzone (poem 23) that retells the lover's pursuit through the myth of Apollo and Daphne, the political and penitential canzoni, and above all poem 126, "Chiare, fresche et dolci acque," the address to the waters of the Sorgue at Vaucluse where Laura had bathed, which asks the landscape itself to remember her. The sestinas, nine of them, import the troubadour Arnaut Daniel's ferociously difficult rotating form, six end-words permuted through six stanzas, a machinery of recurrence that enacts obsession at the level of rhyme: the same six words return in altered order, as the lover's same six thoughts return on altered days. A reader who knows only the sonnets knows the Canzoniere the way a reader who knows only the balcony scene knows the tragedy, accurately and insufficiently.

The book's deepest structural fact is its division into two parts. Poems 1 to 263 are the poems of Laura alive, conventionally called the rime in vita; poems 264 to 366 are the poems of Laura dead, the rime in morte, opening with the great canzone of self-reckoning, "I' vo pensando," in which the lover thinks, and thinking finds pity and terror assaulting him together. The division converts a collection into a plot with a catastrophe at its center. In the first part, the beloved is present as an absence, glimpsed, celebrated, blamed, and begged; in the second, she is absent as a presence, returning in dreams and visions, reproaching the lover's tears, and drawing his desire upward toward the heaven where she now dwells. Romeo never gets a second part. His Rosaline poems, if they existed, would be all in vita and all stasis, because nothing in the system permits development: the lady must not answer, relent, age, or die within the fiction of the sequence, since any of those events would end the song. Petrarch let his lady die and wrote another hundred poems about what desire becomes afterward. His imitators, with rare exceptions, kept their ladies in the first part forever, and Shakespeare, who understood the source better than the imitators did, built the stasis into his characterization: the Rosaline affair cannot develop, so it is simply dropped, mid-gesture, when a real interlocutor appears.

### Why does Petrarch keep revising and reordering the poems across his whole life?

Because the Canzoniere is a conversion narrative told in lyric order rather than in story: each rearrangement re-weights the lover's error against his repentance, and the surviving drafts and the partly autograph Vatican manuscript show Petrarch adjusting that balance, cancelling, transposing, and polishing, until the sequence itself became the record of a conscience under revision.

That is the first and largest thing the convention lost. Petrarchism inherited the poems as a warehouse of detachable conceits, stripped of their addresses in a numbered, dated, self-correcting whole. The proem sonnet warns the warehouse-raiders in advance. "Voi ch'ascoltate in rime sparse il suono," the book begins, in Robert M. Durling's edition and translation (Petrarch's Lyric Poems, Harvard University Press, 1976): whoever hears in scattered rhymes the sound of those sighs with which the poet nourished his heart, during his "primo giovenile errore," his first youthful error, will find, the older poet hopes, not only pardon but pity. The book opens by renouncing itself. It frames the entire collection as the record of a mistake its author has outgrown, and then spends 365 further poems failing to outgrow it. That tension, between the penitent frame and the unrepentant contents, is the Canzoniere's engine. Every Elizabethan reader who lifted a conceit out of the book left the engine behind, and the conceit, detached, became what Mercutio could laugh at. The play's satire, examined closely, is aimed with unusual precision: it mocks the detached conceits and never touches the frame, because the frame, a lover's idiom judged and found wanting by the lover himself, is exactly the judgment the play is about to perform by other means.

## The Anniversary Machine: How the Canzoniere Tells Time

No lyric book of the European tradition is as saturated with dates as this one, and the saturation is a poetics. Where the English sequences that descend from the Canzoniere float their desires in an unspecified springtime, Petrarch's poems keep a ledger of years. The lover counts: this is the tenth year, the fifteenth year, the twentieth year that the sighs have continued; the beloved's eyes first struck in one April, and every April the wound is audited. Sonnet 61 blesses the entire apparatus of the date, day, month, year, season, hour, instant, and place, as though time itself had conspired in the capture. Other poems open by reckoning elapsed years since the first sight, and the reckoning darkens across the book as the lover's hair whitens and the sum grows. The effect, cumulative across 366 poems, is of desire audited by a calendar that refuses to let it pretend to be a moment. Petrarch's lover is not a young man surprised by feeling; he is a middle-aged cleric reviewing two decades of invoices. That temporal depth, the sense of a passion with a past and an accumulating cost, is the first casualty of transmission. A single sonnet, lifted into a miscellany or a sequence, has no anniversaries; it has weather. The kit that Romeo inherits is the Canzoniere with its calendar torn out, and the loss shows in the borrowing: Romeo's love for Rosaline has duration effects, the dawn vigils, the augmented tears, the artificial night, but no duration, no counted years, nothing at stake across time, because the borrowed posture supplies the symptoms of a long devotion to a devotion of a few weeks.

The calendar is also theological engineering. By placing the first sight on Good Friday, in church, during the liturgy of the Passion, Petrarch builds his lover's error into the holiest day of the Christian year: desire enters under the sign of the Crucifixion, and the poems that follow are shadowed by the question of whether the lover's suffering imitates Christ's or parodies it. The anniversary poems keep the shadow current, since every 6 April is simultaneously the feast of the capture and, after 1348, the feast of the death, joy and grief assigned the same square on the calendar. Roche's calendrical reading presses the design further, finding in the count of 366 a devotional arithmetic keyed to the year and its feasts; skeptics of the scheme reply that lyric poets count poems for many reasons and that the structure may record a lifetime's accretion rather than a blueprint. The adjudication that matters for the play does not require the blueprint in full. Whatever the degree of premeditation, the finished book behaves as though time were its second subject: it is a machine for converting a moment into twenty-one years of return. Shakespeare, whose tragedy famously compresses its action into days and lets its hero complain that the hours proceed too slowly, understood the machine's value by inversion. His lovers get the capture without the calendar, the first sight at a feast with no anniversaries ahead of them, and the play's speed is precisely the tragedy of a Petrarchan book denied its second part: no decades of return, no whitening hair, no audit. The borrowed idiom promised a lifetime of song; the plot allows four days. That mismatch between the tradition's time and the tragedy's time is one of the quiet engines of the catastrophe, and it is set running the moment a boy trained on anniversaries meets a girl who has none to spare.

## The Laura Structure: Absence, Worship, and the Divided Self

To see what Romeo borrowed, the system itself needs describing on its own terms, from the book rather than from the convention's later summary of it. The Canzoniere's love is founded on a single perception, endlessly re-examined: the lover sees the beloved, in church, on Good Friday, 6 April 1327, and the seeing is figured from the start as a wound and a capture. Sonnet 3, "Era il giorno ch'al sol si scoloraro," places the moment under the darkened sun of the Crucifixion: it was the day the sun's rays paled in pity for their maker when the poet was caught, unarmed, by Laura's eyes, at a season when he had lowered his guard against Love's blows because the world's sorrow seemed to guarantee a truce. The blasphemous audacity of the dating is Petrarch's own, and it is the opposite of decorative. Desire arrives on the one day of the year when desire should be impossible, and the lover's whole subsequent life is the working out of that scandal. Sonnet 61 blesses the calendar itself: "Benedetto sia 'l giorno e 'l mese e l'anno," blessed be the day and the month and the year, the season and the time and the hour and the instant, the sweet country and the place where the poet was struck by two beautiful eyes that have bound him. Binding is the system's characteristic verb. The lover does not pursue in any practical sense; he is held.

Held by what, exactly? By a beloved constructed, with full authorial awareness, out of a name. Laura is lauro, the laurel, the crown of poets that Petrarch received on the Capitoline in 1341 and never stopped desiring; she is l'aura, the breeze that moves through the poems of Vaucluse and stirs the golden hair of sonnet 90, "Erano i capei d'oro a l'aura sparsi," her golden hair loosened to the breeze and knotted again in a thousand sweet knots. The pun is the poetics. To love Laura is to love the laurel, to desire the woman is to desire fame, and the Canzoniere's most searching poems, as John Freccero showed in his classic reading of the fig tree and the laurel, know that the beloved is partly an idol the lover has manufactured to worship his own ambition. Daphne fled Apollo and became a laurel tree; Petrarch's lover pursues the laurel and calls the pursuit love. No Elizabethan sonneteer who borrowed the golden hair borrowed the self-accusation folded inside it. Romeo, who wants Rosaline to yield and cannot imagine what he would do with a yielded Rosaline, is the pun's reductio: a lover whose lady is a pure function of his rhetoric, a name with a refusal attached.

The refusal is structural, not temperamental. The Laura of the in vita poems is married, distant, chaste, and almost entirely silent; she speaks, across 263 poems, hardly at all, and her silence is the medium in which the poetry grows, since a beloved who answered would end the asymmetry that generates the song. What she gives instead of speech is the gaze and its opposite: the eyes that wound, the eyes withdrawn, the veil, the glove, the hand glimpsed and gone. Sonnet 90 builds its portrait from a vision already past tense: the golden hair was loosened, the light of the beautiful eyes, once so ardent, is now dimmed, and the poet's wound does not heal though the bow has slackened. The system requires the beloved to be simultaneously the cause of the wound and the only physician, and to withhold the cure; hence the beloved as sweet enemy, the figure Romeo will inherit at several removes. Hence, too, the lover's division against himself, which the Canzoniere states as nakedly as any text of the European tradition. Sonnet 132 opens with a catechism of self-interrogation: "S'amor non è, che dunque è quel ch'io sento?" If it is not love, what is it that I feel? But if it is love, what thing is it, and what kind? If good, why the bitter mortal fruit; if evil, why is every torment sweet? The poem is a man cross-examining his own pulse, and finding no witness reliable. Chaucer thought it the representative Petrarch poem, and made it the first Petrarch translation in English, as will appear below.

Worship completes the structure. The lover's devotion borrows the forms of religion with a deliberateness that Petrarch, a cleric who held benefices and wrote Latin treatises on the religious life, could not have undertaken innocently: Laura is saluted like the Virgin, her eyes are heavens, her coming is an annunciation of pain, and the lover keeps her anniversaries like feasts. The play's most famous borrowing takes this element and, crucially, shares it: the shared sonnet at the Capulet feast casts Juliet as a holy shrine and Romeo's lips as two blushing pilgrims (1.5.92-109), and the religious diction there is usually read as the moment borrowed language starts to live. The difference from the Canzoniere is not the diction but the direction of address. Petrarch's worship is a monologue conducted before a silent altar; the lover's prayers rise and are registered only by the landscape, the river, and the reader. In the play, the shrine answers, corrects the liturgy, and redistributes the lines. Everything the tragedy will become is latent in that redistribution, and it is why the Petrarchan inheritance in this play cannot be described as simple satire: the play mocks the posture and keeps the pressure, mocks the monologue and salvages the devotion by making it dialogue.

## The Portrait and the Relics: Hair, Eyes, Hand, and Glove

The system's worship is conducted through objects, and the objects deserve their own hearing, because they are what the borrowing carried most faithfully. Laura is never described entire. The Canzoniere builds her from a liturgy of parts and contacts, each one a relic: the golden hair of sonnet 90, loosened to the breeze and reknotted; the eyes, whose light wounds and heals; the hand, celebrated in the paired sonnets of poems 199 and 200, where a glove drawn on and off becomes an erotic event of the first magnitude; the foot that touched the grass of Vaucluse; the veil that gives and withholds the face. Fragmentation is the theology of the thing. A whole woman might answer, age, sicken, refuse in words; a constellation of relics can be arranged forever around an absence, each fragment polished by repetition until it shines like the gold of the puns. Sonnet 90, the tradition's favorite portrait, is explicit about the method: it is written in the past tense of a vision, the hair was loosened, the eyes were ardent, the gait was not mortal, and the poem closes with the lover's conservatism of pain, the wound unhealed though the bow has slackened. The portrait preserves by embalming. Everything the English blazon would become, the catalogue of forehead, lip, foot, and thigh that Mercutio chants at the orchard wall, begins here as an act of mourning disguised as praise, the beloved assembled from the parts the lover happened to glimpse on the days the calendar commemorates.

The gloves and veils carry the system's other secret: how little contact it needs. The glove poems make a metaphysics out of a near-touch; the beloved's hand, covered and uncovered, is the closest the lover's own hand comes to hers, and the poems linger over the covering as though the leather were a sacrament. Worship at this distance is sustainable indefinitely, which is the point and the indictment. Petrarch's conscience, audible everywhere in the frame, knows that a devotion fed on glimpses has chosen its diet: the relic system protects the lover from the beloved's reality as surely as it protects her chastity from his pursuit. The play inherits the relics and submits them to the same audit. Romeo enters the feast planning to touch, at most, a hand, and the pilgrim figure he builds from the contact is Laura's liturgy entire, shrine, saint, and suppliant palm; the audit arrives when the supposed relic turns out to be attached to a person, who takes his hand, assigns the parts in the devotion, and informs the suppliant that saints grant prayers without moving. The glove wish that Romeo breathes at the balcony belongs to the same economy and is treated at length in the transformation account cross-linked below; within the source argument its significance is simple. A lover formed by the Canzoniere's relics wishes to be an article of the beloved's clothing because clothing, in the source system, is the maximum of permitted intimacy. The play hears the wish as what it is, the kit's ceiling mistaken for desire's height, and spends the rest of the tragedy replacing articles of clothing with vows, rings, and finally a shared tomb, contacts that no glove could mediate.

## The Myth Under the System: Apollo, Daphne, and Canzone 23

Every system has a foundation myth, and the Canzoniere's is the pursuit of Daphne. In Ovid's Metamorphoses, the god Apollo, mocked by Cupid and struck by the golden arrow of desire, pursues the nymph Daphne, who has been struck by the leaden arrow of aversion and prays for escape; her father, the river god Peneus, answers by transforming her into a laurel tree, and Apollo, embracing the trunk, adopts the laurel as his sacred plant, the crown of poets and victors. Petrarch, whose beloved's name is the laurel, recognized his own story in the myth with an exactness that amounts to self-diagnosis, and he built the recognition into the book's longest early poem, the metamorphosis canzone, poem 23, "Nel dolce tempo de la prima etade." The canzone retells the lover's life as a series of transformations suffered in Laura's pursuit: he becomes a laurel himself, rooted and voiceless where she passed; a swan, singing his own death; a stone on a mountain; a voice crying in the wilderness; Actaeon, torn by his own hounds for having seen Diana bathing. The metamorphoses are not ornament. They state the system's physics: desire unmakes the desirer, form by form, while the beloved, herself the tree, stands unchanged at the center, the one fixed point in a world of liquefying selves. Apollo's consolation becomes the lover's profession: if the woman cannot be had, the tree can be crowned, and the crown can be poetry. The whole Petrarchan enterprise, the conversion of refusal into fame, is Apollo's bargain restated as a literary career.

The play knows the myth and assigns its physics with care. Romeo in Act 1 performs the liquefaction without the bargain: he is smoke, fire, sea, lead, a soul staked to the ground, a chaos of forms, but the transformations signify nothing because no fixed point organizes them; Rosaline is not a tree at the center of his world but a pretext at its edge, and the metamorphoses remain a wardrobe of postures tried on before a mirror. The fixed point arrives with Juliet, and the play's imagery registers the acquisition at once: she is the sun, a star, a jewel against the cheek of night, the still center around which the balcony scene's astronomy arranges itself. But where Petrarch's lover converts the fixed point into a career of song, converting loss into laurel across decades, Shakespeare's lover has no career available to him, only a plot, and the plot's time is four days. The myth thus completes the source argument from below. The Canzoniere is Apollo's bargain elaborated into a book of life; Petrarchism kept the liquefactions and dropped the bargain, which is why its speakers melt so beautifully to no purpose; and the play, restoring the fixed point, restores the stakes, and then, by its speed, denies its hero the one compensation the myth guarantees. Romeo gets the pursuit and the transformation. The laurel, in Verona, is a statue of gold, and it is raised over a grave.

## The Oxymoron Engine: From Sonnet 134 to Romeo's First Speech

If the Canzoniere has a single detachable mechanism, it is the oxymoron, and if the mechanism has a single source text, it is sonnet 134. "Pace non trovo," the poem begins: I find no peace, and I have no war to make; I fear and I hope; I burn and I am ice; I fly above the heavens and I lie on the ground; I hold nothing, and I embrace the whole world. The antitheses accelerate through the sonnet like a man pacing a cell: a prison that neither opens nor shuts holds him; Love neither keeps him nor sets him free, neither wills him to live nor lifts his suffering; he sees without eyes and, tongueless, cries out; he longs to perish and begs for help; he hates himself and loves another; he feeds on grief and, weeping, laughs; death and life displease him equally. Then the sestet's machinery stops on the plainest line in the sequence: "in questo stato son, donna, per voi," in this state I am, lady, because of you. The whole paradox engine exists to deliver that attribution. The lover's incoherence is not a performance for its own sake; it is evidence, tendered to the one witness who could resolve it and will not. Durling's commentary on the poem treats it as the epitome of the lover's condition in the book, the state of war against the self that the sequence everywhere presupposes.

Now set beside it Romeo's first extended speech, delivered to Benvolio in the street outside the Capulet orchard of the plot, in the Arden Third Series text edited by René Weis (2012): "O brawling love, O loving hate, / O anything of nothing first create! / O heavy lightness, serious vanity, / Misshapen chaos of well-seeming forms, / Feather of lead, bright smoke, cold fire, sick health, / Still-waking sleep, that is not what it is! / This love feel I, that feel no love in this" (1.1.176-185). The kinship is not a matter of vague influence; it is mechanical. Petrarch's sestet of contraries has been unpacked into a catalogue, item by item, the balanced clauses of the Italian compressed into paired nouns and adjectives that can be rattled off like coins from a purse. "Cold fire" is sonnet 134's burn-and-ice with the grammar removed. "Still-waking sleep" is the prison that neither opens nor shuts, flattened into a phrase. Even the closing attribution survives, inverted: Petrarch's "in this state I am, lady, because of you" becomes Romeo's "This love feel I, that feel no love in this," a line whose grammatical contortion admits what the catalogue conceals, that the feeling has no object in it at all. The speech is a translation performed by a boy who has learned the gestures of a state he has not reached. Where Petrarch's oxymorons diagnose, Romeo's decorate. The inventory of that decoration, conceit by conceit and mockery by mockery, is drawn up in the companion catalogue of [Petrarchan Conceits and How the Play Mocks Them](/2025/01/10/petrarchan-conceits-romeo-juliet/), which tracks the play-internal career of each figure; the concern here is upstream, with the source the figures were drawn from and the damage done in transit.

The damage has a name in the scholarship: decontextualization. In the Canzoniere, sonnet 134 stands at a specific pressure point in the book's long argument, one station in a numbered pilgrimage of error, and its contraries are weighted by the 133 poems before it and the 232 after. Lifted out, it becomes a party trick. Wyatt, translating the poem in the 1530s or 1540s, already performs the lifting, brilliantly and knowingly: "I find no peace, and all my war is done. / I fear and hope. I burn and freeze like ice. / I fly above the wind, yet can I rise no higher. / I nothing hold, and yet in my arms I hold the world." Wyatt's version keeps the architecture and sharpens the paradoxes into courtier's epigram, but the courtier's context matters: a Henrician courtier singing of a prison that neither opens nor shuts, of a power that will neither loose him nor hold him in bands, is describing his employment as much as his desire. The conceits in Wyatt still bite their surroundings. It is in the printed sequences of the 1590s, where the oxymoron catalogue becomes a set piece every sonneteer must execute, like a scale every pianist must run, that the bite goes out of it. By the time Romeo performs the catalogue in a Verona street, the figures are sixty years into their English afterlife, and the play counts on the audience hearing the performance as performance. Benvolio's response is the audience's cue, and it is not awe; it is laughter, invited in the speech itself: "Dost thou not laugh?" The apprentice lover already half-knows the speech is a routine. That half-knowledge is Shakespeare's refinement of the source, because Petrarch's lover also half-knows: the proem sonnet is the confession of a man who has heard himself performing and winced. The play gives the wince to the wrong character, the performer too young to mean it, and reserves the real self-knowledge for later, when oxymoron returns on Juliet's lips in Act 3, aimed at a husband who has killed her cousin, and the figures stop being furniture and start being pain.

## The Sweet Enemy: Siege, Store, and the Economics of Refusal

The oxymoron is the system's smallest unit; its largest is the campaign. Petrarch's lover figures his service as a war he cannot win and cannot abandon: sonnet 134's opening admits he has no war to make, because the war is internal, and the military conceits that spread from the book through European lyric, the siege of the lady's heart, the assault of the eyes, the fortress of chastity, are all ways of dignifying a stalemate. Romeo inherits the campaign complete. Rosaline, he reports, "will not stay the siege of loving terms, / Nor bide th' encounter of assailing eyes, / Nor ope her lap to saint-seducing gold" (1.1.209-211). She has forsworn love itself: "She hath forsworn to love, and in that vow / Do I live dead that live to tell it now" (1.1.217-218). And she is proof against the system's first weapon: "she'll not be hit / With Cupid's arrow; she hath Dian's wit" (1.1.205-207). Every element here is Canzoniere stock at several removes: the besieged fortress, the chastity of Diana as armor, the archer god whose shafts the lady's resolve turns aside. But notice what the play does with the stock. In Petrarch, the siege fails because the lover's enterprise is wrongly aimed, aimed at a laurel that is also a woman, and the book knows the confusion is the lover's fault. In Romeo's telling, the siege fails because Rosaline is impregnable, and the failure licenses the speech. The conceit has been rotated a hundred and eighty degrees: where the source examines the besieger, the borrower exhibits the fortress. Rosaline's whole function in the borrowed system, as the examination of [Romeo's Rosaline Problem: Love Before Juliet](/2022/05/20/romeo-rosaline-infatuation-before-juliet/) sets out, is to be the lady who keeps the lover singing by keeping him out.

Beside the siege stands the ledger, and here the play's borrowing grows more diagnostic. Romeo complains that Rosaline's beauty is a hoard wrongly kept: "She is rich in beauty, only poor / That when she dies with beauty dies her store," and again, "For beauty, starved with her severity, / Cuts beauty off from all posterity" (1.1.213-216). The argument is that beauty is capital, chastity is miserliness, and the lady owes the world heirs. It sounds like a lover's plea and reads like an accountant's. The Canzoniere stands behind this too, but at an angle the borrower has missed: Petrarch's book is itself the answer to the posterity problem, since the poems preserve Laura's beauty beyond her death and say so, the sequence performing for its lady the very immortality Romeo demands Rosaline manufacture biologically. The lover of the Canzoniere is the one who guarantees the store; he does not invoice the lady for it. Romeo's version, in which the lady's refusal is an economic offense against the future, is Petrarchism with the poet's responsibility removed and the lover's claim retained, the convention at its most entitled. The Friar will answer the ledger later with a rival economy drawn from the earth, where grace and rude will compete within a single plant; the point here is that the play hears the entitlement in the borrowed figure and marks it, quietly, as a symptom. A young man who experiences a stranger's chastity as theft from posterity is not in the grip of a person. He is in the grip of a book he has misread as a manual of claims.

The misreading is completed in Act 1 Scene 2, where the borrowed system shows its theology. Invited to the Capulet feast to compare Rosaline with other beauties, Romeo defends his lady in the language of heresy: "When the devout religion of mine eye / Maintains such falsehood, then turn tears to fires, / And these, who often drowned could never die, / Transparent heretics, be burnt for liars" (1.2.92-95). The eyes are devotees; rival beauties are false doctrine; doubt is heresy punishable by burning. The figure is dazzling and slightly mad, and its madness is the point: a religion with one communicant, one article of faith, and an inquisition for anyone who proposes evidence. Petrarch's religious diction, for all its daring, is haunted by the knowledge that the worship is misdirected, that the creature has been given the Creator's devotion, and the second part of the Canzoniere is the long correction of exactly that error. Romeo's version has the worship without the haunting. He has inherited the altar and skipped the examination of conscience, which is as compact a definition of Petrarchism, as distinct from Petrarch, as the play anywhere offers.

Consider, against the ledger, what the source system's lover offers in exchange for his service, because the offer is the part of the contract the borrower never reads. Petrarch's lover pays in the only currency he commands: fame. The poems are the beloved's monument, and the book knows its own function so precisely that it encodes her name into the verse itself; sonnet 5, "Quando io movo i sospiri a chiamar voi," builds its lines so that the syllables LAU-RE-TA stand at their joints, the lady's name distributed through the poem like a signature worked into a tapestry, present everywhere and visible only to the reader who is told to look. To be sung by this poet is to be made permanent, and the in morte poems redeem the promise in full: Laura dead is more present, more speaking, more durably enthroned in the sequence than Laura alive ever was, because the poems, not the woman, were always the durable party. The bargain is unequal and both parties know it; the lover gets a career, the lady gets eternity, and the book is the contract recording the exchange. Romeo's ledger speech inverts the contract's polarity. He offers Rosaline nothing; he presents her with a bill, beauty owing posterity a return on its capital, and reads her refusal as default. The apprentice has learned the system's accounting vocabulary and reversed its ethics, converting the poet's gift into the lady's debt. No wonder the Friar answers with an earth where nothing is owed and everything is graced and warring in one stem. The play's economics of love begin in that inversion, and they end at the tomb, where the families discharge the debt in the system's original currency after all: not heirs, but monuments, golden images, fame in metal, the Canzoniere's payment rendered by fathers who arrive, as usual in the tradition, one catastrophe too late.

## Petrarch the Poet Versus Petrarchism the Convention

That definition now needs its scholarly grounding, because the distinction between the poet and the convention is not a rhetorical convenience of this article; it is the central finding of the modern study of Petrarchism, and the scholars disagree, instructively, about what the convention was. Leonard Forster, in The Icy Fire: Five Studies in European Petrarchism (Cambridge University Press, 1969), delivered the bleakest verdict. Surveying the cult's spread from Italy through France and the Low Countries to England, Forster argued that in England especially, Petrarchism functioned as a fashion, a courtly accomplishment, the wearing of an elegant foreign manner; the conceits circulated as counters in a game of compliment whose players had little access to, and less interest in, the inward drama of the Canzoniere itself. The icy fire of his title is Petrarch's own figure for a passion that was, in the source, a psychological condition; in the imitators, Forster contends, it became a temperature at which compliments were served. On Forster's account, Romeo's Act 1 speeches are not a parody of the English Petrarchan manner but a documentary of it: this is what the fashion sounded like, and the play's originality lies in putting the fashion on a stage where a plot could break it.

William J. Kennedy, in Authorizing Petrarch (Cornell University Press, 1994), relocates the flattening from the poets to the scholars. Kennedy's subject is the commentary tradition, the massive apparatus of exposition that attached itself to the Canzoniere from the fifteenth century forward: the life of the poet by Antonio da Tempo's successors, the commentaries of Francesco Filelfo, and above all Alessandro Vellutello's 1525 edition, which rearranged the poems into a biographical itinerary and read them as the chaste record of a virtuous attachment, and Giovanni Andrea Gesualdo's exposition of 1533. On Kennedy's showing, the commentators authorized Petrarch, reconstructed the desiring, self-divided lyricist as a moral exemplar whose love was a school of virtue, and thereby made the book safe to imitate at courts and in print. Petrarchism, in this account, is not a failure to read Petrarch; it is the product of reading him under institutional instruction, with the scandal edited out and the system left tidy. The kit Romeo parrots is, on Kennedy's account, a scholarly artifact before it is a poetic one.

Kennedy's side of the quarrel can be checked against Petrarch's own testimony, because the poet staged the authorization problem inside his Latin works. In the Secretum, the dialogue Petrarch composed in the early 1340s between his own persona, Franciscus, and Saint Augustine, with Lady Truth presiding in silence, Augustine arraigns the lover on two counts: the love of Laura and the love of the laurel, mortal woman and immortal fame, the twin chains that bind the will. Franciscus defends himself with a lover's ingenuity and concedes with a penitent's reluctance, and the dialogue closes without conversion, the defendant promising to amend and visibly not amending. The Secretum is the Canzoniere's conscience kept in a separate book, in Latin, under seal, while the vernacular songs circulated. Posterity reversed the filing: the songs were published to the world and the conscience stayed in the study, and Kennedy's commentators, moralizing what they had, were working with a genuinely partial dossier. The play, arriving two centuries later, re-files the evidence correctly by instinct, putting the songs in the hero's mouth and the conscience in everyone else's.

The disagreement is genuine and it matters. Forster's Petrarchism is a fashion that never touched the book; Kennedy's is a construction built by industrious readers who touched nothing else. Heather Dubrow, in Echoes of Desire: English Petrarchism and Its Counterdiscourses (Cornell University Press, 1995), complicates both by listening for the strains inside English Petrarchism itself: the sequences, she argues, repeatedly generate counterdiscourses, moments where the posture cracks, the speaker resents the lady, the system, or the song, and those cracks are not failures of the convention but part of its English life. The adjudication offered here, and defended through the rest of this article, is that the play takes a position in this quarrel avant la lettre, and takes Kennedy's side about the mechanism and Forster's about the sound. What Romeo speaks is exactly a pre-flattened idiom, flattened by transmission, by commentary, by fashion, by print; nobody in the play supposes he has read the Canzoniere, and the idiom he commands arrived with its psychology pre-excised, which is Kennedy's point. But the sound of the excision, the bright hollow rattle of conceits running on empty, is Forster's point, and the play makes the audience hear it in the first street scene. Dubrow's correction then supplies the ending neither scholar quite predicts: the counterdiscourse that English Petrarchism kept generating in asides, this play promotes to a co-author. Juliet is the counterdiscourse with a name and a balcony. Where the sequences let the system crack, the tragedy lets the beloved answer back inside the form, and the system, answered, becomes something else entirely. The verdict, stated plainly: the Canzoniere shapes the play's love language twice, once as the borrowed kit the hero must outgrow, and once, at greater depth, as the buried model of a love book that judges its own lover, a model the play honors precisely by subjecting its hero's borrowed voice to the judgment Petrarch's frame had already pronounced.

## The Road into English: Chaucer, Wyatt, Surrey, and Tottel's Miscellany

Between the Canzoniere and Romeo's street speeches lies two and a half centuries of transmission, and the route matters, because at every stage the cargo was repacked. The first English carrier was the greatest English poet of the age before print. In Book 1 of Troilus and Criseyde, at the moment Troilus first burns for Criseyde, Geoffrey Chaucer halts his narrative and gives his lover a song, the Canticus Troili (1.400-420), and the song is a translation of Petrarch's sonnet 132, the self-interrogating "S'amor non è." Chaucer's opening follows the Italian with a closeness that startles: "If no love is, O God, what fele I so? / And if love is, what thing and which is he? / If love be good, from whennes cometh my woo?" The paradoxes survive; the frame does not. Chaucer presents the song as Troilus's own composition, hides the source inside a fictional Latin authority he calls Lollius, and sets the solitary lyric inside a narrative where the beloved will, in time, answer, relent, and betray. English Petrarchism thus begins with a characteristic double move: the conceits are imported intact, and the solitude that generated them is abolished by narrative. The Canticus is shared ground with the Chaucer parallel treated elsewhere in this series, and it is noticed here strictly as a transmission event: the moment the Canzoniere's central question, what is this thing I feel, first learned to speak English. It learned to speak English inside somebody else's story, which is where Romeo will keep it.

The second carriers were courtiers, and the cargo changed again. Thomas Wyatt, who traveled to Italy in 1527 on embassy and saw the tradition at its source, translated Petrarch more extensively than any Englishman of his century: the freeze-and-burn sonnet 134, the ship sonnet 189, the white hind sonnet 190, and the great canzone 23 among them, alongside the freer imitations through which Petrarch's situations became Henrician ones. Wyatt's versions are translations the way a duel is a conversation. Sonnet 189's lover pilots a ship laden with oblivion through a winter sea; Wyatt's "galley, charged with forgetfulness" passes "thorough sharp seas in winter nights" between rock and rock, steered with cruelness by the lord who is also Love, and the poem's weather is the weather of Henry VIII's court, where a servant's navigation between favor and ruin was not a metaphor in search of an application. The white hind of sonnet 190 wears a collar inscribed by her Caesar; Wyatt's hind, in "Whoso list to hunt," wears one lettered "Noli me tangere, for Caesar's I am, / And wild for to hold, though I seem tame," and the warning under the warning, addressed to hunters who already know whose deer is meant, is that some quarry destroys the huntsman. Transmission through Wyatt sharpened the conceits and politicized them; what it could not transmit was the book, the 366-poem architecture within which the individual lyrics correct one another. Wyatt worked in manuscript, in a coterie, and his Petrarch circulated as single poems, passed hand to hand. The kit form of Petrarchism, detachable conceits without the architecture, is not a corruption of the print age. It begins in the manuscript circulation of the Tudor court, where a sonnet was already a thing a man carried loose, like a coin.

### What did Wyatt keep, and what did he sharpen, in "I find no peace"?

Wyatt keeps sonnet 134's architecture entire, the fearing and hoping, the burning and freezing, the prison that neither opens nor shuts; what he sharpens is the politics latent in it, turning the lover's helplessness before a lady into a courtier's helplessness before power, so the power that neither looses nor binds describes service at Henry's court as much as desire.

Henry Howard, Earl of Surrey, completed the repacking, and his contribution was formal, which is to say decisive. Surrey translated Petrarch too, and imitated him, but his permanent alteration of the cargo was the container: working with Petrarchan material in English, Surrey settled the native sonnet into the pattern of three quatrains and a closing couplet, the ABAB CDCD EFEF GG architecture that English writers would use for the rest of the century and that Shakespeare would make his own. The Italian sonnet's octave and sestet, proposition and response locked in unequal chambers, became in Surrey's hands a sequence of three parallel statements snapped shut by a couplet, a form built for epigrammatic point rather than for dialectical turn. Romeo's oxymoron catalogue is, in miniature, the Surrey form's prose cousin: parallel items, itemized contraries, and a snap at the end. When the cargo reached print, the repacking became permanent and public. In 1557 the printer Richard Tottel issued Songes and Sonettes, the miscellany known ever since as Tottel's Miscellany, gathering the manuscript verse of the dead courtiers, Surrey executed in 1547, Wyatt dead in 1542, and a company of uncertain hands, and offering the whole as a printed commodity to any reader with the price of the book. Petrarchism in England is, from 1557, a thing that can be bought, browsed, and imitated from a page rather than absorbed from a court. The sonnet vogue of the 1590s, the flood of sequences that taught the play's first audience its fluency, is a separate story of print economics told in its own place in this series; what matters for the source study is the state of the cargo by the time it reached the stage. The conceits had been translated, politicized, re-formed, and commodified. The book they came from had been none of those things to its English readers, most of whom had never encountered it whole. Romeo's idiom is the cargo after four repackings, and the play's satire is precise because it knows the difference between the cargo and the source, even when its hero does not. Readers assembling the transmission into a teachable sequence can open the four documents in a browser-based deck through a [PPT viewer for classroom presentation](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) and watch the same conceit change shape from Avignon to the London bookstalls, which is the fastest way to see that what decayed in transit was never the imagery but the self-knowledge.

Before the cargo reached England, Italy repacked it twice, and the English inheritance is unintelligible without the Italian packaging. In 1501 the Venetian printer Aldus Manutius issued Le cose volgari di messer Francesco Petrarcha, the vernacular works in a small, portable format, edited by the young Pietro Bembo from manuscripts he had studied with a collator's zeal. The Aldine Petrarch was a landmark of print: a classic in the pocket, its text purified, its poems numbered and ordered as the author had left them, addressed to readers who would carry a love book the way they carried a breviary. A generation later Bembo completed the canonization in theory. His Prose della volgar lingua of 1525, the dialogue that fixed the rules of literary Italian, decreed that verse had exactly one model, Petrarch, as prose had Boccaccio: every Italian poet who wished to write well was to write as a Petrarchist, in the diction, the figures, and the postures of the Canzoniere. The decree worked. Sixteenth-century Italy produced Petrarch editions, commentaries, imitations, and Petrarchist poets in industrial quantities, and the Petrarch who traveled north was thus already a curriculum: the most edited, glossed, and mandated love poet in Europe, his book's wildness surveyed into a syllabus. When Kennedy describes the commentators authorizing Petrarch, the Aldine text and Bembo's decree are the machinery of authorization at full power. England did not encounter a wild Italian singer and tame him; England imported a classic pre-tamed by its own culture's schoolmasters, and the kit form, conceits detachable for imitation, is the form a curriculum naturally takes.

One more repacking deserves naming, because it is the repacking through which the play's first audience received the idiom. Through the 1580s and into the 1590s, the Petrarchan idiom passed from print into the general ear: into ballads, into the love talk of the stage itself, into the compliment currency of any literate courtship. Sir Philip Sidney's Astrophil and Stella, composed in the early 1580s and printed in 1591, had given the idiom its most brilliant English performance, a sequence whose speaker is, like Petrarch's, divided against his own performance, and whose opening sonnet announces the whole problem: Astrophil, "loving in truth" and fain in verse his love to show, bites his truant pen and beats himself for spite, until a voice, the Muse or the heart, instructs him to look in his heart and write. Even Sidney's anti-Petrarchan correction is delivered in Petrarch's form, by a speaker named Star-Lover addressing a Star. The correction never escapes the system; it annotates it. Shakespeare's play is the period's boldest annotation because it changes the dramatis personae: it gives the annotating voice a body, a name, a father, a balcony, and forty per cent of the dialogue. No sequence could do that. A stage could.

The stage's audience had also been schooled in the system by its schoolmasters, a fact too easily forgotten when the vogue is told as a story of books alone. English rhetorical culture had been naturalizing Petrarch for a generation before the sequences boomed: George Gascoigne's Certayne Notes of Instruction of 1575, the first English treatise on vernacular verse-making, instructs the beginner in the sonnet's measure and matter as a standard exercise, and George Puttenham's Arte of English Poesie of 1589 surveys the Italian masters with Petrarch at their head as the pattern of the amorous ditty. A grammar-school boy learned his figures from manuals that stocked the oxymoron, the blazon, and the lover's complaint as standard tropes with standard names; a gallant learned his compliments from the same stock at one remove. Fluency in the kit was therefore not a literary accomplishment confined to sonneteers. It was a general social literacy, the small change of courtship and wit, and a playwright could spend it freely, confident that a pit audience would catch a misspent conceit as surely as a false coin. This is the condition that makes Mercutio's satire possible as popular theatre rather than coterie in-joke: the diagnosis of Romeo's idiom is delivered in a public playhouse to hearers who had all, at some level, been issued the same phrasebook, and who could savor the spectacle of a boy reciting it to a wall.

## Romeo's Borrowed Voice: The Apprentice at His Exercises

The play introduces its hero as a reader before it introduces him as a lover. Montague's report of his son in the opening scene is a checklist of the Laura posture performed without a Laura in sight: Romeo haunts the sycamore grove at dawn, augments his tears with the morning dew, shuns the light, makes himself "an artificial night" behind closed curtains (1.1.133-140). This is the in vita lover's daily office, tears, solitude, darkness at noon, executed by a boy whose beloved is a name he has chosen to be refused by. Benvolio's diagnostic language gives the game away: Romeo's sadness has a grammar before it has a cause, and when he finally speaks, the grammar is the message. "Love is a smoke raised with the fume of sighs; / Being purged, a fire sparkling in lovers' eyes; / Being vexed, a sea nourished with loving tears" (1.1.189-191). The definitions proceed by schoolbook division, as though love were a term to be parsed in a rhetoric lesson, substance by substance: smoke, fire, sea. Petrarch's sea, in sonnet 189, is a place a particular ship is sinking in; Romeo's sea is a definition, nourishment and all, of a category. The apprentice has learned that the tradition deals in elemental metaphors and has not learned that in the source the metaphors are addresses, each one spoken to a situation, a landscape, a remembered hour. His elements are spoken to Benvolio, for marks.

The exercises continue through the act with a consistency that is itself the characterization. Told that the remedy for love is looking at other women, Romeo does not argue feeling; he argues doctrine, in the heresy speech already examined, and then reaches for the system's most threadbare courtroom trick, the appeal to the beloved's incomparability staged as a legal incapacity: his eye, he says, never saw true beauty until Rosaline, and any contrary evidence is perjury. At the feast, sent to test the doctrine, he stands at the edge of the dance with a torch, announcing that he is too sore enpierced with Cupid's shaft to soar with Cupid's wings, too bound to bound, a soul of lead staking him to the ground (1.4.13-22). Lead, binding, the sore shaft: the kit again, itemized. Even his foreboding before entering the feast borrows the system's machinery of omens, his mind misgiving some consequence yet hanging in the stars. Nothing in Act 1's Romeo is felt in the wrong way; it is felt in the read way, at one remove, and the remove is the author's subject. The account of [From Petrarch to Passion: Romeo's Changing Voice](/2022/05/27/romeo-petrarchan-language-transformation/) charts what happens to this voice across the whole tragedy; the source question is what the voice is made of at the start, and the answer is now specifiable: Petrarch's oxymoron engine with the psychology removed, Petrarch's campaign imagery with the responsibility removed, Petrarch's religion of the eye with the examination of conscience removed. What remains when all three removals are made is exactly what Mercutio hears when his friend reappears in Act 2, and says so.

## Mercutio Names the Source: Satire as Diagnosis

Shakespeare's boldest stroke in handling the inheritance is to let the satire cite its source. Mercutio, searching the Capulet orchard wall for his vanished friend, performs the borrowed system as a conjuration, summoning Romeo by the inventory of a lady he has never been introduced to: "I conjure thee by Rosaline's bright eyes, / By her high forehead and her scarlet lip, / By her fine foot, straight leg, and quivering thigh, / And the demesnes that there adjacent lie" (2.1.17-20). The blazon, the catalogue of the beloved's parts that descends from the Canzoniere's celebrations of Laura's eyes, hair, hand, and foot, is here exposed as necromancy: a list of body parts chanted at a wall, in the dark, to raise a spirit. The reduction is surgical. Petrarch's blazon scatters the beloved across landscape and heaven because the lover cannot possess her whole; Mercutio's blazon scatters her across an orchard wall because the form, detached from any real woman, was always a list. The laughter has a target more precise than Romeo: it is aimed at the form's claim to be knowledge. A man who can recite the forehead, lip, foot, leg, and thigh of a woman who has never spoken to him knows the convention perfectly and the woman not at all.

Then, in the daylight of Act 2 Scene 4, Mercutio files the diagnosis under its author's name. Greeting the returned Romeo, sociable again and trading quibbles, he delivers the series' most explicit piece of literary criticism: "Now is he for the numbers that Petrarch flowed in! Laura to his lady was a kitchen-wench, marry, she had a better love to berate her, Dido a dowdy, Cleopatra a gipsy, Helen and Hero hildings and harlots, Thisbe a grey eye or so, but not to the purpose" (2.4.38-42). Every term in the sentence works. "Numbers" are verses, measures, the metrical flow of lyric; to be "for the numbers" is to be in the market for ready-made measure, and Petrarch is named as the fountain the numbers flowed from. The roll call that follows is the lover's own superlative machinery turned against itself: the Petrarchan beloved must outshine every predecessor, so Romeo's Rosaline, by the system's arithmetic, demotes Laura to a kitchen wench, Dido to a dowdy, Cleopatra, Helen, Hero, and Thisbe to hildings and harlots, each inflation purchased by a corresponding deflation of the canon. Mercutio has understood the system better than its speaker has: he sees that the beloved's elevation is mechanical, that the machinery requires a Laura to scorn, and that in naming Petrarch he names the patent holder of the machinery. René Weis, editing the play for the Arden Third Series, glosses the passage within exactly this frame, as the play citing the tradition it has been ventriloquizing; the line is the moment the text itself supplies the footnote the audience's fluency had already written. Earlier in the same scene Mercutio had described Romeo as already dead, "stabbed with a white wench's black eye, shot through the ear with a love-song, the very pin of his heart cleft with the blind bow-boy's butt-shaft" (2.4.12-14): the campaign imagery of the siege, the wounding eyes, Cupid's archery, all the Canzoniere's martial stock, reduced to a coroner's report on a casualty of clichés.

The satire's other set piece deserves its place in the diagnosis, because Queen Mab is the anti-Canzoniere entire, a rival account of where love dreams come from. Romeo, on the way to the feast, reports a dream and flags his misgiving; Mercutio answers with the Mab speech (1.4.53-94), in which the fairies' midwife gallops night by night through lovers' brains, and then they dream of love, through courtiers' knees, lawyers' fingers, ladies' lips, a whole census of desires assigned not by Cupid's arrows or a lady's eyes but by appetite, profession, and digestion. The speech is the materialist counter-encyclopedia to the Petrarchan cosmos: where the borrowed system derives the lover's state from a metaphysics of glances and stars, Mercutio derives states from stomachs and ambitions, and the dream that Romeo wants read as an omen is reclassified as a symptom. Its dramatic function inside the source argument is exact. Before Romeo has produced a single Petrarchan set speech at the feast, his friend has already supplied the alternative physics in which such speeches are unnecessary, and Romeo's interruption, complaining that Mercutio talks of nothing, draws the system's epitaph from the materialist himself: dreams are the children of an idle brain, begot of nothing but vain fantasy. The borrowed book says the lover's visions are messages from a heaven of love. Mab says they are weather in an empty head. The tragedy will spend four acts deciding between the two accounts, and its decision is written into the structure: the visions that matter, Juliet on the balcony, Laura-like in her window, are not dreams at all, while the one dream Romeo trusts, in Mantua, is the false one. Mercutio's counter-encyclopedia loses the argument about love and wins the argument about dreams, a split verdict worthy of the source itself.

### What does "the numbers that Petrarch flowed in" mean?

"Numbers" in the period's usage are verses or metrical measures, so Mercutio is saying that Romeo has recovered his appetite for the flowing lyric measures Petrarch originated: the returning Romeo, he jokes, is once again a customer for ready-made sonnet music, the very commodity the Canzoniere's English afterlife sold by the sheet.

The satire's precision is completed by its timing, and the timing is the play's mercy. Mercutio delivers the diagnosis at the one moment it has ceased to be true. Romeo, returning from the balcony, has already met a beloved who answers; the borrowed voice has already cracked, in dialogue, the night before. The mockery lands on a convert who no longer holds the mocked opinions, which is why Romeo can take it in good part and answer in prose and quibbles, the anti-lyric register Mercutio himself speaks. The Friar, hearing the news an hour later in the play's time, files the same diagnosis in the pastoral register: Romeo's love was a recitation, a young man's love lying not in his heart but in his eyes, and the salt tears for Rosaline were seasoning wasted on a dish never tasted (2.3.67-84). Between the two of them, Mercutio and the Friar perform the function of Petrarch's own proem sonnet: they pronounce the borrowed book a youthful error. Shakespeare has redistributed the Canzoniere's frame among his supporting cast. The lover cannot yet judge his own idiom; the idiom's judgment arrives, as it does in the source, from a voice positioned outside the desire, except that where Petrarch's judging voice is the lover grown old, the play's judging voices are a friend who will die of the feud and a friar whose remedies will fail. The frame has been kept and darkened, which is the play's way with everything it borrows.

The map's pattern is consistent enough to state as a law of the play's intertextual practice. Every borrowing is accurate at the level of the figure and false at the level of the situation: Romeo has the conceits right and the love wrong, because in the source each conceit is the scar tissue of a specific history, twenty-one years of anniversaries, refusals, relapses, and at last a grave, whereas in the borrowing each conceit is a credential, produced to prove the speaker is in love. The mockery, accordingly, never attacks the figures themselves. Mercutio does not call the oxymoron a bad figure; he calls Romeo's recitation of it a symptom, and the play vindicates the figures by reissuing them under real pressure: the freeze and burn returns when Juliet, holding the news of Tybalt's death and Romeo's banishment in a single hour, finds the contraries are no longer a catalogue but a weather system, and the sweet enemy stops being a formula the moment an actual enemy house stands behind the beloved's name. The Canzoniere's own career foretells the pattern. Petrarch's figures earn their keep across a lifetime of revision; borrowed without the lifetime, they are small change. The play is the story of a boy whose small change is progressively replaced by real currency, at an exchange rate set by the feud.

One channel of transmission the map cannot tabulate is music, and it deserves a paragraph because the Canzoniere was, for its first English hearers, partly a sound before it was a text. Petrarch's title promises songs, canzoniere means songbook, and sixteenth-century Italy obliged: the madrigal composers, from the early masters through Luca Marenzio to Claudio Monteverdi, set Petrarch's sonnets and canzoni more often than any other poet's words, so that Laura's hair, eyes, and anniversaries circulated as polyphony in chambers where no one present could have construed the Italian unaided. England imported the sound deliberately. Nicholas Yonge's Musica transalpina of 1588, a printed anthology of Italian madrigals with English singing translations, was the landmark of the import trade, and the English madrigal school that flowered in the decade of the play fed on Petrarchan texts and situations: the freezing and burning lover, the sighing shepherd-poet, the cruel fair, all harmonized for five voices and sung after supper by the same class of people who bought the sonnet sequences and filled the theatres. The relevance to the play is atmospheric and precise. When Romeo performs the oxymoron catalogue in a Verona street, the figures he rattles off were not merely read properties; they were the period's love-music, the refrains of the madrigal repertory, and an audience's ear caught them as tunes half-remembered from other rooms. Shakespeare's phrase for the condition, placed in another play of the same years, is that the lover's discourse is music with its text forgotten; in this play the forgetting is the characterization. Romeo hums the Petrarchan airs with complete confidence and no idea what they were about, because the transmission that reached him had carried the melodies of the conceits farther than their meanings. Mercutio, demanding to know whether his friend is for the numbers that Petrarch flowed in, uses the vocabulary of measure and tune advisedly: numbers are what a song is made of, and Romeo, at that point in the action, is all numbers and no song.

## What Breaks the System: A Beloved Who Answers

The system's founding condition, established above, is the beloved's silence. Laura's near-silence across 263 in vita poems is not an oversight of Petrarch's art but its load-bearing wall: the lover's monologue requires an auditor who does not reply, because a reply would introduce a second will into a structure built to house one will and its echo. The second part of the Canzoniere discovers the only reply compatible with the structure: the dead Laura returns in vision, in sonnet 302 lifted by his thought to the heaven where she waits, takes the lover by the hand, speaks comfort and reproach, and withdraws. Even answered, the lover is answered by a spirit under the discipline of heaven, who confirms the separation while soothing it. The earthly beloved, alive and answering in her own person, is the one event the book cannot accommodate, and it is precisely the event Shakespeare stages.

The accommodation fails at the level of form first. The lovers' first exchange at the feast is a sonnet, the system's signature artifact, but it is a sonnet for two voices: Romeo opens with the pilgrim's approach to the holy shrine (1.5.92-95), and Juliet, instead of receiving the compliment in the silence the form prescribes, takes the next quatrain, develops his figure better than he did, corrects his theology of touch, and returns the ball with the rules of the game rewritten, saints do not move, though they grant prayers. The analysis of [The Sonnet Form Hidden Inside the Play](/2024/12/27/sonnet-form-in-romeo-and-juliet/) lays out the fourteen lines and their division; the source point is that the division is the meaning. A Petrarchan sonnet is a room with one chair. The play puts a second chair in it, and the furniture rearranges itself around dialogue for the rest of the night: the balcony scene is the anti-sequence, a long duet in which every convention the kit supplies is produced and inspected in real time. Offered the oath machinery of the tradition, swearing by the moon, Romeo reaches for the stock prop and is stopped: "O, swear not by the moon, th' inconstant moon, / That monthly changes in her circled orb, / Lest that thy love prove likewise variable" (2.2.109-111). The beloved is editing the lover's borrowings as he utters them, striking the inconstant moon from the liturgy, refusing the whole apparatus of vows by variable lights, and proposing instead the one oath the system never contemplated, swearing by the self. Petrarch's lover swears by Laura's eyes, her hair, the day, the hour, the place; the vows proliferate because they bind nothing. Juliet's counter-proposal binds everything to the speaker's own constancy, and it comes from the person the system had kept silent for three hundred years of imitations.

### What is the first thing Juliet does to the sonnet Romeo offers her?

She completes it, from inside: taking up his pilgrim figure at the quatrain break, she corrects its doctrine, grants its premise on revised terms, and converts a monologue of worship into an exchange between equals, so that the form built for one voice finishes with two speakers sharing its lines and its kiss.

Here the deepest affinity between the play and the Canzoniere, as opposed to the convention, comes into view. Petrarch's book, for all its monologue, is the record of a man arguing with himself about his own idiom, promoting the argument into the proem, the anniversaries, the closing appeal to the Virgin; the dialogue the book lacks between lover and beloved exists within the lover, between the desiring self and the judging self. Shakespeare externalizes that internal dialogue. He gives the judging self a body and calls her Juliet; he gives the desiring self's idiom to a boy and lets the judgment proceed by conversation rather than by penitence. This is why the satire never feels like contempt for the source. The play treats the Canzoniere the way the Canzoniere treats its own lover: as a great inheritance requiring correction by a voice it had excluded. And the correction, in both books, is what makes the love legible as love rather than as literature. Romeo's speeches for Rosaline are literature about love, sourced, fluent, and empty. His speeches after the balcony, broken, plain, increasingly prose-haunted as the catastrophe gathers, are the record of the idiom being spent down to experience. The transformation had begun, in the tradition, with Chaucer setting the solitary song inside a story where the lady answers; the play completes the trajectory by letting the lady's answers rewrite the songbook in performance.

## The Second Part Nobody Borrowed: The Rime in Morte

For all the cargo that crossed into English, the heaviest single item was left on the dock: the second part of the Canzoniere, the hundred poems of Laura dead. The English sequences are, with few exceptions, in vita machines; their ladies neither die nor release their lovers, because the form's economics required the song to continue and a death would foreclose it. Yet the in morte poems are where Petrarch's book does its most original work, and the play, alone among the convention's English heirs, engages them, not by quotation but by structural refusal. Consider what the second part contains. It opens with the canzone "I' vo pensando," the lover's self-arraignment, pity and terror contesting his soul over the corpse of his old life. It proceeds through grief audited against doctrine: the canzone "Che debb'io far? che mi consigli, Amore?" (poem 268), in which the newly bereaved lover asks Love what he is to do now that the sun of his life is quenched, and receives no serviceable answer; the dream visions in which Laura returns, not as the distant lady of the relics but as a speaking spirit, reproaching the lover's excessive tears, reporting her blessedness, and instructing him to convert his sorrow into hope; and, late in the part, the great dialogue of poem 359, "Quando il soave mio fido conforto," where the comforter comes to the bedside of his grief and the two hold the longest conversation of the book, in which Laura explains that she is not cruel, was never his enemy, and waited for his conversion as heaven waits. The beloved who could not speak in life speaks fluently in death, and what she says dismantles the system that silenced her: the refusal was never the point; the lover's slow education was.

The play takes up this structure at its most exposed joint and reverses the polarity. Romeo also receives a dream visitation: in Mantua, at the opening of Act 5, he reports that he dreamed his lady came and found him dead, and breathed such life with kisses in his lips that he revived and was an emperor (5.1.6-9). In the Canzoniere's grammar, the dead beloved's kiss is heaven's pedagogy, the vision that teaches the survivor to live on transfigured terms. In the play's grammar, the dream is a false comfort arriving on the morning of the catastrophe, and its lesson is inverted within the hour by Balthasar's news. More fundamental is the refusal that follows. Petrarch's lover, bereaved, does the one thing the system ultimately exists to make possible: he survives her, and writes the survival, for twenty-six further years of anniversaries, until the book can close with the appeal to the Virgin. Romeo, bereaved as he believes, declines the entire office. Confronted with the choice the Canzoniere's second part embodies, the choice to become the survivor-poet who converts loss into a hundred poems, he chooses the dagger and the poison instead, compressing the in morte into a single speech over a body he believes dead. The tragedy can be stated in source terms with unusual exactness: Shakespeare gives his hero the in vita idiom to speak, the answering beloved the tradition denied, and then denies him the in morte vocation that made the tradition's book cohere. Romeo cannot write Part Two. He can only join the beloved in the tomb, becoming, in the final tableau of the golden statues, the relic he once worshipped, an image in precious metal for a city to contemplate. The statues are the last repacking of the Canzoniere's cargo, the blazon's beloved and the worshipping lover alike cast as monuments, and they accuse every earlier repacking, the commentaries, the miscellanies, the sequences, of the same preference for the image over the life. Only the source itself escapes the accusation, because only the source insisted, in its second part, that the image was not enough and the survivor had to go on living inside the loss. The play honors that insistence by showing, in negative, what happens to a lover who cannot perform it.

## The Verdict: Petrarch Behind the Lines, Petrarchism on Trial

The evidence permits a verdict with two clauses, and the clauses must be kept separate because the play keeps them separate. First clause: the Canzoniere shapes the play's love language at the level of deep structure, not of ornament. The oxymoron engine, the campaign of siege and archery, the economics of the hoarded store, the religion of the eye, the blazon, the superlative arithmetic that demotes every other lady of story, the solitary voice addressing an answering silence: each of these descends from Petrarch's book through a traceable English transmission, Chaucer's narrative embedding, Wyatt's politicized translations, Surrey's re-formed container, Tottel's printed commodity, and the sequence culture of the early 1590s. Romeo's Act 1 is unintelligible as characterization without the source, because the characterization is the source, quoted. The cliché hears those speeches as a young man's feelings overflowing; the record shows a young man's reading overflowing, which is a different and more interesting condition, and the one the tragedy is actually about.

Second clause: what the play puts on trial is not the source but the source's afterlife. Petrarchism, the kit assembled by commentators who moralized the book, courtiers who carried its conceits loose, printers who sold them by the sheet, and sonneteers who ran its scales as exercises, is the accused, and Mercutio is the prosecution's most quotable witness precisely because he names the patent holder while mocking the licensees. In performance the distinction is audible within minutes, and directors ignore it at the production's cost: an Act 1 Romeo played as a sincere young romantic, his oxymorons delivered as feeling rather than as recitation, dissolves the play's diagnostic design and leaves Benvolio's laughter, the Friar's verdict, and Mercutio's roll call pointing at nothing, three witnesses testifying against a defendant the staging has acquitted. Played as a recitation, the same speeches organize the whole evening: every later plainness in the hero's voice registers as growth, every return of the figures under pressure registers as education, and the audience, fluent in the kit from its own phrasebook of love talk, watches a reader slowly become a man. The source study is therefore not an antiquarian appendix to the tragedy but a stage direction written across its first act, and the Canzoniere, restored as the recitation's textbook, recovers its proper credit in the play's accounts: not the tragedy's quarry of pretty lines, but its first and most patient teacher, present wherever the hero's voice is being measured against the book it came from. The scholarly quarrel between Forster's fashion and Kennedy's authorization dissolves, at the bar of this play, into sequence: authorization made the kit respectable, fashion made it ubiquitous, and ubiquity made it audible as kit, which is all a stage satire needs. Dubrow's counterdiscourse, the crack inside the convention, is where the play files its own plea, on Juliet's behalf. Against every account that treats the play's Petrarchan material as a youthful mannerism Shakespeare outgrew, the record of the text supports the stronger claim: the tragedy is the period's most rigorous reading of the Canzoniere, rigorous because it restores what the transmission removed, the frame in which the lover's idiom is judged, and relocates that frame from the poet's old age into the beloved's mouth. Petrarch ends his book by turning from Laura to the Virgin, asking the Queen of Heaven to make his peace, the lover's last address still a monologue aimed upward. The play ends its love story with two families erecting golden statues to each other's children, the borrowed system's final metamorphosis: the beloved as precious image, worshipped in metal, silent at last, and the silence this time is the tragedy's accusation rather than the convention's convenience. Behind Romeo's lines stands the Canzoniere entire, its splendor and its self-knowledge; in front of them stands Petrarchism, fluent and hollow; and the play, which uses the first to expose the second, remains the sharpest instrument the tradition ever produced for telling them apart.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Petrarch map, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch Petrarch map is the table in this article that sets ten Petrarchan conceits at their source coordinates in the Canzoniere, traces the line Romeo borrows from each, and records where the play mocks the borrowing or breaks the pattern. The pattern it exposes is systematic: every borrowing is accurate as a figure and false as a situation, because in the source each conceit is the scar tissue of a twenty-one-year history, while in Romeo's mouth each is a credential produced to prove a feeling. The mockery therefore never attacks the figures; it attacks the recitation, and the play later reissues the same figures under real pressure to prove them sound.

### Q: What does the title Rime sparse mean, and why does the book carry two titles?

Rime sparse means scattered rhymes, and it comes from the opening words of the first sonnet, "Voi ch'ascoltate in rime sparse il suono," whoever hears in scattered rhymes the sound of those sighs. The book's formal Latin title is Rerum vulgarium fragmenta, fragments of vernacular things, Petrarch's own description of his Italian lyrics as fragments beside his Latin works. The two titles record the book's double nature: the humanist's apology for trifles, and the lifetime's labor that made the trifles into the most carefully ordered lyric collection of the age, revised and rearranged until the year of the poet's death.

### Q: Why does the first sonnet of the Canzoniere apologize for the book that follows it?

Because the Canzoniere is framed as the record of an error its author claims to have outgrown. The opening sonnet addresses its readers from a position of retrospect: the poems preserve the sighs of the poet's "primo giovenile errore," his first youthful error, and he asks whoever has known love by experience for pardon and pity together. The apology is the book's engine, not its embarrassment. The penitent frame pronounces judgment on the 365 poems that follow, and those poems keep failing to ratify the judgment, so the whole collection becomes an argument between the lover who wrote the lyrics and the older man who regrets them.

### Q: How is the Canzoniere divided between the poems of Laura's life and the poems of her death?

The 366 poems divide into two parts at poem 264. Poems 1 to 263, the rime in vita, belong to Laura alive: the first sight in Avignon, the anniversaries, the refusals, the landscapes of Vaucluse, the long stalemate of worship at a distance. Poems 264 to 366, the rime in morte, open with the canzone "I' vo pensando" and belong to Laura dead in the plague year 1348: grief, dreams, reproaches, and visions in which the beloved returns as a blessed spirit. The division converts a collection into a plot with a catastrophe at its center, and it is the architecture the English sonnet sequences almost entirely declined to imitate.

### Q: Why do the two dates of 6 April, 1327 and 1348, govern the book's arithmetic?

Because Petrarch recorded that he first saw Laura on 6 April 1327, Good Friday, in the church of Sainte-Claire in Avignon, and that she died on 6 April 1348, exactly twenty-one years later, during the plague; the memorandum survives in his own note in his manuscript of Virgil, now in Milan. The poems keep returning to the anniversary as a liturgy returns to a feast, blessing the day in sonnet 61 and reckoning years of sighs against it. The book's 366 poems extend the calendrical thinking to the whole design: a poem for every day of the year, with one standing outside the count.

### Q: Can Laura be identified with a real woman of Avignon, and what does the evidence actually show?

The evidence shows less than the tradition claims. Petrarch's own Virgil note names her only as Laura, records the two April dates, and places the first sight in Sainte-Claire; a tradition reaching back to the sixteenth century identifies her with Laura de Noves, wife of Hugues de Sade, an ancestor of the Marquis de Sade. The identification is plausible and unprovable, and Petrarch's poetry resists it by design: Laura in the poems is compounded of a woman, a laurel, a breeze, and a golden shimmer, the l'aura and l'auro of the puns. The Canzoniere needs her partly unidentifiable, because a fully documented lady could not carry the fame symbolism the name is made to bear.

### Q: What does the name Laura allow Petrarch to pun on, and why does the laurel crown matter to him?

The name lets Petrarch pun in three directions at once: lauro, the laurel tree into which Daphne was transformed fleeing Apollo; l'aura, the breeze that stirs Laura's golden hair and moves through the valleys of Vaucluse; and l'auro, gold, the metal of that hair and of worldly glory. The laurel matters because it is the poet's crown: Petrarch was crowned with laurel on the Capitoline in Rome in 1341, the ambition of his life, and the poems keep exposing the overlap between desiring the woman and desiring the crown. To pursue Laura is to pursue fame under a beloved's name, and the book's conscience is troubled by exactly that confusion.

### Q: Which sonnet stands behind Romeo's freeze-and-burn catalogue, and what is its closing attribution?

Sonnet 134, "Pace non trovo," stands behind it: I find no peace and have no war to make; I fear and hope; I burn and am ice; I fly above the heavens and lie on the ground; I feed on grief and laugh weeping. Romeo's "cold fire" and "still-waking sleep" compress its balanced clauses into a rattled catalogue. The sonnet closes by assigning the whole condition a cause: "in questo stato son, donna, per voi," in this state I am, lady, because of you. Romeo's version inverts the attribution into "This love feel I, that feel no love in this," admitting the state while the cause stands empty.

### Q: What does the ship in Petrarch's sonnet 189 carry, and why does its lover despair of reaching port?

The ship of sonnet 189, "Passa la nave mia colma d'oblio," passes laden with forgetfulness through a harsh sea at midnight in winter, between Scylla and Charybdis, with the lover's lord, Love himself, at the helm and his thoughts manning the oars toward shipwreck. The cargo is the point: the vessel of the lover's life is freighted with oblivion, the forgetting of everything except the beloved, and steered by the very power that wrecks it. The lover despairs of port because reason's sail has been abandoned to the wind of desire. Wyatt translated the sonnet as his galley charged with forgetfulness, sharpening its weather into the storms of court service.

### Q: What does the canzone "Chiare, fresche et dolci acque" ask the waters of Vaucluse to remember?

Petrarch's great canzone, poem 126, addresses the clear, fresh, sweet waters of the river Sorgue, the banks and branches and air of the valley where Laura had bathed and rested, and asks the landscape to testify: the lady sat here, her dress scattered the flowers, her foot touched this grass. The poem then projects the poet's own death into the scene, imagining Laura returning to the valley, finding his grave among the stones, and sighing late pity for him. It is the Canzoniere's supreme instance of landscape as memory's archive, the beloved preserved not in a portrait but in a place that the poem teaches to remember her.

### Q: What is a sestina, and why does the Canzoniere hold nine of them?

A sestina is a form invented by the troubadour Arnaut Daniel: six stanzas of six lines each, built on six end-words that rotate through a fixed pattern of recurrence, closed by a short envoi gathering all six. The Canzoniere holds nine sestinas, including the twelve-stanza double sestina of poem 332, because the form enacts the book's subject at the level of rhyme: the same six words return in altered order, as the lover's same thoughts return on altered days, and no stanza can escape the vocabulary assigned to it. Obsession, in the sestina, is not described but mechanically performed.

### Q: Why does the Canzoniere end with a canzone to the Virgin Mary instead of a poem to Laura?

Because the book's frame requires the desire to be handed upward at the close. Poem 366, "Vergine bella, che di sol vestita," beautiful Virgin, clothed with the sun, is a prayer asking the Queen of Heaven to intercede for the lover, to turn his long error toward peace, and to receive the heart that the Laura poems could not save. The ending is a palinode, a formal recantation, and its force depends on everything it recants: three hundred and sixty-five poems of worship addressed to a creature are answered by one addressed to the Creator's mother. The English sequences borrowed the worship wholesale and almost never borrowed the recantation.

### Q: What changes in the second part when Laura answers the lover from heaven?

In the rime in morte the beloved returns in dreams and visions, and for the first time in the book she speaks at length: in sonnet 302 the lover's thought lifts him to the heaven where Laura waits, she takes him by the hand, tells him she is in the third sphere with her lord, and charges him to turn his tears into hope. The answer, when it comes, comes under heaven's discipline: it confirms the separation while soothing it, and it redirects the desire that earthly refusal had only frozen. Shakespeare's boldness, against this background, is measurable. He lets the beloved answer on earth, in dialogue, before any death has licensed her speech.

### Q: Why did Chaucer credit his Petrarch translation to an invented Latin author called Lollius?

Chaucer presents his Troilus as translated from a Latin author he calls Lollius, a figure who appears to be his own invention, assembled perhaps from a misreading of Horace. Behind the screen, Chaucer draws heavily on Boccaccio's Filostrato and, for the Canticus Troili, translates Petrarch's sonnet 132 directly. The invented authority served a period logic: a vernacular poem gained weight by claiming a Latin auctor behind it, and Chaucer's fiction of translation let him import the newest Italian lyric psychology while wearing the modest dress of a mere translator. The habit is the first English repacking of the Canzoniere, four centuries of transmission begin with a courteous concealment.

### Q: What does Wyatt's "Whoso list to hunt" warn about besides love?

Wyatt's version of Petrarch's white hind sonnet keeps the hind and her collar, "Noli me tangere, for Caesar's I am," but relocates the danger. In Petrarch, the hind is unattainable because Caesar has claimed her; in Wyatt, the poem warns the fellow hunters crowding the opening lines that the chase itself is ruinous, that the speaker has spent his wind in vain and fallen behind, caught in nets of his own weaving. Read at Henry VIII's court, where the hind was persistently identified with a lady the king had claimed, the warning is occupational: some quarry is fenced not by the lady's refusal but by the sovereign's prior right, and hunting it is a way to lose a head. The love poem doubles as survival advice.

### Q: Why is Tottel's Miscellany of 1557 a turning point in the sonnet's English history?

Because Richard Tottel's printed collection, Songes and Sonettes, moved the Petrarchan sonnet out of manuscript coteries and into the bookstall. Wyatt and Surrey, dead fifteen and ten years respectively, had circulated their Petrarch versions hand to hand at court, where a sonnet was a social performance preserved on loose sheets. Tottel gathered their work with that of lesser hands between covers, priced it, and sold it to any reader, converting an aristocratic practice into a public form. After 1557 the sonnet in England is a commodity with a history on the page, and every later practitioner, Sidney and Shakespeare included, learned the craft partly from print.

### Q: Did Shakespeare read Petrarch in Italian, and what does the evidence support?

The evidence supports mediation, not direct reading. No record shows Shakespeare owning, quoting, or translating the Canzoniere in Italian; every Petrarchan figure in the play exists in an English form available to him, through Wyatt and Surrey in Tottel's Miscellany, through the printed sequences of the early 1590s, through Sidney, and through the general compliment currency of the stage. Mercutio's naming of Petrarch in Act 2 Scene 4 proves the name and the reputation were common theatrical property, not that the dramatist worked from the Italian text. The distinction matters for the source argument: the play satirizes Petrarchism, the received convention, with full knowledge, while its affinity with the Canzoniere itself is structural, arrived at by a dramatist's intelligence rather than by a scholar's reading.

### Q: What is the difference between Petrarch the poet and Petrarchism the convention?

Petrarch the poet wrote a self-indicting book: a lover who knows his worship is an error, frames the collection with an apology, divides it by the beloved's death, and closes it with a prayer recanting the whole. Petrarchism the convention is what transmission made of that book: a portable kit of conceits, the freezing and burning lover, the besieged fortress, the silent lady, the superlative blazon, detached from the frame that judged them and circulated by commentators, courtiers, printers, and sonneteers as a ready-made language of desire. Romeo speaks the convention. The play, uniquely among the convention's English products, restores the frame's judgment by letting Mercutio mock the kit and Juliet answer it.

### Q: Why does Petrarch's Latin title call his love poems fragments?

Rerum vulgarium fragmenta, fragments of vernacular things, is a double gesture characteristic of its author. On its face the title apologizes: the crowned Latin humanist, author of an epic on Scipio and of treatises on fame and solitude, labels his Italian love lyrics scraps in the common tongue, beneath his serious work. In practice the fragments received more labor than the epics, decades of drafting, ordering, and polishing preserved in the working manuscripts. The title is the proem sonnet's apology extended to the cover, a humility the book's construction contradicts on every page, and the contradiction is the author's own: he could neither stop valuing the fragments nor stop apologizing for them.

### Q: How should an essay argue that Romeo's earliest speeches are borrowed music rather than fresh feeling?

Build the case from mechanism, not from tone. First, match the figures to their sources: the freeze-and-burn catalogue to sonnet 134, the siege and the Cupid machinery to the in vita campaign poems, the hoarded store to the sequence's economics of preservation. Second, show the transmission is English and printed, through Wyatt, Surrey, and Tottel, so the borrowing is of a convention, not of a private reading. Third, use the play's own witnesses: Benvolio's laughter, Mercutio naming Petrarch outright, the Friar's verdict that the love lay in the eyes and not the heart. Conclude that the speeches characterize a reader performing a tradition, and that the tragedy measures his growth by the voice that replaces the performance.
