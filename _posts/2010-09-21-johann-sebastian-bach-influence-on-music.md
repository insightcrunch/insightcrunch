## A worn edition in a Barcelona shop

A teenage cellist in a Barcelona second hand shop picked up a worn edition of six suites for unaccompanied cello that nobody performed complete, and Bach influence on music acquired one of its strangest carriers. By Pablo Casals's own later account, he was thirteen years old in 1890, searching the bins of a music shop near the harbour with his father, when he found a printed copy of the suites by Johann Sebastian Bach, a set of pieces that professional cellists of the period treated as studies, excerpts or curiosities rather than as a cycle for the concert platform. He took the volume home and worked on the music in private for twelve years before playing the suites in public. The scene matters because it overturns the usual shape of an influence story. No famous teacher handed the boy a masterpiece. No academy prescribed the set. A cheap printed edition, produced decades earlier by editors who had kept the suites in circulation as teaching material, sat in a shop until a particular reader recognised what the pages could become in performance.

The thesis of this article can be stated in one sentence. Bach influence on music did not spread by fame but by paper, and every stage of his growing reputation can be traced to a specific manuscript or edition reaching a specific musician who then taught somebody else. Where that chain can be documented, with a named recipient, a named work and a date of access, the influence claim is strong. Where the chain dissolves into general admiration, the claim weakens, and several celebrated claims dissolve exactly there. Casals and the Barcelona shop form the test case in miniature. The suites had never vanished. They existed in print, in a form any purchaser could buy, and teachers knew them as difficult studies. What was missing was not the text but a performer willing to build a public cycle out of them, and the difference between those two conditions, a text in circulation and a work in performance, runs through every section that follows.

Casals's account comes from his own recollection, given long after the event, and the details vary between his tellings, so the shop scene should be read as his account rather than as a courtroom record. That caution does not weaken the larger point. The edition existed. German publishers had issued the suites during the nineteenth century, editors had fingered and bowed them for students, and cellists such as Alfredo Piatti in London knew individual movements. The paper trail is checkable in publishers' catalogues and library holdings. What Casals added was twelve years of private labour and then a public decision, repeated across decades of recitals and eventually fixed in his recordings of the complete set made between 1936 and 1939, that the six suites belonged on the platform as a cycle. Influence, in this case, travelled from an editor's desk to a shop bin to a practice room, and only at the end of that journey did it reach an audience.

The same pattern, paper first and public fame last, explains the keyboard works, the church music and the theoretical reputation. Bach published very little during his lifetime compared with the size of his output, and almost none of the music on which his later standing rests was widely available in print at his death in 1750. The Well-Tempered Clavier moved in handwritten copies. The great Leipzig church works survived in performance parts held by institutions. The organ works passed from teacher to pupil. Each of those channels had gatekeepers, and the gatekeepers decided, often without intending anything grand, which musicians of the next two generations would encounter which pieces. To follow the influence is therefore to follow the copies, and the copies have names, dates and addresses attached to them.

## How music travelled when nothing was printed

Before printing made him available, his music moved hand to hand in copies, which explains exactly who knew it. That is the first rung of this article's ladder, and it is a mechanical point before it is a romantic one. In the German lands of the early eighteenth century, printed music was expensive, print runs were small, and a working Kapellmeister guarded his scores because they were tools of his trade and part of his value to an employer. A composer who wanted a piece by a colleague obtained a copy through a pupil, a relative, a travelling musician or a copyist paid by the hour. Bach himself learned in exactly this economy. As a boy at Ohrdruf and Luneburg he copied music by older masters, and as a young organist he transcribed Vivaldi concertos for keyboard, absorbing Italian concerto design through his pen. The direction of that early traffic matters for later sections of this article, because the copying network ran into Bach before it ran out of him, and the discipline of copying was the medium in which his own music would later travel.

A manuscript copy is a selective object. Somebody chose this piece, paid for the paper, spent the hours, and carried the result somewhere. Surviving copies therefore map real relationships rather than vague reputation. When a copy of the Well-Tempered Clavier in the hand of a particular pupil surfaces in a particular town, the historian can say who had access and roughly when, and can then look for consequences in that musician's teaching or writing. Christoph Wolff, the archival scholar whose work on the Bach sources rebuilt the chronology of the composer's career from paper, ink and handwriting evidence, built much of his account of the early reception on exactly this material. Wolff's method treats each manuscript as a dated witness, and the method sets the standard this article applies to every influence claim: name the technique, name the two works, and name the date one became available to the other.

The copying economy also explains the shape of the early Bach tradition, which was deep in some places and absent in others. Keyboard players and organists, who needed teaching material and service music every week, copied heavily and kept the tradition dense. Singers and orchestral players, whose music came from institutional part sets that stayed in one choir loft or court library, transmitted far less. A cantata could be famous in Leipzig and unknown fifty miles away, while a set of inventions could be in the hands of students across Saxony within a few years. When later writers describe Bach as neglected after 1750, they usually mean the large vocal works, which depended on institutions. The keyboard tradition, carried by individuals with their own copies, behaved differently, and confusing those two channels is the single most common error in the popular version of this story.

Paper had one further property that shaped the legacy. A copy could be annotated, corrected, fingered, and supplied with ornaments or realised figures by its owner, so the text that reached a pupil was partly the teacher's reading of it. The Bach who reached the young Mozart in Vienna in 1782 arrived through Baron Gottfried van Swieten's library and through arrangements and copies shaped by Viennese taste. The Bach who reached Berlin students arrived through the copies of his pupil Johann Philipp Kirnberger and through the collection of Princess Anna Amalia of Prussia, whose library preserved a major body of manuscripts. Each route filtered the composer. Influence history is therefore never a story of a pure text meeting a pure mind. It is a story of editions, copies and teachers, each leaving fingerprints on what the next musician believed Bach to be.

## The pupils and the copying network

The first transmitters were the pupils, and their names survive because their copies and their teaching posts can be traced. Johann Ludwig Krebs studied with Bach at Leipzig and carried his organ teaching into posts at Zeitz and Altenburg, where Krebs's own organ works show the contrapuntal schooling in every movement. Johann Gottfried Muthel, among the last pupils to study at Leipzig in the final months of the composer's life, carried the keyboard tradition to Riga. Heinrich Nicolaus Gerber studied at Leipzig in the 1720s and left an account of his lessons, including the detail that his teacher set him the Inventions first and then the suites and the Well-Tempered Clavier in sequence, a curriculum that later teachers repeated for two centuries. Johann Christian Kittel took the organ tradition to Erfurt and taught a generation of Thuringian organists. Gottfried August Homilius carried the church music discipline to Dresden. Each pupil is a documented channel, and the channels can be distinguished by what each man copied, taught and published.

Johann Philipp Kirnberger deserves separate weight because he converted private transmission into public theory. Kirnberger studied with Bach at Leipzig between 1739 and 1741, entered the service of Princess Anna Amalia in Berlin, and published treatises on harmony and strict composition that presented his teacher's practice as a system. His collection of chorale settings circulated under Bach's name, and his advocacy placed the older composer inside Berlin's musical argument at the moment when the galant style was declaring counterpoint obsolete. Johann Friedrich Agricola, another Leipzig pupil, wrote the obituary notice of 1754 with Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach and later directed Berlin opera while holding his teacher in open respect. Lorenz Christoph Mizler, who had studied at Leipzig, founded a musical society whose journal carried learned discussion of fugue and canon, and Bach himself joined that society in 1747, contributing the Canonic Variations on Vom Himmel hoch as his visiting card. The network, in other words, published as well as copied.

Family copies widened the circle beyond enrolled pupils. Anna Magdalena Bach, the composer's second wife and a professional singer, copied large quantities of music for the household, and her hand appears in sources that later editors used, including material connected with the cello suites. Johann Gottfried Walther, a cousin by marriage and organist at Weimar, copied and compiled theoretical material in the same years. Johann Christian Bach, the youngest son, carried family manuscripts to London. The household was itself a copying workshop, and the boundary between pupil, relative and copyist barely existed. For the reader who wants the life behind these arrangements, the biography of the composer, covering the Leipzig household and the teaching load that filled it, is told in full in [the companion account of Bach's life and career](/2010/08/07/johann-sebastian-bach-life-and-career/), and this article compresses that material deliberately, because its subject begins where the life ends.

Counting the network matters more than praising it. Between pupils who published theory, pupils who held organ lofts for fifty years, sons who taught in Hamburg, Berlin, London and Dresden, and copyists whose hands appear in surviving sources, the number of musicians with direct documentary access to Bach's scores in the three decades after 1750 runs into the hundreds. None of that access required the public to know his name. A tradition can be professionally continuous and publicly invisible at the same time, and the Bach tradition between 1750 and 1829 is the clearest case in European music of exactly that split. Later sections return to the split when they reach the Mendelssohn performance, because the popular account of 1829 depends on ignoring the professional line entirely.

## The sons as the first transmitters

The sons stood closer to the paper than anyone, and what they did with it decided what the next generation could know. Wilhelm Friedemann Bach, the eldest surviving son and by family testimony the most gifted organist of the household, inherited a large share of the manuscripts, held posts at Dresden and Halle, and taught pupils who carried the keyboard tradition forward. His later years were financially disordered, and manuscripts passed out of his hands in ways that scholars have traced only partly, a dispersal with real losses attached. Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach, employed at the Berlin court of Frederick the Great and then as music director at Hamburg, kept his father's works in his library, cited them in correspondence, and placed the Well-Tempered Clavier at the centre of serious keyboard teaching. His Essay on the True Art of Playing Keyboard Instruments, published in two parts in 1753 and 1762, became the keyboard treatise of the age, and its authority carried the family teaching method, if not always the father's name, into every German music room.

Johann Christoph Friedrich Bach at Buckeburg and Johann Christian Bach at London transmitted selectively, each reshaping the inheritance toward the galant and early classical styles in which they built their own careers. Johann Christian, the London Bach, taught the young Mozart during the boy's London visit of 1764 and 1765, and the influence line there runs through a son rather than through the father's scores, a distinction this article insists on wherever the chain has an intermediary. When Mozart later absorbed Johann Christian's elegance of phrase and keyboard manner, he was receiving a filtered family inheritance, one generation removed and thoroughly modernised. The direct encounter with the father's counterpoint came later, in Vienna, and it left different marks.

The sons also acted as the first editors and witnesses. Carl Philipp Emanuel supplied information to Johann Nikolaus Forkel for the biography of 1802, the first book devoted to the composer, and the obituary of 1754 had already fixed a family authorised outline of the life. Forkel dedicated his biography to the hope that patriotic Germans would preserve the works, and he obtained his material from Wilhelm Friedemann and Carl Philipp Emanuel, which makes the book both a primary witness and a family document. That double character matters. Everything the nineteenth century first believed about Bach came substantially through the sons, including the selection of which works represented him. The sons prized the keyboard music and the learned counterpoint. The Leipzig church music, cantatas above all, they treated as occasional work of local use, and the low value they placed on it helps explain why the vocal revival took so long.

Transmission by the sons was therefore preservation and filtering in one act. They kept the pedagogical core intact, kept the name respected among professionals, supplied the biographer, and maintained the performing tradition in organ lofts and teaching studios. They also let large parts of the estate scatter, endorsed a taste that found their father's densest manner old fashioned, and passed on a partial catalogue. A reader tracing influence has to hold both halves together, because the nineteenth century inherited the filtered Bach and spent a hundred years discovering, edition by edition, how much more paper there was.

## What the sons kept and what they rejected

His own sons regarded his style as outdated, and said so, which is the first serious critical verdict on him. That sentence carries the second rung of the ladder, and it deserves care, because the sons' judgement was professional rather than filial. Carl Philipp Emanuel wrote to Forkel that his father's compositions were out of fashion compared with newer music, and he meant the observation as description, not betrayal. The galant generation valued clarity, periodic phrasing and expressive melody over dense imitation, and by that standard the Leipzig cantor's learned counterpoint looked like a survival from an earlier world. Wilhelm Friedemann, whose own music kept far more of the paternal manner, struggled for posts in a market that rewarded the newer ease. The family that preserved the scores also pronounced the style dated, and both facts entered the tradition together.

What the sons kept tells a reader what they believed would last. They kept the keyboard works in teaching use, above all the Well-Tempered Clavier, the Inventions and the suites, because those pieces trained hands and ears regardless of fashion. Carl Philipp Emanuel kept his father's portrait, manuscripts and memory in Hamburg, answered enquiries from collectors, and sold or gave copies to serious musicians. The Berlin circle around Princess Anna Amalia, where Kirnberger taught and where Sara Levy, a harpsichordist from the Itzig banking family, performed and collected, drew heavily on what the sons and pupils supplied. Sara Levy's collection, assembled across the late eighteenth century, preserved Bach manuscripts that her family later gave to the Berlin Sing-Akademie library, and her great nephew Felix Mendelssohn grew up in a house where that collection was family property. The line from the sons to the revival runs through her music room.

What they rejected, or simply let go, is equally specific. The cantatas, tied to Leipzig liturgy and written for forces the sons did not command, received no advocacy as a body of work. The Passions survived because Berlin institutions kept parts and scores, not because the family promoted them. Wilhelm Friedemann sold manuscripts when money ran short, and some sources vanished into private hands. Carl Philipp Emanuel's own enormous reputation during his lifetime, greater in print than his father's, set the family name in the public mind as Emanuel's, and obituaries of the period often treat the father as the distinguished parent of a famous son. Inverting that judgement took the nineteenth century, and the inversion began only when musicians outside the family, Forkel in print and van Swieten in Vienna among them, argued that the father's work outranked the sons'.

The critical lesson for influence studies sits inside this family story. Preservation is never neutral. The Bach available to Haydn's and Mozart's generation was the Bach the sons, pupils and Berlin collectors had chosen to copy, teach and praise: a keyboard master and contrapuntist, not a church composer of vast vocal cycles. When Mozart arranged Bach fugues in 1782, he arranged the transmitted Bach. When Berlin audiences heard the St Matthew Passion in 1829, they heard a work the transmission had carried almost privately, through institutional cupboards rather than through the teaching line. Two different Bachs travelled by two different channels, and the article keeps them separate from here on.

## The dispersal of the manuscripts

At the composer's death the estate divided among the widow and children according to Saxon custom, and the autograph scores, the closest thing to an authoritative text, scattered along family lines. Carl Philipp Emanuel took a major share to Berlin and Hamburg. Wilhelm Friedemann took another share and parted with pieces of it over decades. Anna Magdalena received materials she later sold or placed. The Thomasschule at Leipzig retained performance parts for church works used by Bach's successors as cantor, and those parts stayed in institutional custody, copied for use and worn by handling. Other sources travelled with pupils. The result by 1800 was not a library but an archipelago: Berlin court and Sing-Akademie holdings, Hamburg remnants, Leipzig school parts, the Amalienbibliothek of Princess Anna Amalia, private collections in Vienna, and single manuscripts surfacing in sales across the century.

Dispersal created the conditions for both loss and recovery. Works copied in several places survived even when one source burned, rotted or was sold as waste paper, a fate documented for some eighteenth century music collections. Works existing in a single autograph lived dangerously. The St Mark Passion music disappeared entirely, leaving only a printed libretto, and several cantatas are known by title alone. Against those losses stand the recoveries that patient source work produced. The Berlin Sing-Akademie archive, which held a major Bach collection assembled through Zelter and the Levy family gifts, vanished during the Second World War, was traced to Kyiv decades later, and returned to Berlin in 2001, restoring sources that scholars had mourned as destroyed. That recovery falls inside this article's period and illustrates the governing rule of the whole transmission story: the influence could only ever be as wide as the surviving paper, and the paper kept moving.

Editors inherited the archipelago and had to build a text from it. The nineteenth century complete edition, treated in a later section, worked source by source, comparing autographs, pupil copies and early prints, and its editors sometimes disagreed about which witness deserved authority. The dispersal therefore shaped not only who could study Bach but what Bach text they studied. A pianist working from the first printed Well-Tempered Clavier of 1801 met a text assembled from copies that differed in ornaments, accidentals and even notes from the autograph tradition that later scholarship reconstructed. Influence flowed from imperfect texts for more than a century, and the imperfections are part of the record rather than an embarrassment outside it.

## Who was studying Bach in the decades after his death

The decades after 1750 had teachers, theorists and collectors at work on the scores, and naming them replaces the myth of total oblivion with a map. Friedrich Wilhelm Marpurg published his treatise on fugue in 1753 and 1754, drawing on Bach practice and corresponding with the family. Kirnberger issued his theoretical works through the 1770s. Forkel lectured on music history at Gottingen and planned the biography years before its 1802 publication. In Berlin, Fasch founded the Sing-Akademie in 1791 and trained its choir on Bach motets, which the institution rehearsed as a matter of discipline. Carl Friedrich Zelter, who directed the Sing-Akademie from 1800, taught the young Mendelssohn and guarded the Passion scores with a possessive caution that later frustrated his own pupil. In Vienna, van Swieten assembled his library of Bach and Handel manuscripts and employed the young Mozart to arrange fugues for his Sunday readings. Haydn owned and studied theoretical material from the Bach circle and revered the learned style even where his own music pursued clarity.

### Was Bach really forgotten?

No. Public concert life largely passed Bach by after 1750, yet professional study never stopped. Kirnberger, Marpurg and Forkel taught and wrote from his scores, the Berlin Sing-Akademie rehearsed his motets, and copyists kept the keyboard works circulating among teachers, pupils and serious amateurs across German speaking Europe.

The answer matters because the question conceals two different publics. The concert public of the late eighteenth century, such as it was, heard little Bach, and the church public heard the Leipzig successors performing newer music in a changed liturgical taste. The professional public of organists, teachers, theorists and collectors never lost the thread. Organists in central Germany tested themselves on the great preludes and fugues as a professional rite. Teachers set the Inventions because nothing else taught two part independence as efficiently. Collectors such as the Viennese official and bibliophile Raphael Georg Kiesewetter, whose library and historical concerts in the 1820s presented older masters to invited audiences, treated Bach as a summit of the learned tradition. A reputation can contract to a profession and remain fully alive inside it.

That distinction opens the second loop of this article, and the loop stays open deliberately. If Bach was never forgotten by professionals, what exactly happened in 1829? The question cannot be answered by pointing at a score, because the scores were in Berlin hands the whole time. It can only be answered by examining what kind of event the 1829 performance was, who organised it, what forces it used, and what changed in public musical life afterwards. Sections on the first printed editions and on Mendelssohn's preparation build toward that answer, which lands in full in the section on what the revival actually revived. Hold the professional line in mind until then: continuous study above, interrupted public performance below, and a famous concert that acted on the lower line rather than the upper one.

Geography sharpens the map further. Study clustered where the paper was: Saxony and Thuringia through the organist line, Berlin through the court, the Amalia library and the Sing-Akademie, Hamburg through Carl Philipp Emanuel's estate, Vienna through van Swieten. London knew Bach mainly through Johann Christian and through immigrant musicians, and the English Bach tradition deepened later through Samuel Wesley, who promoted the keyboard works in the first decade of the nineteenth century and issued, with Charles Frederick Horn, an edition of the Well-Tempered Clavier in parts between 1810 and 1813. Paris learned the organ works through touring players and through the century's later editions. The pattern is archival, not mystical. Where copies sat, teaching followed. Where no copies sat, silence followed, however musical the town.

## Mozart's encounter at van Swieten's Sunday gatherings

Vienna supplies the best documented encounter in the whole influence record, with a place, a host, a schedule and datable consequences. Baron Gottfried van Swieten, imperial librarian and prefect of the court library, held musical gatherings at his Vienna residence on Sunday mornings, where older contrapuntal music, above all Bach and Handel, was played and studied by a circle of aristocratic amateurs and professional musicians. Mozart, newly settled in Vienna and seeking the Baron's patronage, attended from 1782, played at the gatherings, and undertook arrangements of fugues from the Well-Tempered Clavier for string ensembles, because the Baron's taste ran to hearing counterpoint in the forces his salon commanded. The shareable fact of this article belongs here and deserves plain statement. Mozart met Bach's and Handel's scores at Baron van Swieten's Sunday gatherings in Vienna from 1782, and the counterpoint in his later works changes after that date.

### Did Mozart know Bach's music?

Yes, and the meeting place and date are documented. From 1782 Mozart attended Baron Gottfried van Swieten's Sunday gatherings in Vienna, where Bach and Handel scores were read and played. Mozart arranged five Bach fugues for string quartet that year, and contrapuntal writing enters his own scores immediately afterward.

The arrangements give the claim its hard evidence. Mozart's set of five fugues from the second book of the Well-Tempered Clavier, arranged for string quartet and catalogued as K. 405, dates from 1782, the year of the encounter, and further arrangements for string trio of Bach and Handel fugues, K. 404a, belong to the same Viennese study period. These are not stories told by grandchildren. They are surviving scores in Mozart's hand, made from identifiable Bach originals, BWV 871, 874, 876, 877 and 878 among them, and they show the younger composer working inside the older one's voice leading bar by bar. A fuller account of the Viennese years and the patronage web around them belongs to [the life of Mozart told in his own cluster article](/2010/10/21/wolfgang-amadeus-mozart-life-and-career/), which this section compresses to the encounter itself, as the ownership rules of the series require.

Van Swieten's role deserves its own sentence of credit, because hosts transmit as surely as teachers. The Baron had served in Berlin, where the Bach tradition at court and in the Amalia circle formed his taste, and he carried Berlin paper and Berlin preferences to Vienna in his luggage and his library. His Sunday gatherings were a deliberate archaism, an argument that the learned style of Bach and Handel carried values that the fashionable galant manner lacked. Mozart arrived at those gatherings as a virtuoso pianist and opera composer of twenty six with a thorough Salzburg training that included his father's counterpoint discipline and the Italian church style, yet the surviving evidence shows the Bach encounter struck him with the force of discovery. Constanze Mozart reported that her husband spoke of the sessions as a revelation of a kind of music he had not known existed at that level, and whatever discount a widow's memory requires, the arrangements and the changed scores corroborate the direction of her report.

Access dates discipline the story. Mozart cannot have been formed by Bach fugues he had not seen, so claims about Bach influence on the boy Mozart fail the test this article applies throughout. The London Bach who taught the eight year old was Johann Christian, the son, whose galant keyboard manner left clear marks on the early concertos. The father's influence begins in 1782, when Mozart was twenty six and fully formed in his own style, which is precisely why its effects are so legible: a mature composer encountered a technique outside his habits and absorbed it into work that can be dated before and after the meeting. Few influence stories in music history offer so clean a before and after, and the next section reads the scores for the difference.

## What changed in Mozart's writing afterwards

Mozart's late counterpoint changes measurably after a specific encounter at a specific address. That claim, the third rung of the ladder, can be checked in dated scores, and checking it shows what a verified influence looks like from the inside. The Mass in C minor, K. 427, begun in 1782 and performed in Salzburg in 1783, opens its Gloria with a chorus whose imitative entries and driving string figures belong to the older church tradition the Vienna gatherings had put in front of him, and the Qui tollis builds a grave double chorus argument unlike anything in his earlier sacred music. The keyboard Suite in the style of Handel, K. 399, from the same period, shows the study turned toward his own instrument, its allemande and courante written as deliberate exercises in a manner he had encountered through the Baron's library. A set of preludes and fugues for piano, K. 394 among them, records the practice room side of the absorption, Mozart writing fugues for his wife Constanze, who had taken a liking to the form at the Sunday sessions.

The mature evidence arrives in the instrumental works of the final decade. The finale of the Jupiter Symphony, K. 551, completed in 1788, combines five distinct themes in invertible counterpoint during its coda, a display of learned technique placed at the climax of a public symphony rather than hidden in a church work. The themes themselves are simple, almost plainchant plain, and the art lies in their combination, voices entering in imitation, turning upside down, and stacking without collision. Nothing in the symphonies before 1782 attempts that feat, and the skill it required had a named source and a practice trail through the K. 405 arrangements. The string quartets dedicated to Haydn, published in 1785, deepen their slow movement textures and developmental imitation in the same direction, and the Adagio and Fugue in C minor for strings, K. 546 of 1788, takes an earlier piano fugue and gives it a severity of manner that points directly at the older models.

Opera absorbed the lesson too, in a place audiences rarely associate with fugue. The armed men in Die Zauberflote, first performed in 1791, sing a chorale melody in strict accompaniment derived from the Lutheran tradition, their two voices moving over an orchestral texture of imitative strings, a moment of archaic discipline inside a popular Singspiel. The Requiem, left unfinished at his death in 1791, builds its Kyrie as a double fugue, the old form carrying the most solemn text of the mass. In each case the technique can be named, the earlier Viennese access can be dated, and the scores on both sides of 1782 can be compared. That is the standard. Where a later section of this article finds a claim that cannot meet it, the claim gets marked down, however beloved the story.

Honesty requires the counterweight in the same section. Mozart did not become a Bach imitator, and most of his music owes its melody, its opera craft and its piano brilliance to other sources entirely: to Johann Christian Bach in London, to Haydn in the quartet, to Italian opera in the theatre works. The Viennese encounter added a technique and a standard of workmanship. It changed what his counterpoint could do inside forms he already commanded. Influence at this level is specific and bounded, and its boundedness is what makes it provable. A claim that a composer influenced everything about a successor is a claim that can never be tested, and untestable claims are the ones this article refuses to print as fact.

## Beethoven and the Well-Tempered Clavier as a childhood text

The Bonn years gave the next great recipient his text in childhood, which makes the Beethoven case the mirror of the Mozart case: absorption before style had hardened, rather than encounter after. Christian Gottlob Neefe, court organist at Bonn and the boy's most consequential teacher, set his pupil the Well-Tempered Clavier as the foundation of keyboard study, and in 1783 Neefe published a notice praising the twelve year old who played the greater part of that collection. The detail is documented in print from the boy's own lifetime, not reconstructed by later admirers, and it establishes access about as firmly as the record allows. The young pianist who could play those preludes and fugues had the older composer's voice leading, sequence building and key architecture in his fingers before he wrote a bar of consequence. [The biography of those Bonn and Vienna years, with the deafness and the patronage crises that shaped the later work](/2011/01/07/ludwig-van-beethoven-life-and-career/), belongs to its own article, and this one keeps to the paper trail.

Vienna renewed the connection through the same host who had shaped the Mozart encounter. Van Swieten, whose library and Sunday gatherings had introduced the earlier composer to the fugues, became an early Viennese patron of the younger man as well, and the Baron's circle kept Handel and the Leipzig cantor at the centre of its serious music making through the 1790s. The younger composer played the keyboard works in that milieu, studied the scores the library held, and absorbed the standing lesson of the circle: that fugue was not a school exercise but a living resource for large scale composition. Carl Czerny, who studied with him from 1801, reported a teacher who could play the fugues from memory and who set the collection before his own pupils, Czerny among them, as the daily bread of a pianist's training. Czerny in turn taught Liszt, and the teaching line from the Bonn lessons runs unbroken into the virtuoso century.

The early works show the training in their bones rather than on their surfaces. The piano sonatas of the 1790s build development sections on sequential patterns drilled by the preludes, and the variation sets test a theme the way the older keyboard suites test a dance. Yet the decisive evidence waits for the final period, when the pupil returned to fugue as the centre of his art. That return is the subject of the next section. What the childhood text supplied was fluency: fugue was never a foreign language to be translated with effort, but a native grammar available the moment the composer's late style went looking for a means to hold enormous spans together under pressure.

A discipline point belongs here, because Beethoven stories attract legend the way the Barcelona shop story attracts embroidery. The famous claim that the composer called the Well-Tempered Clavier his musical bible circulates in later anecdote and fits the documented facts, yet its wording rests on reported speech rather than a dated letter, and this article treats it as colour rather than evidence. The evidence is stronger than the anecdote anyway. A published notice of 1783, a teacher's known library, a pupil's testimony and a shelf of late fugues outweigh any single quotable sentence, and the influence case stands on those four legs without needing the quote at all.

## Beethoven's late fugues and the debt

The late works repay the childhood debt in public, at the largest scale the composer ever attempted. The fugue that closes the Hammerklavier Sonata, Op. 106, completed in 1818, announces itself with a trill introduction and then subjects its theme to inversion, augmentation and stretto across a movement of punishing length, a deliberate confrontation with the older master's keyboard art on the modern piano. The Missa Solemnis, completed in 1823, builds the conclusions of its Gloria and Credo as full fugues, church counterpoint carrying a concert mass for a vast public occasion. The finale of the Cello Sonata Op. 102 No. 2 and the fugue inside the Piano Sonata Op. 110, which collapses in exhaustion and then rebuilds itself inverted, show the form used as drama, the voices enacting loss and recovery. Each case names the technique, and each stands downstream of a documented childhood access, which is why the Beethoven claim passes the test that vaguer claims fail.

The Grosse Fuge stands at the end of the line. Composed in 1825 as the finale of the String Quartet Op. 130 and published separately as Op. 133 after the composer agreed to supply a lighter ending, the movement drives a jagged subject through a sequence of fugal sections in different keys, tempos and characters, treating counterpoint as a force capable of violence as well as order. Publishers of the period called it incomprehensible, and early performers resisted it, yet its ancestry is plain to any player who knows the Well-Tempered Clavier: the confidence that a single subject, rigorously pursued, can sustain a huge span, and that an audience can be asked to follow argument rather than tune. The distance travelled is equally plain. The Leipzig models balance their voices within a shared Affekt, while the Viennese heir makes the voices fight. Influence here means inheritance transformed under pressure, not imitation preserved in glass.

The debt also ran through performance. The composer's insistence that pupils master the fugues, Czerny's testimony about memorised playing, and the Bonn notice together describe a musician for whom the older keyboard art was a working tool across fifty years. When the late quartets baffled their first audiences, the composer reportedly answered that they were written for a later age, and the later age, when it came, reached for the same keyboard collection to understand him. Teachers still set the two composers in sequence, the pupil's late fugues explained by the master's preludes, and the pairing rests on documents rather than on the romance of genius recognising genius across a century.

One boundary keeps the claim honest. The symphonies, the concertos and the middle period quartets owe their drama chiefly to Haydn, to Mozart and to the composer's own theatre sense, and fugue plays a modest part in them. The debt concentrates in the keyboard training and in the late style, roughly the final decade, where counterpoint returns as the load bearing structure. A bounded claim of that kind can be argued with, dated and tested. The unbounded version, in which the older master secretly authored everything the younger man did, collapses on first contact with the scores, and no serious scholar named in this article defends it.

## The first published collections

Print changed the transmission from a network into a market during the first years of the nineteenth century, and the change can be dated to a single publishing season. In 1801 three editions of the Well-Tempered Clavier appeared almost at once: from Simrock at Bonn, from Nageli at Zurich, and from Hoffmeister and Kuhnel at Leipzig. Hans Georg Nageli, the Zurich publisher whose edition carried a serious editorial hand, believed the collection a cornerstone of keyboard art and issued it as the opening of a series devoted to major keyboard works. Competing editions in one year signal a market, not a charity. Publishers who had ignored the composer for fifty years now judged that teachers and amateurs across Europe would pay for his fugues, and their judgement created the conditions in which a Mendelssohn, a Schumann or a Chopin could meet the music without a Berlin patron or a Viennese baron opening a library door.

Other works followed in a revealing order. The keyboard music came first, because the teaching market wanted it: the Inventions, the suites and the partitas in editions aimed at students and connoisseurs. Motets appeared in print at Leipzig in 1802 and 1803, giving choirs a performable Bach beyond the Sing-Akademie walls. The organ works travelled more slowly, since the market of organists was smaller and the music harder to sell, and the great choral works waited longest of all, dependent on institutions willing to print performing material for forces few towns could assemble. Forkel's biography of 1802 belongs to the same print moment and served it deliberately: a life in print created readers for editions in print, and the publishers advertised into the curiosity the biography aroused. Print also fixed a canon within the output. What publishers issued became the known composer, and what they delayed, above all the cantatas, remained unknown to musicians who trusted print as the map of the repertory.

Print introduced errors with the same efficiency it introduced access. Editors worked from copies of copies, regularised notation, added dynamics and phrasing according to the taste of 1800, and sometimes attributed works wrongly in either direction. The pianist who learned the Well-Tempered Clavier from an early edition learned a text at several removes from the autographs, filtered through editors who believed they were helping. That filter explains part of the nineteenth century manner of playing the music, heavily phrased and dynamically sculpted, which the period performance movement of a later century would challenge as an accretion. The printed page, in short, did not end the mediation of the legacy. It industrialised the mediation, and every subsequent argument about how the music should sound is partly an argument about which layer of editors to trust.

Here the first loop of the article tightens without closing. A cellist in Barcelona in 1890 could buy the suites because print had kept them in the catalogues as study material for decades. How did a cellist end up rescuing six works that were sitting in print the whole time? The question sounds paradoxical only while print and performance are treated as the same thing. The editions existed, the public cycle did not, and the distance between a study on a student's stand and a cycle on a concert platform is exactly the distance Casals travelled in twelve years of private work. The closing of that loop waits for the section on his performing career, where the recordings and the recital programmes supply the evidence.

## The 1829 revival performance

On 11 March 1829 the Berlin Sing-Akademie gave the St Matthew Passion under the direction of the twenty year old Felix Mendelssohn, and the evening became the most retold date in the whole reception history. The forces were large by the standards of the work's own century: a choir of several hundred drawn from the Sing-Akademie ranks, soloists including the actor and singer Eduard Devrient, who had pressed his young friend to attempt the performance and sang the part of Christ, and an orchestra placed and balanced according to the practice of 1829 rather than of 1727. Mendelssohn cut the score substantially, shortening recitatives, omitting arias and chorales, and reshaping the pacing for a concert audience hearing a two part sacred drama in a hall. The performance was repeated twice more that spring, once on 21 March, the composer's birthday by the old calendar reckoning the family kept, and the Berlin musical world, critics included, treated the event as a civic occasion. Johann Sebastian Bach entered the general concert repertory through that door.

### Who revived Bach in the nineteenth century?

Felix Mendelssohn conducted the headline event, the 1829 Berlin St Matthew Passion, yet he stood on prepared ground. Zelter and the Sing-Akademie had kept the work in rehearsal, Sara Levy had preserved manuscripts and performance habits, and publishers had issued keyboard editions decades before Mendelssohn raised his baton.

Preparation explains the possibility. Mendelssohn had received a copy of the Passion score as a boy, a gift within a family whose collection and connections ran back through Sara Levy to the Berlin court tradition, and he had studied with Zelter, the Sing-Akademie's director, who knew the work intimately and doubted the public readiness for it. Celia Applegate, the historian of the revival whose study of Mendelssohn and the German musical public remains the standard account of these years, shows a young conductor working through institutions rather than against them: academy choir, family capital, press allies and a subscription audience had all to be assembled before the first rehearsal. Her reading replaces the lone rescuer with a network achieving a public act, and the evidence of rehearsals, correspondence and ticket demand supports the network at every point.

The aftermath spread by the usual paper channels, accelerated. Mendelssohn conducted the work again at the Lower Rhine Festival at Dusseldorf in later years and carried the cause into his Leipzig Gewandhaus programmes after 1835. Devrient published his recollections, fixing the heroic version of the story for later readers. Other German towns requested scores and performing material, and the demand strengthened the case for a complete edition, since every new performance exposed the chaos of competing copies. In England the cause advanced through the Bach Society founded in London in 1849, with William Sterndale Bennett, a Mendelssohn admirer who had heard the Leipzig tradition at first hand, among its driving spirits. The revival, measured by performances, editions and institutions founded, is a European process running from 1829 across three decades, with Berlin as its loudest single evening rather than its sole cause.

## What the revival actually revived

The 1829 performance revived public concert life, not scholarly knowledge, and the two are constantly confused. That sentence is the fourth rung of the ladder, and it closes the second loop of this article, the question opened in the section on the decades after 1750. Professional study had continued without a break: theorists taught from the scores, organists played the preludes, collectors guarded the manuscripts, and the Sing-Akademie itself had rehearsed the Passion in part under Zelter years before Mendelssohn persuaded the institution to perform the work in public. What had lapsed was performance before a paying general audience, above all performance of the large sacred works, which required choirs, money, parts and a public willing to sit through an archaic Lutheran drama. The revival restored that public function. It converted a professional inheritance into a civic possession, and the conversion, not a rescue from oblivion, is what the date 1829 properly names.

Precision about the conversion protects the rest of the story. The Mendelssohn performance did not sound like the Leipzig performances of the 1720s, and nobody in 1829 expected or wanted it to. Cuts removed perhaps a third of the score. Modern instruments, a massed choir and concert hall dynamics presented the work as a monument of German sacred art for a post Napoleonic public building a national culture. Applegate's account stresses that national framing: the Passion served Berlin as proof of a German artistic inheritance, and the composer, a Lutheran cantor little published in his lifetime, was recast as a national classic. Revival always edits its object. The nineteenth century received a Bach shaped by the institutions that revived him, and the shaping explains both the century's devotion and its blind spots, including the long delay before the cantatas, too liturgical and too numerous for monument treatment, entered general knowledge.

What the revival also revived, less visibly, was an argument about ownership of the past. Zelter's caution had rested on a guild judgement: the work belonged to trained musicians who could meet its demands. Mendelssohn's success asserted a public judgement: the work belonged to any listener the performance could move. German musical life organised itself around the second claim for the next century, building the societies, editions and concert series that the following sections describe. The professional line did not disappear inside the public triumph. It supplied the editors, the teachers and the sceptics, and its standards kept testing the public version whenever enthusiasm outran evidence, a tension the article meets again in the period performance debates of the twentieth century.

## The Bach Society and the complete edition

In 1850, the centenary of the composer's death, musicians and scholars founded the Bach Gesellschaft at Leipzig with the declared aim of publishing the complete works in a critical edition. Robert Schumann, Moritz Hauptmann and Otto Jahn stood among the founders, and the first volume, containing the church cantatas, appeared in 1851 under Hauptmann's editorship. The edition proceeded year by year for half a century, reaching completion in 1900 with forty six annual volumes, and it changed the practical basis of the entire legacy. For the first time a musician in any city with a good library could consult the cantatas, the Passions, the Mass in B minor, the keyboard works, the organ works and the instrumental music in one uniform series, edited from the best sources the society's scholars could locate. Influence before 1851 had depended on access to scattered paper. Influence after 1851 could begin from a library shelf.

The edition was a scholarly achievement with scholarly limits, and both sides belong in the record. Its editors worked before modern source studies had mapped the manuscripts, and they sometimes chose readings from late copies, misattributed works in both directions, and printed texts that the next century's research would correct in detail. Singers and players nonetheless built the modern performing tradition on those volumes: the choral societies that multiplied across Germany, Britain and America learned the Passions and the Mass from Gesellschaft scores, and conductors from Mendelssohn's successors to the early recording era treated the edition as the work itself. When teachers in this article's later sections set a cantata aria or a keyboard suite before a student, the page in front of the student descends from the Gesellschaft enterprise, through the corrections of its successor, far more often than from any autograph.

The successor arrived with the Neue Bach Ausgabe, the new complete edition launched in 1954 by the Johann Sebastian Bach Institute at Gottingen and the Bach Archive at Leipzig, published by Barenreiter at Kassel under editors including Alfred Durr and Georg von Dadelsen. Working volume by volume with full critical reports, the new edition redated works, separated authentic compositions from doubtful ones, and rebuilt texts from the autograph tradition that source studies had clarified. Its main series reached completion in 2007, giving performers and scholars by the end of this article's period a documented text of a reliability the nineteenth century could not approach. The Gesellschaft had made the composer universally available. The new edition made him accurately available, and the difference between those two gifts structures the modern reception.

Institutional scholarship grew around the editions. The Bach Jahrbuch, published from 1904, carried research year by year. The Bach Archive at Leipzig, founded in 1950, gathered copies of every known source. Wolfgang Schmieder's thematic catalogue, the Bach Werke Verzeichnis of 1950, revised in 1990, gave every work the BWV number by which musicians across the world identify pieces in conversation, on programmes and in record catalogues. A BWV number is a small thing, yet it standardised the language of the legacy: a student in Tokyo, a choirmaster in Chicago and an editor in Kassel can name BWV 232 and mean the same pages. Standardised naming is influence infrastructure, and the Gesellschaft generation built it.

## Mendelssohn, Schumann and the romantic reception

The romantic generation received the composer through the revival institutions and answered with works of their own, each answer naming its technique. Mendelssohn, beyond the conducting, wrote the older master into his own scores: the oratorio St Paul of 1836 opens with chorale treatment learned from the Passions, the organ sonatas of 1845 place fugal movements inside a romantic instrument's repertory, and his all Bach organ recital at the Thomaskirche in Leipzig in August 1840, played to raise funds for a Bach monument, presented the preludes and fugues to a public audience as concert music on the composer's own ground. The monument followed in 1843, the first public statue of the composer, funded by the recital and the advocacy around it. Mendelssohn took from the scores a discipline of choral writing and a model of the composer as learned craftsman, and he passed on institutions: Gewandhaus programming, the Leipzig Conservatory founded in 1843, and a performing tradition that treated the Passions as repertory rather than archaeology.

Schumann's engagement ran through the study and the composing desk. He kept the Well-Tempered Clavier as a daily standard, urged Clara Schumann's pupils toward the fugues, and in 1845, his counterpoint year with Clara, produced the Six Fugues on the name BACH, Op. 60, built on the four note motif formed by the letters of the composer's name in German notation. The Studies and Sketches for pedal piano of the same period show a romantic composer deliberately schooling himself in strict forms. Clara, in turn, carried the keyboard works into the recital hall across a sixty year career, programming fugues at a time when audiences expected operatic display from pianists, and her editions and teaching transmitted a performing Bach shaped by romantic piano technique. The Schumann circle also supplied the Gesellschaft with a founder and the press campaign that launched it, so their reception joined composition, performance and institution building in one household.

Chopin, whose name rarely enters the revival story, supplies one of its best documented private facts. He took the Well-Tempered Clavier with him to Majorca in the winter of 1838 and 1839, practised from it during the composition of the Preludes Op. 28, and taught from the collection in Paris, requiring pupils to prepare fugues before lessons. His counterpoint is woven inside a harmonic language far from Leipzig, which is exactly why the case instructs: the influence operated as discipline and voice leading conscience rather than as surface imitation. The chain of access runs through the printed editions of the 1800s and the Paris teaching world, and the surviving testimony of pupils records the practice in detail. Romantic reception, section by section, keeps resolving into the same pattern: an edition, a teacher, a technique taken, a work altered.

## Brahms and the scholarly inheritance

Johannes Brahms inherited the legacy in its scholarly form, as a subscriber's set of volumes and a collector's cabinet, and his case shows the complete edition working as an influence channel. He subscribed to the Gesellschaft edition, studied its volumes as they appeared, collected autographs and early editions, and bequeathed his library, rich in the older master's sources, to the Gesellschaft der Musikfreunde in Vienna. His correspondence with Joseph Joachim records an exchange of contrapuntal exercises in the 1850s, the two young musicians correcting each other's canons and fugues by post in a private discipline modelled on the strict school. The technique taken can be named in the scores: the motets Op. 29, Op. 74 and Op. 110 build choral counterpoint on baroque foundations, the Handel Variations Op. 24 of 1861 answer a baroque theme with a closing fugue, and the finale of the Fourth Symphony of 1885 constructs a passacaglia, thirty variations over a ground bass adapted from the cantata Nach dir, Herr, verlanget mich, BWV 150. That last borrowing is documented in the notes themselves, an ostinato lifted from an early cantata the Gesellschaft volumes had made available, and it closes the argument of the movement with a technique three centuries old.

Brahms also edited and advocated in practical ways. He prepared performing material, supported the publication of older music beyond his own century's repertory, and wrote for the organ a set of chorale preludes, Op. 122, published after his death, that stand as a late romantic organist's homage to the Leipzig chorale tradition. His famous answer about the Chaconne from the D minor Partita, given in a letter to Clara Schumann in 1878, describes the piece as a world of thought and feeling on one staff, and his own left hand piano arrangement of the Chaconne turned admiration into a performable text for pianists who lacked a violin. The scholarly inheritance, in his hands, never stayed on the shelf. It became editions used, exercises exchanged, a symphony finale engineered, and a private standard against which he measured his own counterpoint and found it, by his letters, perpetually wanting.

The contrast with Mendelssohn instructs. The earlier romantic received the composer through family and academy, the later one through print and the archive. Between those two receptions the legacy changed its social form, from a tradition carried by connected households to a public scholarly possession that any serious musician could buy, borrow and cite. Brahms stands as the first great composer whose Bach was primarily the edition's Bach, complete, ordered and critically framed, and the completeness altered the influence. Earlier recipients chose from what paper reached them. Brahms could survey the whole output, and his borrowings range accordingly, from an obscure early cantata to the famous Chaconne, chosen by reading rather than delivered by a teacher.

## Liszt, the transcription tradition and the piano

Franz Liszt converted organ counterpoint into piano spectacle, and the conversion created a transmission channel of its own. His piano transcriptions of the great organ preludes and fugues, including the Prelude and Fugue in A minor BWV 543 and the set of six preludes and fugues published in the 1840s and 1850s, placed music written for pedals and twin manuals under ten fingers in the concert hall, where audiences who never entered a Lutheran organ loft encountered the fugues as virtuoso drama. The transcription tradition always changes its object: registration becomes dynamic shading, pedal lines become thumb melodies or crossed hand thunder, and sustained organ tone becomes repeated attack. What survives intact is the architecture, the subject entries, the sequence logic and the proportions, and architecture was the element the piano century most wanted from the older master. Liszt's pupils and rivals carried the arrangements across Europe and America, and conservatory students met the organ works at the piano decades before recordings made the originals familiar.

The name motif supplied Liszt with a second, more personal channel. His Prelude and Fugue on B-A-C-H, composed for organ in 1855 and revised later, builds a vast romantic structure on the four notes the composer's name spells in German notation, the same motif Schumann had used in Op. 60. The piece treats the motif as both homage and raw material, subjecting it to chromatic transformation the Leipzig composer never attempted while depending entirely on the fugal discipline he codified. Around the motif a whole sub tradition gathered: Schumann's fugues, Liszt's prelude, Rimsky Korsakov's variations within his piano works, Busoni's Fantasia after J. S. Bach of 1909, and later Schoenberg and Webern, each generation signing the older name into its own harmonic language. Few influence lines in music can be followed so exactly, note by note and decade by decade, through dated published scores.

Liszt the performer also served the cause directly. His Weimar years placed older music at the centre of a court culture that younger composers visited as pilgrims, and his advocacy for editions and monuments raised money and attention. The transcription tradition he led drew criticism from purists in his own century and from the period performance movement in the next, and the criticism identifies a real cost: a generation learned the fugues with romantic swell and rubato as if those features were in the text. The defence identifies an equal gain: without the piano versions, the organ works would have remained a specialists' preserve through the very decades when the composer's general fame was being built. Both judgements can be true, and the record of programmes and editions shows both effects operating at once in different rooms.

## Nineteenth century arrangements and what they changed

Arrangement was the century's distribution system, and its products deserve audit one by one, because each changed the music in a specific direction. Charles Gounod's Ave Maria of 1859, first published in 1853 as a meditation, lays a new melody over the Prelude in C major from the first book of the Well-Tempered Clavier, BWV 846, turning a keyboard prelude into a devotional song known to millions who never heard the original set. The borrowing is exact and documented, the prelude used as a complete harmonic frame, and the case belongs in any honest account because it carried the harmony of the collection into drawing rooms, weddings and funerals across the world. What it took was the arpeggiated chord pattern and its serene regularity. What it changed was everything else: function, text, forces and meaning.

August Wilhelmj's arrangement of 1871 took the Air from the Orchestral Suite No. 3 in D major, BWV 1068, slowed it, and set it for violin on the G string alone with piano accompaniment, creating the Air on the G String, a concert staple under a title the composer never knew. The original movement, a graceful dance in a festive suite for trumpets and drums, became a meditation, and the transformation taught audiences to hear the older music as an emblem of depth and consolation. Ferruccio Busoni's piano transcription of the Chaconne, published in the 1890s, moved in the opposite direction, expanding a solo violin movement into monumental pianism while insisting, in his editorial writing, that transcription was an honest art with its own laws. His edition of the Well-Tempered Clavier, issued from 1894, supplied fingerings, phrasing and analytical commentary that shaped teaching for generations, an editor's Bach layered over the text in print.

The pattern across these cases is consistent enough to state as a rule. Arrangers selected the movements that suited the nineteenth century's preferred image of the composer, serene, monumental or profound, and adjusted tempo, dynamics and instrumentation until the music matched the image. Movements that resisted the image, the dance suites played at dance tempo, the witty concertos, the cantatas with their specific Lutheran texts, waited for later advocates. The arrangements therefore transmitted a partial composer at enormous scale. Millions met the harmony of BWV 846 through Gounod and the Air through Wilhelmj before they met any work whole, and first impressions of that kind set expectations that complete editions and later scholarship spent a century correcting. Arrangement is influence with an editor's thumb on the scale, and the honest historian credits the scale and the thumb together.

## Schoenberg, Webern and the modernist claim on Bach

The modernists claimed him for a project he would not have recognised, and the claim shapes how he is taught. That is the fifth rung of the ladder, and the section that carries it also opens the third loop of this article. Arnold Schoenberg, who described himself as a conservative forced into radicalism, arranged the older music for modern orchestra, orchestrating the Prelude and Fugue in E flat major BWV 552 and two chorale preludes in 1922, and he taught counterpoint from the keyboard works throughout his career in Vienna, Berlin and America. His claim was genealogical: the German tradition, he argued, ran from the Leipzig master through Mozart, Beethoven, Brahms and Wagner to his own school, and his method of developing variation, which he traced to Brahms, rested on the motivic discipline of the fugues. The claim served a purpose. A composer accused of destroying tradition presented himself as its heir, and the Leipzig scores supplied his documentary evidence.

Anton Webern's orchestration of the six part Ricercar from the Musical Offering, made in 1935, pressed the claim into sound. Webern distributed the older counterpoint note by note among the instruments of a modern orchestra, each phrase changing colour as it passes, so that the fugal structure becomes audible as a succession of timbres. The arrangement is an analysis performed aloud: it demonstrates Webern's belief that the Ricercar already contained the seed of his own pointillist art, its lines poised between unity and fragmentation. Scholars have admired the arrangement as illumination and criticised it as appropriation, and both responses are recorded in the literature. What cannot be disputed is the chain: a specific score, the Musical Offering of 1747, a specific arranger with a documented lifelong study of the older counterpoint, and a dated arrangement that taught two generations of composers to hear the eighteenth century source as a modernist ancestor.

Here the third loop opens, and it stays open until near the end of the article. Can an influence claim be tested, or is it just reputation? The modernist case forces the question, because Schoenberg's genealogy mixes documented study, real technique transfer and self serving myth in proportions that differ claim by claim. The fugues in his teaching: documented. The line from developing variation to the Well-Tempered Clavier: arguable, with evidence on both sides. The claim that the twelve note method fulfils a destiny latent in the older counterpoint: a manifesto, not a finding. Sorting those layers requires a test with explicit steps, and the article builds the test in its closing sections, applying it to cases from every century including the modernists themselves. Until then, hold the distinction this section has drawn: arrangement and teaching are evidence, genealogy is argument, and the two travel together in modernist writing more tightly than anywhere else in the reception.

## Stravinsky and the neoclassical use

Igor Stravinsky's neoclassicism reached for the older composer with a cooler hand than Schoenberg's, and the contact points are again datable scores. The Concerto for Piano and Wind Instruments of 1924 and the Dumbarton Oaks Concerto of 1938 turned to baroque concerto procedure, motoric rhythm, terraced dynamics and transparent counterpoint as correctives to romantic rhetoric, and Stravinsky spoke of the Brandenburg Concertos as a model for the Dumbarton Oaks scoring and spirit. His direct engagement with a specific text came late: in 1956 he arranged the Canonic Variations on Vom Himmel hoch, BWV 769, the organ variations the Leipzig composer had presented to Mizler's society in 1747, reworking them for chorus and orchestra in his own serial inflected idiom. The arrangement is a signature across two centuries, one contrapuntist signing another's exercise book, and it appeared in the same decade as his other late reworkings of Gesualdo and Pergolesi, a deliberate programme of composition by commentary.

The neoclassical use differed from the romantic use in what it took. Mendelssohn took choral monumentality, Liszt took organ grandeur, Gounod took harmonic serenity. Stravinsky took procedure: the concerto grosso layout, the ritornello habit of return, the discipline of a figure worked until its possibilities declare themselves. Critics of the period accused neoclassicism of wearing the past as a mask, and Richard Taruskin, the historian whose writing on Stravinsky and on the early music movement presses hardest on claims of historical fidelity, reads the neoclassical turn as a modernist strategy dressed in period clothing. That reading, applied here, does not dissolve the influence. It locates it correctly, in selected techniques carried across by a composer who knew the scores intimately and used them for purposes the originals never served. Influence does not require agreement with the source. Stravinsky's cool, ironic distance from his model is itself a documented relation, traceable in scores on both sides.

A second Stravinsky channel ran through teaching and the piano. His Sonata of 1924 and the Serenade in A placed contrapuntal keyboard writing before pianists in a modern accent, and his recorded remarks on counterpoint as the true discipline of composition echoed the Leipzig teaching tradition through the century's middle decades. Composers as different as Elliot Carter in his early works and the young Pierre Boulez, who later distanced himself from neoclassicism, passed through a musical education in which the older counterpoint, filtered by Stravinsky's example and by the conservatory curriculum, counted as the measure of craft. The filter matters and gets named each time in this article, because a line that runs through an intermediary belongs in part to the intermediary.

## Casals and the cello suites in performance

The Barcelona loop closes here, on the evidence of programmes, recordings and a changed profession. Pablo Casals worked on the six suites in private for twelve years after the 1890 purchase, by his account studying them daily and withholding them from public performance until his technique and his conception settled. He began playing individual suites in recital around 1901 and built, across the following decades, the performing tradition the works had lacked: complete cycles, programmed as serious architecture rather than as studies, played with a singing line and a dancer's sense of the sarabande and gigue movements. His recordings of the complete set, made in London and Paris between 1936 and 1939, fixed that tradition in a form any cellist anywhere could study, and they remained the reference against which later players defined their own readings, in agreement or in revolt.

### How did Casals find the cello suites?

By his own later account, the thirteen year old Pablo Casals found a worn printed edition of the six suites in a Barcelona second hand music shop in 1890, while searching with his father for sheet music. Nobody performed the set complete in public then, and he studied the edition privately for years.

So the answer to the question opened in the section on the first printed collections stands plainly. A cellist rescued six works that were sitting in print the whole time because print preserves and performance persuades, and between preservation and persuasion stood a gap that only a performer could close. The suites had been available as studies since the first edition of 1824, issued at Paris from a manuscript copy, and editors through the century had treated them as advanced exercises, sometimes doubting they were performable at all. Casals took from the edition the complete cycle, the insight that six suites in six keys form a recital architecture, and the conviction that the dance movements wanted rhythm and air rather than apology. He passed on a repertory: after Casals, a cellist's education included the suites entire, auditions asked for them, and the recording catalogue filled with rival cycles, from Fournier and Rostropovich to the period instrument players who later challenged his tempi and tone with evidence of their own.

The source caution from the opening section applies to the end. The shop, the father's presence and the boy's age come from the cellist's own later recollection, and tellings differ in small details, so this article presents the scene as his account throughout. The checkable facts stand independent of the scene: the editions existed, the public tradition did not, the recordings date from 1936 to 1939, and the repertory changed. Influence history built on those four legs survives any embroidery the first leg may carry.

## The period performance movement and the argument it started

The twentieth century's loudest argument about the legacy concerned not who received the music but how it should sound, and both sides deserve their strongest statement. The period performance case, built by scholar performers across the century, runs as follows. The romantic tradition performed the music on instruments the composer never knew, with forces several times his scale, vibrato he never heard and phrasing drawn from later repertory, so the object audiences praised was a translation. Arnold Dolmetsch's early experiments, Wanda Landowska's harpsichord advocacy from the 1900s onward, and the postwar generation of Nikolaus Harnoncourt, Gustav Leonhardt and their colleagues set out to recover the original terms: period instruments, smaller ensembles, dance based tempi, rhetoric shaped articulation. Their complete recording of the cantatas, issued by Telefunken across the 1970s and 1980s using period instruments and boys' voices, presented the church music entire on those terms for the first time, and the project changed what listeners expected a cantata to sound like.

The counter case, pressed most forcefully by Taruskin in essays gathered in Text and Act of 1995, answers that the movement's supposedly recovered past sounds remarkably like modernist taste: lean, brisk, unsentimental and suspicious of expression, a twentieth century preference projected backward. On this reading, authenticity functions as a marketing and moral claim rather than a recoverable fact, since no recording of the eighteenth century exists and the evidence underdetermines hundreds of performing decisions. John Butt, the scholar and performer whose Playing with History of 2002 examines the movement's cultural logic, occupies the mediating ground with care: he grants the critique of naive authenticity, documents how period performance grew from modern musical culture, and defends the movement's results as a genuine expansion of the choices available to performers and listeners. Butt's position, held consistently in his writing on the subject, treats the argument itself as productive, a permanent audit of habit by evidence.

The sharpest technical dispute concerned voices. In 1981 Joshua Rifkin presented evidence that the Leipzig church performances used one singer per part in most choral works, a choir of soloists rather than a massed body, and his recordings with the Bach Ensemble demonstrated the clarity the disposition allows. Supporters, including Andrew Parrott, whose book The Essential Bach Choir of 2000 assembled the documentary case, argue that payment records, surviving part sets and the music's own balances converge on small forces. Opponents answer that the parts do not exclude ripieno reinforcement, that special occasions plainly used larger groups, and that a universal rule outruns the documents. Christoph Wolff, approaching from the source studies, treats the question as one the evidence settles differently for different works and occasions. The dispute remained open at the end of this article's period, and its openness is the honest result: what would settle it is a class of document, a complete Leipzig payment roster for a known performance, that the archive has not produced.

For the influence story the argument matters because it redefined the transmitted object for a third time. Print had standardised the notes. The Gesellschaft had standardised the catalogue. Period performance contested the sound, and after the contest no performer, however traditional, could claim innocence of the questions it raised. Even mainstream orchestras lightened textures, trimmed choirs and reconsidered tempo in the Passions, absorbing the critique while rejecting its strictest conclusions. Influence, once again, flowed through identifiable channels: named performers, dated recordings, published essays and the students each side trained.

## Bach in jazz

The jazz line is real, traceable and narrower than its publicity, and it runs through named musicians who said what they took. Jacques Loussier formed his trio in Paris in 1959 and built its repertory on the keyboard works, the Play Bach recordings selling in the millions through the 1960s by setting fugues and preludes over a swinging bass and drums. What Loussier took was structure: the written counterpoint survives nearly intact while the rhythm section reframes its metre, and his audiences, many of them classical listeners crossing over, heard the architecture hold under a foreign accent. The Swingle Singers, formed in Paris in 1962 by Ward Swingle, recorded the keyboard and instrumental works for scat voices in 1963, winning American success with arrangements that treated the lines as vocal jazz parts, precise, light and rhythmically exact. Their arranger's craft depended on the music's voice leading, which tolerates redistribution among voices without losing its logic, a property of the writing itself that jazz musicians recognised as kinship.

The Modern Jazz Quartet made the deepest claim, because John Lewis, its pianist and musical director, built original compositions on fugal procedure rather than arranging the older scores. Lewis, classically trained and explicit in interviews about his sources, wrote pieces such as Vendome of 1952 and the fugal exchanges of the group's 1970s album Blues on Bach as working counterpoint for a jazz quartet, taking the technique, imitation, inversion and balanced voices, and leaving the notes behind. That is influence of the strongest documented kind: a named musician, a named technique, datable works on both sides, and access through the printed editions and the conservatory curriculum Lewis had passed through. Other jazz encounters run shallower. Solo pianists from Art Tatum onward displayed a harmonic fluency that critics compared with the older master, yet comparison is not transmission, and where no statement, score or study trail connects the players, this article records admiration rather than influence and moves on.

The jazz chapter also demonstrates the intermediary rule in reverse. Many jazz listeners met the counterpoint first through Loussier or the Swingles and travelled from the arrangements to the originals, reversing the direction of the classical story. Record shops filed the crossover albums where browsers could find them, radio used the Swingle sound until it became a period signature in itself, and the keyboard works acquired a second public that owed nothing to the church, the conservatory or the Gesellschaft. The legacy by 1970 had several publics who barely overlapped, each holding a different selection of the output and a different account of why it mattered, and the jazz public's selection, keyboard counterpoint heard as rhythm and line, was as defensible as any.

## Bach in popular music and film

Wendy Carlos carried the counterpoint into the electronic age on a single album. Switched-On Bach, released in 1968 and recorded with Glenn Gould's enthusiasm and collaboration in its presentation, realised the keyboard works and the Brandenburg Concertos on the Moog synthesizer, an instrument whose monophonic lines forced every voice of a fugue to be built track by track. The album won three Grammy Awards, sold beyond any classical expectation of its decade, and demonstrated to a mass audience that the writing survived a timbre its composer could not have imagined. What Carlos took was the note text entire, from the printed editions. What the album changed was the sound world and the public: synthesizer musicians acquired a classical credential, classical listeners acquired an electronic one, and the album's success funded and legitimised a generation of electronic experiment. The chain is documented in contracts, sales and the album itself, and it needs no embellishment.

Rock musicians reached the scores through the same record shops and school music rooms. Jethro Tull's recording of the Bouree from the Suite in E minor for lute, BWV 996, on the 1969 album Stand Up, recast the dance movement as a flute led rock instrumental with the original bass line walking underneath, and Ian Anderson's arrangement credits the source openly. The case is small and perfect: a named movement, a named arranger, a dated record, and a technique, the independent bass line as groove, transferred whole. Progressive rock widened the channel through the 1970s, with keyboard players such as Keith Emerson treating baroque counterpoint as a virtuoso resource, though Emerson's direct sources ran as often through arrangements and through intermediaries like Jacques Loussier as through scores, and the honest account names the filter each time rather than drawing a straight line from Leipzig to the stadium.

Film gave the music a narrative function that concert life could not. Directors reached for the scores when a scene needed intelligence, order or menace rendered in sound, and the Goldberg Variations acquired a particular screen life through Glenn Gould's 1955 recording, heard in The Silence of the Lambs of 1991 behind a character whose courtesy and horror the aria's poise renders more disturbing. The Cello Suites, carried into general awareness by the Casals tradition and its successors, became cinema's shorthand for solitary reflection, and the Air from BWV 1068, already softened by Wilhelmj's arrangement, underscored reconciliation scenes across decades of popular film. These uses transmit fragments rather than works, and their influence runs toward the composer's image more than toward any musician's technique. The image mattered commercially and pedagogically: film audiences who could not name a fugue learned to associate the name with depth, and some fraction of them travelled, as the jazz listeners had, from the fragment to the whole.

## Bach as a teaching text worldwide

The deepest channel of all runs through the lesson, repeated weekly in rooms the concert historians never visit. By the middle of the nineteenth century the keyboard works stood at the centre of piano teaching across Europe, and the conservatory systems of Leipzig, Vienna, Paris and London exported the curriculum to America, to Japan and to every country that built conservatories on the European model. Graded examination boards in Britain and the Commonwealth set the Inventions and the easier preludes at defined grades, so a child's progress up the syllabus ladder was measured partly in this repertory. In Japan, where piano study expanded enormously across the twentieth century, the same graded collections, in editions descending from the European tradition, placed the two part Inventions in the hands of millions of students. The Soviet piano school, one of the century's most formidable teaching machines, built its early training on the notebooks and inventions with equal centrality. Three rival musical cultures agreed on the teaching text while agreeing on little else.

The reasons are functional and were already stated by the composer. The title page of the Inventions in their 1723 fair copy promises learners a method for playing in two and three voices, for handling ideas and developing them, and above all for achieving a singing style, and teachers across three centuries have found the promise kept. A two part invention fits small hands and short lessons while containing a complete education in motive, imitation, sequence and cadence. No other teaching repertory packs so much transferable grammar into pieces a twelve year old can finish. Teachers who organise a pupil's scores, practice notes and examination lists across years of such lessons need their own order kept straight, and a student who keeps that growing archive of editions and marked copies in order can obtain the offline encrypted notes app from the [VaultBook download page](https://vaultbook.net/download/) for the purpose, since private study records deserve private storage.

Worldwide teaching also standardised an interpretation tradition that recordings then disrupted. The editions of Busoni, of Czerny before him and of the great teaching editors carried fingerings and phrasings that pupils absorbed as the music itself, and a student in Buenos Aires and a student in Osaka might learn the same invention through nearly identical editorial layers. The period performance argument reached the teaching studio last and changed it least, since the piano lesson had its own economy of examinations and parental expectation. Yet even there the questions travelled: teachers debated harpsichord touch, dance tempo and ornament realisation with a seriousness that would have surprised their own teachers, and examination boards adjusted their guidance across the decades in response to scholarship. The teaching text, precisely because it changes slowest, preserves the clearest sediment of every era's Bach, layered edition upon edition in the cupboards of piano teachers everywhere.

## The inventions and the piano student

### Why do piano students still play Bach?

Because the Inventions teach two skills at once in very little space. Each short piece trains independence between the hands, clear articulation and singing tone while also teaching how a motive generates an entire composition. Teachers can grade the set from two voices to three, matching difficulty to progress precisely.

The set began as a father's teaching book. The fifteen two part Inventions and fifteen three part Sinfonias were assembled in final form in 1723, drawn from the little keyboard book begun in 1720 for Wilhelm Friedemann, the eldest son, whose education the collection was built to serve. The sequence is a curriculum in disguise: the two part pieces teach a hand to sustain an independent line and answer it, the three part pieces add a middle voice and with it the management of density, and the keys proceed in an order that introduces new terrain gradually. A student who completes the set has handled imitation, canon, sequence, invertible counterpoint and the shaping of a binary span, the complete grammar of the style, inside thirty short pieces none of which requires a large hand or a long attention span. Pedagogical design of that economy explains the set's survival better than reverence does.

The student's path through the wider keyboard works follows the same graded logic the editions established. From the Inventions the road runs to the English and French Suites, where dance movements add character and ornament, then to the Well-Tempered Clavier entire, where fugue becomes the centre and every key must be inhabited, and onward for the strongest players to the Partitas and the Goldberg Variations. Gerber's eighteenth century account of his lessons describes his teacher setting exactly this staircase, inventions first and the great collection as the summit, and examination syllabi two centuries later preserve the same staircase with grade numbers attached. Few curricula in any art have demonstrated that durability, and the durability rests on the fit between the pieces and the developing hand, tested weekly by teachers who keep what works and discard what does not.

The piano itself is an intermediary the honest account names. The Inventions were written for harpsichord and clavichord, instruments whose touch rewards clarity over weight, and their transfer to the piano succeeded because the music's argument lives in the voices rather than in the sonority. Pianists argue about how much pedal, dynamic swell and legato the pieces will bear, and the argument reproduces, in miniature, the whole period performance debate inside the teaching studio. The beginner decision between instruments, treated in the questions at the end of this article, matters less than the voices: a student who can hear two lines as two lines has received what the set was built to give, on any keyboard that answers the fingers.

## Bach in music theory curricula

Theory teaching made the composer into a standard of measurement. The chorales became the laboratory in which harmony students learn voice leading, because the four part settings solve, hundreds of times over, the exact problems a student faces: how to move four voices between two chords without forbidden parallels, how to handle dissonance so it resolves with grace, how to let a bass line walk with purpose. Albert Riemenschneider's collection of 371 chorales, published in 1941, placed a uniform text in American classrooms, and its numbering made the chorales citable in examinations across the English speaking world. Counterpoint teaching drew on the fugues with the same practicality. Kirnberger and Marpurg had built eighteenth century theory partly from these scores, Fux's earlier Gradus ad Parnassum of 1725 supplied the species method the century inherited, and the modern curriculum layers the two: species exercises for discipline, the fugues for art.

University and conservatory courses across the twentieth century set the same analytic tasks: harmonise a chorale melody in the style, complete a fugue exposition, analyse the Goldberg Variations as a system of variations over a bass. The style being imitated in those exercises is a classroom construction, distilled from the chorales and keyboard works into rules the composer himself breaks whenever expression requires, and scholars of pedagogy have noted the gap between the classroom standard and the historical practice. The gap does not cancel the influence. Generations of composers learned their grammar against this standard, including composers who reacted against it, and a reaction against a named standard is a documented relation, as the modernist sections of this article showed from the other direction. A lecturer assembling slides to compare a chorale harmonisation with a student's attempt, voice by voice, can open the files in a browser through the [ReportMedic presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) when presenting the comparison to a class.

Nicholas Kenyon, the writer and editor whose work on the composer addresses general readers and musicians together, has described the educational centrality as a mixed gift: the teaching Bach is real but partial, weighted toward keyboard and chorale, and students can complete advanced training having sung no cantata and heard little of the orchestral music. His observation identifies the curriculum's blind spot from inside a sympathetic career. The theory classroom preserved the grammar and narrowed the composer, and the narrowing fed back into composition, since the fugues and chorales a young composer imitates are the works the classroom selected. Influence through curricula is influence with a syllabus committee as co author, and the committee's choices, Riemenschneider's 371 among them, deserve the same documentary credit as any performer's.

## Where the influence claim is overstated

The test this article has applied throughout now turns against the legacy's own publicity, and several famous claims fail it. The first failure is chronological. Influence cannot run backward, yet popular accounts repeatedly credit the Leipzig composer with shaping musicians whose formative access came too late or never came at all. His own formation shows the traffic running the other way at the start: the young organist transcribed Vivaldi concertos around 1713 to 1714, BWV 593 and its companions, learning Italian ritornello design from the Venetian whose influence on him is documented in his own handwriting. Any account that enrols Vivaldi among the influenced reverses a documented arrow. The second failure is generic admiration mistaken for transmission. Statesmen, novelists and film directors praised the music across two centuries without taking a technique from it, and their praise belongs to reception history, not to the influence ledger this article keeps.

The third failure concerns harmonic mythology. Claims that the composer invented the tempered system, founded modern harmony or single handedly created the conditions for classical music collapse against the record: equal temperament theory circulated before him, the Well-Tempered Clavier demonstrates a circulating unequal temperament for all keys rather than the modern equal system, and Rameau's harmony treatise of 1722 built a parallel theory in France without Leipzig input. What the keyboard collection did was prove, in music of unmatched quality, that a composer could inhabit every key, and proof by example influenced teachers and players profoundly. Precision about the mechanism strengthens rather than shrinks the achievement. The fourth failure is the jazz and popular shorthand that hears any busy counterpoint or walking bass as a direct borrowing. Where the line runs through Loussier, through a school curriculum or through a harmony textbook, the intermediary owns part of the credit, and drawing a straight arrow from 1720 to a recording studio erases the very transmission history that makes the true cases interesting.

Scholarship itself supplies the corrective habit. Wolff's source studies repeatedly replaced romantic transmission legends with dated documents, sometimes enlarging the composer's reach and sometimes cutting a cherished story down to an anecdote without a paper trail. Nicholas Kenyon's writing for general readers performs the same service at the level of reputation, separating the documented revival from the mythology that accumulated around Mendelssohn's evening. The overstated claims share a signature worth memorising: they name no technique, cite no pair of works and offer no date of access. A claim with all three can be argued. A claim with none of the three is publicity, and the reader who applies that filter will find the genuine influence, which is enormous, standing in cleaner relief once the publicity is cleared away.

## Composers who were not influenced by him

The negative cases bound the positive ones, and four major figures show the boundary clearly. George Frideric Handel, born in 1685 in Halle, some eighty miles from Eisenach, in the same year as his contemporary, built a career in Italy and London on Italian opera and English oratorio, and the two men never met despite a near approach in 1719 when the younger visitor missed the traveller by a day. Handel's fugues are formidable and entirely his own, formed by Italian and English practice and by his study of German predecessors he encountered before the Leipzig cantor's mature works existed in any form he could reach. Parallel mastery is not influence, and the Handel case proves that the style's resources were available from shared sources: where two composers drink from the same wells, the historian must not draw an arrow between the cups.

Antonio Vivaldi belongs on the uninfluenced list for the directional reason already given: the documented arrow runs from Venice to Weimar, in the transcriptions of 1713 to 1714, and Vivaldi died in 1741 without any record of studying the younger German's church music, which in any case circulated in a Lutheran professional world sealed off from Venetian practice. Jean Philippe Rameau built French harmony theory from his own acoustical reasoning, publishing the Traite de l'harmonie in 1722, the year of the Well-Tempered Clavier's first book, and the two systems grew in mutual ignorance across a political and linguistic frontier. Hector Berlioz, in the next century, knew the revival repertory and kept his distance: his memoirs and criticism reserve their worship for Gluck, Beethoven and Shakespeare, his own contrapuntal schooling was famously resisted, and his harmony springs from the theatre and the orchestra rather than from the fugue. A great composer can stand adjacent to the legacy, hear it, and take almost nothing, and Berlioz's case disciplines every claim made elsewhere in this article by showing what absence of uptake looks like.

The boundary cases matter methodologically. If the influence is claimed for everyone, it explains no one, and the specific chains documented for Mozart, for Beethoven, for Mendelssohn and for John Lewis gain their force precisely from the contrast with Handel's parallel path and Berlioz's indifference. Tchaikovsky offers the instructive middle position: he revered Mozart, respected the older master's craft, orchestrated and edited with professional knowledge, yet his own melodic and orchestral genius drew its water from other wells, and attempts to enrol him among the influenced dissolve, on inspection, into the general conservatory curriculum he passed through like every student of his century. Curriculum exposure without compositional uptake is the weakest form of the claim, and this article counts it as context rather than influence wherever it appears.

## The reputation as a measure of other composers

By the later nineteenth century the name had become an instrument for measuring other musicians, and the measuring habit is itself a form of influence, exerted on careers rather than on notes. To call a composer a new Bach, as critics called Brahms in some moods and Reger by his own ambition, was to assign a rank and a programme in one gesture. Organists were certified by the great preludes and fugues as pianists were certified by the Well-Tempered Clavier, and a performer's handling of the scores served juries and critics as a diagnostic of seriousness, a use Gould's two Goldberg recordings of 1955 and 1981 turned into the century's most famous diagnostic case, the same work read by the same player at opposite ends of a career. Composers who wished to declare ambition announced it in the older forms: a mass, a set of variations on a ground, a fugue that closes a symphony. The declaration only worked because the audience had learned the standard, through the editions, the teaching and the revival institutions this article has traced.

The measuring habit also distorted judgement, and the distortion deserves its sentence. Critics who treated contrapuntal display as the supreme proof of worth undervalued composers whose genius ran to melody, colour or theatre, and the standard helped marginalise figures the canon later recovered. The broader story of how such standards form, harden and govern taste across the arts belongs to [the series account of how critics shaped reputations](/2026/07/21/how-critics-shaped-reputations/), and the long arc of the classical tradition within which this measuring took place is mapped in [the pillar history of classical composers](/2010/03/07/history-of-classical-composers/), both of which this article draws on without retelling. What belongs here is the mechanism: a reputation becomes a measure when institutions, examinations, editions and criticism converge on it, and the convergence in this case can be dated piece by piece, from Forkel's biography through the Gesellschaft to the examination syllabus.

Max Reger shows the measure at full stretch. His organ works, including the Fantasy on the chorale Wie schon leuchtet der Morgenstern of 1899 and the Introduction, Passacaglia and Fugue, deliberately courted comparison with the Leipzig organ loft, and his chamber music pursues a density of counterpoint that announces its standard in every bar. Admirers took the announcement as proof of rank and detractors as proof of academicism, and the argument about Reger across the twentieth century was conducted substantially as an argument about his chosen measure. A reputation strong enough to organise another composer's ambition, technique and critical fate has passed beyond influence on works. It has become part of the profession's constitution, and constitutions, as the period performance argument showed, generate their own reform movements in time.

## The estate, editions and who controls the text

Control of the text is the quiet question underneath the whole reception, and it has a concrete institutional answer for each period. In the first period the family and the Leipzig institutions controlled it by possession: whoever held the autograph or the parts decided who could copy, and the sons' and pupils' choices, generous and careless by turns, set the boundaries of the possible. In the second period the Gesellschaft controlled it by publication, its forty six volumes defining the works for the world's libraries, its attributions admitting and excluding compositions, its readings standing unchallenged in most rooms for want of an alternative. In the third period the Neue Bach Ausgabe, edited at Gottingen and Leipzig and published by Barenreiter, rebuilt the text from the sources with critical reports that expose every editorial decision to audit, and its authority rests precisely on that exposure: a user can see which reading comes from which manuscript and disagree with documentation in hand.

The catalogue is part of the control. Schmieder's BWV numbering of 1950, revised in 1990, decides what counts as a work and in what order the works stand, and the doubtful and spurious categories at the catalogue's edges, the Anhang listings, govern what performers record and what publishers print. Works have crossed those borders in both directions as evidence accumulated: pieces once cherished under the famous name reassigned to contemporaries or pupils, and pieces long doubted restored by a watermark, a hand or a payment record. The Bach Archive at Leipzig, founded in 1950, and the Gottingen institute hold the source copies and the research apparatus, while the manuscripts themselves rest in libraries from Berlin to Krakow, the Berlin Sing-Akademie collection among them after its return from Kyiv in 2001. No single owner controls the whole estate, because copyright in the music expired centuries ago and the physical sources are scattered by the dispersal this article described, yet the edition and the catalogue together exercise a practical sovereignty that any honest answer must name.

The practical consequence for influence is direct. A composer, student or performer meets the text that the controlling edition supplies, with its readings, its attributions and its ordering, and the meeting shapes the work that follows as surely as a teacher's lesson. Busoni's Bach was the Gesellschaft Bach with his own editorial layer added. The period performers' Bach was the Neue Bach Ausgabe read against the sources. The next generation's text will be a digital one, assembled from the same archives, and the sovereignty question will be asked again in new terms. The estate, in short, never stopped being an active agent in the legacy, long after every heir of 1750 had followed the testator into the archive.

## Tracing one technique from him to a living practice

An influence claim is testable: name the technique, name the two scores, name the date one became available to the other, and most popular claims fail that test. That sentence, the sixth and final rung of the ladder, closes the third loop of this article, and the closing section earns it by running the test on a single technique in full view. Take the four note motif spelled by the composer's name in German notation, B flat, A, C and B natural, a signature the composer himself worked into the final, unfinished fugue of the Art of Fugue, composed in the 1740s and published in 1751, where the signature enters as a countersubject shortly before the score breaks off. The two scores for the first link are the Art of Fugue, in print from 1751 and therefore available to any later musician, and Schumann's Six Fugues on BACH, Op. 60 of 1845, which take the same four notes as their subjects and say so in their title. Access is proven by publication. Technique is named in the notes themselves. The test passes.

The chain continues under the same discipline. Liszt's Prelude and Fugue on B-A-C-H of 1855 stands downstream of Schumann and of the printed Art of Fugue, which Liszt knew as an organist and editor manqué of the repertory, and Busoni's Fantasia after J. S. Bach of 1909 folds the signature into a meditation built from the older chorale fantasia tradition. Anton Webern then built the row of his String Quartet Op. 28 of 1938 from the four note cell and its transformations, a fact documented in his sketches and correspondence, so the signature that signed a fugue in the 1740s became the generative cell of a twelve note quartet two centuries later, through a chain in which every link names its scores and its dates. Living practice keeps the chain warm: organists programme the Liszt and the Schumann as companion sermons on the name, composers set the four notes as a cipher in memorial works, and theory classes use the chain to teach the difference between a motif and a method, since the notes stayed constant while the method around them changed beyond recognition.

Run the same test backward over this article's major claims and the sorting holds. Mozart: technique, imitative counterpoint inside classical forms; scores, the K. 405 arrangements against the Well-Tempered Clavier; access, Vienna, 1782; pass. Beethoven: technique, fugue as structural climax; scores, Op. 106 and Op. 133 against the childhood collection; access, Bonn lessons documented in 1783; pass. John Lewis: technique, fugal procedure in a jazz quartet; scores, Vendome against the printed fugues; access, conservatory training and his own testimony; pass. The Handel claim: no access, no arrow; fail, correctly. A test that passes the true and fails the false, on evidence a reader can inspect, converts influence from a compliment into a finding, and the conversion is the standard this article leaves with its reader.

## Influence by paper rather than fame

The evidence of thirty four sections converges on the thesis stated in the first: the influence travelled by paper, and the paper has keepers, dates and addresses. A copying network of pupils carried the keyboard works through the decades when the public forgot the name, and the sons, praising and discounting their father in the same letters, filtered what the network carried. A baron's Sunday gatherings in Vienna delivered the fugues to a twenty six year old Mozart in 1782 and altered the counterpoint of the Jupiter finale. A Bonn teacher put the great collection under a child's hands in 1783 and received the Grosse Fuge as the distant repayment. Print in 1801 turned a network into a market, Berlin in 1829 turned a professional inheritance into a public possession without creating the inheritance, and the Gesellschaft in 1851 turned scattered paper into a library shelf from which Brahms could lift a passacaglia bass out of an obscure cantata. Each stage names its mechanism, and no stage requires the vocabulary of miracle.

The later centuries extended the same machinery into new media without changing its logic. Arrangements by Gounod, Wilhelmj and Busoni carried selected movements to millions in altered dress. Schoenberg and Webern claimed the counterpoint for modernism and left the claim half proven, which is more than most manifestos achieve. Casals closed the strangest loop of all by performing, complete and in public, a set of suites that print had preserved as studies since 1824, proving that preservation and persuasion are different acts and that both are necessary. Jazz musicians took the procedure and left the notes, electronic musicians took the notes and replaced the sound, teachers took everything and built the curriculum that still decides, in examination rooms on several continents, what a trained musician is expected to know. Through every transfer the intermediaries earned their share of the credit, and this article has named them as carefully as the source.

What remains, beyond the chains and the tests, is the standing invitation the scores extend to any musician willing to do the work. The paper is all in print, the editions sit in libraries and the recordings in catalogues, and the route from a Barcelona shop bin to a concert platform is open to any reader who picks up a worn edition and gives it twelve years. Fame followed that labour in case after case, arriving late, dressing plainly and owing everything to the copies. Influence by paper rather than fame is not a modest claim for this legacy. It is the largest claim the evidence supports, and the evidence, section by section, is the article the reader has just finished.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Why is Bach called the father of Western music?

The title is honorary rather than literal, and it rests on the teaching tradition more than on chronology. Composers before him, from Palestrina to Handel, built the same musical world, so no single father exists. The phrase gained currency because generations of composers learned counterpoint and harmony from his scores, making him the profession's common teacher in a way no contemporary matched. Mozart studied the fugues in Vienna, Beethoven learned the Well-Tempered Clavier as a child, and theory courses continued setting the chorales as models into the twentieth century and beyond. When musicians call him a father, they mean that his works functioned as the textbook from which the tradition taught itself, a role documented in curricula, editions and pupils' testimony rather than a claim about who composed first.

### Q: Who were Bach's most important pupils?

The pupils who mattered most to the transmission were the ones who taught, published or held major posts. Johann Philipp Kirnberger carried the teaching to Berlin, published treatises presenting his master's practice as a system, and instructed Princess Anna Amalia, whose library preserved major sources. Johann Ludwig Krebs held organ posts at Zeitz and Altenburg and wrote organ music steeped in the Leipzig schooling. Heinrich Nicolaus Gerber left a first hand account of his lessons, recording the sequence of Inventions, suites and the Well-Tempered Clavier that later teachers repeated. Johann Christian Kittel taught a generation of Thuringian organists from Erfurt, Gottfried August Homilius carried the church discipline to Dresden, and Johann Gottfried Muthel, among the final pupils, took the keyboard tradition to Riga. His sons Wilhelm Friedemann and Carl Philipp Emanuel stand in a class apart, as heirs as well as pupils.

### Q: How did Bach's music survive without publication?

It survived in manuscript, through a copying economy in which pupils, relatives and paid copyists reproduced scores by hand for use in teaching, church services and private study. Bach published little in his lifetime, so the Well-Tempered Clavier, the organ works and the teaching pieces circulated as handwritten copies passed between musicians who needed them weekly. The Leipzig church works survived as performance parts held by the Thomasschule and the town's churches, stored in institutional cupboards and copied when successors required them. Collectors then gathered the scattered paper: Princess Anna Amalia's Berlin library, the Sing-Akademie archive and Carl Philipp Emanuel's estate each preserved major bodies of material. Survival was therefore social rather than accidental. Every surviving source sat in somebody's working collection, and the map of those collections is the map of who could study the music in each decade after 1750.

### Q: What did Bach's sons think of his music?

They respected the mastery and judged the manner dated, and both positions survive in their own words and actions. Carl Philipp Emanuel, the most famous son during the eighteenth century, kept his father's keyboard works at the centre of serious teaching, supplied Forkel with material for the 1802 biography, and also wrote that the compositions had fallen out of fashion beside newer music. Wilhelm Friedemann, the organist son closest to the paternal style, preserved and taught the keyboard tradition while struggling in a market that rewarded galant ease. Johann Christian, the London son, built his career on the modern manner and transmitted the family inheritance to the young Mozart in a thoroughly updated form. The sons thus acted as preservers and critics at once, keeping the scores in circulation while telling their century, in effect, that the style belonged to the past.

### Q: How did Mozart encounter Bach?

Mozart encountered the scores in Vienna in 1782, at the Sunday musical gatherings of Baron Gottfried van Swieten, the imperial librarian whose Berlin formed taste centred on Bach and Handel. At those morning sessions the older contrapuntal repertory was played and studied by professionals and aristocratic amateurs, and Mozart, newly settled in Vienna and seeking the Baron's patronage, attended, played and began arranging. His hand provides the proof: the five fugues from the second book of the Well-Tempered Clavier arranged for string quartet, K. 405, date from 1782, and further trio arrangements, K. 404a, belong to the same study period. Constanze Mozart later described the sessions as a revelation to her husband. The datable arrangements corroborate her account, and the contrapuntal deepening of the Mass in C minor and the later instrumental works shows the encounter at work in original scores.

### Q: Did Beethoven study Bach?

Yes, and the evidence begins in his childhood rather than in later anecdote. His Bonn teacher Christian Gottlob Neefe set the Well-Tempered Clavier as the foundation of his keyboard training, and Neefe's published notice of 1783 praised the twelve year old for playing the greater part of the collection. In Vienna the connection renewed itself through Baron van Swieten's circle, where the older repertory stood at the centre of serious music making, and Carl Czerny, Beethoven's pupil from 1801, reported a teacher who played the fugues from memory and set the collection before his own students. The scores complete the case. The fugue closing the Hammerklavier Sonata, the Gloria and Credo fugues of the Missa Solemnis and the Grosse Fuge all draw on the contrapuntal fluency that the childhood text installed, making this one of the best documented study lines in music history.

### Q: What happened at the 1829 performance?

On 11 March 1829 Felix Mendelssohn conducted the St Matthew Passion at the Berlin Sing-Akademie, in the work's first public performance of the modern era, before a large audience that treated the evening as a civic event. The choir numbered several hundred, drawn from the Academy's ranks, Eduard Devrient sang Christ and had championed the project, and Mendelssohn cut perhaps a third of the score, reshaping pacing and forces to the concert practice of his own day. Two further performances followed that spring, and demand for scores and parts spread to other German towns. The event's significance needs the distinction this article maintains: professional musicians had studied the composer continuously for decades, so 1829 revived public performance of the great vocal works rather than rescuing a forgotten figure from oblivion.

### Q: What is the Bach Gesellschaft edition?

The Bach Gesellschaft edition is the first collected edition of the composer's works, published at Leipzig by the Bach Society founded in 1850, the centenary of his death, with Robert Schumann, Moritz Hauptmann and Otto Jahn among the founders. The first volume appeared in 1851, and the edition proceeded annually until completion in 1900, reaching forty six volumes covering the cantatas, Passions, Mass in B minor, keyboard, organ and instrumental works. Its importance was practical above all: for the first time any musician with access to a good library could study the whole output in one uniform series, and choral societies across Europe and America built their performances on its pages. Its readings reflected nineteenth century source knowledge and were superseded in detail by the Neue Bach Ausgabe, launched in 1954 and completed in 2007, yet the Gesellschaft volumes remain the foundation on which the modern performing tradition was built.

### Q: How did romantic pianists adapt Bach?

Romantic pianists adapted the music chiefly by transcription, arrangement and programming, and each method changed the object in identifiable ways. Franz Liszt transcribed the great organ preludes and fugues for piano, including BWV 543, converting pedal lines and registration into pianistic texture so concert audiences outside the organ loft could meet the fugues. Ferruccio Busoni expanded the solo violin Chaconne into monumental piano writing and issued an influential edition of the Well-Tempered Clavier from 1894, with fingerings and phrasing that shaped teaching for decades. Clara Schumann programmed fugues in recitals across a sixty year career, presenting them as serious concert music when audiences expected operatic display. The adaptations used the instruments, dynamics and rubato of the romantic piano, so listeners received the architecture of the fugues inside a sound world the composer never knew. Purists objected then and later, yet the transcriptions carried the repertory into halls the originals could not reach.

### Q: Why did modernist composers claim Bach?

Modernist composers claimed him because his counterpoint offered them a usable ancestry at the moment they were accused of severing tradition. Arnold Schoenberg arranged the organ works for modern orchestra, taught from the keyboard scores and argued that the German tradition ran from Leipzig through Mozart, Beethoven and Brahms to his own school, making his method an inheritance rather than a rupture. Anton Webern orchestrated the six part Ricercar from the Musical Offering in 1935, distributing its lines among changing instrumental colours as a demonstration that the older counterpoint already contained his own art in seed. Igor Stravinsky took baroque concerto procedure into neoclassicism and arranged the Vom Himmel hoch variations in 1956. The claims mixed documented study with self serving genealogy in proportions that differ by case, which is why this article tests each claim separately rather than accepting the family tree whole.

### Q: How is Bach used in jazz?

Jazz musicians used the music in three documented ways, at three depths. Jacques Loussier's trio, formed in Paris in 1959, performed the keyboard works with a swinging rhythm section on the Play Bach recordings, keeping the written counterpoint nearly intact while reframing its metre, and the albums sold in the millions. The Swingle Singers, from 1963, sang the instrumental lines as scat vocal arrangements whose precision depended on the voice leading of the originals. Deepest of all, John Lewis of the Modern Jazz Quartet composed original fugal pieces for a jazz quartet, taking the procedure of imitation and balanced voices rather than the notes, and said so in interviews. Other jazz connections run shallower: critics compared many pianists' fluency with the older master without a study trail to support transmission. The documented cases name the musician, the technique and the recording, and those are the cases that count as influence.

### Q: Is period performance more authentic?

The question divides into a factual part and a judgement part, and honest answers keep them separate. The factual part is strong: period performers recovered instruments, tunings, ensemble sizes and articulation practices documented in treatises, payment records and surviving parts, and their performances differ measurably from the romantic tradition that preceded them. The judgement part is contested. Richard Taruskin argued that the movement's lean, brisk manner expresses twentieth century taste projected backward, since no eighteenth century performance can be heard and the evidence leaves hundreds of decisions open. John Butt grants much of that critique while defending period performance as a genuine widening of choice grounded in serious historical enquiry. The settled ground is narrower than either camp's publicity: period performance is better documented in its means, not provably identical in its results, and listeners judge results with their own ears.

### Q: Should a beginner learn Bach on piano or harpsichord?

Either instrument delivers the essential lesson, and the choice should follow access, teaching and the student's aims rather than doctrine. The Inventions and suites were written for harpsichord and clavichord, and a harpsichord teaches the clarity, articulation and dance pulse the music assumes, since its touch rewards precision and punishes blurred voicing. A piano offers dynamic range, universal availability and a direct path into the wider graded examination system, and the music's argument, which lives in the independence of its voices, survives the transfer intact, as three centuries of piano teaching demonstrate. Beginners rarely own the choice in any case, since lessons happen on the instrument the teacher keeps. What matters is hearing two lines as two lines. A student who reaches that hearing has received what the teaching pieces were built to give, on whichever keyboard the lessons provide.

### Q: How do I test whether an influence claim is real?

Apply three questions in order, and let the claim fail if any answer comes back empty. First, name the technique precisely: not brilliance or depth, but a transferable procedure such as fugue construction, invertible counterpoint or a passacaglia ground, stated so a musician could practise it. Second, name the two scores, the source work and the receiving work, and compare them directly, since real transfer leaves fingerprints in voice leading, structure or figuration that comparison can find. Third, name the date of access and the channel that carried it: a lesson, a library, an edition or a performance, dated before the receiving work was written. Mozart at van Swieten's gatherings in 1782 passes all three tests, as the K. 405 arrangements prove access and the Jupiter finale shows uptake. Claims that offer admiration without technique, resemblance without access, or access after the fact fail, and a reader equipped with the three questions can grade any influence story in any programme note.

### Q: How do I trace a theme between two composers?

Begin with the notes and work outward to the documents, in that order, because resemblance is cheap and paper is rare. Write out the theme from each score and compare interval by interval, noting rhythm, metre and harmonic function, since a genuine borrowing usually preserves the contour while adapting the surroundings, and an accidental resemblance dissolves under exact comparison. Then establish the channel: find the edition, copy, lesson or performance that could have carried the theme to the later composer, and date it before the receiving work. The BACH motif offers a model exercise, running from the Art of Fugue of 1751 through Schumann's Op. 60 fugues of 1845 and Liszt's organ prelude of 1855 to Webern's String Quartet Op. 28 of 1938, with publication dates closing every link. Finally, look for acknowledgement in letters, titles or sketches. A composer who names the source in a title has done the historian's work, and silence combined with weak resemblance should end the enquiry.

### Q: Which recordings show Bach's influence most clearly?

Choose recordings that pair a source with its receiver, so the ear can audit the claim directly. Set Glenn Gould's 1955 Goldberg Variations beside Wendy Carlos's Switched-On Bach of 1968 to hear the same counterpoint under two sound worlds, each performance shaped by the other era's technology. Pair a Well-Tempered Clavier recording with Mozart's K. 405 fugue arrangements and the Jupiter Symphony finale, and the 1782 transfer becomes audible as a change in what a classical finale attempts. Casals's complete cello suites of 1936 to 1939, heard against any period instrument cycle recorded after the 1970s, display two eras of the legacy arguing over one text. Jacques Loussier's Play Bach recordings beside a harpsichord performance of the same preludes isolate exactly what a rhythm section changes and what it cannot touch. Influence lives in those comparisons, and comparative listening teaches more than any single classic performance heard alone.

### Q: What should I listen for when comparing Bach and Mozart?

Listen first for what each composer does with a single idea. In a fugue from the Well-Tempered Clavier, one subject generates the entire movement, entering in each voice, turning in sequence and stretto, with every bar accountable to the opening thought. In Mozart's Jupiter finale of 1788, five themes combine in invertible counterpoint at the coda, the older discipline placed at the service of symphonic brilliance, and the passage is the sound of the 1782 Vienna encounter bearing fruit. Then compare the surfaces: the earlier music sustains one Affekt with even intensity, while Mozart phrases in questions and answers, lightens textures and lets opera into the room. Finally compare the bass lines, walking with purpose in both, since Mozart absorbed the older practice of a bass that argues rather than accompanies. The differences define Mozart. The shared grammar defines the influence.

### Q: Which composers were not influenced by Bach?

Four major cases mark the boundary. Handel, born the same year and raised in the same region, formed his style in Italy and England, never met his contemporary, and built his fugues from Italian and English sources and earlier German masters, a parallel path from shared wells rather than a line of influence. Vivaldi stands on the other side of the arrow entirely, since the young Bach transcribed Venetian concertos around 1713 to 1714 and learned from them, making Vivaldi a source rather than a receiver. Rameau published his harmony treatise in 1722 from independent French reasoning, in ignorance of the Leipzig work. Berlioz heard the revival repertory in the next century and took almost nothing, reserving his worship for Gluck and Beethoven and building his harmony from theatre and orchestra. Naming the uninfluenced protects the genuine cases, since a claim that covers everyone explains no one.

### Q: Is Bach's influence overstated?

In its popular form, yes, and in its documented form, no, and the two answers belong together. The popular form credits him with inventing temperament, founding harmony and secretly authoring the achievements of every successor, claims that fail the elementary tests of date, technique and access, and collapse further against counterexamples such as Handel's parallel mastery and Vivaldi's documented influence running into Bach rather than out of him. The documented form is narrower and larger at once: a copying network, a Vienna salon in 1782, a Bonn classroom in 1783, a Berlin concert in 1829, a complete edition in 1851, a Barcelona shop in 1890, each link proven by scores, letters, catalogues or recordings. Measured by proven transfers of technique between named musicians, the influence remains the most extensive in Western music. Measured by the slogans, it shrinks on inspection. The honest verdict keeps the chains and discards the slogans.

### Q: Who owns the authoritative Bach text today?

No single owner holds it, and the practical authority is shared between an edition, a catalogue and the archives that keep the sources. The Neue Bach Ausgabe, the critical complete edition edited at Gottingen and Leipzig and published by Barenreiter, completed its main series in 2007 and supplies the text performers and scholars treat as standard, with critical reports that document every editorial decision from the surviving manuscripts. Wolfgang Schmieder's BWV catalogue, first published in 1950 and revised in 1990, governs what counts as a work and how works are numbered and classed as authentic, doubtful or spurious. The manuscripts themselves rest in libraries across Europe, the Berlin collections chief among them, with the Bach Archive at Leipzig coordinating copies and research. Copyright in the music expired centuries ago, so authority rests on scholarship rather than ownership, and any reader may test the edition against the sources it cites.
