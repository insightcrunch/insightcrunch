The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016 is usually introduced as a recovery law, and that introduction explains most of the confusion that follows. The Code is not principally a machine for getting money back, although money sometimes comes back through it. It is a machine for changing who decides. On the day a corporate insolvency process is admitted, the directors of a defaulting company lose control of it, an outside professional takes charge of its affairs, and its financial creditors, sitting as a committee, decide whether the business continues under a new plan and new ownership or is closed and sold. Everything else in the statute, the moratorium, the timelines, the eligibility bars, the distribution order, exists to serve that single transfer of control and to make it happen quickly enough that the business still has value when the decision is taken.

That design was a deliberate break with everything that came before it. For decades, Indian insolvency law left a failing company's management in possession while proceedings drifted, and distributed the decision among forums that could each block the others. The Code reversed both choices at once. It put creditors in control, it imposed a clock, and it made the tribunal a gatekeeper and referee rather than the decision-maker. The Bankruptcy Law Reforms Committee, whose report is the Code's intellectual foundation, stated the principle plainly in its reasoning: the question whether a business can be saved should be answered by the people with money at stake in the answer, not by a court reconstructing commercial judgment it does not have.

![The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016, showing how admission of a corporate insolvency case transfers control from directors to creditors - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-06.webp)

There is a second fact about the Code that matters almost as much as the first, and it is the fact most general guides leave out. The Code was enacted as a complete insolvency law for companies, firms and individuals, but it has never been switched on in full. The corporate provisions began operating in December 2016, within months of enactment. The provisions for individuals and partnership firms were notified only for one narrow class, personal guarantors to corporate debtors, in December 2019. The Fresh Start Process, the part of the Code written for debtors with the smallest incomes and assets, has never been brought into force at all. A reader who does not know which parts of the statute are live law and which are still dormant text cannot know who can actually use the Code, and that question, who can use it, is where any honest account of the statute has to begin.

## What Indian insolvency law looked like before the Code

The system the Code replaced was not a system at all. It was an accumulation of statutes, each built for a different problem, which between them gave a distressed company several doors to hide behind and gave its creditors no door that led anywhere quickly.

For industrial companies in sickness, the governing regime was the Sick Industrial Companies (Special Provisions) Act, 1985, and its tribunal, the Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction. That regime was debtor-protective by design. A company whose net worth had eroded could register itself as sick, and registration triggered a shield against recovery while the board considered a rehabilitation scheme. Management stayed in place throughout. The premise was that industrial sickness was a misfortune to be nursed; the result, in a large share of cases, was that the reference itself became the asset, a means of keeping creditors at bay while value leaked out of the business. By the time a scheme failed and winding up was recommended, there was frequently little left to wind up.

For secured creditors, principally banks, the parallel route was enforcement outside the courts. The Recovery of Debts Due to Banks and Financial Institutions Act, 1993 created Debt Recovery Tribunals for recovery claims, and the Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act, 2002 allowed secured creditors to enforce security without court intervention. That enforcement route survives alongside the Code and follows its own logic; the working of the [SARFAESI enforcement route and its safeguards](/2013/02/15/sarfaesi-act-2002-explained/) is treated in its own article in this series. What neither route could do was resolve the company as a whole. Each creditor enforced against its own security, and the collective question, whether the business was worth more alive than dead, had no forum competent to answer it for everyone at once.

Winding up under the Companies Act offered a collective process of a kind, but it was a process for ending companies rather than rescuing them, conducted through the High Courts at a pace measured in decades in the worst instances, and directed at dissolution rather than continuation. Individuals, meanwhile, were governed by two colonial statutes, the Presidency Towns Insolvency Act, 1909 for the old presidency towns and the Provincial Insolvency Act, 1920 elsewhere, which were built around the individual trader of another century and were little used for anything else.

The practical consequence was a ranking India kept earning in international comparisons of contract enforcement and insolvency: resolution took years, recovery was a fraction of the claim, and the rational creditor response was to lend against collateral and personal guarantees rather than against the cash flows of the business. Credit priced itself for a world in which default was close to irreversible. The Bankruptcy Law Reforms Committee, constituted by the Ministry of Finance in 2014 and chaired by T. K. Viswanathan, a former Law Secretary, was asked to design a single law for that world, and its report, submitted in November 2015 with a draft Bill attached, supplied the architecture Parliament enacted with modifications the following year.

## The Bankruptcy Law Reforms Committee and the drafting choice

The Committee's report matters to a reader of the Code because the courts keep returning to it. When the Supreme Court upheld the Code's constitutionality in Swiss Ribbons in 2019, it reasoned from the Committee's diagnosis. When the Court explained why financial and operational creditors are treated differently, it cited the Committee's analysis of the difference between the two kinds of debt. The report is therefore not background colour; it is part of the Code's working materials, and its central moves deserve statement.

The first move was unification. One statute would cover corporate persons, partnership firms and individuals, replacing the patchwork described above, so that the law of failure was a single text with a single philosophy rather than a shelf of survivals. The second move was the creditor-in-control model. In the debtor-in-possession systems the Committee examined and rejected for India, existing management runs the company during insolvency under court supervision. The Committee reasoned that where promoters had brought a company to default, leaving them in charge during the rescue attempt rewarded the failure and invited the delays that had destroyed the earlier regime. Control would pass instead to a licensed professional answerable to the creditors, and the creditors would take the commercial decisions. The third move was institutional. The process would be run not by generalist courts but by specialised adjudicating authorities, the National Company Law Tribunal for companies and the Debt Recovery Tribunal for individuals and firms, supervised by a new regulator, and serviced by a new profession of insolvency professionals and a new evidentiary institution, the information utility, which would hold authenticated records of debt and default so that the existence of a default could be proved in days rather than litigated for years.

The fourth move was time. The Committee treated delay as the mechanism by which insolvency value dies, and built the design around strict, short periods: a fixed window for the resolution process, after which liquidation follows as the default outcome rather than as a fresh contest. A reader who keeps those four moves in mind can predict the shape of almost every provision that follows, and can also see where later amendments and judgments have pulled against the design, which is a fair part of the Code's subsequent history.

## The architecture of the Code

The Code as enacted contains 255 sections arranged in five Parts, with schedules that amend a series of other statutes to fit them around the new text. Part I is preliminary: it fixes the extent of the Code, its application to different classes of debtor, and the definitions, in Section 5 for corporate insolvency and Section 3 generally, on which everything else depends. Part II, running from Section 4 to Section 77, is the corporate code in substance: insolvency resolution for corporate debtors, the pre-packaged process inserted later for micro, small and medium enterprises, liquidation, and the fast-moving offences that police the process. Part III, running from Section 78 to Section 187, is the individual and firm code: a Fresh Start Process, an insolvency resolution process, and a bankruptcy process, administered through the Debt Recovery Tribunal. Part IV, from Section 188 to Section 223, creates and empowers the regulator and the regulated professions. Part V, from Section 224 to Section 255, carries the miscellaneous provisions, among them the override in Section 238, the bar on civil court jurisdiction in Section 231, the repeal of the two colonial insolvency statutes in Section 243, and the rule-making powers.

Three institutions created by the Code do the daily work, and a pillar account should name what each is for. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India, established on 1 October 2016 under Part IV, is the regulator: it registers and disciplines insolvency professionals, insolvency professional agencies and information utilities, writes the regulations that fill in the process, and publishes the data through which the Code's working is known. The insolvency professional is the licensed individual who takes control of the debtor during resolution, and the profession is the Code's answer to the question of who runs a company whose board has been displaced. The information utility is the evidentiary utility the Committee imagined: a regulated repository that accepts, authenticates and stores records of debt and default, so that a creditor's application can be tested against a neutral record rather than reconstructed from contested correspondence. The first information utility registered by the Board was the National e-Governance Services Limited.

The adjudicating authorities divide by debtor. For corporate persons the forum is the National Company Law Tribunal, the tribunal created under the Companies Act 2013 whose composition and appellate structure are examined in the article on the [National Company Law Tribunal and its appellate tribunal](/2012/05/01/nclt-nclat-explained/), with appeals going to the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal and then to the Supreme Court on questions of law. For individuals and partnership firms the forum is the Debt Recovery Tribunal, with one important exception produced by the way Part III was commenced: for personal guarantors to corporate debtors, the authority is the National Company Law Tribunal, so that the guarantor's case travels with the company's case before the same bench. That exception is examined below, because it is the only door into Part III that has ever been opened.

## The regulator and the professions the Code created

Part IV deserves more than the summary it usually receives, because the Code is administered by institutions it invented, and their design explains much of its behaviour. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India is a regulator of a particular kind: it does not decide cases, admit applications or approve plans. Its functions under Section 196 are to register and regulate the three service populations the Code depends on, to write the regulations that supply the process detail the statute leaves open, to promote the development of the market for insolvency services, and to publish information and statistics. The Board's regulatory output is the Code's second text: the regulations on the corporate insolvency resolution process, on liquidation, on the professionals themselves and on information utilities are where a practitioner finds the operative deadlines, forms and duties that the bare sections frame but do not fill.

The insolvency professional is the statute's central private actor. A professional must be enrolled with an insolvency professional agency, registered with the Board, and qualified through the examinations and experience conditions the regulations prescribe, and is governed by a code of conduct whose first obligations run to the integrity of the process rather than to the party that proposed the appointment. The design choice embedded here is worth noticing. The professional who controls the debtor is appointed through a process the creditors influence, is paid from the estate or as the committee directs, can be replaced by the committee at the prescribed majority, and is personally exposed to disciplinary proceedings, including cancellation of registration, for failures in a role that combines custody of assets, verification of claims, conduct of meetings, and certification of the plans placed before the committee. The Code loads the professional with fiduciary-flavoured duties while exposing the professional to pressure from every constituency in the case, and the tension between those two facts is one of the system's live structural problems, visible in the case law on professionals' fees, removals and liability.

The information utility completes the triangle. Sections in Part IV provide for regulated utilities that store financial information submitted by creditors and debtors, authenticate it through the process the regulations prescribe, and issue records of default. The evidentiary design is the point: a record of default from an information utility is the statute's preferred proof that a default occurred, converting the admission hearing from a trial of documents into a verification of records. In practice the profession's and the tribunals' reliance on utility records has grown unevenly, and other evidence of default, bank certificates, records of the debt, findings of other forums, continues to be used, but the institutional idea remains distinctive to this Code: an insolvency system that builds its own evidence utility because it does not trust the paper trail litigation produces.

## Regulation as the Code's second text

No reader should leave the pillar believing the Act's 255 sections are the working law by themselves. The Code is a framework statute whose daily operation is supplied by the Board's regulations, and the division of labour is deliberate: Parliament fixed the architecture, the rights and the majorities, and delegated the process engineering. The corporate insolvency regulations prescribe the forms and manner of the public announcement, the proof and verification of claims, the conduct of committee meetings and voting, the information memorandum's contents, the invitation for expressions of interest and the request for resolution plans, the evaluation matrix, and the compliance certificate in which the professional certifies a plan's conformity before the tribunal sees it. The liquidation regulations likewise supply the claims, sale and distribution mechanics, including the structured modes of sale and the consultation committee of stakeholders. The professional regulations govern enrolment, examinations, registration, the code of conduct and disciplinary proceedings. Two consequences follow for the reader. The first is that significant changes in the system's behaviour can arrive by regulation rather than amendment, and have: adjustments to timelines, to the treatment of dissenting creditors' payments, to the disclosure of the successful applicant's commitments and to the conduct expected of committees have repeatedly been made at the regulatory level, faster than Parliament moves. The second is that the Board's discussion papers and the Insolvency Law Committee's reports form a running official commentary, and the tribunals cite them, so the Code is best read as a three-layer text, statute, regulation and jurisprudence, in that order of authority and the reverse order of daily use.

## The definitions that decide cases

Insolvency litigation under the Code is, to a remarkable degree, litigation about definitions, and Section 5 contains the load-bearing ones. A financial debt, under Section 5(8), is a debt disbursed against the consideration for the time value of money, together with the items the clause enumerates: money borrowed against interest, amounts raised under acceptance credit facilities, notes and debentures, finance lease obligations, receivables sold or discounted other than on a non-recourse basis, derivatives at market value, counter-indemnity obligations in respect of guarantees, and liabilities in respect of guarantees for any of these. The phrase about time value of money does the analytical work, and the Supreme Court has returned to it repeatedly, holding in the line of cases following Pioneer Urban that the question is whether the transaction has the commercial effect of borrowing, so that the amounts raised from homebuyers under a real estate project qualified, the developer's obligation to deliver or refund carrying the time value element the clause requires. An operational debt, under Section 5(21), is a claim in respect of the provision of goods or services, including employment, and dues under any law payable to government. The classification matters at every stage: it decides which door a creditor enters by, whether the creditor sits in the committee, where the claim stands in the Section 53 queue, and what minimum the creditor must receive under a plan.

Default, defined in Section 3(12), is non-payment of a debt when it has become due and payable and has not been paid, in whole or in part or in instalment. The simplicity is the design. The Code does not ask whether the debtor is balance-sheet insolvent, whether its liabilities exceed its assets, or whether it is commercially viable; it asks whether a due debt is unpaid, a cash-flow test that a creditor can apply from outside the company without discovery into its books. Much of the Code's speed, and much of the criticism of its trigger-happiness in the early years, is contained in that choice. A corporate person, under Section 3(7), includes a company, a limited liability partnership, and any other person incorporated with limited liability, but excludes a financial service provider, which is why the Code reaches government companies and public sector undertakings in their corporate form while stopping at the boundary of regulated finance. A related party, defined at length in Section 5(24), drives the disqualifications from committee voting and the lengthened look-back periods for avoidance, and its width, reaching directors, key managerial personnel, their relatives and the entities they control, is a recurring battlefield in claims and committee challenges.

## The commencement map: which parts of the Code are law in action

Section 1 of the Code allows the Central Government to bring its provisions into force on different dates, and that ordinary-looking power has produced the statute's most distinctive feature. The Code was assented to on 28 May 2016 as Act 31 of 2016. The regulator was established in October of that year. The corporate insolvency provisions were brought into force from 1 December 2016, and the corporate liquidation provisions followed within the same month, so the transfer-of-control machinery for companies was operating within a little over six months of enactment. The rest of the statute arrived selectively, late, or not at all, and the pattern of what was switched on and what was left dormant is the findable fact about this Act.

The map supports the claim this article names asymmetric commencement. The parts of the Code that move power from debtors to creditors were live within six months. The parts that would have given the smallest debtors a statutory fresh start, and the general machinery for individual insolvency outside the guarantor class, have never been commenced, and the colonial statutes they were to replace, the Presidency Towns Insolvency Act, 1909 and the Provincial Insolvency Act, 1920, continue to govern the individuals the Code has not reached, because the repeal in Section 243 operates with the provisions it belongs to. The order in which a staged statute is switched on is a more reliable guide to its operative priorities than its objects clause, and in this statute the order is legible: corporate resolution first, guarantor liability second, the individual debtor's fresh start not at all.

A practical reader should draw one discipline from the map. Arguments and advice about the Code routinely describe Part III as if it were working law, and examination answers and professional notes repeat the error. It is not working law for a proprietor, a partner, or an individual borrower without a corporate guarantee behind the debt. For those debtors the Code is a text, and the living law remains the older statutes and the ordinary civil process.

## Who can set the corporate process in motion

Part II opens with the threshold. Section 4 applies the Part to corporate insolvency where the minimum amount of default is met. As enacted, that minimum was one lakh rupees, a figure low enough to admit small trade claims, and the proviso allowed the Central Government to raise it by notification to not more than one crore rupees. By notification S.O. 1205(E) of 24 March 2020, issued as the pandemic reached India, the government raised the minimum to the statutory ceiling of one crore rupees, where it stands in the text's operation. The change was a hundredfold, and its effect on the population of cases is direct: defaults below one crore rupees, however real, cannot start a corporate insolvency case at all, which removed the smallest operational creditors from the process and converted the Code into an instrument for substantial defaults only.

Three doors lead into the process, and they are not symmetrical. A financial creditor, a person to whom a financial debt is owed, meaning a debt disbursed against the consideration for the time value of money, applies under Section 7. The tribunal's task at that door is deliberately narrow. It must ascertain the existence of the debt and the default, for which the record of an information utility is the designed evidence, and the application is to be decided within fourteen days. An operational creditor, a person owed for goods, services, employment or a statutory due, must first serve a demand notice under Section 8, and may apply under Section 9 only if the debtor neither pays nor shows, within ten days, a genuine pre-existing dispute about the debt. The third door, Section 10, is the corporate debtor's own application, the company applying against itself, which requires the shareholders' special resolution the section prescribes.

### Is admission automatic once a default is shown?

For a financial creditor's application, in substance yes. In Innoventive Industries v ICICI Bank, decided in 2017, the first substantial Supreme Court judgment on the Code, the Court held that the tribunal's inquiry under Section 7 is confined to whether a default has occurred; if it has, and the application is complete, the tribunal must admit it.

The judgment also held, on a conflict with a Maharashtra statute that shielded the debtor, that the Code overrides inconsistent state law under Section 238.

That narrow gate was disturbed once and then restored. In Vidarbha Industries Power Limited v Axis Bank Limited in 2022, the Court read a discretion into Section 7, suggesting the tribunal might decline admission even where debt and default were established, on the facts of a debtor whose inability to pay turned on a regulatory tariff dispute. In M. Suresh Kumar Reddy v Canara Bank in 2023, the Court confined Vidarbha to its own facts, held that it laid down no view contrary to Innoventive, and stated that once the tribunal is satisfied of a financial debt and a default there is hardly any discretion left to refuse admission. The sequence is the settled position: admission on proof of default is the rule, and settlement negotiations that have not cured the default are not a ground to keep a financial creditor out.

The operational creditor's door is narrower by design, and the difference is doctrinal rather than accidental. In Mobilox Innovations v Kirusa Concepts, the Supreme Court held that the dispute which defeats an operational creditor's application must be genuine and pre-existing, not a spurious defence raised to stall, but also that the tribunal does not decide the dispute; it asks only whether a plausible controversy exists that predates the demand notice. The Bankruptcy Law Reforms Committee had reasoned that operational creditors, often small suppliers, should not be able to use the threat of a collective process as a debt-collection lever for contested invoices, and the demand notice with its dispute filter is that reasoning enacted. The distinction between the two creditor classes, and its constitutional defence, runs through the Code's case law and is taken up again in the judicial treatment below.

## The debtor's own door, and the bars of Section 11

The third initiating route, Section 10, is the corporate debtor's application for its own insolvency, filed with the approval of its shareholders by special resolution, or of three-fourths of its partners where the debtor is a limited liability partnership. The door exists for the honest board that reaches the conclusion the creditors would reach, and its existence matters to the system's moral logic: a board that files early, while value remains, is doing what the Code's discipline is meant to encourage, and the process that follows is identical whichever door admitted it. Section 11 then bars the doors against the system's repeat players and its own outputs. A corporate debtor undergoing a resolution process or a liquidation cannot apply again; a debtor that has completed a resolution process within the preceding twelve months cannot be brought back, a cooling interval against serial filings; a debtor that has violated the terms of an approved plan is barred by the route of a fresh process, its remedy lying in the consequences of contravention; and a debtor against whom a liquidation order has been made is out of the resolution system altogether. The bars are the Code's memory: the process is a single, consequential passage, not a revolving shelter, and they pair with Section 29A to express the statute's suspicion of repetition by the same actors.

## The operational creditor's road, walked carefully

Because the operational creditor's application fails more often on procedure than on merits, the road deserves its own walk-through. The debt must be an operational debt as defined, a claim for goods or services supplied, including employment dues, or a statutory due, and it must be due and unpaid. Before any filing, the creditor must deliver a demand notice in the prescribed form on the corporate debtor, or a copy of an invoice attached to the notice, demanding payment of the amount in default. The debtor then has ten days from receipt to bring to the creditor's notice either payment or the existence of a dispute, or the pendency of a record of dispute before the notice arrived. Only on the expiry of that window without payment or a qualifying dispute may the creditor file under Section 9, and the application must carry the certificate and record the section requires, including confirmation that no notice of dispute has been received or, where one has, the material showing why it does not qualify.

The dispute filter is where applications live and die, and the Mobilox test has three edges worth fixing precisely. The dispute must exist before the demand notice; a dispute manufactured in reply to the notice is the very thing the filter excludes. The dispute need not be likely to succeed; the tribunal does not try it, and a defence that is plausible and not plainly spurious is enough, because the operational creditor's remedy for a genuinely contested debt is a suit or arbitration, not a collective process that displaces the debtor's management. And the dispute may be shown by the record of an arbitration or suit pending before the notice, which the statute treats as notice of dispute in itself. The asymmetry with the financial creditor's road is therefore total: the bank proves a record of default and enters; the supplier must first survive a correspondence test about whether its bill was ever really contested. Commentators divide on whether the filter is the Code's wisdom or its concession to the fear of blackmail by invoice, and the Bankruptcy Law Reforms Committee's reasoning, that operational creditors are typically unable to assess the debtor's overall viability and should not command a process that decides it, is the strongest form of the defence. The strongest form of the criticism is the queue at the end: the same creditor class that faces the hardest entry also stands lowest in the distribution the process can produce, and the two choices compound.

Section 65 completes the gatekeeping by punishing abuse of the doors themselves: a person who initiates a process fraudulently or with malicious intent for a purpose other than resolution, or a corporate debtor whose officers act to defraud creditors in the initiation, faces penalty, and the tribunal's residuary jurisdiction under Section 60(5) to decide questions arising out of the process has been used, among other things, to police collusive and colourable filings. The Code's speed depends on its triggers being honest, and these provisions are the penalty for treating the trigger as a weapon.

## The gatekeeping detail: defects, limitation and the fourteen days

Three procedural provisions decide more applications than the headline doctrines do, and a pillar account should arm the reader with them. The first is the rectification discipline. An application that is incomplete is not dismissed out of hand: under Sections 7, 9 and 10 alike, the tribunal must give the applicant notice to rectify the defect within seven days before rejecting the application, a small mercy that litigation has converted into a standing skirmish over what counts as a defect, an absence of proof of default being a failure of the case rather than a rectifiable defect in the form. The second is the tribunal's own clock: the tribunal is to ascertain the existence of a default from the record within fourteen days of receiving a financial creditor's application, and the Supreme Court in Innoventive treated the scheme's time limits on the tribunal as integral to the design, although practice, with benches carrying thousands of matters, observes them in the breach; the gap between the fourteen days of the text and the months of the queue is the subject of the record article, but the reader of the pillar should know the text promises speed at the gate as well as inside it.

The third is limitation, the gate that closes on old debts. The Code as enacted said nothing about limitation, and the question whether a time-barred debt could trigger a process was answered first by judicial application of the Limitation Act and then by Parliament, which inserted Section 238A in 2018 to apply the Limitation Act to proceedings under the Code. In B. K. Educational Services v Parag Gupta and Associates, decided in 2018, the Supreme Court held that the Limitation Act applied from the Code's commencement, that the period for an insolvency application is the three years of Article 137 of the Limitation Act running from when the right to apply accrued, and that delay beyond it could be condoned for sufficient cause under Section 5 of that Act. The consequence is that the Code is not a device for resurrecting dead claims: a default too old to sue on is, subject to condonation and to the acknowledgment rules of limitation law, too old to resolve on, and the admission hearing regularly turns on the archaeology of the debt, balance confirmations, part payments and written acknowledgments that restart the limitation clock.

## Day one: the moratorium and the fall of the board

Admission is the moment the statute's transfer of control executes. Section 17 vests the management of the corporate debtor's affairs in the interim resolution professional from the insolvency commencement date, and the powers of the board of directors stand suspended, to be exercised by that professional. The directors are not removed from the company's life altogether; they owe duties of cooperation, and the professional can compel the assistance of the personnel of the company, but they no longer decide anything. Section 19 obliges the suspended management to extend all assistance and cooperation the professional requires, and the tribunal can direct compliance. Officers who obstruct face the Code's penalty provisions. A promoter who was, the day before admission, the effective sovereign of the enterprise becomes, the day after, a person the process may call upon for information and may investigate for the transactions that preceded the failure.

### What does the moratorium actually freeze?

Section 14 imposes a moratorium from the commencement date, prohibiting the institution or continuation of suits and proceedings against the corporate debtor, the transfer or encumbrance of its assets, action to foreclose or recover a security interest, including under the SARFAESI legislation, and the recovery of property in the debtor's occupation.

Its purpose is the breathing space the Committee called a calm period, in which the business is valued as a going concern rather than dismembered by the fastest creditor. The freeze has edges a reader should know. It does not protect a guarantor: Section 14(3) excludes a contract of surety given for the debtor, so a creditor may proceed against the guarantor's assets while the company's own case runs, a point the Supreme Court confirmed in the personal guarantor litigation discussed below. It protects supply rather than punishing it: the Code and its regulations treat essential supplies as continuing, and a government licence, permit or concession cannot be suspended or terminated on the ground of insolvency alone while the process runs, so that the debtor is not killed by the withdrawal of the permissions it trades under. And it binds proceedings, not the process itself: criminal prosecution of the company or its officers is not a suit for recovery, and courts have had to work out, case by case, which proceedings the freeze reaches and which it leaves alone.

The professional who takes charge is first appointed as interim resolution professional, ordinarily the person proposed in the application, and the committee of creditors, once formed, decides whether to confirm that person as resolution professional or replace them. The professional's duties under Sections 18, 20, 23 and 25 form a single job description: take custody and control of the assets, preserve the business as a going concern, receive and collate claims, constitute the committee, prepare the information memorandum on which bidders will price the company, run the invitation of resolution plans, and place before the committee the plans that comply with the statute. The professional manages but does not decide the outcome; the statute is careful to separate the conduct of the process, which belongs to the professional, from the commercial decision, which belongs to the creditors.

## The committee of creditors and the politics of the vote

The committee of creditors is constituted under Section 21 of the financial creditors of the debtor. Where a class of creditors is too numerous to deliberate, the statute provides for representation: allottees under a real estate project, the homebuyers whose position is examined below, vote through an authorised representative, as do debenture holders and other classes the section names. A financial creditor that is a related party of the corporate debtor has no right of representation, participation or voting in the committee, a disqualification that prevents the promoter's allied lenders from capturing the body that displaced him.

Voting is by share of debt. Ordinary decisions of the committee require a majority of the voting share, and the statute lists a class of consequential decisions, set out in Section 28, that require sixty-six per cent: raising interim finance beyond the permitted measure, creating security over the debtor's assets, changing the capital structure, recording a change in ownership, amending the constitutional documents, and others of that order. Approval of a resolution plan sits at the same sixty-six per cent level under Section 30(4). The figures are not decoration. A plan that cannot assemble two-thirds of the financial debt by value fails, however elegant, and a single large lender with more than a third of the debt holds a blocking position, which is why the committee's internal alignments, bank against bondholder, secured against unsecured, decide cases as surely as the merits do.

### How binding is the committee's commercial wisdom?

The courts have made it close to absolute. In K. Sashidhar v Indian Overseas Bank in 2019, the Supreme Court held that the legislature has not endowed the tribunal or the appellate tribunal with jurisdiction to reverse the commercial decision of the committee, whether the decision is to approve a plan or to reject every plan and let liquidation follow.

In the Essar Steel judgment later that year, the Court applied the same principle to distribution: the committee may approve a plan that distributes differently among creditors of different classes, and neither tribunal may substitute an equal-treatment formula of its own, provided the statutory minimums in Section 30(2) are met. The reasoning is the Committee's founding premise returned as doctrine. The tribunal polices legality, feasibility in the narrow statutory sense, and the floor payments the Code guarantees; the bargain itself belongs to the creditors, because it is their money that the bargain disposes of.

Withdrawal is the one door out of an admitted process, and Section 12A keeps it nearly shut. An application admitted under Sections 7, 9 or 10 may be withdrawn only on the applicant's application with the approval of ninety per cent of the committee's voting share, a threshold deliberately set above the plan threshold so that a settlement between the debtor and one creditor cannot quietly undo a collective process the others have joined. Before the committee is formed, the tribunal may allow withdrawal on the applicant's own application; after it, the ninety per cent rule governs, and many a negotiated settlement has failed on exactly that arithmetic.

## The committee at work, and the claims discipline beneath it

The committee is usually described by its voting numbers, but its deliberative life determines outcomes the numbers only ratify. The resolution professional convenes and chairs its meetings, issues notice with the agenda and the materials, and holds no vote, a separation of chairmanship from interest that keeps the professional an officer of the process rather than a participant. Financial creditors who cannot attend vote electronically on the minutes' circulation in the manner the regulations provide, and the committee's business extends well beyond the plan: it approves the process costs and the professional's fee, sanctions interim finance and the other reserved actions of Section 28, appoints the valuers' outputs' custodians in the professional, and may at any stage, by the sixty-six per cent majority, resolve that the debtor should go into liquidation and ask the tribunal to order it under Section 33, a power committees exercise where the estate is hopeless and the process costs of hope exceed its value. The committee, in short, is a legislature for one company, with the professional as its secretariat and the tribunal as its constitutional court.

Beneath the committee sits the claims discipline, the least visible and most unforgiving part of the process. On the public announcement, creditors submit claims with proof within the period the announcement and regulations prescribe; the professional verifies each claim against the debtor's records and the creditor's documents, admits or rejects in the collated list, and maintains the list of creditors as the case's register of who is owed what. Collation is provisional in the sense that claims may be updated as documents arrive, and tribunals entertained late claims with indulgence in the early years, but finality has hardened with the doctrine: a creditor that sleeps through the announcement, the collation, and the committee's approval of a plan cannot expect the plan to reopen for its benefit, and the Supreme Court has refused to disturb an approved plan for a claim surfaced after the committee had approved the plan, because a process whose register never closes can never conclude. The discipline is the mirror image of Ghanshyam Mishra: the Code extinguishes unfiled claims at approval, and in exchange it demands that creditors treat the announcement as the summons it is.

## Why the financial creditors hold the pen

The committee's exclusivity is the Code's most debated design decision, and the pillar should state the argument at the strength its makers made it, because the criticism is unintelligible without it. The Bankruptcy Law Reforms Committee reasoned that the decision whether a company can be rescued, and on what terms, is at bottom a judgment about the viability of the business against the value of its assets, and that financial creditors, banks and bondholders whose business is the pricing of credit risk and who hold the information and the staff to assess viability, are the constituency equipped and incentivised to make it. Operational creditors, by contrast, are numerous, heterogeneous, frequently small, and interested in their own invoice rather than the enterprise, so that a committee of all creditors would be a meeting that could not decide; the operational side is protected instead by the tribunal's admission filter, by the plan floor, and by its place in the liquidation queue. The argument has a second, harder edge: he who will bear the loss of a wrong rescue should choose the rescue, and in the capital structure the financial creditors' exposure dwarfs the trade debt in the cases the Code was built for. The criticism answers on both points. Viability judgments made by lenders proved, in the early mega cases, to be judgments about their own recovery dressed in the language of the enterprise, with operational creditors and the public exchequer absorbing losses the committee's arithmetic allocated to them; and a design that silences the constituency with the most granular knowledge of the debtor's trade, its suppliers, is not obviously the design that maximises information. The Supreme Court sustained the architecture in Swiss Ribbons as a legitimate legislative classification, which settles the law and leaves the policy argument exactly where the Committee and its critics left it, available to every reader of the cases.

## The people inside the process: workmen, employees and the dues that follow them

The position of those who work in the debtor is the least examined part of the Code and the part where its categories cut hardest, so a pillar account should state it without varnish. Wages and salary dues are operational debt, which means the workforce enters the process as operational creditors: they may file claims, they are paid through the plan at not less than the Section 30(2) floor, and in liquidation their recent dues take the protected rungs of Section 53, twenty-four months for workmen ranking with the relinquishing secured creditor and twelve months for other employees below that rung. Where the workforce is numerous, the statute and regulations provide for representation through an authorised representative in the committee's deliberations affecting their class interests in plan voting mechanics where they qualify as a class of creditors in the prescribed circumstances, although employees as operational creditors do not sit in the committee of financial creditors that decides the plan. Sums held for workmen in provident, pension and gratuity funds are not assets of the estate at all: Section 36(4) excludes them from the liquidation estate, so the fund money is not the liquidator's to distribute, a protection that depends in practice on the employer having actually paid the contributions into the funds, which is precisely what a failing employer often has not done. The gap between the fund as a legal category and the fund as money actually held is where the workforce's real exposure sits, and no provision of the Code closes it; the moratorium equally does not pay wages, and whether a resolved company trades and pays its staff during the process is a question of interim finance and going-concern cash, not of entitlement. The honest summary is that the Code protects the workforce's past dues in the queue more carefully than it protects their jobs, since a plan may restructure operations and a liquidation ends employment altogether, and the statute contains no mechanism by which continuity of employment can be made a condition of rescue.

## Two special populations written into the corporate process

Two classes forced amendments because the original design fitted them badly, and both amendments are part of the statute a reader must know. The first is the promoter of a micro, small or medium enterprise. Section 29A as enacted would have barred many MSME promoters from bidding for their own companies on the strength of the non-performing asset clause and the guarantee clause alone, and since the MSME promoter is frequently the only realistic resolution applicant for an enterprise of that size, Parliament inserted Section 240A in 2018, which disapplies clauses (c) and (h) of Section 29A in the case of resolution applicants for MSMEs, subject to the applicant not being a wilful defaulter or otherwise disqualified. The exception is a legislative admission that the eligibility bar's logic, designed for large defaulting groups, misfires at the small end of the market, and it anticipates the debtor-in-possession philosophy the pre-packaged chapter later generalised for the same population.

The second is the homebuyer class, whose entry has already been recorded in the judicial discussion. The machinery deserves its detail. Allottees vote in the committee not individually but through an authorised representative appointed under Section 21(6A), and under Section 25A the representative votes on the class's behalf in accordance with the decision of more than fifty per cent of the voting share of the class present and voting, so the class speaks inside the committee with a single aggregated voice weighted by its debt. The 2020 amendment's threshold for an allottee application, not fewer than one hundred allottees or ten per cent of the total number under the same real estate project, whichever is less, was Parliament's answer to the fear of the single disgruntled purchaser deploying a collective process against a project, and it converted the homebuyer's door from an individual remedy into a class action in substance. Project finance reality added a further adaptation outside the text: tribunals confronted with developers whose distress was confined to one project among several entertained project-specific resolutions, an experiment the appellate tribunal approved in the Winter Hills line of cases under the description of reverse insolvency, in which the process is run for the project rather than the whole company so that completed projects are not dragged into a group collapse. The Code's text knows nothing of the device, and its career illustrates how tribunal practice fills the spaces a company-by-company statute leaves.

## The resolution plan: eligibility, approval and the clean slate

A resolution plan is a proposal to resolve the debtor as a going concern: it may restructure debt, infuse capital, merge or sell the business or its parts, change management, or combine these, and it is prepared on the basis of the information memorandum the resolution professional circulates to invited applicants. Two provisions decide whose plan can win and what a winning plan does.

The first is Section 29A, inserted by an ordinance of November 2017 and carried into the Act by the Amendment Act of 2018 with effect from that ordinance date, which lists the persons ineligible to submit a plan. The disqualified include an undischarged insolvent, a wilful defaulter, a person whose account, or the account of a company they control, has been classified as a non-performing asset for the prescribed period and remains unpaid, a person convicted of specified offences, a person disqualified as a director, a person barred by the securities regulator, a person against whom orders have been made in respect of preferential, undervalued, extortionate or fraudulent transactions, a person who has given a guarantee for a debtor that went through resolution unpaid, and persons connected to any of these. The section's target was the pattern the early cases exposed, of defaulting promoters proposing to buy back their own companies at a discount through the process their default had triggered. Its breadth is its controversy, taken up in the assessment below: the bar reaches not only the promoter but a wide circle of connected persons, and it applies to bidders with no responsibility for the default whose only taint is a transaction or a status the section lists.

The second is the approval machinery. The resolution professional examines each plan against Section 30(2), which requires, in substance, that the plan provide for payment of the process costs in priority, that operational creditors receive not less than the amount the amended text fixes by reference to what they would receive in liquidation under Section 53 or on a distribution following the same order, that dissenting financial creditors receive not less than their liquidation entitlement, that the management of the debtor after approval be provided for, that the plan not contravene any law in force, and that it meet the requirements the Board's regulations specify, including feasibility and viability as the committee assesses them. The committee then votes at sixty-six per cent. The tribunal under Section 31, if satisfied that the approved plan meets the statutory requirements, approves it, and approval changes the plan's legal nature: by Section 31(1) it binds the corporate debtor, its employees, its members, its creditors, expressly including the Central Government, any State Government and any local authority to whom dues are owed, its guarantors, and other stakeholders. A plan is not a contract that binds its signatories. It is an order-backed settlement that binds the world the statute lists, including creditors who voted against it and creditors who never filed a claim at all.

That last consequence produced the Code's most commercially important judgment. In Ghanshyam Mishra and Sons Private Limited v Edelweiss Asset Reconstruction Company Limited in 2021, the Supreme Court held that once a plan is approved, all claims that are not part of the plan stand extinguished, and no person is entitled to initiate or continue any proceeding in respect of a claim the plan did not provide for. The holding, called the clean slate principle in argument, is what makes a resolved company purchasable: the successful applicant takes the business free of the undisclosed and unfiled liabilities of the old regime, including tax and statutory demands that surface after approval. Its converse is equally sharp, and the Court accepted it: a creditor who sleeps through the process, or a department that files late, loses the claim against the resolved company, which is why the public announcement of a process and the discipline of filing claims carry the weight they do. Section 32A completes the design by providing that, where an approved plan produces a change in control to persons unconnected with the old management, the corporate debtor's liability for offences committed before the process began ceases, and its property is protected from attachment for those offences, subject to the section's conditions, so that the new owner is not buying the old management's prosecutions.

### Does approval of a plan release the personal guarantor?

No. The plan binds the guarantor as a stakeholder, but binding is not discharge. The Supreme Court held in Lalit Kumar Jain v Union of India in 2021 that the approval of a resolution plan does not itself extinguish the guarantor's liability.

That judgment also upheld the notification bringing personal guarantors into Part III. The guarantee is an independent contract, and the creditor's rights under it survive the company's resolution unless the plan's terms, accepted by the creditor, provide otherwise. The practical consequence shapes lending practice: the company is resolved under the plan, and the promoter who guaranteed its debts remains exposed personally for the shortfall, pursued under the very Part III machinery whose commencement the same judgment sustained.

## The process in sequence

A pillar account should leave the reader able to narrate a case from filing to order without a gap, because most misunderstandings of the Code are sequence errors. An application under Section 7 or Section 9 or Section 10 is filed before the tribunal bench for the place of the debtor's registered office, with the record of default and the name of the professional proposed as interim resolution professional. On admission, the tribunal declares the moratorium, makes a public announcement inviting claims, and appoints the interim professional, and that date, the insolvency commencement date, starts every clock in the case.

The interim professional takes custody of the assets and records, constitutes the committee of creditors from the claims received, and manages the debtor as a going concern under the committee's eye. The committee in its first meeting appoints the resolution professional, who may be the same person or another, and the professional then prepares the information memorandum, the confidential dossier of the debtor's assets, liabilities, guarantees, transactions and litigation from which bidders must price a rescue. The professional issues an invitation for expression of interest, applies the eligibility criteria the committee approves under Section 25(2)(h) and the shadow of Section 29A, issues the request for resolution plans with the evaluation matrix the committee has settled, and receives the plans. Compliant plans go to the committee, which may negotiate, and which votes at sixty-six per cent of voting share. The successful plan goes to the tribunal under Section 31, which examines statutory compliance and approves or rejects; it does not rewrite. On approval, the plan is implemented under its own terms and monitoring arrangements, the moratorium ends, and the company exits the process under new ownership or restructured debt. On failure at any of the gates described in the liquidation discussion that follows, the estate passes to liquidation instead.

Between admission and approval, the estate is kept alive by interim finance, money raised during the process to fund its costs and the debtor's working needs, which the resolution professional may raise and which ranks within the process costs that Section 53 pays first and Section 30(2) protects in a plan. Because interim finance is repaid ahead of the old creditors, it is the one new money a lender can safely advance to a company in resolution, and its availability, or its absence where lenders judge the estate hopeless, frequently decides whether a business trades through its process or shuts its gates during it. The committee's approval is required to raise interim finance beyond the measure the statute permits the professional, and the creation of security for such finance is among the Section 28 decisions reserved to the sixty-six per cent majority, a distribution of power that keeps the creditors who will bear the cost in control of the borrowing that primes them.

The avoidance provisions, introduced in the plan discussion, operate in this sequence rather than beside it. The professional forms opinions on whether the debtor entered preferential, undervalued, extortionate or fraudulent transactions in the look-back windows the sections prescribe, two years for related parties and one year for others in the case of preferences and undervalued transactions, and files applications for the tribunal's orders while the process runs. A preference under Section 43 is a transfer that puts a creditor in a better position than the Section 53 queue would give it, made in the twilight before commencement; an undervalued transaction under Section 45 includes gifts and transactions for significantly less than the value provided by the debtor; Section 66 reaches trading carried on with intent to defraud creditors and, unusually, wrongful trading by directors who failed to exercise due diligence once insolvent liquidation looked unavoidable, a provision aimed squarely at the suspended board's conduct in the run-up to the case. The recoveries feed the estate or shape the plan economics, and the applications are also the process's accountability mechanism for the old regime.

## The information memorandum and the discipline of diligence

Between the committee's constitution and the invitation of plans stands the document on which the market for the debtor's rescue is made, the information memorandum. Section 29 obliges the resolution professional to prepare it from the debtor's records, the claims collated and the professional's enquiries, containing the assets and liabilities, the financial statements, the guarantees, the litigation, the shareholding and the other prescribed particulars from which an applicant can price a plan. Access to it is a regulated privilege: a prospective resolution applicant receives it on furnishing the undertakings the section and regulations require, of eligibility under Section 29A, of confidentiality, and against use of the information to the debtor's detriment, because the memorandum exposes the company's commercial interior at its weakest moment. The design reflects a judgment about why rescues fail in opaque systems. Under the older regimes, an outsider could not learn enough about a sick company to bid rationally for it, so value defaulted to insiders and asset sales; the Code answers by manufacturing the information asset itself, standardised, verified by the professional and traded under confidentiality, so that the committee's choice among plans is a choice among informed offers. Diligence remains the bidder's burden, and applicants price the risk that the memorandum is incomplete into their offers, one reason bids for debtors whose records are chaotic arrive discounted; but the direction of the design, from possession of information by the defaulting management to its controlled publication by a fiduciary, is one of the Code's quiet revolutions, and it explains why the professional's own standard of care in preparing the memorandum has become a contested question in the cases where plans failed on facts the memorandum misstated.

## The valuation apparatus beneath the plan

The floors and negotiations of the corporate process run on numbers produced by a valuation machinery the statute creates and the regulations operate, and the machinery deserves a paragraph of its own because its outputs are both decisive and deliberately hidden. The resolution professional appoints registered valuers to estimate the fair value and the liquidation value of the corporate debtor. Fair value is the estimated realisable value of the assets on a going concern premise if exchanged on the insolvency commencement date between willing parties; liquidation value is the estimated realisable value if the debtor were liquidated on that date. Where the two valuers' estimates differ significantly, a third valuer may be appointed, and the operative figures are averaged in the manner the regulations prescribe. Liquidation value does the legal work: it is the measure of the Section 30(2) floor for operational creditors and dissenting financial creditors, because it states what those creditors would receive in the liquidation the plan displaces. Yet the liquidation value is kept confidential during the process and shared only on the conditions the regulations impose, a counterintuitive design with a clear purpose, to prevent the floor from becoming the ceiling in negotiation, so that applicants bid against each other for the committee's favour rather than anchoring their offers to the number the statute guarantees the creditors anyway. The tension is structural and acknowledged: creditors must approve a plan whose statutory minimum depends on a figure they have not seen, trusting the professional's certification and the tribunal's review, and challenges to valuations have accordingly become a standard skirmish in approval litigation even though the courts have refused to convert them into a re-pricing of the committee's bargain.

## What a resolution plan may contain

Section 5(26) defines a resolution plan as a plan proposed by a resolution applicant for the insolvency resolution of the corporate debtor as a going concern in accordance with Part II, and the breadth of that spare definition is filled by practice. A plan is a financial and constitutional reconstruction in one document. On the financial side it states the amount payable to each class, the source of the money, whether upfront, deferred or contingent, the treatment of the process costs and interim finance in priority, the capital infusion the applicant commits, and the conversion of debt into equity or other instruments where creditors are asked to continue as owners. On the constitutional side it provides for the management and supervision of the debtor during implementation, changes to the board and shareholding, merger, demerger or sale of undertakings where the reconstruction requires them, and amendments to the memorandum and articles that the plan itself effects on approval, without a separate company law proceeding for each step. The committee negotiates against the evaluation matrix issued with the request for plans, which scores the competing offers on the criteria the committee has chosen, upfront value, net present value, security offered, the applicant's track record and creditworthiness among them, and the professional certifies the chosen plan's compliance with Section 30(2) before it goes to the tribunal.

Two features of the plan device repay attention because they differ from a scheme of arrangement under company law, the nearest older analogue. A plan binds dissentients within its statutory classes by the committee's supermajority plus the tribunal's approval, and it binds non-participants, the unfiled creditor and the government among them, by Section 31 as construed in Ghanshyam Mishra; a scheme, by contrast, works through separate class meetings under court supervision and a different fairness tradition. And a plan is decided by the financial creditors alone in committee, with operational creditors protected by a floor rather than a vote, a distribution of voice that company law never attempted. The plan is therefore best understood as a statutory composition with cram-down, closer to a chapter of a bankruptcy code than to a contract, which is why arguments that treat it as a bargain among consenting parties consistently mislead.

Implementation is the plan's unlitigated half. An approved plan operates through its own monitoring arrangements, frequently a committee or a professional charged with supervision, and contravention of an approved plan is itself a ground on which an affected person may seek the debtor's liquidation under Section 33, so a plan that its new owners abandon converts back into the terminal process. The tribunal's approval, it should be added, is not a rubber stamp of legality alone: the tribunal must be satisfied the plan has provisions for effective implementation, a requirement the regulations elaborate, although the merits judgment remains the committee's under the Sashidhar and Essar discipline described earlier.

## The avoidance provisions and their defences

The avoidance jurisdiction has been invoked often enough in outline that its internal structure should be fixed before the clock section closes the process account. A preferential transaction under Section 43 requires a transfer of the debtor's property or interest for the benefit of a creditor, surety or guarantor on account of an antecedent debt, which puts that beneficiary in a better position than the Section 53 distribution would. Section 43 then disarms two categories that would otherwise condemn ordinary commerce: a transfer made in the ordinary course of the business or financial affairs of the debtor or the transferee, and a transfer creating a security interest whose property value is proportionate to the new value given, the new value defence that protects fresh lending in the twilight period. An undervalued transaction under Section 45 covers gifts and transactions in which the consideration received is significantly less than the value of the consideration provided by the debtor, judged at the time, and Section 46 fixes the relevant look-back, two years for related parties and one year for others, which Sections 43 and 49 share in their respective forms. Extortionate credit transactions, Section 50, reaches credit taken on exorbitant terms within two years. The applications are decided by the tribunal on the professional's report, and the orders can restore position, vest property back in the debtor, or require payment of sums, which is why the avoidance docket is where the old management's twilight conduct is actually adjudicated, usually years after the plan or liquidation that the same creditors voted for.

## The clock, and what happens when it runs out

Section 12 fixes the process period. The resolution process is to be completed within 180 days of the insolvency commencement date. The committee may, by the prescribed majority, direct the resolution professional to seek one extension, which the tribunal may grant for a period not exceeding ninety days, and which cannot be granted more than once. The Amendment Act of 2019 then superimposed an outer limit: the process, including any extension and including the time taken in legal proceedings, was to be completed within 330 days, and the amending text said mandatorily.

The Supreme Court in the Essar Steel judgment, delivered in November 2019 in Committee of Creditors of Essar Steel India Limited v Satish Kumar Gupta, struck the word mandatorily from the provision as manifestly arbitrary, while leaving the 330 day limit standing as the ordinary rule. The effect, stated carefully because summaries often get it wrong, is that the process must ordinarily be completed within 330 days, but the tribunal may extend beyond it in an exceptional case where a short period remains for completion, where putting the debtor back on its feet serves all stakeholders better than liquidation, and where the delay is largely attributable to the tribunal process itself rather than to the litigants. The 330 day figure is therefore a real deadline with a narrow judicial safety valve, not an absolute wall and not an aspiration.

If the clock runs out without an approved plan, Section 33 supplies the consequence, and it is deliberately automatic in structure. The tribunal shall order liquidation where no plan is received before the expiry of the process period, where an approved-intent plan is rejected for non-compliance, where the committee, by sixty-six per cent of voting share, decides before approval of a plan to liquidate the debtor and communicates that decision, or where the debtor contravenes an approved plan and an affected person applies. Liquidation converts the company into an estate. The resolution professional ordinarily continues as liquidator, the assets are collected and sold, by private sale or auction, and, where the regulations permit, as a going concern, and the proceeds are distributed by the liquidator under Section 53.

The distribution order in Section 53 is the Code's social philosophy written as a queue, and it repays reading in prose. First, the insolvency resolution process costs and the liquidation costs are paid in full; the process feeds itself before anyone else. Next, ranking equally, come workmen's dues for the twenty-four months preceding the liquidation commencement date and the debts of a secured creditor who has relinquished security to the estate under Section 52, the section that lets a secured creditor instead stand outside and enforce its security itself. Third come wages and unpaid dues of employees other than workmen for the preceding twelve months. Fourth, financial debts owed to unsecured creditors. Fifth, ranking equally, come amounts due to the Central and State Governments for the two years preceding the liquidation commencement date and the unpaid balance of a secured creditor who enforced its own security and did not recover in full. Sixth, any remaining debts and dues. Seventh, preference shareholders. Eighth and last, equity shareholders and partners. Two features of the queue are regularly misunderstood. Government dues are not a first charge under the Code; they sit fifth, for a two year window, which displaced older tax statutes' priority claims and has been litigated accordingly. And operational creditors as a class are given no named rung of their own: an unpaid supplier's claim, if it is not a financial debt, falls into the remaining debts at the sixth level, below unsecured financial creditors, which is the textual basis of the trade creditor's complaint examined in the assessment below.

## Who pays for the process

A collective process consumes money before it distributes any, and the Code's answer to the costs question is part of its fairness design. The insolvency resolution process costs, the professional's fee and expenses, the valuers, the costs of running the debtor as a going concern and of preserving its assets, are quantified through the process, approved in the resolution context by the committee, and paid in priority: Section 30(2) requires a plan to provide for payment of process costs in priority to other debts, and Section 53 puts the insolvency resolution process costs and the liquidation costs at the head of the waterfall, ahead of the secured creditor and the workmen. Interim finance raised to keep the debtor alive is repaid within that priority, which is what makes it lendable. The applicant that files bears its own initial costs and, for operational creditors, the discipline of the demand notice regime; a debtor pushed into a process that fails does not refund the creditors who triggered it, their recovery being whatever the estate produces after the costs are fed. The order of payment is a value judgment with distributive consequences the reader should see plainly: the machinery is paid first because without the machinery there is nothing to divide, and every rupee of process cost in a small estate is a rupee taken from the queue, which is one reason the small case economics of the Code are contested and the pre-pack's lighter procedure was built. The professional's fee illustrates the tension in miniature. The fee is negotiated within the process, approved by the committee that the professional serves, and scrutinised against the value the professional's work realises; set it too low and the ablest professionals decline difficult estates, set it by the estate's size and the costs consume the recovery the process exists to produce. The Board's regulations and fee circulars have circled the problem without abolishing it, because the problem is the economics of fiduciary work in failed companies and not an accident of drafting.

## Liquidation in depth, and the voluntary route

Liquidation under the Code is not the winding up of the old Companies Act in new dress, and the differences are structural. The liquidator, appointed under Section 34, forms a liquidation estate under Section 36 from which certain assets are expressly excluded, among them assets owned by third parties in the debtor's possession, bailment and trust assets, and the sums due to workmen from provident, pension and gratuity funds, which are not the company's to distribute. The liquidator verifies claims, may constitute a stakeholders' consultation committee under the regulations, and sells the assets as the regulations permit, including as a going concern or in parcels, since the object remains value rather than ceremony. A secured creditor faces the election Section 52 frames: relinquish the security to the estate and take the high ranked position in Section 53, or stand outside, realise the security itself, prove for the shortfall at the fifth level, and account for any surplus. Dissolution follows under Section 54 when the assets are completely liquidated, and with dissolution the company ceases to exist, ending a process designed to conclude in months the work the High Courts' winding up measured in decades, even if practice frequently falls short of the design.

Alongside compulsory failure sits Section 59, voluntary liquidation, available to a solvent corporate person that has not committed a default and whose affairs can support a declaration of solvency by a majority of its directors, verified by auditors, approved by creditors representing two-thirds in value of the debt where the company owes debts, and conducted by a liquidator for the orderly return of surplus to members. Its inclusion matters to a reading of the Code's scheme: the statute is the exit code for companies generally, not only a creditors' remedy, and the same tribunal and professional infrastructure serves the solvent close-down and the insolvent collapse.

## The dormant half: individuals, fresh start and the guarantor exception

Part III was drafted as a complete code for individuals and partnership firms, with three procedures arranged by the size of the problem. The Fresh Start Process in Chapter II, Sections 80 to 93, is for the smallest debtors: a debtor whose gross annual income does not exceed sixty thousand rupees, whose assets do not exceed twenty thousand rupees in aggregate value, whose qualifying debts do not exceed thirty-five thousand rupees, and who meets the section's other conditions, including not owning a dwelling unit, may apply for an order discharging the qualifying debts after a moratorium and a professional's examination. The figures are those of the enacted text, and they describe a debtor population of the genuinely poor. The insolvency resolution process in Chapter III, Sections 94 to 120, is the general negotiation track, producing a repayment plan approved by creditors and sanctioned by the tribunal. The bankruptcy process in Chapter IV, Sections 121 to 178, is the terminal track, vesting the bankrupt's estate in a trustee for distribution, with discharge ordinarily after a year.

None of this operates for the general individual. The notification of 15 November 2019, S.O. 4126(E), brought Sections 94 to 187 and the surrounding machinery into force from 1 December 2019 only in so far as they relate to personal guarantors to corporate debtors, and expressly carved the Fresh Start Process out of even that limited commencement. The Fresh Start Process has never been notified for anyone. The consequence is a two-track personal insolvency law in fact: the promoter who guaranteed company debt faces the Code's modern machinery before the company law tribunal, under rules and regulations made for guarantors in 2019, while the individual borrower, the trader and the partner face the Presidency Towns Insolvency Act, 1909 or the Provincial Insolvency Act, 1920, statutes whose procedures the Bankruptcy Law Reforms Committee examined and found wanting. In Lalit Kumar Jain, the Supreme Court upheld the selective notification against the charge that the executive could not commence a provision for one class of debtor alone, holding that the commencement power in Section 1(3) permits a phased and class-specific bringing into force and that the classification of personal guarantors to corporate debtors was not arbitrary. The judgment is the legal foundation of the asymmetric map set out earlier in this article: selective commencement is not an oversight awaiting correction but a constitutionally sustained choice, which is a stronger and more uncomfortable fact.

Two further dormant provisions deserve record. Sections 234 and 235, the Code's cross-border provisions, empowering the Central Government to enter reciprocal agreements with other countries and providing a letter-of-request mechanism to foreign courts, have never been brought into force, so the Code contains no working cross-border insolvency regime, and the Insolvency Law Committee's later recommendation that India adopt a chapter modelled on the UNCITRAL Model Law on Cross-Border Insolvency remains unimplemented in the statute. And financial service providers stand outside the corporate process unless brought in: Section 227 empowers the government, in consultation with the appropriate financial regulator, to notify financial service providers or categories of them for resolution under the Code, and the framework made under that power was first used in 2019 when the Reserve Bank referred Dewan Housing Finance Corporation Limited, a housing finance company, into a process under the Code administered through the tribunal. Banks and insurers otherwise remain under their own resolution and winding-up regimes, a boundary that matters every time a large financial failure is discussed as if the Code plainly governed it.

## The guarantor process as actually commenced

Since the guarantor class is the only population inside Part III, its working shape belongs in this pillar. An application against a personal guarantor may be filed by a creditor, or by the guarantor, under Chapter III of Part III, and because the guarantor's liability is usually pursued alongside the company's case, Section 60 directs the application to the tribunal seised of the corporate debtor's process where one is pending, so company and guarantor are heard by the same bench. Filing triggers the interim moratorium of Section 96, which stays legal action and proceedings against the guarantor's property in relation to any debt while the application is examined, and admission brings the fuller moratorium of Section 101. A resolution professional is appointed, claims are invited, and the guarantor must submit a repayment plan for the creditors' approval under the chapter's voting rules; the plan, if approved and sanctioned, binds as the corporate plan does within its sphere. If no plan is approved, or the plan fails, the bankruptcy chapter is available, under which a bankruptcy order vests the guarantor's estate in a bankruptcy trustee for realisation and distribution, with the disabilities and the discharge the chapter prescribes. The process imports the Code's philosophy into personal liability: the guarantee, traditionally enforced by suit and execution over years, became an insolvency event with a collective process of its own, and Lalit Kumar Jain's twin holdings, that the selective commencement was valid and that the corporate plan does not discharge the guarantor, are what make the machinery worth a creditor's while. The population actually reached remains narrow, promoters and directors who signed personal guarantees for company borrowing, but for credit practice the narrowness is incidental: banks price and pursue guarantees differently because this process exists.

## The financial service provider door in operation

The Section 227 framework sketched in the dormant-half discussion is the Code's only functioning exception to its own exclusions, and its working form should be stated here because the financial service provider boundary is otherwise apt to mislead. In November 2019 the Central Government notified rules for the insolvency and liquidation proceedings of financial service providers, applying the Code with modifications to notified categories, principally non-banking financial companies above a stated asset size, and assigning the regulator the initiating role the statute reserves: only the regulator may file, depositors and trade creditors may not, and the tribunal appoints an administrator in place of the insolvency professional, exercising the professional's powers under the regulator's supervision. The Reserve Bank of India used the framework within days of its notification to supersede the board of Dewan Housing Finance Corporation Limited and refer it to the tribunal at Mumbai, the first and defining case under the rules, which ended in an approved resolution plan. The design concedes the Code's premise and reverses its trigger: financial firms fail systemically, so the collective process is borrowed while the creditor's door is locked, and the episode belongs in the pillar because it shows the statute functioning as a chassis onto which a supervised regime for finance has been bolted, pending the comprehensive financial resolution legislation that has been proposed and not enacted.

## The government as creditor, and the first charge problem

The treatment of government dues in the Code contains a fault line that a careful reader should see before it surprises them in a case. Section 53 places amounts due to the Central and State Governments, for the two years preceding liquidation, at the fifth level of the queue, and Section 30(2) protects operational creditors, among whom statutory dues usually travel, by the liquidation-value floor in a plan. Against that stands the doctrine of the statutory first charge: several fiscal statutes declare their dues a first charge on the property of the defaulter, and in State Tax Officer v Rainbow Papers Limited in 2022 the Supreme Court held that the first charge under the Gujarat Value Added Tax Act made the State a secured creditor within the Code's definitions, entitled to treatment on that footing rather than as an ordinary operational claimant in the queue. The decision unsettled the tidy statement that government dues rank fifth, because it made the answer depend on the terms of the fiscal statute creating the due, and it has been argued over in the tribunals since, alongside the separate line of authority on whether a plan can bind a government creditor whose claim was never filed, which Ghanshyam Mishra answered in favour of finality. The durable position for a reader is twofold: the Code's own queue subordinates the exchequer as a matter of design, and a fiscal statute's first charge can reclassify a particular due out of that subordination, so the classification of each government claim is a question about its parent statute as much as about the Code.

## The threshold question argued properly

The move of the Section 4 minimum default from one lakh rupees to one crore deserves a slower treatment than the commencement map gives it, because it is the single most consequential jurisdictional choice in the Code's administration. The original figure was set low by design: the Bankruptcy Law Reforms Committee wanted the trigger within reach of small creditors, and the early years vindicated the fear that accompanied the choice, as the process filled with small operational claims deployed as recovery pressure, benches spent their capacity on disputes worth less than the cost of the process, and companies of consequence faced displacement of management over defaults trivial beside their balance sheets. The notification of March 2020 raised the gate a hundredfold at the moment the pandemic made a wave of small defaults a certainty, and the stated object was to protect micro, small and medium enterprises from being pushed into insolvency by the shock. The distributive asymmetry in that protection is the point critics press: the shield protects the MSME as debtor while disarming the MSME as creditor, since the small supplier owed eighty lakh rupees by a large buyer lost the Code's door at the same notification, and remains remitted to a suit, to the delayed payment machinery of the MSMED Act, or to negotiation from weakness. Parliament's own later design concedes half the criticism, since the pre-packaged chapter for MSMEs fixes its minimum default at ten lakh rupees, an implicit acknowledgment that the one crore gate sits above the scale at which small enterprise debts exist. The threshold's durability will turn on which constituency's access the system decides it exists to serve, and the pillar can record the choice cleanly: the gate stands at a crore for the corporate process generally, ten lakh for the MSME pre-pack, and the difference between those two numbers is a policy argument the statute has not finished having.

## The pandemic interventions of 2020

Two interventions in 2020 changed who could use the Code, and a pillar account must record them precisely because they are routinely misstated. The first was the threshold increase already described: S.O. 1205(E) of 24 March 2020 raised the Section 4 minimum default from one lakh rupees to one crore rupees. That was a permanent change effected under an existing power, and it survived the emergency that prompted it.

The second was suspension. By an ordinance promulgated in June 2020, Parliament inserted Section 10A, which barred the filing of any application under Sections 7, 9 or 10 for a default arising during the suspension period. The period ran from 25 March 2020 and, extended in stages, covered a full year, to 24 March 2021. The bar was drafted with a permanence that matters: the proviso to Section 10A states that no application shall ever be filed for a default arising in that window, so pandemic-period defaults were not merely postponed as triggers; they were removed from the Code's reach altogether, although the same debtor could be proceeded against for a default arising after the window closed. A companion bar prevented applications against such debtors under the fraudulent and wrongful trading provision for defaults of the same period. The design choice is instructive beside the threshold change. The threshold governs the size of default that counts; the suspension governed the date of default that counts; between them, the State redrew the Code's jurisdiction twice in a single year, in a statute whose founding argument had been that its triggers should be mechanical and beyond discretionary adjustment.

## The pre-packaged process for smaller companies

The Code's most substantial structural addition after enactment is Chapter III-A, inserted by an ordinance of 4 April 2021 and carried into the Act by the Amendment Act of that year, creating a pre-packaged insolvency resolution process for corporate debtors classified as micro, small or medium enterprises under the Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises Development Act, 2006. It is a deliberate hybrid, and its departures from the main process are the point.

In the main process, admission displaces management. In the pre-packaged process, the existing management retains possession and control, and the resolution professional supervises, monitors and reports, stepping into management only if the tribunal vests it on grounds of fraud or gross mismanagement. The debtor's own promoters produce the first plan, called the base resolution plan, which the committee considers; the market is invited to compete only in the prescribed circumstances, principally where the base plan impairs the claims of operational creditors, in which case competing plans are invited and the base plan may have to win a comparison, a mechanism practitioners call the Swiss challenge. The process runs on a shorter clock, 120 days from its commencement date for the committee's decision and the tribunal's order as the chapter structures it. Eligibility is narrower than the main process in one respect and broader in effect: the debtor must meet the chapter's conditions, including a minimum default of ten lakh rupees as specified for the chapter, must not have undergone a pre-packaged or corporate process within the prescribed recent period, and must not be undergoing a corporate process or be subject to a liquidation order.

The chapter's significance for reading the Code as a whole is that it qualifies the founding premise. For smaller enterprises, Parliament accepted in 2021 what it had rejected in 2016: that displacing the promoter at the door can destroy the very value the process exists to save, because in an MSME the promoter frequently is the business, its relationships, its licences in practice and its order book. The pre-pack keeps creditor control of the outcome, since the committee must still approve and the tribunal must still sanction, while returning possession to the debtor. Whether that concession should travel up the size scale is a live design argument, and the Code's text contains both answers, one in Chapter III and one in Chapter III-A.

## Section 29A at work, and the cure Parliament built into it

Because eligibility contests decide real cases, the section's operation needs more than the summary the plan discussion gives. Section 29A disqualifies the applicant and, through clause (j), the persons connected to the applicant, and it is applied by the resolution professional in the first instance through the affidavit and eligibility process the regulations require, subject to the tribunal when a rival or a creditor challenges. The bar grounded in a non-performing asset, clause (c), attaches where the applicant, or a company under the applicant's management or control, has an account classified as non-performing for a year or more at the time the process commenced, and the 2018 amendment built a cure into the section: the disqualification does not apply if the applicant pays all overdue amounts with interest and charges before submitting the plan. The cure is a considered judgment that a default cured in money is different from a default being escaped, and its presence qualifies the common description of the section as an unyielding lifetime ban. It is not: it is a ban with a price of absolution, set at full payment.

The Supreme Court's treatment of the section's first great contest, the Essar Steel bidding war that reached the Court as ArcelorMittal India v Satish Kumar Gupta in 2018, fixed two construction rules that still govern. The disqualification is tested at the stage the statute specifies and looks through corporate layering to the person in control, so that an applicant cannot launder a disqualification through a subsidiary; and the cure requires actual payment of the overdue amounts of the companies the applicant controlled, not an undertaking to pay. Between Swiss Ribbons, which sustained the section's constitutionality including its retrospective application to pending processes, and ArcelorMittal, which sustained its strictness of construction, the section stands as the most heavily defended provision in the Code, and the most criticised in the commentary for the bidder-pool cost its defence accepts.

## The Code among its neighbouring statutes

No pillar account is complete without the map of displacements, because the Code governs by amending its neighbours as much as by its own text. Through its schedules, principally the Eleventh Schedule, the Code amended the Companies Act 2013 to remove insolvency as a ground and mechanism of winding up there and to leave the tribunal's company jurisdiction reshaped around the Code; amended the securitisation and debt recovery statutes so that their machinery runs subject to the Code's moratorium and priorities when a corporate process opens; and repealed, by Section 243, the Presidency Towns Insolvency Act, 1909 and the Provincial Insolvency Act, 1920, with the repeal's operation tied to the commencement of the Part III provisions it accompanies, which is why the colonial individual statutes continue to function for the debtors Part III has not reached. Section 255 performs the consequential surgery across the listed enactments. The Sick Industrial Companies (Special Provisions) Repeal Act, 2003, enacted years earlier but kept in suspense, was itself brought into force on 1 December 2016 in coordination with the corporate provisions, so that the sickness regime and its board ended on the day the Code's corporate process began, the story told fully in the article on the [SICA repeal and the end of the BIFR regime](/2012/06/01/sica-1985-bifr-repeal/). Two override provisions police the borders: Section 238 gives the Code effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent in any other law, the provision Innoventive used against the Maharashtra shield statute, and Section 231 bars civil court jurisdiction over matters the tribunal and the Board are empowered to determine, channelling the system's disputes into its own forums and the appeal ladder that runs from the tribunal to the appellate tribunal and onward to the Supreme Court on a question of law under Sections 61 and 62.

One frontier the Code conspicuously lacks is a group insolvency chapter. Indian business is conducted through groups, and failure arrives in groups, but the Code proceeds company by company, each debtor with its own process, committee and plan. Tribunals have improvised at the edges: in the Videocon group proceedings, the tribunal at Mumbai ordered a substantive consolidation of a set of group companies' assets and liabilities into a single process, reasoning from the interlaced finances before it, an exceptional judicial construction rather than a statutory scheme. The Insolvency Law Committee examined the question and recommended a framework for group coordination in stages rather than full consolidation. The gap remains: coordination among the separate processes of affiliated debtors rests on practice and tribunal case management, and cross-border groups meet the further gap left by the uncommenced Sections 234 and 235 recorded in the commencement map. Practice has bridged that second gap once, conspicuously: in the collapse of Jet Airways, where proceedings ran in India and the Netherlands, the appellate tribunal approved a cross-border insolvency protocol agreed between the Indian resolution professional and the Dutch administrator, coordinating the two estates by consent because no statute authorised it to do so by law. The episode is the cross-border position in miniature, a sophisticated workaround standing where a Model Law chapter should be.

## The personal exposure of the old management

A reader of the pillar should understand what the Code does to the human beings who ran the debtor, because the exposure extends beyond losing control of the company. Chapter VII of Part II creates offences calibrated to the ways a failing company is stripped: concealment of property, transactions in fraud of creditors, misconduct in the course of the process, falsification of books, wilful omissions and false representations to obtain credit, with punishments graded across the chapter up to imprisonment and substantial fines, and liability reaching the officers in default responsible for the conduct. Civil exposure runs beside the criminal. The avoidance provisions already described can recover the value of preferential and undervalued transfers from their beneficiaries, Section 66 can order the directors who traded wrongfully to contribute personally to the assets, and the disqualification in Section 29A closes the door on the same persons returning as owners through a plan for any company they remain connected with. The combined effect is a code that treats the pre-insolvency twilight as a period of trust: conduct that an older, slower system rarely examined is audited as a matter of routine by the professional's avoidance opinions, and the knowledge that the audit is coming is part of the discipline the Code is credited with imposing on credit culture, a deterrent claim that belongs to the design argument even though its measurement belongs to the record article.

## The judicial treatment in brief

The Code's constitutional position was settled early and in its favour. In Swiss Ribbons Private Limited v Union of India, decided in January 2019, the Supreme Court upheld the Code against a comprehensive challenge. The holdings a reader needs are four. The differential treatment of financial and operational creditors has an intelligible basis in the nature of the two debts, financial creditors being equipped, through their loan documents and their position, to assess viability and to restructure, and the classification survived Article 14. The exclusion of operational creditors from the committee, tempered by the protections the judgment catalogued, was not arbitrary. Section 29A was upheld, including its reach to persons connected with a disqualified promoter, the Court accepting the legislative judgment that the provision's object, keeping those responsible for the default from regaining the company through the process, would be defeated by a narrower bar. And the whole statute was read, following the Bankruptcy Law Reforms Committee, as a beneficial reorganisation law rather than a recovery statute, an interpretive instruction that has governed the case law since.

Two further lines of authority complete the brief treatment here; the full doctrinal sequence belongs to the cluster's judicial interpretation article. The homebuyer litigation ended in Pioneer Urban Land and Infrastructure Limited v Union of India in 2019, where the Court upheld the 2018 amendment that treats allottees under a real estate project as financial creditors, so that a defaulting developer can be taken into the process by the purchasers it failed to deliver to, voting in the committee through an authorised representative and subject, after a later amendment, to a minimum number of allottees who must join in an application. And the admission jurisprudence, Innoventive's narrow gate, Vidarbha's disturbance and the confinement of Vidarbha in Suresh Kumar Reddy, has been set out in the initiation discussion above. Around these anchors runs a large body of tribunal case law on the avoidance provisions, the sections that let the resolution professional and, in liquidation, the liquidator attack transactions entered before the process began: preferential transactions under Section 43, undervalued transactions under Section 45, and the contribution liability for fraudulent and wrongful trading under Section 66, with look-back periods that lengthen where the counterparty was a related party. Those provisions are the process's memory, and they matter practically because a plan's value to creditors often depends on what can be clawed back into the estate.

The enforcement spine is completed by Chapter VII of Part II, which criminalises the conduct that would make a mockery of the transfer of control: concealment of property, false representations to creditors, transactions in fraud of creditors, misconduct by officers, and falsification of books, with punishments that include imprisonment. A general account of a statute is information about the law and not advice on any particular default or defence, and the Code's timelines and bars operate without regard to the merits of an individual case, which is why parties act on advice taken for their own facts.

## Appeals, and the finality the system protects

The appeal ladder is short by design, and shortness is a substantive choice. From an order of the tribunal, an appeal lies to the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal under Section 61 within thirty days, extendable by fifteen days for sufficient cause, and from the appellate tribunal to the Supreme Court under Section 62 on a question of law within the period that section prescribes. Around the ladder sits the discipline the courts have built: a plan approved by the tribunal is not reopened on the merits by an appellate bench substituting its commercial judgment for the committee's, the appellate jurisdiction extending to the statutory compliance of the plan and the legality of the process, within the Sashidhar and Essar boundaries already described. The pattern across the appellate case law is consistent with the statute's theory. Errors of law at admission, misclassification of debt, a dispute wrongly brushed aside, a plan that breaches Section 30(2), these are corrected on appeal, and corrected with consequences for the case; dissatisfaction with the price the committee accepted is not, and the appellate courts' refusal to re-price is what gives the committee's vote its meaning at the negotiating table. Finality is also temporal: the Supreme Court has treated the timelines of the appeal provisions strictly, because an insolvency that remains appealable in substance for years reproduces, inside the new system, the precise pathology the Code was enacted to end.

## The amendment cycle as design maintenance

The Code has been amended so often in its first years that the amendment arc is part of the statute's identity, and the pillar should record its direction without poaching the amendment trail article's detailed work. The changes cluster into four purposes. The first cluster repaired the trigger and the gate: Section 29A by ordinance within eighteen months of commencement, the homebuyer recognition and the withdrawal mechanism in the Amendment Act of 2018, and the applicability of the Limitation Act through Section 238A in the same round. The second cluster hardened the plan: the 330 day outer limit, the express binding of governments in Section 31, the strengthened floors for operational and dissenting creditors in Section 30(2), and the offence shield of Section 32A, across the amendments of 2019 and the turn of 2020. The third cluster was emergency government of the market itself: the threshold increase and Section 10A in 2020, which rewrote the Code's jurisdiction in response to the pandemic. The fourth cluster extended the design to a population the original fitted badly: Section 240A for MSME promoters in 2018 and the pre-packaged chapter in 2021. Read together, the amendments show a legislature maintaining a machine it expects to keep, tightening the eligibility and finality provisions that litigation exposed, and repeatedly choosing the creditors' process over the debtors' pleas for room, with the pandemic year the standing exception that proves the disposition.

Standing behind the amendments is an institution worth naming, the Insolvency Law Committee, a standing committee of experts and officials constituted in 2017 to review the Code's working and recommend changes, whose reports of 2018 and 2020 supplied the drafting for much of the amendment cycle and the group insolvency and cross-border recommendations that remain unimplemented. The Committee on the model of the Bankruptcy Law Reforms Committee represents a style of statute maintenance unusual in Indian legislation: the Code was treated from the start as a system under observation, with a named body charged to watch its case law and propose repairs, and the density of reliable commentary the Code enjoys, committee reports, Board publications and tribunal jurisprudence in a tight feedback loop, is one reason its doctrine has settled faster than that of statutes left to the courts alone.

## The Code in the examination hall

The aspirant is among this article's named readers, and the Code is examined in the civil services, judicial services and company secretary papers with a predictability that rewards structural knowledge over anecdote. What is tested is the architecture and the distinctions: the difference between a financial and an operational creditor and the different doors each must use; the moratorium's scope and its exclusion of the guarantor; the committee's composition and the sixty-six and ninety per cent thresholds that govern plans and withdrawals; the Section 53 queue, which candidates are asked to reproduce in order and to apply to a stated set of claims; the Section 29A disqualifications and the MSME relaxation in Section 240A; the timeline of 180, 90 and 330 days with the Essar qualification; and the commencement map, above all the fact that Part III operates only for personal guarantors and that the Fresh Start Process has never been notified, which is set as a trap for candidates who describe the Code as a complete personal insolvency law. The case sequence that carries the doctrine, Innoventive on admission, Swiss Ribbons on constitutionality, Sashidhar and Essar on commercial wisdom, Pioneer Urban on homebuyers, Ghanshyam Mishra on the clean slate and Lalit Kumar Jain on guarantors, is the retrievable spine, and this article has been built so that each of those holdings sits in the section where its subject is decided.

## The record question this article does not answer

Whether the Code works, measured in cases admitted, time consumed, money realised and businesses saved, is an evidence question with its own article in this cluster, and this pillar does not poach it. Two structural observations belong here because they frame that evidence rather than supply it. The Board's published quarterly series records admissions since December 2016 in the thousands and resolutions in a smaller number, with liquidation the outcome in a large share of closed cases, a distribution the Code's critics and defenders read in opposite ways: as proof that companies arrive at the tribunal too late to save, or as proof that the process is being used as a liquidation funnel its design did not intend. And the realisation debate, the argument about haircuts, is in this article's submission the wrong debate conducted on the wrong premise. A resolution law should be judged by whether viable businesses continue and unviable ones exit quickly with their assets redeployed, and by whether credit is priced differently because default has become a governed event rather than an open-ended stalemate. Recovery percentages measure the state of the companies that entered the process at least as much as the quality of the process, a distinction the [implementation record of the Code](/2012/04/15/ibc-implementation-record/) examines on the evidence.

The trade-offs, by contrast, are this article's business, because they are in the text. Section 29A keeps defaulting promoters from buying back their companies at a discount, and it also narrows the bidder pool for exactly the businesses where the promoter may be the only buyer who understands the assets, which can depress what creditors recover; Parliament chose integrity of the process over depth of the market, and the choice is defensible and costly at once. The distribution order in Section 53 protects workmen and recent wages and puts the process's own costs first, and it leaves the ordinary trade supplier, the operational creditor who kept the company stocked, at the sixth level behind unsecured financial creditors, a ranking that reflects the Committee's view of who can price and monitor risk and that sits hard on the smallest creditors in the queue. The homebuyer solution cured a real injustice, purchasers who had paid for unbuilt flats standing outside the process while banks decided, and it imported into the committee a numerous class of creditors whose interests are in delivery rather than value, changing the politics of every developer resolution since. None of these is a drafting accident. Each is a chosen allocation, and the honest criticism of the Code is an argument with the choices, not a discovery that choices were made.

For the practitioner and the researcher, the working habit the Code rewards is documentary discipline: claims are proved from records, plans are priced from the information memorandum, and the outcome often turns on the file assembled before admission. Those who follow a live resolution closely tend to [open the orders, plans and claim records in an office file viewer for Excel, Word and PowerPoint files](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html), because the papers of a contested insolvency are commercially sensitive long after the hearing ends.

## What this Code is not

Template discipline requires the negative space, and the Code's negative space is frequently where public argument goes wrong. The Code is not a recovery law. A recovery law returns a creditor's money by force against assets; the Code displaces a failed management and subjects all claims to a collective decision in which the initiating creditor may receive less than a rival's plan offers its rivals and nothing like a decree, a distinction the Supreme Court drew in Swiss Ribbons by characterising the legislation as a reorganisation statute whose object is the revival of the debtor and the maximisation of value, with recovery the consequence rather than the purpose. The Code is not a winding up law either, although it ends in liquidation where rescue fails; liquidation is its sanction, not its object, and the sequence, resolution first and the terminal process on failure, is the statute's own statement of priorities. It is not a shield for debtors: the debtor in possession philosophy of the old regime is the precise doctrine the Code abolished, and its only debtor-friendly chapter, the pre-pack, keeps the incumbent management because small companies cannot be run by anyone else, under supervision and a market test, not by grace. It is not a complete insolvency law, as the dormant half shows: it is a corporate insolvency law with a guarantor annex, awaiting the commencement that would make it the general code its title claims. And it is not self-executing: its outputs depend on benches, professionals and markets it created but cannot command, which is why the pillar and the record can be written about the same statute and read like accounts of two different laws. Nor is it a charter for the strongest creditor to seize the company for itself: the committee decides collectively, the eligibility bar binds the powerful applicant with a disqualified past, the moratorium restrains the secured creditor's private enforcement along with everyone else's, and the tribunal's approval stands between the vote and the vesting of the company in new hands. The Code's collectivity is not a slogan laid over a scramble; it is the operating system, and each of its signature devices, the moratorium, the committee, the clean slate, the queue, is that collectivity expressed at a different stage of the case.

## The verdict on the statute

The Code is the most consequential piece of Indian economic legislation of its generation, and its consequence does not depend on the recovery statistics its argument is usually conducted over. It changed the cost of default. Before it, a promoter who stopped paying kept the company; after it, a promoter who stops paying on a substantial debt stands to lose the company to the creditors within a statutory season, and that single change repriced the relationship between borrower and lender across the economy, including in the vast majority of cases that settle precisely because the process exists. Its architecture is coherent in a way Indian statutes often are not: one trigger, one gatekeeper inquiry, one displaced management, one deciding committee, one clock, one exit queue.

Its incompleteness is of a piece with its design rather than an accident befalling it. The same commencement power that let the corporate machinery start in six months let the individual machinery wait indefinitely, and the courts have sustained that selectivity. The Code therefore governs Indian business failure comprehensively and Indian personal failure barely at all, and a reader who holds both halves of that sentence has the measure of the statute. The companies cluster's remaining specialist articles take the parts in turn: the tribunal that runs the process, the sick companies regime the Code displaced, whose repeal story is told in its own article in this cluster, and the winding-up jurisdiction that the 2013 Companies Act had been preparing when the Code relocated insolvency winding up into itself, a relocation traced in the guide to the [Companies Act 2013](/2012/02/01/companies-act-2013-complete-guide/).

The unfinished business can be listed from the statute's own silences, and listing it is the proper close of a pillar, since the next phase of the Code will be written in these gaps. Part III awaits commencement for the population it was written for, the individual debtor and the partnership firm, and with it the Fresh Start Process, the only provision of Indian law that has ever offered the honest small debtor a discharge by design rather than by a creditor's mercy. The cross-border provisions await the enactment that would let Indian processes speak to foreign courts in the Model Law's language instead of by improvised protocol. Group insolvency awaits the framework the Insolvency Law Committee recommended, in a business landscape where failure is a group event. The tribunals await the benches and the members their docket arithmetic demands, since a statute of deadlines administered by an institution without capacity converts its timelines into aspirations, and the record of that conversion belongs to the implementation article. And the eligibility bar, the queue and the committee's composition await the verdict of experience on whether the distribution the Code chose is the distribution a maturing credit economy will keep. The statute that emerges from this article is therefore best read as a completed architecture with an incomplete building, its design questions answered and its construction questions open, which is exactly the condition in which a pillar should leave it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What problem was the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016 designed to solve?

The Code was designed to solve the fragmentation and delay of the insolvency law that preceded it. A failing company's creditors were scattered across sickness proceedings before the Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction, debt recovery tribunals, security enforcement under the securitisation statute, and winding up in the High Courts, each forum moving at its own pace and none able to deliver a binding collective decision. Promoters kept control of defaulting companies while creditors queued, exits took years, and the value of the business drained away in the interval. The Bankruptcy Law Reforms Committee proposed one code with one trigger, a time-bound collective process, control passing to creditors on default, and a single distribution queue, and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016, Act 31 of 2016, enacted that design.

### Q: Who can start an insolvency process under the Code?

Three applicants can start the corporate insolvency resolution process. A financial creditor, a person to whom a financial debt is owed, applies under Section 7 on proof of a default at or above the minimum amount in Section 4, which stands at one crore rupees. An operational creditor, owed an operational debt for goods, services, employment or statutory dues, applies under Section 9, but only after serving a demand notice under Section 8 and surviving the debtor's opportunity to show payment or a genuine pre-existing dispute. The corporate debtor itself applies under Section 10, with the shareholders' special resolution the section requires. Each application goes to the National Company Law Tribunal bench for the debtor's registered office, and admission follows the single inquiry whether a qualifying default exists.

### Q: What is the difference between a financial and an operational creditor?

A financial creditor is owed a financial debt, defined in Section 5(8) as a debt disbursed against the consideration for the time value of money, such as a loan, debentures, a finance lease or a guarantee liability; the substance is borrowed money. An operational creditor is owed an operational debt under Section 5(21), a claim for goods or services supplied, including employment dues, or dues payable to government under a law. The classification decides the creditor's whole position. The financial creditor files directly under Section 7, sits in the Committee of Creditors and votes on the resolution plan. The operational creditor must first serve a demand notice and defeat the dispute filter under Sections 8 and 9, does not sit in the committee, and is protected in a plan by the liquidation value floor in Section 30(2) instead of a vote.

### Q: What does the moratorium stop once insolvency proceedings begin?

On admission, Section 14 imposes a moratorium until the resolution process completes. It prohibits the institution or continuation of suits and proceedings against the corporate debtor, including execution of judgments and decrees; the transfer, encumbrance or disposal of the debtor's assets by the debtor itself; action to foreclose, recover or enforce a security interest, including under the securitisation statute; and the recovery of property in the debtor's occupation by an owner or lessor. Essential supplies cannot be terminated or suspended during the moratorium for that period's dues. The moratorium does not protect a surety or guarantor, whose assets the creditor may still pursue, and notified categories of arrangements, including certain financial contracts, stand outside it. Its purpose is a calm period in which the business is preserved as a going concern while the creditors decide its fate.

### Q: What is Section 29A and who does it disqualify?

Section 29A, inserted in 2017 and recast in 2018, makes specified persons ineligible to submit a resolution plan, so that those responsible for a company's failure cannot buy it back through the process at a discount while shedding its debts. It disqualifies undischarged insolvents, wilful defaulters, persons whose or whose controlled companies' accounts have been classified as non-performing for a year or more, persons convicted of offences carrying two years' imprisonment or more, directors disqualified under company law, persons barred by the securities regulator, those who executed an invoked guarantee that remains unpaid, and persons connected to any of these, including promoters and persons in management or control of the applicant. The bar reaches through corporate layering to the person in control. For micro, small and medium enterprises, Section 240A disapplies the non-performing asset and guarantee clauses, and the non-performing asset disqualification can be cured by paying all overdue amounts with interest before the plan is submitted.

### Q: How does the Committee of Creditors take decisions?

The Committee of Creditors consists of the financial creditors of the corporate debtor, each voting in proportion to the debt owed, with related parties of the debtor excluded from representation, participation and voting. Where a class of financial creditors is numerous, such as homebuyers under a real estate project, the class acts through an authorised representative who votes according to the majority decision of the class members present and voting. Ordinary decisions of the committee require at least fifty-one per cent of the voting share. The decisions that matter most are reserved to a higher majority of sixty-six per cent: appointing the resolution professional, approving the actions listed in Section 28, and approving a resolution plan under Section 30(4). Withdrawal of an admitted application under Section 12A requires ninety per cent. The committee's commercial wisdom in evaluating plans is, under the Supreme Court's rulings in K. Sashidhar and the Essar Steel case, not reviewable on its merits by the tribunal.

### Q: What is the waterfall of distribution on liquidation?

Section 53 distributes the proceeds of the liquidation estate in a fixed order. First come the insolvency resolution process costs and liquidation costs, paid in full. Next, ranking equally, come workmen's dues for the twenty-four months before the liquidation commencement date and the debts of a secured creditor that has relinquished its security to the estate. Then come wages and unpaid dues of employees other than workmen for the twelve preceding months. Then come financial debts owed to unsecured creditors. Then, ranking equally, come Central and State Government dues for the two preceding years and the shortfall of a secured creditor that enforced its security outside the estate. Then any remaining debts and dues, then preference shareholders, and lastly equity shareholders. Fees payable to the liquidator are deducted proportionately before distribution, and a secured creditor may alternatively stand outside, realise its security itself, and prove in the queue only for any shortfall.

### Q: Which parts of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code are still not in force?

The Code was commenced in stages by notification, and whole territories remain uncommenced. The Fresh Start Process, Sections 80 to 93 of Part III, which would let an eligible small debtor obtain a discharge of qualifying debts, has never been notified. Part III generally, Sections 94 to 187, covering insolvency resolution and bankruptcy for individuals and partnership firms, is in force only for personal guarantors to corporate debtors, brought in by notification with effect from 1 December 2019. The cross-border provisions, Sections 234 and 235, which would let the Central Government enter reciprocal arrangements and issue letters of request to foreign courts, have never been notified, and the UNCITRAL Model Law chapter recommended by the Insolvency Law Committee has not been enacted. The corporate provisions of Part II, Part IV on the Board and the professions, and most of Part V operate fully, so the working Code is a corporate insolvency law with a guarantor annex.

### Q: What is the minimum default needed to file a case under the Code?

Section 4 sets the minimum amount of default for the corporate insolvency resolution process. As enacted in 2016 the figure was one lakh rupees, with a proviso allowing the Central Government to raise it by notification to a ceiling of one crore rupees. The government exercised the full power on 24 March 2020, issuing notification S.O. 1205(E), which raised the minimum default to one crore rupees, where it stands. A default below one crore, however genuine, cannot found an application under Section 7, Section 9 or Section 10, and the creditor is left to a suit, arbitration or other recovery machinery. The pre-packaged process for micro, small and medium enterprises is an exception in scale: its minimum default is ten lakh rupees, reflecting the smaller debts of that sector.

### Q: What happens to a company's directors when insolvency proceedings are admitted?

The board of directors is not dissolved, but its powers are suspended. Under Section 17, from the appointment of the interim resolution professional the management of the corporate debtor's affairs vests in the professional, the powers of the board stand suspended and are exercised by the professional, the officers and managers must report to the professional and give access to documents, and the debtor's bank accounts operate on the professional's instructions. The directors remain in office as a matter of company law and owe duties of cooperation: Section 19 obliges the personnel and promoters to extend all assistance and access to books, assets and information, and the professional may apply to the tribunal for directions where cooperation is refused. Directors also face exposure for the period before admission, through the avoidance provisions on preferential and undervalued transactions and the fraudulent and wrongful trading jurisdiction of Section 66, and through the offences in Chapter VII of Part II.

### Q: What does a resolution professional do during a corporate insolvency process?

The resolution professional is the officer who runs the process. Appointed first in an interim capacity and confirmed or replaced by the Committee of Creditors, the professional takes custody and control of the debtor's assets and records, manages the debtor as a going concern, receives and verifies claims, constitutes the committee, prepares the information memorandum, forms opinions on avoidance transactions and files the applications they require, invites expressions of interest and resolution plans, checks each plan against Section 30(2) and Section 29A, presents compliant plans to the committee, and applies to the tribunal for approval of the chosen plan or, on failure, for liquidation. The professional may raise interim finance and must preserve the value of the debtor's property. The office is regulated by the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India, which registers professionals, prescribes their code of conduct and disciplines them, and a professional can be replaced by the committee at sixty-six per cent of the voting share.

### Q: Can an insolvency application be withdrawn after the process has begun?

Yes, but the ease of withdrawal depends on how far the process has travelled, because admission converts a private dispute into a collective proceeding in rem. Before admission, the tribunal may permit withdrawal under the procedural rules on the applicant's request. After admission, Section 12A governs: the tribunal may allow withdrawal only on an application by the original applicant with the approval of ninety per cent of the voting share of the Committee of Creditors, the supermajority reflecting that the creditors collectively, not the filing creditor, own the process by then. Where the committee has not yet been constituted, the procedural route applies with the tribunal's permission. Settlement between the debtor and one creditor cannot by itself end the case, a deliberate design: otherwise the admission of a process would become a lever for extracting preferential settlements, and the claims of every other creditor would be defeated by a private bargain they never approved.

### Q: Are homebuyers treated as creditors under the Code?

Yes. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy (Amendment) Ordinance of 2018, followed by the Amendment Act, inserted an Explanation into the definition of financial debt providing that an amount raised from an allottee under a real estate project is deemed to have the commercial effect of a borrowing, which makes the allottee a financial creditor. The Supreme Court upheld the amendment in Pioneer Urban Land and Infrastructure Limited v Union of India in 2019, reasoning that the developer's obligation to deliver the apartment or refund the amount carries the time value of money the definition requires. Homebuyers therefore sit in the Committee of Creditors, acting through an authorised representative who votes the class's aggregated share as the majority of the class directs. An allottee application itself has a threshold: since the amendment of 2020 it must be filed jointly by not fewer than one hundred allottees, or ten per cent of the total number of allottees under the same project, whichever is less.

### Q: What is a pre-packaged insolvency process for MSMEs?

The pre-packaged insolvency resolution process, inserted as Chapter III-A of Part II from Section 54A by the Amendment Act of 2021 after an ordinance of 4 April 2021, is a lighter process available only to a corporate debtor that qualifies as a micro, small or medium enterprise, for defaults of at least ten lakh rupees. Unlike the ordinary process, the existing management retains control of the company, the debtor in possession model, while a resolution professional supervises. The debtor itself prepares a base resolution plan. That plan is put to the Committee of Creditors; if it does not impair the claims of operational creditors, it can be approved without a market contest, but if it does impair such claims, competing plans are invited and the base plan must survive a Swiss challenge against the best rival offer. The process must conclude within 120 days of commencement, and control passes from the management to the professional only on a finding of fraud or gross mismanagement.

### Q: Can a personal guarantor be taken into insolvency under the Code?

Yes, within the limits of what has been brought into force. Part III of the Code, which governs insolvency and bankruptcy for individuals and partnership firms, was commenced with effect from 1 December 2019 only in relation to personal guarantors to corporate debtors, by the notification S.O. 4126(E) dated 15 November 2019. A creditor may therefore apply under Chapter III of Part III in respect of a defaulted guarantee, and where the corporate debtor's own resolution process is pending, Section 60 sends the guarantor's case to the same National Company Law Tribunal bench. An interim moratorium under Section 96 operates from the filing, the guarantor may propose a repayment plan for the creditors' approval, and bankruptcy under Chapter IV is the consequence of failure. In Lalit Kumar Jain v Union of India in 2021 the Supreme Court upheld the selective notification and held that approval of the company's resolution plan does not discharge the guarantor, whose liability survives to the extent the plan does not pay it.

### Q: What is the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India?

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India is the regulator established under Part IV of the Code with effect from 1 October 2016. It does not adjudicate insolvency cases; the tribunals do that. The Board registers and regulates the three service populations the Code depends on, insolvency professionals, insolvency professional agencies and information utilities, conducts or oversees the examinations and enrolment through which professionals qualify, and disciplines professionals, including by suspending or cancelling registration. It writes the regulations that supply the Code's process detail, on claims, meetings, valuations, sales and conduct, and it collects and publishes data on the system's working, including the quarterly statistics on admissions, resolutions and liquidations. Its chairperson and members are appointed by the Central Government. The design follows the Bankruptcy Law Reforms Committee's recommendation of a dedicated regulator for the insolvency profession, on the premise that a market of regulated professionals, rather than a government department, should operate the process.

### Q: What happens if no resolution plan is approved in time?

Liquidation follows. Section 33 requires the tribunal to order the liquidation of the corporate debtor where no resolution plan is received before the expiry of the resolution period, where the tribunal rejects the plan for non-compliance with the Code, where the committee resolves by sixty-six per cent of the voting share to liquidate and notifies the tribunal, or where the debtor contravenes an approved plan and an affected person applies. The tribunal appoints a liquidator, usually the resolution professional unless replaced, a public announcement invites claims, and the debtor's assets form a liquidation estate under Section 36, excluding third party assets and sums due to workmen from provident, pension and gratuity funds. The liquidator verifies claims, sells the assets, and distributes the proceeds under the Section 53 waterfall. When the assets are fully liquidated, the tribunal orders the dissolution of the company under Section 54, and the company ceases to exist.

### Q: What happens to claims that are left out of an approved resolution plan?

They are extinguished. Section 31(1) makes an approved resolution plan binding on the corporate debtor, its employees, members and guarantors, and on the Central Government, State Governments and local authorities to whom statutory dues are owed. In Ghanshyam Mishra and Sons Private Limited v Edelweiss Asset Reconstruction Company Limited in 2021, the Supreme Court held that claims which are not part of the approved plan, including statutory dues owed to governments, stand frozen and extinguished once the plan is approved, and no proceedings for their recovery may continue against the revived company. The rule is the statutory clean slate: the resolution applicant takes the company free of the undisclosed and unfiled past, which is what makes a rescue priceable at all. The discipline it imposes is on creditors, who must file their claims in the process, since a claim that sleeps through the announcement and the plan will not survive the approval order.

### Q: Does the Code apply to banks and other financial service providers?

In its ordinary operation, no. The definition of a corporate person in Section 3(7) excludes a financial service provider, and the definitions in Sections 3(16) to 3(18) place banks, insurers, pension funds and other regulated financial firms outside the corporate insolvency process, on the reasoning that their failure raises systemic questions for their sectoral regulators rather than for a general creditor process. Section 227 is the exception: it empowers the Central Government, in consultation with the financial sector regulators, to notify financial service providers or categories of them for insolvency and liquidation under the Code with modifications. Acting on it, the government notified the insolvency framework for specified non-banking financial companies in November 2019, under which the Reserve Bank of India alone may initiate, an administrator replaces the insolvency professional, and the Reserve Bank referred Dewan Housing Finance Corporation Limited to the tribunal under that framework. A general financial resolution law has been proposed in the past and not enacted.

### Q: What is an information utility under the Code?

An information utility is a regulated repository of financial information created by Part IV of the Code. Creditors and debtors submit information about debts and defaults to the utility, which verifies and authenticates the submissions through the process the Board's regulations prescribe, stores the record, and issues records of default that creditors and tribunals can rely on. The design purpose is evidentiary: a financial creditor applying under Section 7 proves default most directly by the record of default of an information utility, which converts the admission hearing from a contest over documents into a verification of an authenticated record, suited to a process the statute wants concluded in fourteen days at the gate. The National E-Governance Services Limited was the first information utility registered by the Board. Utilities are companies registered and regulated by the Board, their core services and technical standards are prescribed, and submission of financial information to them is obligatory for the creditors the regulations specify.
