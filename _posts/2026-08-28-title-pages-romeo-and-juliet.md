Every early edition of Romeo and Juliet opens with a page that is not the play. Before the Prologue speaks its first line, "Two households, both alike in dignity" (Prologue.1), a title page has already made a series of claims: what kind of play this is, how often it has been performed, whose servants performed it, who printed the book, who financed it, and where a copy could be bought. Those pages are the oldest documents of the play's public existence, older than any review, any cast list, any diary entry, and they survive for every early edition: the first quarto of 1597, the second of 1599, the third of 1609, the fourth quarto in two distinct issues from about 1622, and, in transformed shape, the First Folio of 1623, where the play loses its own page altogether and takes a single line in a catalogue instead. Read one at a time, each page is an advertisement. Read as a sequence, the six pages compose the play's first biography, written not by a biographer but by the book trade, in the trade's own shorthand of formulas, billings, and imprints.

![Title pages of the early quartos of Romeo and Juliet - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The verdict of this article can be stated at once, because the evidence supports a clean division. The hard matter of the title pages, the printers, publishers, shop addresses, dates, and company billings, checks out against independent records wherever independent records exist, and can be trusted. The soft matter, the applause, the promises of correction and amendment, is sales language, written in formulas shared with dozens of other playbooks, and one famous boast turns out to be false in the letter even where it is fair in the spirit. The most eloquent evidence of all is neither the facts nor the puffery but the changes between one page and the next: a company billing that tracks a troupe's changes of patron, a venue named for the first time in 1609, an author's name that reaches the play only in the 1620s, a correction formula that outlives its meaning and hardens into part of the title itself. Title pages lie the way advertisements lie, in their adjectives. Their nouns, their names, dates, and places, are a record; and the differences between their successive versions are the closest thing the play has to a diary of its first twenty-five years.

That sequence also corrects a story often told too quickly. The third quarto of 1609 is sometimes described as the edition that dropped the second quarto's boast and first named the author. The pages say otherwise, and the pages are the documents: the 1609 page keeps "Newly corrected, augmented, and amended" word for word, and carries no author's name at all. The author's name arrives later and stranger, in the second of the fourth quarto's two issues, where "Written by W. Shake-speare" appears on some copies of an undated edition while other copies of the same edition go without it. Bibliography begins in that kind of precision, and the census below observes it throughout: what each page actually says, in its own spelling, before any argument is built on it.

The method deserves one sentence of its own, because everything that follows depends on it. Since Greg's Bibliography of the English Printed Drama, the discipline's standard has been quasi-facsimile transcription: the page copied line for line and letter for letter, its spelling, capitalization, and line breaks preserved, so that a reader who cannot stand before the surviving copies in London, Washington, or San Marino can still reason from the document rather than from a summary of it. The transcriptions in this article follow that standard, and the census table built from them is only as strong as the standard is strict. A summary would have merged the fourth quarto's two issues into one page and lost the author's arrival in the merging; the discipline exists to prevent exactly that loss.

The 1609 error shows the cost of the looser habit in its commonest form. A tidy narrative wanted the third quarto to mark a stage in the play's emancipation, the boast discarded, the author enthroned, and the tidy narrative was repeated from handbook to handbook because it improved the story at the price of the document. What the page actually holds is more interesting than the improvement: a publisher too economical to reset a formula he no longer needed to believe, a company renamed for a king, a theatre named for the first time, and an author still nowhere. Every correction the census makes in this article follows the same pattern. The documents are less symmetrical than the summaries built on them, and more informative, because their asymmetries are the facts: a boast retained past its truth, a name inserted on one leaf of an undated book, a venue admitted a decade late. Bibliography's subject is those asymmetries, and Romeo and Juliet, whose pages vary at exactly the points where its history turned, is the ideal school for the discipline.

## What Was a Playbook Title Page For in the Early Book Trade?

A title page in the hand-press book trade was a piece of commercial equipment before it was anything else. Books were commonly sold unbound, in sheets or in temporary stitching, and the title page was the sheet a prospective buyer saw first, whether the copy lay on a bookseller's stall, hung among other new books as an advertisement, or served as the wrapper of the folded sheets themselves. Its lines had to do several jobs in a few square inches: identify the work, classify it by genre, promise a quality or a novelty, attach it to whatever names carried weight in the market, and say where the book could be obtained and from whom. Every element on the early pages of Romeo and Juliet belongs to one of those jobs, and reading the pages well means assigning each line to its job before deciding how much belief it deserves.

### What part of the book did a browsing buyer actually see first?

The title page was the visible surface of an unbound book, the sheet displayed at the stall and often pasted up as an advertisement in its own right. A buyer in Paul's Churchyard or Fleet Street met the title page before the text, and usually instead of the text.

Playbooks were bought on the strength of the play's stage reputation and the page's promises rather than on inspection of the printing inside, a habit of the trade that shaped everything the page says and everything it leaves unsaid.

Playbooks occupied a peculiar corner of that trade. Peter W. M. Blayney, the bibliographer whose account of playbook publication remains the standard caution on the subject, has shown that plays made up a small share of the output of the London press, that they were cheap productions sold for a few pence, and that the companies who owned the manuscripts parted with them irregularly and for modest sums. The title page of a playbook therefore spoke to a market that already knew the commodity from the playhouse. Its performance claims are not descriptions written for scholars; they are the playhouse's reputation carried into print, compressed into a line. That origin explains both their value and their danger as evidence, the two qualities the rest of this article keeps separate.

The page worked at wholesale as well as at retail. Copies moved from the publisher's shop to other booksellers in London and the provinces in bundles, and the title page was the form in which an edition announced itself to the trade that would stock it: the same sheet that faced the browsing buyer faced the provincial bookseller deciding his order, which is one reason its language is the trade's common stock rather than any publisher's invention. An imprint naming a single shop, Burby's near the Exchange or Smethwick's under the Dial, never meant the book could be had nowhere else; it named the source from which the trade itself was supplied, the address at the root of the edition's distribution. The title page is thus a document with two audiences and two corresponding registers, persuasion for the reader and identification for the trade, and the census finds its most reliable lines exactly where the trade audience, which could verify them, was listening.

The vocabulary of the imprint, the block of trade information at the foot of the page, is exact and worth learning, because the quartos of this play use its variants to tell a property story. "Printed by" names the printer, the craftsman who owned the press and set the type. "Printed for" names the publisher, the stationer who financed the edition, owned the copy, the right to print the work, and took the commercial risk. "Are to be sold at" gives the retail outlet, located not by a numbered address, which did not exist, but by the painted sign of the shop, the Exchange, the Dial, and by the churchyard or street where the sign hung. A page that names a printer only, as the 1597 quarto does, and a page that names a publisher only, as the 1609 and later quartos do, are making different disclosures, and the difference matters: the publisher is the man whose property the play had become, and the sequence of publishers on these pages is the sequence of the play's owners in print.

One further piece of trade machinery stands behind the pages: the Stationers' Register, in which a stationer entered his claim to a copy. Romeo and Juliet was never entered before its first editions appeared, a silence the first quarto's page shares with many playbooks of the 1590s; the Register speaks about this play only in 1607, when the copy changed hands twice in a single year. The title pages must therefore carry evidentiary weight that, for better-documented books, the Register would share, and their imprint lines are the primary record of who printed and who owned the play at each appearance.

The economics underneath the rhetoric were modest, and modesty shaped the pages. A playbook retailed for about sixpence, the price of a seat in the yard several times over but small change in a bookshop, and an edition ran to the hundreds or low thousands of copies, printed on cheap paper with minimal correction. No publisher grew rich on one play; the profit lay in steady sellers and in the ownership of copies that could be reprinted across decades, which is precisely the asset the imprints of this play track. Because the sums were small, the title page had to work without assistance: the quartos of Romeo and Juliet carry no dedication, no epistle to the reader, no commendatory verses, none of the prefatory machinery with which a poem or a history courted its buyers. The contrast with the author's own narrative poems is sharp. Venus and Adonis and The Rape of Lucrece had appeared in 1593 and 1594 with dedications to the Earl of Southampton signed with the writer's full name, among the most personal documents of his career. The poems were signed offerings; the plays were unsigned stock, and their pages gave the space a dedication would have filled to the company, the claim, and the shop.

The survival of the evidence is as fragile as the books were cheap. The cheapest print of any age is the least likely to be kept, and the early editions of this play survive in handfuls of copies: five of the first quarto are recorded, and the other quartos in numbers that, while larger, still reduce each edition's page to a small set of physical witnesses, scattered among the great collections. Bibliography's care over transcription is the answer to that fragility. Where an edition's whole testimony may rest on the title leaves of a dozen copies, each leaf must be described as though it were unique, because for evidentiary purposes it nearly is, and the variant issue of the fourth quarto, where the author's name stands on some leaves and not on others, could only have been recovered by a discipline that treats single leaves as documents in their own right.

## The First Quarto's Title Page: "An Excellent Conceited Tragedie" of 1597

The first page in the sequence is also the most theatrical. In the spelling of the original it reads: "AN EXCELLENT conceited Tragedie OF Romeo and Iuliet. As it hath been often (with great applause) plaid publiquely, by the right Honourable the L. of Hunsdon his Seruants. LONDON, Printed by Iohn Danter. 1597." Every clause earns its place. The title offers a genre, tragedy; a quality, excellent; and a stylistic promise, conceited, a word whose period sense of ingeniously wrought belongs to the vocabulary of praise. The lovers are named, with the heroine spelled Iuliet, the form the early pages prefer before the modern J settles in. Then comes the performance claim, the fullest in the whole sequence: the play has been played publicly, often, and with great applause. Then the billing, the right Honourable the Lord of Hunsdon his servants. Then, in the imprint, a printer and a date, and nothing else: no publisher, no shop, no author.

### What does the phrase "with great applause" claim, and who was expected to believe it?

The phrase claims that the play had already succeeded in the playhouse, repeatedly and publicly, and it was aimed at the book buyer at a stall, for whom stage success was the readiest warrant that a sixpenny quarto was worth its price.

The claim cost the publisher nothing to make and could not be checked by the reader, which is why applause language appears on playbook title pages across the decade, as regular in its way as the imprints beneath it.

One word in the performance claim locates the play socially as precisely as the billing locates it in time. The play was "plaid publiquely": the claim is not performance in the abstract but performance in the public playhouse, before a paying crowd whose applause the parenthesis then certifies. The word draws the period's great distinction between the commercial amphitheatre and private playing at court or in great houses, and the contrast with a sibling page is instructive: Love's Labour's Lost, published the next year, sold itself as "presented before her Highnes this last Christmas", a court credential for a courtly comedy. The tragedy of the lovers is sold the opposite way, on the crowd's verdict in the open theatre. Each page chose the witness its buyers would credit, and the choice is itself evidence about where each play's reputation was understood to live.

The billing is the line on the page with the longest scholarly afterlife. The Lord of Hunsdon's servants were the company known before and after by another style. Henry Carey, first Lord Hunsdon, who as Lord Chamberlain had been the company's patron, died in July 1596; his son George Carey, second Lord Hunsdon, held the patronage until his own appointment as Lord Chamberlain in 1597 restored the company's customary style of the Lord Chamberlain's servants. A title page billing the company as Hunsdon's servants therefore belongs, in strictness, to the interval between those events, and bibliographers have used the line to corroborate a first performance of the play in the middle 1590s, before the quarto itself. The page does not date the play's composition, and no responsible reading asks it to; it certifies the name under which the play was being performed when the book went to press, and that name was already history by the time many copies were sold. The year of publication adds a footnote the page could not foresee: in the summer of 1597 the London playhouses stood closed for a season in the suppression that followed the Isle of Dogs scandal, and the claim of frequent public playing was circulating in print while public playing was suspended. Formulas do not consult calendars; that is the whole of their limitation, stated by the calendar itself.

The imprint's economy is as telling as the billing. John Danter, named as printer, began the book, and the printing was finished in the shop of Edward Allde, who is nowhere named on the page; the standard bibliographical account of the edition assigns the opening sheets to Danter and the remainder to Allde. Shared printing was a normal expedient for speed, two shops dividing an edition's sheets to halve the time at press, and bibliographers detect the seam in changes of type, ornament, and presswork at the handover point. In this book the seam invites a biographical reading. Danter had form in the playbook trade and a reputation for haste, and the shared, hurried manufacture matches what the text inside shows: a short version of the play, roughly four-fifths the length of the familiar text, whose status as a reported or memorially reconstructed text has been debated ever since. The page and the book agree with each other. A company billing already superseded, a printer of doubtful regularity, no publisher's name, no entrance in the Stationers' Register: the first quarto's title page is the portrait of an edition produced at the edge of the trade's formal machinery, selling a stage success it names but does not document. The full case on that text, its origins, and the fairness of its traditional label is made in the study of [the first quarto of 1597, the so-called "bad" quarto](/2026/03/27/first-quarto-1597-bad-quarto/), and is taken as read here; the page is this article's exhibit, the text is that article's.

The disproportion of the page's importance becomes clear against the documentary silence around the company. For the rival Admiral's Men, Philip Henslowe's diary preserves payments, inventories, and performance records in day-by-day detail; for the Lord Chamberlain's Men, no equivalent archive survives, no diary, no account book, no bundle of playhouse papers. The historian of this play's first performances works from a handful of external notices, Meres among them, from the texts, and from the title pages, which accordingly carry a weight for the Chamberlain's repertory that they carry for no other company of the decade. A billing that for another troupe would corroborate a diary must here substitute for one, and the care bibliographers lavish on these few lines is proportioned to the darkness around them.

Danter's name connects the page to the decade's other great anonymous tragedy. Three years earlier he had printed the first quarto of Titus Andronicus for Edward White and Thomas Millington, another company play issued without an author and billed to its performers' patrons, in that case two companies in succession on one page. The two books are the twin monuments of the mid-1590s playbook trade at its most irregular: texts of permanent importance reaching print as cheap company merchandise, their makers unnamed, their pages asserting performance because performance was the only pedigree the trade thought a buyer required. That the 1597 Romeo and Juliet belongs to this small family, and not to the respectable world of entered copies and named publishers that 1599 would give it, is written across its page in every particular, from the shared printing to the absent imprint furniture. The first quarto's page is not a failed version of the second quarto's; it is a perfect specimen of an earlier settlement between stage and print, one the play was about to outgrow.

The page's billing can be checked against a sibling page of the same season, and the comparison shows how specific it is. The first quarto of Richard III, also published in 1597, bills its play to "the Right honourable the Lord Chamberlaine his seruants" and names its printer, Valentine Sims, and its publisher, Andrew Wise. Two plays from the same company, in the same year, carry different patrons on their pages, because the Richard III copy reached print under the resumed Chamberlain style while the Romeo and Juliet copy preserves the intervening Hunsdon style. Boilerplate would have levelled the two pages; the record did not. Whoever supplied the copy for the 1597 Romeo and Juliet supplied its billing from the play's performance life, not from a stationer's stock of phrases, and that is the strongest internal sign that the page's company line, at least, was drawn from the playhouse rather than the printing house.

The title's one distinctive word worked the same trade circuit. "Conceited", in the period sense of ingeniously devised, was current coin for clever workmanship: the next year it fronts another company play, "A Pleasant Conceited Comedie called, Loues labors lost". The word promised wit in the making, a play built with artifice the buyer could admire in the reading. Its presence in 1597 and its disappearance in 1599, treated below as a change of formula, also marks a change in what the book claimed to be: the first page sells a piece of workmanship, the second sells a corrected and lamentable tragedy, and the shift from craft to pathos is the shift from the playhouse's vocabulary to the bookshop's.

No author's name appears, and the absence deserves a cooler reading than it sometimes gets. In 1597 the commercial identity of a playbook was the play and the company, not the writer. The page gives the buyer a tragedy, a reputation, and a troupe; the man who wrote the dialogue was not yet a selling point for this play, whatever his standing in the playhouse. The sequence of later pages measures exactly when that changed, and it changed later than is often supposed.

## The Second Quarto's Title Page: A New Formula and the Famous Boast of 1599

Two years later the play came back to press wearing a new face. The 1599 page reads: "THE MOST EXCELLENT and lamentable Tragedie, of Romeo and Iuliet. Newly corrected, augmented, and amended: As it hath bene sundry times publiquely acted, by the right Honourable the Lord Chamberlaine his Seruants. LONDON Printed by Thomas Creede, for Cuthbert Burby, and are to be sold at his shop neere the Exchange. 1599." Three changes announce themselves at once. Even the genre word does quiet work across the first two pages. Brooke had called his poem a "Tragicall Historye", history being the novella tradition's claim to truth, and Painter's collection likewise trafficked in histories. The 1597 page calls the play a "Tragedie" outright, enrolling the book in the playhouse's genre system rather than the tale-tellers'; the word is a stage word, promising not a true relation but a performed catastrophe, and every subsequent page keeps it. The play entered print already translated, from the literature of its sources into the literature of the theatre, and the translation is announced in the title's last word before the lovers are even named.

The title has been rebuilt: "An Excellent conceited Tragedie" gives way to "The Most Excellent and lamentable Tragedie", a superlative formula with a new adjective, lamentable, that advertises the ending as a feature, promising the buyer the pathos of the tomb in the title itself. The performance claim has been trimmed: the play has been acted publicly sundry times, and the parenthetical great applause of 1597 has been dropped, as though frequency alone now carried the point. And the imprint has become complete and commercial: a printer, Thomas Creede; a publisher, Cuthbert Burby; a retail address at Burby's shop near the Royal Exchange; and a date.

Between the title and the performance claim stands the most argued-over line in the play's bibliography: "Newly corrected, augmented, and amended". As a piece of salesmanship it is perfectly aimed. It tells the owner of the 1597 quarto that the earlier book was defective and that this one repairs it; it tells the new buyer that the fullest and best text is this one. Collation gives the claim a mixed report. Augmented is plain fact: the 1599 text runs to roughly three thousand lines against the first quarto's twenty-two hundred, restoring, among much else, the full architecture of the balcony scene and the Queen Mab speech at the length readers know, "O, then I see Queen Mab hath been with you" (1.4.57) and all that follows it. Corrected is largely fair: hundreds of manifest errors of the earlier print disappear. Amended is where the page overreaches, since the new text carries faults of its own that no amended edition should carry, most famously a passage in the dawn parting that the printer set twice in rival forms, leaving the author's alternatives standing side by side in the printed book. The boast is therefore best read as trade rhetoric that happens to point at a real superiority, a claim whose three verbs deserve three different grades. That trial belongs to the article that puts [the second quarto's boast, "newly corrected, augmented and amended", on the stand](/2026/08/07/newly-corrected-augmented-amended-q2/); here the line stands as the sequence's pivotal datum, the moment a title page stops merely announcing a play and starts arguing about texts.

The augmentation the verb promises can be mapped, and the map shows a publisher who knew his selling points. The restored material is not padding at the margins but the play's most famous architecture: the second Chorus sonnet, absent as a full set piece from the earlier print; the balcony scene at its full lyric extent; the Queen Mab speech entire; the Friar's scenes at full argumentative length; the death scenes with their complete sequence of entrances in the tomb. A buyer who collated the two quartos in the shop, as the boast invited, would find the promise kept at every famous place. Whoever worded the claim had the measure of both texts, which is why the boast persuaded generations of bibliographers: it is the work of a publisher describing, in the superlative idiom of his trade, an advantage his edition genuinely possessed, and extending the description by one verb beyond what his compositors had delivered.

Set the two imprints side by side and the 1599 page reads as a certificate of regularity. The 1597 page had named a printer and stopped: no publisher to own the edition, no shop to sell it, no entrance to register it. The 1599 page completes every element the trade possessed, printer, publisher, retail address, date, current billing, and the completeness is part of the edition's argument before a word of the text is reached: this is a financed, owned, and answerable book. Title pages could certify respectability as well as promise quality, and the recovery of the play's text and the recovery of its bibliographical manners are the same event, announced on the same sheet. The play's second edition is the moment it becomes a normal piece of trade property, with a known owner whose rights could later be assigned, inherited, and litigated, the precondition for everything the Register records in 1607.

The new title formula also re-shelved the play. "Lamentable" is the vocabulary of the prose tragedy trade, the world of the novella collections from which the story itself had come through Brooke's "Tragicall Historye of Romeus and Juliet" and Painter's rendering of the tale. A buyer who knew the story from the tale collections met in 1599 a title that spoke their language: not a conceited piece of stage workmanship but a most excellent and lamentable tragedy, a book of doomed love whose ending the title itself advertises as the attraction. The lovers in the tomb are the play's last image, and the Prince closes the action by naming the commodity the new title sells: "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3.309-310). The 1599 page markets precisely that woe, where the 1597 page had marketed applause and artifice.

The company billing has moved with the times: the Lord Chamberlain his servants, the style restored when George Carey took the chamberlainship in 1597. The synchrony is quiet but important. Where the 1597 page preserved a superseded billing, the 1599 page is current, and its currency matches the edition's general air of regularity: entered ownership implied by a named publisher of standing, a complete imprint, a respectable printer in Creede, whose shop produced much of the decade's drama. Burby was a substantial stationer, and his name on the page is a commercial fact of the first order, because the copy he published in 1599 remained the root of title for every later edition: the rights in Romeo and Juliet that changed hands in 1607 were Burby's rights. The author, however, is still missing. A reader of 1599 who wanted to know who wrote the play learned from this page that Creede printed it, Burby sold it, and the Lord Chamberlain's servants acted it. Print had not yet learned to sell this play by its writer.

The omission cannot be charged to the trade's innocence of authorial naming, because Burby's own list refutes it. The year before, in 1598, the same publisher had issued Love's Labour's Lost with the line "Lastely corrected and augmented By W. Shakespere", the first appearance of the dramatist's name on the title page of one of his plays, formula and attribution together. Naming was therefore available, current, and Burby's own practice when the copy called for it. The 1599 Romeo and Juliet, escalating the correction formula beyond even the Love's Labour's Lost wording, nevertheless stops short of the name. The likeliest reading is commercial rather than mysterious: the earlier edition had sold the play as the company's, the new edition's argument was textual, a better book of a known play, and the author's name, worth a line on a comedy being launched as corrected, added nothing the boast had not already claimed for a tragedy being relaunched against its own first edition. The pages measure the market's valuation of authorship play by play, and in 1599 this play's name was still worth less than its verbs.

Burby's address adds a geography to the transaction. His shop stood near the Royal Exchange, in the City's richest retail ground, among the merchants and the luxury trades; a playbook bought there was bought in the course of City business, by the class that filled the playhouse galleries and bought the tale collections the title's "lamentable" courted. Smethwick's Dial, a decade later, stood in St Dunstan's churchyard in Fleet Street, in the bookselling quarter that served the Inns of Court and the western parishes. The play's two great retail homes face two different Londons, the merchant's and the lawyer's, and the imprints record the migration without comment: the census of addresses is also a census of audiences, the readership of the tragedy widening westward as the century turned.

## Reading a Page Line by Line: The 1599 Page Dissected

A title page argues with its typography before its words are read, and the 1599 page is the sequence's most accomplished piece of visual rhetoric, worth taking apart line by line. The eye meets the superlative first: "THE MOST EXCELLENT" set large, the rank claimed before the thing ranked. Then the genre and its new epithet, "and lamentable Tragedie", and then the names, "of Romeo and Iuliet", the lovers given the page's most intimate scale, the title settling from shout to statement across its first four lines. A reader who stopped there would know the play's kind, its quality as the publisher graded it, and its subject. The boast follows in a visually subordinate position, "Newly corrected, augmented, and amended", italicized in the original, the type itself marking the line as a different register of discourse, a trade assurance rather than a title. Then the performance claim and the billing, set as prose fact; then the imprint, the smallest matter on the page, where the commercial machinery signs itself.

The hierarchy is a map of the publisher's confidence. What is largest is what he most needs believed before purchase: that the play is excellent, lamentable, and about Romeo and Iuliet. What is italicized is a warranty offered to the trade and to owners of the earlier book. What is smallest is what he expects to be taken on trust, and it is precisely the smallest matter, printer, publisher, shop, and date, that four centuries of cross-checking have confirmed in every particular. The page's visual order inverts its evidentiary order, and learning to read a playbook title page is largely learning to perform that inversion: the quieter the line, the harder the fact.

Set against the 1597 page, the design has also grown calmer. The earlier page crowds its claims, the excellence, the conceit, the applause, the performance, the billing, in a rush of assertion suited to a book with no publisher's name to underwrite it. The 1599 page spaces its elements like a document that expects to be believed, each claim on its own line, the white space of a tradesman who no longer needs to shout. Design follows credit. The sequence's later pages hold the 1599 settlement almost line for line, and the Folio, enlarging the unit from the play to the author, repeats the gesture at monument scale, its engraved portrait occupying the visual summit the superlative once held. To read the pages as designs is to watch the play's credit rating rise across twenty-six years, from the crowded assertions of an unowned venture to the spaced confidence of a classic.

## The Third Quarto's Title Page: A King's Company and a Named Theatre in 1609

The third quarto of 1609 is where a careless summary most often parts company with the document, so the page must be set out in full: "THE MOST EXCELLENT AND Lamentable Tragedie, of Romeo and Juliet. As it hath beene sundrie times publiquely Acted, by the KINGS Maiesties Seruants at the Globe. Newly corrected, augmented, and amended: LONDON Printed for IOHN SMETHVVICK, and are to be sold at his Shop in Saint Dunstanes Church-yard, in Fleetestreete vnder the Dyall, 1609." Read against 1599, the changes are three, and none of them is the change most often reported. The correction formula has not been dropped; it stands word for word, inherited from the second quarto although the 1609 text is a reprint of that quarto and corrected nothing in any systematic sense. The author's name has not appeared; there is no "Written by" line on the page. What has changed is the frame around the inherited words: the company is now the King's Majesty's servants, the style the troupe bore after the royal patent of May 1603 made them the King's Men; the billing adds, for the first time in the play's print history, a named venue, the Globe; and the publisher is new.

### What changed in the company line between 1599 and 1609?

The troupe passed from a lord's patronage to the king's. The patent of 1603 licensed the Lord Chamberlain's servants as the King's Men, and the 1609 page registers the promotion in the play's billing: no longer a nobleman's servants but the King's Majesty's servants.

The same billing adds, in a novelty for this play's pages, a named playhouse, the Globe on Bankside, where the company had performed since 1599.

The new publisher explains the new page. The Stationers' Register records the copy's journey with unusual completeness: in January 1607 Cuthbert Burby's rights in the play passed to Nicholas Ling, and in November of the same year Ling transferred them, among a parcel of plays, to John Smethwick. Smethwick's 1609 edition is the fruit of that purchase, and its imprint is a small geography of his business: printed for John Smethwick, to be sold at his shop in St Dunstan's churchyard in Fleet Street, under the Dial, the painted sign by which the shop was known. The printer is not named; the book's types and ornaments assign the work to the shop of John Windet, Smethwick's regular printer. Where Burby's page had given the trade's full cast of printer, publisher, and shop, Smethwick's gives the property owner and the point of sale, and lets the manufacture go unsigned. The play as a book now belongs to a named man's stock, in a named churchyard, under a named sign, and it will stay in that stock for decades.

Smethwick's parcel had made him, almost accidentally, the custodian of the age's two most durable tragedies in print. The same Ling assignments that brought him Romeo and Juliet brought him Hamlet, whose quartos he issued from 1611 onward, printed for him by William Stansby and others under the same churchyard address. The two plays' pages in his hands make an instructive pair: the Hamlet quartos experiment with attribution and formula across their issues, while the Romeo and Juliet page, the boast and billing once settled, is reissued nearly unaltered for twenty-eight years. Smethwick evidently knew which of his properties profited from novelty and which from familiarity. The lovers' tragedy was his annuity, and an annuity's paperwork does not change.

The Register trail behind the edition deserves a closer look, because it shows how plays moved as assets. Burby's assignment to Ling in January 1607 and Ling's assignment to Smethwick that November, the latter covering sixteen titles in a single entry, were portfolio transactions: play copies changed hands in parcels, valued as stock rather than as literature, and the receiving stationer bought the right to keep proven sellers in print. Smethwick's subsequent conduct fits the asset exactly. He held Romeo and Juliet for thirty years, issued it three times in quarto, and guarded the formula and billing that identified his property, varying only what time forced, the company's royal style, and what the market rewarded, eventually the author's name. A modern publisher's backlist management, conducted in churchyard geography and painted signs.

The third quarto's genealogical importance outruns its reputation, and its page is where the disproportion begins. Because the fourth quarto reprinted the third and the Folio was set from an annotated copy of the third, the text of 1609 became the conduit through which the play's words, spelling, and punctuation flowed into the collected edition and from there into the whole later tradition; readings and errors born in Smethwick's reprint were still being solemnly edited centuries afterward. The page that announces the least, a straight reissue wearing an inherited boast, is therefore the ancestor page of the received text, a reminder that in textual history influence flows through availability, not merit, and that the census of pages and the genealogy of texts are the same map read at different scales.

The 1609 text also exposes, in its manufacture, the distance the page could travel from the sheets behind it. The edition is a page-for-page reprint of its predecessor, ninety-two pages answering to ninety-two, set from a copy of the 1599 quarto; its compositors corrected a reading here and there and introduced new errors elsewhere, the ordinary drift of reprint work. Nothing in that process answers to "corrected, augmented, and amended" in any sense a collator would sign. The page even repeats the performance formula, "sundrie times publiquely Acted", in a period when plague had closed the London playhouses for long stretches and the King's Men were as often on tour or at court as on the Globe's stage. A claim that can be printed unchanged through a plague year is not reporting performances; it is wearing the play's livery.

The retention of the boast deserves emphasis, because it changes what the boast means. In 1599 "Newly corrected, augmented, and amended" was a claim about work done on a defective predecessor. In 1609 the same words describe no work at all: Smethwick's compositors reprinted the 1599 text, introducing fresh errors as readily as they removed old ones. A claim that persists unchanged when its truth has lapsed has stopped being a claim and become part of the title, a formula the trade attached to this play the way it attached "most excellent and lamentable". Bibliographers who quote the 1609 page as though its verbs still testified are reading an advertisement as an affidavit. The page's real testimony is elsewhere: in the royal billing, the named Globe, and the Dial in Fleet Street, three facts that fix the play in 1609 as a King's Men's property in performance and a Smethwick property in print, still running, still selling, ten years after the edition that made its text.

## The Fourth Quarto's Two Title Pages: The Undated Pair of About 1622

The fourth quarto multiplies the evidence by existing twice. Its title page carries no date in either of its forms, and bibliographers assign the edition to about 1622, with R. Carter Hailey arguing on typographical evidence for 1623; the two forms, distinguished as issues of one edition, differ in exactly one line, and the line is the most consequential in the whole census. The first issue's page follows the 1609 pattern: the most excellent and lamentable tragedy, acted sundry times publicly by the King's Majesty's servants at the Globe, "Newly corrected, augmented, and amended", printed for John Smethwick, to be sold at his shop in St Dunstan's churchyard under the Dial. The second issue inserts, between the Globe line and the inherited boast, a line no earlier page of this play had carried: "Written by W. Shake-speare."

### What does an undated title page suggest about how the edition was sold?

An undated page suggests an edition meant to sell steadily rather than seasonally, a backlist book whose publisher saw no advantage in proclaiming its year and some risk in letting the printed stock look old before the sheets had sold through.

Smethwick had held the copy since 1607 and the play sold across decades; a page without a date could front the sheets for as long as the sheets lasted, and the two issues show him refreshing the book's face without resetting its text.

The physical mechanics of the insertion repay attention, because they show how cheaply a book's identity could be revised. An issue is created when the publisher cancels the original title leaf, cutting it out and pasting or sewing in a replacement printed for the purpose; surviving copies can betray the operation in the leaf's attachment, its paper, and its type. The economics are the argument in miniature. Resetting a play cost a printing house weeks; replacing a leaf cost an afternoon, and for that afternoon's work Smethwick bought the play a new identity as an authored book, aligned with the Folio venture then repricing authorship across the trade. Bibliographers who distinguish the issues are not splitting hairs; they are recovering the exact moment, priced in a single leaf, at which this play's name acquired an author in the eyes of the market that sold it.

The insertion matters more than its placement, modest as the placement is. For twenty-five years the play had gone through three editions and four title pages as a company's play and a stationer's property, its author's name absent from every one. Print culture outside the playhouse had known the name for years: as early as 1598, Francis Meres had listed "his Romeo and Juliet" among the tragedies of "the most excellent" English dramatist in Palladis Tamia, attaching the play to Shakespeare in print while its own pages still billed only servants and stationers. The second issue of the fourth quarto is the moment the play's own title page catches up with that knowledge. The name arrives hyphenated, Shake-speare, set as one trade element among others, smaller than the boast it precedes; yet its arrival reorders the page's meaning. Every earlier element, the genre formula, the performance claim, the billing, the imprint, describes the play as an event and a commodity. "Written by" introduces a maker, and with a maker the book begins its long migration from playbook to literature, the migration the Folio would complete a year or so later by gathering the plays under the name as a collected works.

The undated page forced bibliography to earn the date it quotes. Hailey's argument for 1623 rests on the printing shop's material biography: the progressive damage visible in the types and ornaments the edition shares with dated books from the same house, which lets an undated book be placed in the shop's sequence the way tree rings place a beam. The method matters beyond this edition, because the early 1620s are crowded with undated Smethwick quartos, and a year either way changes the story an edition tells. Placed in about 1622, the fourth quarto is the Folio's contemporary and competitor, a last quarto issued as the collected edition gathered; placed in 1623, it is the Folio's stablemate, printed in the very season the monument appeared. The two issues complicate the choice further, since the issue carrying the author's name belongs most naturally to the moment when the name's value was being repriced by the Folio venture itself, a venture in which, as the colophon of the Folio records, Smethwick was himself a partner.

The coexistence of the two issues is itself a datum about the trade's pace. An issue, in the bibliographer's sense, is a re-marketing of sheets already printed, distinguished by an altered title leaf; Smethwick, or the trade, evidently judged at some point in the edition's life that the author's name had become worth printing, and spent a single leaf on the improvement rather than a new edition. The play's text inside did not change to match: the fourth quarto reprints the third, with a scattering of corrections that show consultation of the first quarto. The face modernized, the body aged; the pattern of the whole sequence in miniature.

The edition behind the refreshed face holds one more irony for the census. The fourth quarto's text, while reprinting the third as its base, was corrected in places by consultation of the first quarto, the despised 1597 print, whose readings its printers evidently had on the desk and used. The edition that first puts the author's name on the play thus also marks the first quarto's quiet rehabilitation as a textual witness, decades before editors formalized the practice of adopting its readings where the later tradition errs. The name and the rehabilitation belong to the same moment in the play's print life: the early 1620s, when the preparations for the collected edition were teaching the trade to treat these old playbooks as an author's papers, to be gathered, compared, and credited, rather than as a company's leavings, to be reprinted until the type wore smooth.

A brief coda completes the quarto line. In 1637 a fifth quarto appeared, printed by R. Young for John Smethwick, its page carrying both the attribution, "Written by W. Shake-speare", and, beneath it, the by then venerable "Newly corrected, augmented, and amended", still unretired after thirty-eight years. The formula coined to bury the 1597 text had become a permanent heraldic device, and its survival on the 1637 page is the cleanest proof in the census that title-page verbs, given time, testify only to themselves.

## The First Folio's Catalogue: A Play with No Title Page of Its Own

In 1623 the play entered the book by which posterity would know it, and its reward was to lose its title page entirely. The First Folio, Mr. William Shakespeares Comedies, Histories, & Tragedies, spends its one title page on the author and the corpus: the Droeshout portrait, the three genres, and the claim "Published according to the True Originall Copies", a truth formula for thirty-six plays at once, the lineal descendant of the correction boasts of the quarto pages and applied with the same confidence to texts of very different descent. Inside, Romeo and Juliet appears among the Tragedies, its presence registered by a line in the volume's Catalogue and by the head title over its first page, "The Tragedie of Romeo and Juliet", where the text begins on the fifty-third page of the Tragedies section. No company is billed. No printer of the play is named, no shop, no date, no applause. The apparatus of selling that every quarto page carried has been struck away, because the Folio's buyer was not being sold a play but a monument.

### Why does the Folio spend its one title page on the author instead of the plays?

Because the Folio's commodity was the author himself. The quarto pages sold a performance and a text; the Folio sold a collected Shakespeare, "according to the True Originall Copies", and its title page therefore carries the portrait and the name where a playbook page carried a billing and a boast.

The play that entered the volume surrendered its own advertising surface and gained, in exchange, a permanent place in an author's works.

The Folio's own front matter continues the title-page wars by other means. John Heminges and Henry Condell, the company's sharers who fronted the volume, addressed their readers against the earlier editions in terms any quarto publisher would have recognized as hostile advertising: the plays, they wrote, had previously reached print in "stolne and surreptitious copies, maimed, and deformed by the frauds and stealthes of iniurious impostors", while the Folio offered them "cur'd, and perfect of their limbes". The sentence is the correction boast promoted to a corpus, and it deserves the same grading: some Folio texts were indeed new and authoritative, others, this play's among them, were reprints of the very quartos the sentence disparages. Pollard later built the phrase "bad quarto" in the neighbourhood of this rhetoric, a reminder that the folio's marketing did not merely sell a book but supplied the vocabulary in which its rivals would be judged for three centuries.

Even the portrait submits to the trial's grading. The Droeshout engraving that fronts the volume is the Folio title page's visual claim, a face offered as the guarantee of the works, and it is a claim of the same family as the quarto pages' verbs: a conventional image, worked up for the book trade, of a man seven years dead, whose likeness no collation can certify. The volume that opens by promising the true original copies opens its title page with an unverifiable representation, and the parallel is not a scandal but a summary of the whole subject. Every early presentation of this play, from Danter's crowded sheet to the Jaggards' monument, mixes document and assertion in proportions the reader must separate; the Folio differs from the quartos not in kind but in scale, and in the permanence its scale achieved.

The Folio's preliminaries can be read as the playbook page's functions redistributed and dignified. The portrait on the title page does the work the company billing once did, supplying the identity under which the commodity is offered, except that the identity is now a man rather than a troupe. The commendatory verses do the work of the applause clause, except that the applause is signed: Ben Jonson's great memorial poem and the verses of Hugh Holland, Leonard Digges, and others stand where "(with great applause)" once stood, testimony with names attached, offered by named witnesses a reader could weigh. The epistle's disparagement of the earlier copies does the work of the correction boast. Nothing was abolished; everything was promoted. The quarto page had been a tradesman's handbill for a play; the folio's front matter is a monument's inscription for an author, and Romeo and Juliet is the play whose documents let both forms be watched at the moment of exchange, since its publisher stood in both ventures and its text passed from the one to the other.

The volume's ownership completes the circle the imprints began. The Folio's colophon records that the book was printed at the charges of William Jaggard, Edward Blount, John Smethwick, and William Aspley: the play's own publisher, the stationer under the Dial who had owned the copy since 1607, sat inside the syndicate that produced the monument. His presence explains the text's descent as no conspiracy could. The Folio's Romeo and Juliet was set from an annotated copy of the third quarto because the copy's owner was at the table, and the asset he had managed in quarto for sixteen years passed, with his participation, into the collected works. The title page that carries no billing for the play thus conceals the most continuous ownership in the play's print history: one stationer's stock, from the 1609 quarto to the 1623 Folio.

Even the play's position in the volume was settled by manufacture rather than judgement. Charlton Hinman's analysis of the Folio's printing showed that the order in which the plays were set and imposed followed the availability and condition of the copy the syndicate had assembled, with interruptions where rights or copy failed; the sequence a reader meets in the Tragedies, Coriolanus, Titus Andronicus, then this play, is a printing-house artefact before it is a critical statement. Canonization, at the moment it happened, looked like logistics: gatherings signed and imposed, copy bought in from the men who owned it, a catalogue compiled to match the sheets. The title pages of the quartos, for all their puffery, at least addressed the play directly; the Folio absorbed it, and the absorption was transacted in the same currency of copies and charges that the imprints had always recorded.

The exchange defines the endpoint of the biography the quarto pages began. The Folio text of the play descends from the quarto line, set from a copy of the third quarto apparently annotated with reference to the fourth, and an annotated quarto is a modest ancestor for a text prefaced by "True Originall Copies"; the claim must be graded like its quarto ancestors, as a formula of the house rather than a collation report. Yet what the Folio withholds in apparatus it returns in classification. The Catalogue's single line places the play in a genre, in an order, and in a body of work: the lovers' story, which the 1597 page had offered as an excellent conceited tragedy acted by a lord's servants, now stands in the national author's works as "The Tragedie of Romeo and Juliet", spelled with the old I in the head title, allotted its twenty-seven pages between Titus Andronicus and Timon of Athens in the Tragedies' sequence. The Catalogue's accuracy about this play is itself a small datum: the same list famously omits Troilus and Cressida altogether, a casualty of the volume's troubled printing, while the lovers' tragedy stands duly entered in its place. Classification is a form of care, and the care the Folio took over this play, whatever the ancestry of its text, was real: it was counted, headed, and shelved among the tragedies of the works, and it has never been uncounted since. The Prince's closing couplet had promised that "never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3.309-310); the Folio's catalogue line is where print first treats that story as an item in a canon, to be shelved, cited, and inherited rather than merely applauded and sold.

## The Men in the Imprints: Printers and Publishers of the Sequence

The census is also a group portrait of the trade, because each imprint preserves the men who handled the play and the order in which they handled it. John Danter, the first name in the sequence, was a printer of cheap and popular literature whose shop turned out ballads, news, and playbooks at speed; the early quartos of the drama passed through his presses repeatedly, and his name on a page of the 1590s signals the popular end of the market. Edward Allde, who finished the 1597 volume without being named, belonged to the same world of high-volume trade printing, a producer of the ephemeral print in which the decade's plays first clothed themselves. Between them they made a book exactly as regular as its text: serviceable, fast, and answerable to no publisher's reputation, since no publisher stood behind it.

Thomas Creede, who printed the 1599 quarto, stands a step higher in the trade's hierarchy: a substantial printing house that executed much of the period's drama for the publishers who owned it, competent, capitalized, and chosen by stationers who expected an edition to last. His presence on the page is part of the second quarto's certificate of regularity, the manufacturing equivalent of Burby's name beside it. Cuthbert Burby himself, bookseller near the Royal Exchange, was a publisher with a list and a future in the company's governance of the Stationers, and his acquisition of the play, followed by the parcel transfers of 1607, shows the copy behaving as copies behaved, an asset traded among professionals who valued it without sentiment. Nicholas Ling, the intermediate owner of 1607, was one of the leading literary publishers of the age, the stationer of the Hamlet quartos, and his brief holding of the play between two assignments in a single year has the look of a speculation or a settlement rather than a publishing programme.

John Smethwick dominates the portrait by duration. From the November 1607 assignment until his death in the 1640s the play was his: three quarto editions across twenty-eight years, a share in the Folio that canonized it, and a shop, under the Dial in St Dunstan's churchyard, that functioned as the play's retail home for a generation of buyers. His imprint's stability is the point. While the company's billing changed above his name and the author's name eventually appeared above his boast, "printed for Iohn Smethwicke" and the Dial persist from 1609 to 1637, the longest-lived line in the census. The play's textual history belongs to editors; its commercial history belongs, for most of its early life, to this one stationer's stock management, and the title pages are where that management left its signature. R. Young, who printed the 1637 fifth quarto for the ageing Smethwick, closes the sequence's personnel as Allde had opened it, a trade printer executing a publisher's property, the names rotated, the structure unchanged.

## The Company Line on the Title Pages: Billing as a Dating Instrument

Set the four quarto billings in a row and they spell a company's curriculum vitae. 1597: the right Honourable the Lord of Hunsdon his servants. 1599: the right Honourable the Lord Chamberlain his servants. 1609 and after: the King's Majesty's servants, at the Globe. Theatre historians prize these lines because a patronage style is a datable artifact: a company could bill itself only by the patron it then served, and the sequence of patrons is a matter of patent, livery, and record outside the book trade altogether. Andrew Gurr, the leading historian of the Shakespeare company, built much of the modern account of that troupe's career on the interplay of title-page billings, court payment records, and legal documents, treating the billings as the company's own public statements of identity. On that reading, the billings on this play's pages date the worlds the editions came from with a precision the texts cannot match: the Hunsdon style fixes the first quarto's copy in the brief 1596-97 interval of George Carey's patronage; the Chamberlain style fits 1599 exactly; the royal style certifies the post-1603 company, and its Globe address names the house the troupe had occupied since 1599.

The honorifics in the billings grade the patrons as precisely as the names identify them. The 1597 and 1599 pages style the patron "right Honourable", the due of a peer and privy councillor, and attach the company's identity to his household: the Lord of Hunsdon his servants, the Lord Chamberlain his servants, the possessive doing the legal work, since the servants were, in the statute's sense, his. The 1609 page replaces honour with majesty, "the KINGS Maiesties Seruants", and the possessive survives the promotion unchanged, the company still grammatically owned, now by a crown. The sequence of styles is a ladder of the company's ascent told in forms of address, and it is climbed in full view of the book-buying public, one rung per edition.

### Which single line on the 1597 page does the most dating work?

The billing, "by the right Honourable the L. of Hunsdon his Seruants", does the most dating work on the page, because that style was available to the company only between Henry Carey's death in July 1596 and his son's appointment as Lord Chamberlain in 1597.

The imprint's year dates the book; the billing dates the performance world the book came from, and the two dates together bracket the play's stage life before print.

The venue's late arrival has its own explanation in the company's unsettled housing. When the first quarto appeared, the troupe was in the last phase of its tenancy at the Theatre in Shoreditch, the lease dispute that would drive it out was already brewing, and it played for a time at the Curtain before the Globe rose on Bankside in 1599 from the Theatre's timbers. A page of 1597 or 1599 had no settled house to advertise; a page of 1609 had the most famous playhouse in Europe, a decade old and identified with the company in the public mind. The naming of the Globe is thus not a bibliographical courtesy but a second advertisement nested in the first, the house's fame added to the play's at the moment the house had fame to lend.

The billing's reliability has a legal root that the applause never shares. Under the statute of 1572, players who were not the retained servants of a lord of sufficient rank were liable to be treated as rogues and vagabonds; a company's patronage was its licence to exist, rehearsed in the liveries its members wore and the warrants under which it travelled. A title page's billing is therefore a public statement of legal identity, made in a market where rivals, magistrates, and the patron's own household could read it. A false billing invited consequences a false superlative never could, and the trade's practice respects the distinction: billings change exactly when the underlying patronage changes, on this play's pages in 1597, 1599, and 1603-09, while superlatives drift, inflate, and fossilize. E. K. Chambers built the framework of The Elizabethan Stage on that hierarchy of trust, gathering the billings of hundreds of playbooks into the record of the companies, and Gurr's work inherits the method. The court payment accounts, which record the same company paid for performances at court under its successive styles, close the loop from an independent archive: the pages and the payments agree, because both were answerable to the same facts.

The instrument has limits, and the first quarto itself displays them. A billing certifies the style under which the copy's performance tradition ran, not the day the edition was printed; the 1597 page was still selling a superseded style within months of publication, since the company had resumed the Chamberlain name before the year was out. Billings can also lag, be copied from copy, or be chosen for dignity. Used with those cautions, they remain the hardest facts on the pages, harder than dates, which a publisher could fudge for the new year, and far harder than applause, which no one could fudge because no one could test it. What the billings collectively prove is continuity: one company owned this play in performance across three decades and three styles of name, from a lord's servants to the king's, and the wider record of that company's repertory, examined in the study of [Romeo and Juliet on the Elizabethan stage](/2027/08/06/romeo-juliet-elizabethan-stage/), shows the play holding its place in the stock throughout.

The provincial records extend the cross-examination beyond London. Town chamberlains' accounts across England, the material gathered by the Records of Early English Drama project, note payments to playing companies under their patrons' styles when the troupes toured, and the styles in the town books march with the styles on the title pages: a company touring as a lord's servants in one season appears under its new dignity in the next, because the licence it travelled on had changed. No town account names this play, the records rarely name plays at all, but the instrument's calibration is what matters. The billing system the pages use is the same system the whole kingdom's record-keepers used, and it is consistent wherever it can be tested, which is why a single billing line on a single quarto page can bear the weight bibliographers put on it.

Independent testimony lets the billings be cross-examined, and the play is lucky in its witness. Francis Meres, in Palladis Tamia of 1598, compiled the first printed assessment of the English drama's standing, naming Shakespeare "the most excellent among the English" in both comedy and tragedy and listing among the tragedies "his Romeo and Juliet". Meres matters to the title pages twice over. He confirms, from outside the trade, the standing the 1597 page's applause clause merely asserts; and he attaches the author's name to the play in print twenty-four years before any title page of the play does it, proving that the knowledge was public and the pages' silence a commercial choice, not an ignorance. The fuller account of that list and what it certifies is given in the article on [Meres' 1598 list and the play's early fame](/2026/09/11/meres-1598-list-early-fame/); for the census, Meres is the control experiment, the external record against which the pages' claims can be marked.

Three readings fall out of the alignment. First, persistence: the title formula settled in 1599 and the correction boast coined the same year run unchanged to 1637, proof that the trade treated both as fixtures of the commodity rather than as statements to be re-examined at each edition. Second, lateness: the venue waits until 1609 and the author until about 1622, although the Globe had housed the play since 1599 and Meres had printed the author's name in 1598; the pages disclose late what everyone in the business already knew, because disclosure follows selling value, not knowledge. Third, the Folio's silence is systematic, not accidental: every category the quarto pages fill, company, venue, printer of the play, shop, boast, is left empty, because the folio page that does the selling belongs to the author and the corpus, and a play inside the works needs no advertisement of its own.

The performance-claim row traces a decay curve that is itself a reputation index. It opens at maximum assertion, often and with great applause, subsides in 1599 to frequency alone, persists as formula through the Smethwick reprints, and vanishes entirely in the Folio, where no claim is made because none is needed. Claims of this kind are loudest where the commodity is newest and least secure; their gradual quieting across the sequence measures the play's passage from a novelty that had to be vouched for to a classic that vouched for the volumes containing it. The curve is the applause's true content, recoverable only by reading the row whole: not that the play was applauded in 1597, which the page cannot prove, but that publishers needed to say so then and needed to say less with every passing decade.

Two rows, read vertically, tell the trade's own story. The publisher row is the biography's spine: none, Burby, Smethwick, Smethwick, Smethwick, syndicate, a progression from unowned venture to single-owner annuity to corporate monument, each transition documented outside the pages as well as on them. The printer row runs the other way, from named craftsmen, Danter and Creede, to silence: after 1599 no quarto page names the play's printer, and the manufacture goes anonymous at exactly the period when the property becomes most valuable and most stable. The pattern is the book trade maturing around the play. In the 1590s a playbook advertised its making; by the 1610s it advertised its ownership, and the men who set the type had become what they would remain in the trade's public presentation, labour inside another man's asset.

The last row repays a glance of its own. The heroine's printed name travels from Iuliet through the 1609 page's Juliet, back to the fourth quarto's monumental IVLIET, and into the Folio head title's Ivliet, a small orthographic weather system that the modern fixed form Juliet conceals. The spelling study of the lovers' names across the early printings belongs to another article in this series and is not pursued here; the census simply records that even the play's most famous name had no settled printed shape for the first twenty-six years of the play's life in books, and that the title pages, which agree about so much else, never agree for long about how to spell her.

## Are Title Pages Marketing or Testimony? The Reliability Trial

The complication the brief insists on, how far title-page claims can be trusted, has a scholarly history with two swings. The first bibliographical generation, A. W. Pollard and W. W. Greg, read the pages as testimony. Greg's transcriptions in his Bibliography of the English Printed Drama treated the title page as the edition's birth certificate, and the 1599 boast in particular was taken at face value as evidence that a corrected text had superseded a corrupt one, a reading that fed directly into the classification of the 1597 quarto as a bad quarto, stolen and surreptitious. On that foundation an entire theory of the play's transmission was built: the first text a reported piracy, the second the author's play restored. The pages seemed to say so, and for decades scholarship repeated what the pages said. The recursion is worth pausing on, because it is the census's sharpest lesson. The categories in which the play's texts were judged for a century were themselves minted from front-matter rhetoric: the Folio preface's "stolne and surreptitious copies" supplied the piracy, the 1599 page's three verbs supplied the restoration, and Pollard's label merely soldered the two advertisements together. Marketing copy, believed by scholars, hardened into bibliography; the trial of the title pages is, in part, the unmaking of that solder.

### When a title page and the text behind it disagree, which should a reader trust?

The text, every time collation can be performed, because a title page is a publisher's statement of intent while the text is the artifact itself. Bibliography's first rule is to grade the claim by the collation, never the collation by the claim.

Where the two disagree, as the 1599 boast and the duplicated dawn passage disagree, the page records what the publisher wanted believed and the text records what was done.

The discipline that generation founded had, it should be said, a sterner side than its credulity about boasts. R. B. McKerrow's Prolegomena for the Oxford Shakespeare laid down that the title page is evidence first of the edition's manufacture and ownership, to be weighed with the Register and the book's physical makeup, and the Greg and Bowers tradition of copy-text editing built its whole method on that ordering: establish the documents, then judge their claims. The trial of the title pages is therefore not a trial of bibliography by common sense; it is bibliography correcting itself with its own instruments, the boast demoted by the same profession that had promoted it, once the profession learned to ask what kind of sentence a boast is.

The counter-swing came from two directions at once. Laurie E. Maguire's audit of the suspect-text category in Shakespearean Suspect Texts tested the diagnostic habits of the Pollard and Greg tradition against controls and found the tradition's confidence, including its confidence in what title pages implied, systematically under-evidenced; a boast, on her showing, is a publisher's sentence, not a bibliographer's datum. Blayney's work on the playbook trade supplied the economic mechanism for that scepticism: in a trade where playbooks were minor, cheap, and sold on stage reputation, title-page language was boilerplate, drafted from a stock of phrases, "often acted", "with great applause", "newly corrected", whose recurrence across hundreds of playbooks empties any single instance of testimonial force. Lukas Erne pressed from the opposite side in Shakespeare as Literary Dramatist, arguing that the sustained quarto publication of the plays, this play's five quartos across forty years among them, shows a real reading market that publishers served deliberately, and that Shakespeare's texts were prepared for readers as well as players; on Erne's account the pages address a reading public whose existence the boilerplate theory underrates. Modern editors have had to practice among these positions. Jill L. Levenson's Oxford edition introduction prints the title-page evidence in full and uses it with visible caution, taking the imprints and billings as the record and the claims as the noise; Brian Gibbons, in the Arden edition, likewise builds the publication history on the pages' facts while subjecting their boasts to collation.

The quantitative turn in book history has since given the dispute a control it lacked. Alan B. Farmer and Zachary Lesser, revisiting the popularity of playbooks, measured demand by reprint frequency across the whole trade rather than by the claims of individual pages, and their method supplies the title pages of this play with the external check the applause clause always needed: editions are behaviour, and behaviour can be counted. Five quartos of Romeo and Juliet between 1597 and 1637 place the play among the more persistently reprinted playbooks of the period, in the company of the acknowledged repertory successes. The count does not rescue any single claim, the 1597 parenthesis least of all, but it validates the standing the parenthesis asserted, by a route that never asks the publisher's word for anything. That is the mature form of the trial's logic: every element of a page is graded against evidence the page's maker did not control, the Register for ownership, the patent rolls and payment accounts for billings, collation for correction, reprint counts for popularity, and the residue, the adjectives that no archive can grade, is priced accordingly, as the sound a book makes when it is being sold.

The base rate of the performance formula completes the calibration. Claims of frequent public performance appear on the title pages of plays whose stage lives are documented, on plays known from a single court performance, and on plays for which no performance record exists anywhere else; the formula's presence therefore raises the probability of a stage success only modestly, and its absence proves nothing at all, since successful plays also appeared without it. A Bayesian discipline, though no bibliographer calls it that, is the trial's real verdict: each element of a page is a witness with a known rate of reliability, examined in light of everything else on file. The applause of 1597 is a weak witness corroborated by strong ones, Meres and the reprint record; the billing of the same page is a strong witness corroborated by the patent sequence; the boast of 1599 is a witness caught exaggerating once, on "amended", and thereafter heard with caution on everything. The pages do not divide into the true and the false. They divide into the tested and the untestable, and the census, which is the article's whole method, is simply the filing system that keeps the two apart.

The adjudication this article defends distributes the honours by element, because the elements differ in kind. On the applause and the verbs of correction, Maguire and Blayney win outright: the phrases are formulas, their retention by editions that performed no correction proves their mechanical character, and no argument about a text's origin should rest on them. On the aggregate pattern, Erne wins a real point: formulas explain each page, but they do not explain six editions, and a play reprinted in 1599, 1609, about 1622, and 1637, then gathered into the Folio, was being bought by readers as well as applauded by audiences, whatever any one page claims. On the hard skeleton, imprints, billings, addresses, dates, the older generation's trust is vindicated by the cross-checks: the Register confirms the ownership chain the imprints imply, the patent records confirm the billings, and the shop signs locate real shops. The editor's practice that Levenson models, facts from the page, verdicts from the collation, is the correct settlement, and it leaves the title pages neither bible nor trash but a graded source, reliable in proportion as each element can be tested against something the publisher did not control.

Applied page by page, the settlement yields a reading protocol for the whole census. Believe the 1597 page's billing and its printer, and discount its applause to the status of a hope expressed in the trade's idiom. Believe the 1599 page's imprint, its billing, and its claim of augmentation, and grade its amended as unproven until collation, which in the event returns a verdict of partial credit. Believe the 1609 page's publisher, billing, and venue, and treat its inherited verbs as part of the furniture, evidence about branding and about nothing else. Believe the fourth quarto's imprint, and read its two issues as a market signal, the author's name crossing the threshold of commercial value in the early 1620s. Believe the Folio catalogue's classification in full, and its "True Originall Copies", for this play, not at all, since the play's Folio text stands in demonstrable descent from the quartos the volume's own preface disparaged. A reader who grades the pages so loses nothing the pages contain and is deceived by nothing they pretend.

## What the Title Pages Finally Prove About the Play's Early Life

The biography the six pages write, graded by the trial's settlement, runs as follows. A tragedy of two households, "both alike in dignity" (Prologue.1), reached print in 1597 as a going stage success in the keeping of a nobleman's company, printed in haste by two shops and sold on its applause. In 1599 it was reissued as a corrected commodity by a named publisher of standing, wearing a superlative title and a boast that overstated its amendment while understating nothing about its augmentation. In 1609, its copy having passed through two Register transfers into John Smethwick's stock, it reappeared under a king's company billing and a named playhouse, its text reprinted and its boast inherited unexamined. About 1622 its publisher refreshed the undated page to admit, at last, that the play had an author, and in 1623 the Folio closed the sequence by retiring the play's page altogether and entering its name in the catalogue of a collected works. Company property, trade commodity, royal repertory piece, author's work, canonical text: the play passed through five identities in twenty-six years, and the only witnesses that register every transition are the pages that face its text.

As dating and placing evidence, the pages deliver a bracket and an address. The bracket: a play in the repertory of George Carey's company by 1596-97, in print that year, named among its author's best tragedies by a public critic in 1598, and never out of the company's stock thereafter, a frame within which the composition belongs to the middle 1590s and the first performances precede the first edition by an interval the applause clause asserts but cannot measure. The address: London throughout, the public playhouse first and the Globe by name from 1609, the bookshops of the Exchange district and St Dunstan's churchyard as the points of sale, the whole geography of the play's early life recoverable from six sheets of advertising. No other class of document gives the play both its chronology and its map; the records that could rival the pages, the company's own papers, are lost, and the allusions that survive are scattered and incidental. The title pages are what the play's first twenty-six years left behind, and they are enough.

The cliché the series thesis warns against, the play as a timeless love story floating above its circumstances, cannot survive its own title pages. These documents are circumstantial to the last comma: a patron's death legible in a billing, a property dispute legible in a publisher's succession, a marketing decision legible in an author's name inserted on a single leaf. What the title pages tell us, finally, is that the play's early life was a life in institutions, a playing company and a book trade, each of which described the play in its own interest and, in doing so, left the evidence by which both can now be known.

The protocol the census teaches transfers to any early playbook a reader meets. Read the imprint first, since ownership and manufacture are the facts a page had least reason and least room to falsify; read the billing second, with the company's patent history open beside it; read the date third, remembering the trade's habits with years; read the formulas fourth, as the record of what the publisher thought the market wanted, which is evidence about the market if about nothing else; and read the superlatives last and lightest, as the noise every selling document makes. Run the same protocol across the six presentations of Romeo and Juliet and the play's first biography assembles itself, dated, addressed, and signed, first by everyone except its author, and at last, in a single inserted line, by him. The pages remain what they were in the churchyards where they were sold: the front doors of the early editions. Four centuries of scholarship have walked through them, sometimes reading the notices posted on them with care, sometimes mistaking the notices for the house. The census restores the distinction, and with it the evidence.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch title-page evidence, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch title-page evidence is the aligned census of the play's six early presentations in print, the quarto title pages of 1597, 1599, and 1609, the two issues of the undated fourth quarto, and the First Folio's catalogue presentation of 1623, compared element by element: title formula, performance claim, company billing, venue, boast, author attribution, printer, publisher, place of sale, and date. The pattern it exposes is persistence at the commercial core and lateness at every point of credit: the title formula and the correction boast, once set in 1599, never change again, while the venue is named only in 1609 and the author only in about 1622.

### Q: What information did an early playbook title page normally carry?

An early playbook title page normally carried the play's title with genre and quality epithets, a claim about performance frequency or success, the acting company billed by its patron, and an imprint naming the printer, the publisher, or both, with the place of sale and usually the year. The page was the book's advertisement and its trade record in one sheet, since playbooks sold unbound and a buyer met the title page before the text. Romeo and Juliet's pages are typical in structure and unusually rich in what their variation preserves, because the play stayed in print, under changing billings, for four decades.

### Q: How many editions of Romeo and Juliet appeared in print before the First Folio?

Four quarto editions appeared before the First Folio: the first in 1597, the second in 1599, the third in 1609, and the fourth, undated, in about 1622, the fourth surviving in two issues distinguished by their title pages. A fifth quarto followed the Folio in 1637. Each edition reprinted or replaced the text of its predecessor, and each renewed the play's presence in the bookshops, so that between 1597 and 1623 a reader could buy the play in quarto in most years, a continuity of availability that the Folio then converted into permanence.

### Q: Why does the author's name reach the play's title pages only in the 1620s?

The author's name arrives late because playbook pages sold the play and the company, not the writer, and a dramatist's name added little to a playbook's price until collected editions made authorship itself the commodity. The knowledge was never secret: Francis Meres had named Shakespeare as the play's author in print in 1598. What changed by about 1622, when the fourth quarto's second issue inserted "Written by W. Shake-speare", was the market judgement that the name now helped sell the book, a judgement the Folio's author-centred volume confirmed the following year.

### Q: What are the two issues of the fourth quarto, and how do their title pages differ?

The two issues are re-marketings of the same printed sheets, distinguished by an altered title leaf. The first issue's page repeats the 1609 form: the most excellent and lamentable tragedy, acted by the King's Majesty's servants at the Globe, "Newly corrected, augmented, and amended", printed for John Smethwick and sold under the Dial in St Dunstan's churchyard. The second issue inserts one line, "Written by W. Shake-speare", between the Globe billing and the boast. That single line is the first author attribution on any title page of the play, a quarter century after the first edition.

### Q: Why is the fourth quarto dated by scholars rather than by its own title page?

Because neither issue of the fourth quarto prints a date. Bibliographers assign the edition to about 1622 from material evidence: the types and ornaments of the unnamed printing shop, the state of Smethwick's stock and devices, the edition's place in the sequence of the copy's exploitation, and the correction patterns in its text, which show consultation of the first quarto. R. Carter Hailey's typographical analysis argues for 1623. The undated page is itself evidence about the edition's marketing, since a backlist play with steady sales gained nothing from proclaiming a year and risked looking stale by doing so.

### Q: What does the phrase "at the Globe" add to the later quartos that the earlier pages lack?

The phrase adds a fixed address for the performance being advertised. The 1597 and 1599 pages claim public performance by a company but name no playhouse, although the company played at the Theatre and the Curtain before building the Globe in 1599. From 1609 the page ties the play to the Globe, the company's famous Bankside house, converting a general claim of stage success into a specific association between play, troupe, and venue. The addition reflects the Globe's own fame as much as the play's: by 1609 the house had become part of what a buyer was purchasing a share in.

### Q: Why did the 1609 quarto keep the boast first printed in 1599?

Because the boast had become part of the play's trade identity rather than a report on new work. In 1599 "Newly corrected, augmented, and amended" described, with pardonable exaggeration, a genuine replacement of a defective text. The 1609 edition merely reprinted the 1599 text, correcting nothing systematically, yet Smethwick's page repeats the three verbs unchanged, and the fourth quarto and the 1637 fifth quarto repeat them again. A formula retained after its occasion has passed is functioning as a title, not a testimony, which is why bibliographers grade the verbs as evidence about marketing and not about the texts behind them.

### Q: How does the company billing change from page to page across the quarto sequence?

The billing follows the troupe's patrons in exact sequence. In 1597 the company is the Lord of Hunsdon's servants, George Carey's style during his brief patronage after his father's death. In 1599 it is the Lord Chamberlain's servants, the customary style resumed when Carey took the chamberlainship. From 1609 it is the King's Majesty's servants, the royal style conferred by the patent of 1603, with the Globe added as the venue. No other element on the pages changes so regularly or so informatively, and the sequence amounts to a dated register of the company's public identity across the play's first three decades.

### Q: What does "printed for Iohn Smethwicke" reveal about who owned the play as a book?

The phrase reveals that John Smethwick owned the copy, the exclusive right to print the play, and had financed the edition as its publisher. Stationers' Register entries confirm the route of that ownership: the copy passed from Cuthbert Burby, the 1599 publisher, to Nicholas Ling in January 1607, and from Ling to Smethwick in November 1607. Smethwick then held the play for the rest of its early print life, issuing the quartos of 1609, about 1622, and 1637 from his shop under the Dial. In the trade's terms the play, as a book, was his stock, and the imprint is the deed.

### Q: Why do the imprints locate a shop by a sign, the Exchange or the Dial, instead of an address?

Because London shops were found by their painted signs, not by numbered addresses, which did not exist. A stationer's sign was his landmark and his brand: Burby's shop was "neere the Exchange", placing him by the Royal Exchange in the City's commercial heart, and Smethwick's was "vnder the Dyall" in St Dunstan's churchyard in Fleet Street, within the churchyard bookselling district. The imprint's geography is practical information for a buyer on foot, and its precision is one reason bibliographers trust imprint lines: a false shop sign would have been useless to the very readers the page addressed.

### Q: What happened to the words "with great applause" across the quarto title pages?

They appear once and never return. The 1597 page claims the play had been "often (with great applause) plaid publiquely", the fullest performance boast in the sequence. The 1599 page drops the parenthesis and keeps only frequency, "sundry times publiquely acted", and the 1609 and fourth quarto pages keep the leaner form, adding the Globe instead. The applause clause was evidently a launch formula for a new book, expendable once the play's reputation was established. Its single appearance is a reminder that title-page superlatives belong to editions, not to plays, and date the marketing moment rather than the merriment.

### Q: Why does the word "conceited" vanish from the play's title after the first quarto?

Because the whole title was rebuilt in 1599 and the rebuilt formula had no slot for it. The 1597 style, "An Excellent conceited Tragedie", praised the play's ingenious workmanship in a word of the moment; the 1599 style, "The Most Excellent and lamentable Tragedie", chose superlative rank and advertised pathos instead, and every later quarto kept the 1599 settlement. The disappearance is a marketing datum, not a judgement on the word: when a publisher recasts a title to bury a predecessor edition, the predecessor's distinctive epithet is the first thing discarded, since its survival would blur the claim that the new book supersedes the old.

### Q: How can a company billing on a title page help date a play's first performances?

A billing helps because a company could perform under only one patron's style at a time, and changes of patron are documented in patents and payments outside the book trade. The 1597 quarto's style, the Lord of Hunsdon's servants, was current only between Henry Carey's death in July 1596 and George Carey's appointment as Lord Chamberlain in 1597, so the copy behind the edition belongs to a performance tradition of those months. The method dates the play's stage currency, not its composition, and it must allow for billings copied from older copy, but cross-checked against records it gives the firmest external bracket the play's early staging has.

### Q: What can the title pages not tell us about the play's early life?

The pages cannot say when the play was written, who first played its parts, how any single performance went, how many copies were printed or sold, or whether the applause they claim was loud. They are silent on the author's intentions, the company's internal finances, and the audience's experience, and their dates mark editions, not compositions. Their silences are structural: a page designed to sell a book in a churchyard had no use for such information. The discipline of the census is to let the pages testify only where their trade function gave them reason to be accurate, and to look elsewhere, to records, allusions, and the texts themselves, for the rest.

### Q: How should a reader weigh a title page's applause claim against independent evidence?

As a claim requiring corroboration, neither dismissed nor believed on its own credit. Applause language is a stock formula on playbook pages of the period, so the 1597 claim proves by itself only that the publisher expected the claim to be plausible. Corroboration for this play exists: Meres' 1598 list places the tragedy among the admired works of its author, and the edition sequence itself, a fuller quarto within two years and reprints across four decades, is behaviour no publisher wastes on a failure. Weighed together, formula and record support a genuine stage success; weighed alone, the page's parenthesis would support nothing but its own existence.

### Q: Why do bibliographers transcribe title pages letter by letter instead of summarizing them?

Because the evidence lives in details a summary destroys. The fourth quarto's two issues differ by one inserted line; the author's name is present or absent according to which leaf a copy carries, and a summary saying the edition names Shakespeare would be true of some copies and false of others. Spellings, line breaks, and ornaments identify printers the imprint omits, distinguish issues, and date undated books. Greg's Bibliography made quasi-facsimile transcription the standard for the drama precisely because, for playbooks, the title page is often the only birth record an edition has, and a birth record must be copied, not paraphrased.

### Q: How did the play's printed title itself change between 1597 and 1599?

The 1597 title, "An Excellent conceited Tragedie of Romeo and Iuliet", promises an ingeniously wrought tragedy and names the lovers plainly. The 1599 title, "The Most Excellent and lamentable Tragedie, of Romeo and Iuliet", escalates the praise to a superlative, adds "lamentable" to advertise the pathos of the ending, and prefixes the correction boast that repositioned the book against its predecessor. The lovers' names stay constant, the heroine still spelled Iuliet, while the selling frame around them is rebuilt: from workmanship to rank, from a new play to a corrected classic in the making. Later quartos changed the frame again; the title itself never changed after 1599.

### Q: What does the Folio catalogue entry do that no quarto title page could do?

The catalogue entry places the play inside an author's collected works, assigning it a genre, an order, and a permanent bibliographical identity. A quarto title page could praise the play, bill its company, and direct buyers to a shop, but it addressed a single commodity at a single moment. The Folio's one line among the Tragedies subordinates the play to a corpus and a name, converting a repertory piece into a canonical item that libraries would shelve and editors would cite for centuries. It is the least informative presentation of the play in the sequence and the most consequential, because what it withholds in detail it repays in permanence.

### Q: How can an essay use the InsightCrunch title-page evidence to argue the play began as a company's property?

Begin from what the earliest pages name and omit. The 1597 and 1599 pages identify the play by its company and its stationers, the Lord of Hunsdon's and the Lord Chamberlain's servants in performance, Danter, Creede, and Burby in print, and neither names an author, although the author's identity was in print elsewhere by 1598. The property relations the pages disclose are therefore institutional on both sides: a troupe's repertory asset and a stationer's copy. The author's arrival on the page in about 1622, and the Folio's re-founding of the play as an item in his works, then measure the transfer of cultural ownership the essay sets out to prove.
