The instruction to show a wide opponent inside or outside is the most frequently given command in defending and the least frequently justified. It is shouted from touchlines every weekend, it is written into pre-match briefings at every level of the game, and if you ask ten coaches to explain why they chose the one they chose, you will get four answers about the opponent's weaker foot, three about what their side has always done, two about what they saw a professional team do, and one honest admission that nobody has ever asked before.

That is not a criticism of coaches. It is a description of a genuine gap, because the decision is harder than it looks and the online discussion of it is close to useless. Most of the argument is people meaning different things by the same words, and the rest is absolutism: you always show a player down the line, or the idea is outdated because everyone is two-footed now. Both positions are wrong, and the reason they persist is that neither side has stated the variables that actually decide it.

![Show him inside or outside, the defending decision resolved by cover travel, block height and flank - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

This article gives a decision rule. It resolves the question separately by the height and compactness of your block, by whether your covering centre-back can travel wide, by the recovery speed available behind your full-back, by the quality and occupation of the opponent's box, and by the flank, because the geometry is not symmetrical. It gives four verdicts for four contexts, names the deciding factor in each, lists the in-match signals that tell you the choice was wrong, and ends with a one-sentence version simple enough to teach a nine-year-old.

## The two instructions, defined precisely

Much of the disagreement dissolves once the terms are fixed, so they are fixed here before anything else.

Showing outside means angling the defender's body so that the touchline route is the available one. The defender positions himself on the inside shoulder of the attacker, with his hips oriented to make the inward path costly and the outward path open. He is not blocking the inside; he is making the inside the harder choice, and the attacker retains the option of taking it at a price.

Showing inside means angling the body so that the central route is the available one, with the explicit expectation that support closes it. The defender positions on the touchline shoulder, hips oriented to make the outward path costly. This instruction is incomplete on its own and dangerous when given on its own, because the central route is only acceptable if somebody is arriving to close it. Showing inside without support is not a defensive instruction; it is an invitation.

Three clarifications matter because they are where the online argument goes wrong.

The first is that both are structural decisions rather than individual preferences. A full-back does not choose this himself, or should not, because the correct answer depends on the positions and capabilities of at least three other players. When a defender is choosing on his own, the side has no plan, and that is a coaching failure rather than a player one.

The second is that neither instruction is a block. Showing outside does not mean the attacker cannot come inside; it means coming inside costs him something. Coaches who deliver the instruction as an absolute produce defenders who overcommit to one side and are beaten by the other, which is the single most common execution failure of both instructions.

The third is that showing is not the same as forcing. Forcing implies the defender determines the route. He does not. He determines the relative price of two routes, and a good attacker will sometimes pay the higher price because he has judged it worth paying. A coach who expects the instruction to eliminate one route will conclude it has failed the first time an opponent takes it, which is how good plans get abandoned at twenty minutes.

### What is the difference between showing a player inside and forcing him?

Showing sets the relative price of two routes by body angle; forcing implies the defender removes one entirely. He cannot. A good attacker will occasionally pay the higher price, and a coach who treats that as failure will abandon a working instruction far too early.
## The five variables that decide it

The decision is settled by five things, and none of them is the opponent's weaker foot. That belongs on the list, well down it, as a tiebreaker rather than a determinant.

### Variable one: whether your covering centre-back can travel

This is the largest single factor and it is examined in full below, because it carries the article's namable claim. Stated briefly: showing inside requires a second defender to close the central route, and the second defender is usually the near centre-back. If he cannot arrive before the attacker's next action, showing inside concedes the most dangerous area on the pitch to an opponent with a running start.

### Variable two: the height and compactness of your block

A high, compact block has short distances between its players, which means support arrives quickly and the central route is genuinely closable. It also has a large space behind the defensive line, which means the outside route is expensive if the attacker beats his man, because the recovery run is long and the delivery arrives against a scrambling defence.

A deep block has the opposite profile. Support distances are short because everyone is compressed near the goal, but the central area is crowded with bodies, which changes the calculation entirely: an attacker shown inside from wide arrives into traffic rather than into space. Meanwhile the outside route leads to a byline position against a box that is already full, which is a lower-value outcome than it is against a high line.

The consequence is counterintuitive and it is the source of most confusion in this debate. A deep block is the structure in which showing inside is most defensible, and a high block is the structure in which showing outside is most defensible, which is precisely the opposite of what most people assume, because they associate showing inside with modern high-pressing sides. The association exists for a different reason, developed later.

### Variable three: recovery speed behind the full-back

Showing outside accepts that the attacker may get past on the touchline side, and the cost of that is paid by whoever recovers. If the full-back is quick, or the covering centre-back is quick and the distance is short, the cost is a cross from a poor position. If neither is quick, the cost is a byline entry, which is a different event entirely.

This variable is about the specific players available rather than the system, and it is the one that most often makes a theoretically correct instruction wrong for a particular side.

### Variable four: the quality and occupation of the opponent's box

Showing outside is a bet that the delivery is survivable. That bet is good against a side with one static striker and poor against a side that occupies the box with three runners arriving on staggered timing and a genuine aerial focal point.

Showing inside is a bet that the central congestion is survivable, and that bet is good against a side without a shooting threat from the edge of the area and poor against one that has rehearsed the pull-back to the top of the box.

A coach who has not looked at the opponent's box occupation has not looked at the input that determines which bet he is making.

### Variable five: the flank

The geometry is not symmetrical, and this is the variable that is almost universally ignored. A right-footed attacker on the left flank and a left-footed attacker on the left flank are different problems, and the same instruction applied to both flanks will be correct on one and wrong on the other. This gets its own treatment below because it is the most under-discussed element of the whole question.

### Where the weaker foot fits

It fits as a tiebreaker, applied after the five variables have narrowed the choice, and it is weaker evidence than most coaches believe for three reasons.

The first is that elite wide players are rarely as one-sided as their reputation, and the gap between a strong and a weaker foot at that level is frequently smaller than the gap between a good and a poor defensive structure.

The second is that the weaker foot governs the finish and the delivery, not the take-on. An attacker can beat a defender in either direction using either foot for the touch, then adjust. Showing a player onto his weaker side reduces the quality of his final action, which is worth having and is not the same as stopping him.

The third is that the opponent knows his own weaker side and has spent his career being shown onto it. The routes he takes when forced that way are practised, not improvised, which is why the instruction so often produces a competent action rather than a mistake.

### Does showing an opponent onto his weaker foot always work?

No. It is a tiebreaker rather than a determinant. The weaker foot degrades the final action rather than preventing the take-on, elite wide players are less one-sided than their reputations, and any player has spent a career being shown that way, so his routes from that side are rehearsed rather than improvised.
## The cover-travel test

The decision resolves on a single question, and it is the namable claim of this article. Call it the cover-travel test: the choice between showing inside and showing outside is settled by whether your covering centre-back can arrive in the wide area before the delivery, and a coach who cannot answer that question about his own side is not in a position to choose either instruction.

The test is not rhetorical. It is a measurement, and it can be run in a training session in ten minutes.

Place the covering centre-back in his normal position for your block height. Place a full-back in a wide duel and a server who plays the ball in. Have the attacker beat the full-back on the outside at match speed and travel toward the byline. Ask one question: does the centre-back arrive at the near edge of the six-yard box before the delivery is struck? Run it eight times with your actual players at your actual block height, and count.

If the answer is yes six or more times out of eight, your centre-back travels, and the outside route is a controlled concession. You have the option of showing outside, and you also have the option of showing inside if the other variables favour it, because a centre-back who can travel wide is by definition mobile enough to be useful in either scheme.

If the answer is no more than half the time, your centre-back does not travel, and this changes everything. Showing outside now concedes byline deliveries rather than controlled ones. But showing inside is not automatically the answer either, because the same immobility that prevents him travelling wide often prevents him stepping into the central route with any urgency. What you actually have is a defence that must avoid the wide duel rather than direct it, which points to a third answer treated below.

The reason this single test carries so much weight is that it collapses three of the five variables into one observable. Block height determines the distance the centre-back must travel. Recovery speed determines whether he covers it. And the opponent's box occupation determines whether his absence from the box matters. A coach who runs the test with his real players at his real block height has measured all three at once, in the specific combination his side actually produces.

The test also exposes the most common false confidence in defensive coaching, which is the belief that a plan works because it worked in a walkthrough at walking pace. Cover travel is a speed problem, and speed problems do not survive being rehearsed slowly. Any side that has only ever walked through its wide defending has no information about whether its own instruction is viable.

### How do you know whether your side can show a winger outside?

Run the cover-travel test. Have an attacker beat your full-back outside at match speed from your normal block height and count how often your covering centre-back reaches the near edge of the six-yard box before the delivery. Six or more times out of eight means outside is a controlled concession.

## The four contexts, with verdicts

A decision article that refuses to decide is worthless, so here are four verdicts with the deciding factor named in each.

### Elite football with a mobile covering centre-back

Verdict: showing inside is defensible and frequently correct, and it is the context that has produced the modern association between inside-forcing and high-level coaching.

The deciding factor is the specific centre-back. An elite side with a quick, aggressive, front-foot centre-back who steps into the half-space can close the central route before the attacker's second touch, which converts the inside route from the most dangerous area on the pitch into a trap. The instruction then does real work: it steers the attacker into a two-defender problem in an area where a turnover produces a counter-attack against a stretched opponent.

Where it fails at this level is against opponents who anticipate it, receive facing the pitch, and release early into the space the stepping centre-back has left. The counter to a stepping centre-back is a pass rather than a dribble, which is why elite sides using this scheme need a holding midfielder disciplined enough to occupy the vacated area rather than following the ball.

The honest note is that this context is rarer than the discourse implies. It requires one specific player profile, and the number of sides in the world that actually possess it is small. Coaches watching those sides and copying the instruction without the player are copying the visible half of a plan.

### Elite or professional football with a slower, positional centre-back

Verdict: show outside, and build the box for the delivery.

The deciding factor is the cover-travel test, which this side will fail. A positional centre-back who defends the space in front of goal well and does not travel is a valuable player, and the plan has to be built around what he does rather than what he does not. Showing outside directs the attacker toward the byline area, which is dangerous, and the answer is not to prevent it but to prepare for it: near-post contact fixed to a body, a screen at the edge of the area, and a goalkeeper with a stated rule about which balls he attacks.

This verdict is unpopular because it accepts conceding crosses, and accepting a category of concession feels like surrender. It is the correct trade because the alternative concedes central entries, and a central entry is worth substantially more to an attacking side than a cross from a wide position.

### Mid-level professional and semi-professional football

Verdict: show outside as the default, with a rehearsed exception.

The deciding factor is rehearsal time. Showing inside is a coordinated action requiring at least two players to behave correctly within a second of each other, and coordination degrades with reduced training time faster than any individual skill. A side training four times a week can install and maintain it; a side training twice cannot maintain it even if it can install it, and a half-maintained inside-forcing scheme is worse than no scheme at all because the full-back commits to a route his support does not close.

The rehearsed exception worth having is a game-state instruction: show inside in the final fifteen minutes when protecting a lead and the opponent has committed extra bodies forward, because the central area is now full of your own players and the wide areas are the ones you cannot afford to concede.

### Amateur, school, and youth football

Verdict: show outside, always, and do not teach the nuance until the players can execute the default.

The deciding factor is the reliability of the second defender. At this level the second defender arrives late, arrives in the wrong place, or does not arrive, and no amount of instruction changes that quickly, because it is a consequence of limited training time rather than of understanding. An instruction whose success depends on someone else arriving is an instruction that will fail, and the failures will be central entries in front of goal.

Showing outside is robust to the absence of support in a way showing inside is not. A full-back who shows outside and is beaten has conceded a wide position; a full-back who shows inside and is beaten has conceded the middle of the pitch. The first is survivable without help. The second is not.

There is a second reason and it is developmental. Young defenders who are taught to show outside learn to use the touchline as a defensive tool, which is a durable and transferable idea. Young defenders taught to show inside learn to rely on help, and when the help does not come they learn instead that defending does not work.

### When is forcing an opponent down the line the right choice?

When your covering centre-back cannot travel wide quickly, when your block is high with space behind, when your support is unrehearsed or unreliable, or when the opponent's box occupation is thin. It is also the correct default for every level of football below the fully professional game.

### When is forcing an opponent inside the right choice?

When you have a quick front-foot centre-back who steps into the half-space before the attacker's second touch, a deep or compact block that fills the central area with bodies, rehearsed support that arrives within a second, and an opponent with dangerous box occupation whose crosses you cannot afford to concede.
## The flank asymmetry

The most under-discussed element of this whole question is that the geometry does not mirror, and applying one instruction across both flanks is the most common structural error in wide defending.

Start with the simple case. A right-footed attacker on the right flank who is shown outside travels toward the byline on his stronger foot, arriving at a position from which the cross is comfortable and the shot is not. The same attacker shown inside travels onto his weaker side and toward congestion, which degrades both his delivery and his shot.

Now the case that behaves differently. A left-footed attacker on the right flank who is shown outside travels toward the byline on his weaker foot, which degrades his delivery severely, because a byline cross from the weaker foot is the least comfortable action in wide play. The same attacker shown inside travels onto his stronger foot and toward the goal, which is the whole basis of the inverted winger's threat and produces the shooting position that defines the role.

Those two cases produce opposite instructions on the same flank, which is why the blanket rule fails. And they produce a second-order effect that most sides miss entirely: because the instruction differs by attacker footedness rather than by flank, a side can correctly be showing outside on one side and inside on the other in the same fixture, which requires the two full-backs to be given different instructions and the centre-backs to know which is which.

The complication that makes this genuinely hard is that showing an inverted winger outside is correct in isolation and creates a problem elsewhere. The threat degrades, but the attacker is now travelling toward the byline against a full-back on his weaker recovery side, and the resulting delivery, though poorer, arrives against a defence that has shifted toward the ball. The framework for why a known inverted move resists this and every other simple answer is developed in [the analysis of the unfalsifiable move](/2021/10/15/stopping-arjen-robben/), which this article inherits and does not restate.

There is also a subtler asymmetry that has nothing to do with the attacker. Most defensive units are not equally mobile on both sides, because the covering centre-back on one flank is the more mobile of the pair and the covering centre-back on the other is the more positional. That means the cover-travel test frequently returns different answers for the left and right sides of the same back four, and a coach who runs it once has measured only half his defence.

### Should the decision change depending on which flank the ball is on?

Yes, and this is the most commonly missed point. The correct instruction depends on the attacker's footedness relative to the flank, not on the flank itself, so a side can correctly show outside on one side and inside on the other. Cover mobility also usually differs between your two centre-backs.

## The two-footed opponent

There is a case where neither instruction has an intrinsic edge, and it needs its own treatment because the standard answers both dissolve.

An attacker comfortable with both feet removes the tiebreaker entirely. Showing him onto his weaker side is not available because he does not have one in any meaningful sense, which means the choice cannot be made by reference to the opponent at all. It has to be made by reference to your own structure, which is uncomfortable for coaches used to deciding by scouting.

The answer is that the decision defaults entirely to the cover-travel test and the block height, with the opponent removed from the calculation. If your centre-back travels and your block is deep, show inside. If he does not travel or your block is high, show outside. There is no third consideration, because the usual third consideration has been deleted.

There is one genuine addition, and it is a technique change rather than a direction change. Against a two-footed opponent the defender's advantage has to come from distance rather than angle, because angle only works when one route is worth more than the other. The instruction becomes distance-first: hold a stride and a half, do not pre-load the weight to either side, and defend the ball rather than the direction. That is a slower and more reactive job, it produces fewer clean wins, and it produces far fewer catastrophic losses.

The other structural answer available against this profile is to remove the duel rather than to direct it, by pressing the supply so that the reception happens facing his own goal. This is the highest-return option against any wide threat and it is the one most often skipped because it costs a midfielder his attacking position. Against a two-footed opponent it stops being an option and becomes the primary plan, and the full case for it in the no-weaker-side scenario is developed in [the treatment of the wide player with no weak side](/2021/11/01/stopping-franck-ribery/).

### Does the answer change against a two footed opponent?

The opponent drops out of the calculation entirely, so the decision defaults to the cover-travel test and your block height alone. The technique also changes: the defender's advantage must come from distance rather than angle, holding a stride and a half without pre-loading weight to either side.
## What each choice costs

Every defensive instruction buys something and sells something, and a coach who cannot name what he is selling has not finished the decision.

### What showing inside costs your midfield

It costs a midfielder his position, permanently, and it costs the side its counter-attacking structure on that flank.

The mechanism is straightforward. Showing inside is a promise that the central route will be closed, and the closing is done either by the covering centre-back stepping or by the near-side central midfielder dropping. Whichever player does it is committed to a defensive position he cannot leave, because leaving it converts the instruction into an invitation. That player is usually the one your side needs to be higher and facing forward when possession is won.

There is a second and less obvious cost. A midfielder who is responsible for closing the inside route cannot also screen the edge of the penalty area, which means the pull-back and the shot from the top of the box become the opponent's cheapest route. Sides that install inside-forcing and then concede repeatedly from the edge of the area have paid this cost without pricing it.

The third cost is cognitive. Showing inside requires two players to agree, in real time, about which of them is closing, and disagreement produces the worst outcome available: an attacker entering the central area with both defenders watching each other. This is why the instruction needs rehearsal rather than explanation.

### What showing outside costs your box defending

It costs you the delivery, and the price of the delivery depends entirely on how well your box is organised.

Showing outside is a decision to fight in the penalty area rather than in the wide area. That is a good trade when the box is organised, with the near-post contact assigned to a named player, a screen holding the edge, far-side defenders watching runners rather than the ball, and a goalkeeper with a rule about which balls he attacks. It is a bad trade when the box is a crowd of players hoping.

The second cost is territorial. A side that shows outside will spend more of the match defending inside its own penalty area, which suppresses its ability to counter and increases the number of set pieces it concedes, because blocked crosses become corners. Both are real costs and both are usually accepted without being counted.

The third cost falls on the full-back, who ends every sequence deep, facing his own goal, and unavailable for the transition. A side that installs this instruction and then wonders why it cannot counter down that flank is paying a price it did not intend to pay, and the mitigation is to name the opposite flank as the outlet before kick-off.

### What does forcing inside cost your midfield?

It commits a midfielder or a centre-back permanently to closing the central route, removes him from your counter-attacking structure, and usually leaves the edge of the penalty area unscreened, which makes the pull-back and the shot from the top of the box the opponent's cheapest route.

### What does forcing outside cost your box defending?

It converts the contest from a wide duel into a penalty-area contest, which is a good trade only if the box is genuinely organised. It also costs territory, since the side spends more time defending deep, and it raises the number of corners conceded because blocked crosses become set pieces.

## Scoreline and game state

The decision is usually made once, before kick-off, and then held for ninety minutes. That is wrong, and the reason it happens is that changing it mid-match requires telling four players at once, which is awkward, so coaches avoid it.

Three game states genuinely change the answer.

Protecting a narrow lead in the closing stages shifts the answer toward showing inside, and the reason is that the opponent has committed extra bodies forward, which means the central area now contains more of your players relative to theirs and the wide areas contain fewer. The delivery you were happy to concede at nil-nil is now arriving into a box with an extra attacker in it, and the central route you were afraid of now runs into four bodies.

Chasing a match shifts the answer toward showing outside, and the reason is not defensive at all. A side chasing needs its full-backs available to attack, which means it cannot afford the deep recovery position that inside-forcing produces, and it needs turnovers in wide areas rather than central ones because a wide turnover leaves the opponent's counter facing the touchline.

Playing with ten players shifts the answer decisively toward showing outside, and this one should be automatic rather than considered. A side with ten cannot close the central route because the player who was going to close it no longer exists in the structure. Showing outside and defending the box is the only coherent plan available, and sides that fail to make this switch concede the second goal within minutes of the first.

### Should the decision change with the scoreline?

Yes, in three states. Protecting a lead late favours showing inside, because the opponent's committed bodies leave the centre crowded with your players. Chasing favours showing outside, to keep full-backs available and turnovers wide. Playing with ten should switch to showing outside automatically.
## The in-match failure signals

An instruction is a hypothesis and it needs a falsification condition, decided before kick-off, because in the moment every coach's instinct is to demand more effort rather than to change the plan. Five signals tell a coach the choice was wrong, and each has a specific correction.

The first signal is deliveries arriving from the byline band with nobody near the crosser. This means the cover-travel test has failed in live conditions, whatever it said in training. The correction is not to tell the full-back to try harder; it is to drop the block five metres, which shortens the centre-back's travel, or to switch that flank to a pure delay instruction with no forcing component.

The second signal is the attacker turning cleanly into the central area and finding space rather than traffic. This means showing inside is not being supported. The correction is immediate and structural: switch that flank to showing outside, because the instruction is producing the outcome it exists to prevent.

The third signal is the full-back and the nearest midfielder occupying the same space while the attacker passes between them. This is the coordination failure, and it means the two players disagree about who is closing. The correction is to remove the ambiguity by assigning the closing job to one of them explicitly, and the safer assignment is almost always the midfielder, because a centre-back who steps and misses opens the goal.

The fourth signal is repeated shots from the top of the penalty area. This means the screen has been abandoned, usually because the screening midfielder has been drawn into the wide duel. The correction is to forbid him from helping, which sounds counterproductive and is the single most reliable in-match intervention in this whole area.

The fifth signal is the opponent switching flanks repeatedly. This means they have read the instruction and are attacking the side that was not prepared. The correction is to give the same instruction to both full-backs, which should have happened before kick-off, and to check that the far centre-back has not drifted more than the width of the six-yard box from his side.

The general discipline underneath all five is that a coach should decide before the match which signal he is watching for, because a coach watching everything is watching the ball. One chosen signal per flank is enough, and the first is the one to choose if only one is possible.

### How do you know your choice was wrong during a fixture?

Watch for byline deliveries with nobody near the crosser, which means cover is not travelling, or for the attacker turning cleanly into central space, which means inside-forcing is unsupported. Either signal calls for a structural change rather than more effort from the defender.

## Communicating the choice across a back four

An instruction that lives only in the coach's head is not an instruction. Four communication requirements make the difference between a plan and a hope.

The first is that the choice must be stated as a pair rather than as a single instruction. Telling the full-back to show outside without telling the centre-back that he is travelling produces a defender doing the right thing alone. The unit of instruction is the pair, and the briefing should name both players in the same sentence.

The second is that the instruction must be given per flank. Because the geometry is asymmetric, the two full-backs frequently receive different instructions, and each needs to know what the other has been told, otherwise the centre-backs cannot know what to expect when the ball switches.

The third is the in-match call, and it should be a single word agreed in advance rather than a description. Two words are enough: one meaning show him outside, one meaning show him inside, and the call is made by the centre-back rather than by the full-back, because the centre-back is the player whose availability determines which is safe. Making the covering defender the caller is the most valuable communication change most sides can make, and it costs nothing.

The fourth is the handover call when the attacker leaves the flank and enters the half-space. This is a separate call with a separate word, and it belongs to the midfielder who is taking responsibility rather than to the full-back who is giving it up, because the person taking a job must confirm he has it.

What does not work is shouting tactical concepts during a duel. A defender in a live one-on-one has no processing capacity available, and a coach transmitting a sentence at that moment is transmitting nothing. All useful communication happens before the ball arrives.

### How do you communicate the choice across a back four?

State it as a pair rather than as a single instruction, give it per flank because the geometry is asymmetric, agree a one-word in-match call for each direction, and make the covering centre-back the caller, since his availability is what determines which instruction is safe.
## The nine-year-old version

The final test of a decision rule is whether it survives compression, and this one does. Here is the version simple enough to teach a child, along with the age at which each layer of nuance should arrive.

The one-sentence version is this: stand so the touchline is on his far side, and let him run toward it.

That is the whole instruction for a young defender, and it works because it converts an abstract idea into a spatial one. A child cannot hold a concept about relative route pricing. A child can readily understand that the white line is a teammate who never moves, and that the job is to keep the attacker running toward it. The touchline as an extra defender is the correct mental model and it stays correct into senior football, which is why it is worth installing first rather than replacing later.

The second layer arrives around eleven or twelve, and it is the distance rule: stay close enough that he cannot look up, far enough that you can turn with him. This is the layer that prevents diving in, and it should be taught before any tactical nuance because it is the behaviour that decides individual duels at every level.

The third layer arrives around thirteen or fourteen, and it is the concept of cover: you can be braver about letting him go outside when you know somebody is behind you, and you must be more careful when you are alone. This is the first appearance of the cover-travel idea in a form a young player can use, and it is best taught by letting them experience both, in a session where cover is present in one round and absent in the next.

The fourth layer arrives around fifteen or sixteen, and only then, and it is the inside option: sometimes we want him to come inside because we have people there. This should not arrive earlier, for a reason worth stating plainly. A young defender who has been taught the inside option before he can execute the outside default will use the inside option as an excuse for poor positioning, and the habit is hard to remove.

The fifth layer is the footedness tiebreaker, and it belongs last rather than first, which inverts how it is usually taught. Teaching a twelve-year-old to think about the opponent's weaker foot produces a defender who watches the attacker's feet instead of holding his own position, and it produces the belief that the decision is made by scouting rather than by structure.

### What is the simplest rule to teach a young defender here?

Stand so the touchline is on his far side and let him run toward it. The touchline is a teammate who never moves. Distance comes next, cover after that, and the inside option should not be introduced until the outside default is reliable.

## Should a defender ever change it himself?

Yes, in exactly three situations, and a coach should name them in advance so that the changes are permitted rather than improvised.

The first is when the covering defender is visibly absent, meaning the centre-back has been dragged across or is on the ground. Showing inside with no support is worse than any alternative, so a defender who sees no cover should default to showing outside regardless of his instruction. Giving him this permission explicitly is important, because a defender who has been told to show inside and can see there is no help will otherwise do what he was told.

The second is when the attacker has already demonstrated that he will not take the offered route. Some opponents will refuse the outside route entirely and accept the price of coming inside every time, and a defender facing that after three repetitions is entitled to change his angle without waiting for a stoppage. The refusal to take the offered route is real and it is the correct reading, and the framework for opponents who invert the forcing decision this way is developed elsewhere in the series.

The third is at the very end of a match with a lead to protect, when the priority becomes keeping the ball away from the goal at any cost. A defender showing outside toward a corner flag with two minutes left is making a correct decision even if the pre-match instruction said otherwise.

What a defender should not do is change the instruction because he has been beaten once, because he thinks he has spotted the opponent's weaker foot, or because a teammate has shouted something. Those three account for almost every unauthorised change and none of them is a good reason.

### Should a defender ever change the decision on his own?

In three cases only: when the covering defender is visibly absent, when the opponent has repeatedly refused the offered route, and when protecting a lead in the closing minutes. Being beaten once, spotting a weaker foot, or reacting to a shout are not reasons.
## Personnel: matching the choice to the players you actually have

The grid resolves by structure, but two personnel cases come up so often that they deserve direct answers.

### The slow centre-back

A side whose covering centre-back is slow has a narrower set of options than the general framework suggests, and pretending otherwise produces a plan that fails in the first twenty minutes.

The instinct is to show inside, on the reasoning that the outside route requires a chase the centre-back cannot make. That instinct is half right and it leads to the wrong conclusion, because the same lack of speed that prevents him travelling wide also prevents him stepping into the half-space with the urgency inside-forcing requires. A slow centre-back is not a stepping centre-back, and inside-forcing without a stepping centre-back concedes the central area rather than trapping it.

The correct answer for this side is to show outside while lowering the block, which is the combination that makes the slow centre-back's limitation irrelevant. A deeper line shortens every distance he has to cover, puts him closer to the byline area before the sequence begins, and means that the delivery he is defending arrives into a box he is already inside. The cost is territory, and it is a cost worth paying, because the alternative is a plan built on a movement he cannot make.

The second answer, available to sides with the personnel, is to change who covers. Nothing requires the covering defender to be a centre-back. A holding midfielder who drops into the wide area behind a beaten full-back is a perfectly good cover, and in sides with a mobile six and a positional back four this is the better arrangement. It requires rehearsal and it requires the midfielder to accept a job that produces no visible reward.

### Which choice suits a side with a slow centre-back?

Showing outside with a lower block, which shortens every distance he must cover. The instinct to show inside is wrong, because the same lack of speed that stops him travelling wide also stops him stepping into the half-space with the urgency inside-forcing needs.

### The deep-defending side

A side that defends deep by design has the clearest case for showing inside available to any team, and it is the case least often taken.

The logic is that a deep block fills the central area with bodies, which means an attacker shown inside from a wide position arrives into traffic rather than into space. He has no shooting lane because there are four players in it, no passing lane because the distances are short, and no dribbling route because the congestion is precisely what a deep block manufactures. Meanwhile the outside route in a deep block leads to the byline against a box that is already full, which is survivable but hands over the most dangerous delivery position on the pitch repeatedly.

The condition attached is that the deep block must be genuinely compact rather than merely deep. A deep line with a midfield that has not dropped with it produces the worst structure in football, which is space between the lines in front of a retreated defence, and showing inside into that space is the most damaging instruction available. Depth without compactness inverts the verdict entirely.

The second condition is that the deep side must accept the territorial consequence. Showing inside means turnovers happen centrally and close to your own goal, which is dangerous if the recovery is imperfect and excellent if it is clean, because a central turnover in a compact block is the best counter-attacking start in the sport.

### Which choice suits a side that defends deep?

Showing inside, provided the block is genuinely compact rather than merely deep. The central area is full of bodies, so the attacker runs into traffic, and the outside route in a deep block hands over the byline position outright. Depth without compactness reverses this verdict.

## Auditing which instruction a side is actually using

An analyst asked which instruction a side uses will usually answer from the pre-match briefing, which is a description of intent rather than behaviour. Four observations reveal what is actually happening.

The first is the full-back's starting shoulder before the attacker receives. A defender positioned on the inside shoulder is showing outside; on the touchline shoulder, inside. This is legible in the two seconds before the ball arrives and it is the cleanest single observation available.

The second is the covering centre-back's first step when the ball goes wide. A step toward the wide area indicates a side that expects to concede the outside route. A step or hold toward the half-space indicates a side expecting the inside route. If the centre-back does nothing, the side has no instruction, whatever the briefing said.

The third is the near-side midfielder's behaviour, which distinguishes a genuine inside-forcing scheme from a full-back doing it alone. In a real scheme he is occupying the inside route or the edge of the area. In an accidental one he is following the ball.

The fourth is the delivery band distribution across a match, which reveals the outcome rather than the intent. A side showing outside effectively produces deliveries clustered in the deep and edge-of-area bands. A side showing outside ineffectively produces deliveries clustered at the byline. Same instruction, opposite quality, and the distribution tells them apart without any access to the coaching staff.

An analyst who records the first and fourth of these for a season builds a picture of both intent and execution, and the gap between the two is the most useful thing in any opposition file. Keeping that record organised by opponent, rather than scattered across match notes, is what [a preparation planner such as VaultBook, free to use](https://vaultbook.org/tools/football-match-planner.html) is designed for.
## The third option: removing the duel

Both instructions assume the wide duel is going to happen, and the most valuable option available to a coach is frequently neither of them. It is preventing the reception that starts the duel.

The mechanism is upstream pressure. A wide attacker who receives facing the pitch with a defender in front of him is a problem to be directed. A wide attacker who receives facing his own goal, or who does not receive at all because the pass was denied, is not a problem. The intervention happens forty metres from the duel and it is executed by the near-side central midfielder and the wide forward rather than by the full-back.

Three things make this the underused option rather than the standard one.

The first is that it costs an attacking position. The midfielder who presses the supply is not the midfielder who arrives at the edge of the box, and the wide forward who tracks the opposing full-back is not the outlet for the counter. Those costs are real and they are paid in every phase of the match, whereas the benefit only appears when the opponent tries to use that flank.

The second is that it is invisible when it works. A duel prevented produces no highlight and no touchline reaction, so the players executing it receive no reinforcement. This matters more than it sounds, because a behaviour that is never noticed decays, and the decay is gradual enough that nobody identifies when it started.

The third is that it requires the full-back to hold a different position from the one his instinct suggests. If the supply is being pressed, the full-back should be tighter to his opponent rather than deeper, because the reception will be difficult and the opportunity is to attack it. A full-back holding a delay position while his midfielder presses the supply is wasting the pressure.

The verdict is that removing the duel should be the first option considered against any wide threat and the primary plan against two specific profiles: the genuinely two-footed opponent, where neither direction has an edge, and the delivery specialist, whose entire game requires a specific reception. Against a pace threat in behind it is less useful, because that profile does not need a clean wide reception to hurt you.

### Should you press the supply instead of choosing a direction?

Frequently, and it should be the first option considered rather than the last. Preventing the reception removes the duel entirely, which is worth more than directing it. The cost is a midfielder's attacking position, and the behaviour decays because a prevented duel produces no visible reward.

## When the opponent refuses the offered route

A complication that every coach meets and few plan for: some opponents will decline the route you are offering and take the expensive one every time.

This is a legitimate attacking decision rather than stubbornness. If an attacker judges that the priced route is still worth taking, he takes it, and against a defender showing outside this produces a player who repeatedly cuts inside into pressure and accepts contact, congestion, and a narrower angle in exchange for arriving in a central area.

Three responses are available and they are not equally good.

The first is to reverse the instruction, showing him the route he keeps refusing on the theory that he will refuse that one too. This is clever and usually wrong, because his refusal is a preference rather than a reflex and he will take whichever route leads toward the goal.

The second is to hold the instruction and change the support. If he insists on coming inside against an outside-showing defender, then the inside is where the second defender should be, which converts his stubbornness into a trap. This is the correct answer in most cases and it requires only that the near midfielder is told, which can happen at the first stoppage.

The third is to change the technique rather than the direction, moving the full-back to a distance-first approach in which he stops offering a route at all and defends the ball. This produces fewer clean wins and prevents the repeated central entries, and it is the right call when the opponent has demonstrated across several sequences that he cannot be directed.

What should not happen is the response that occurs most often, which is the coach shouting at the full-back to stop letting him inside. The full-back is executing the instruction correctly; the instruction has been read and answered, and the answer belongs to the coach rather than to the defender.
## The back-three and wing-back version

Everything above assumes a back four, and the answer changes in a defined way when the defence has three centre-backs and wing-backs, which is common enough that leaving it out would make the article incomplete.

The structural difference is that a back three supplies a covering defender by design rather than by travel. When a wing-back is beaten on the outside, the nearest of the three centre-backs is already positioned to cover the wide channel, and the remaining two hold the central area. That is a materially better answer to the outside route than a back four offers, because the cover does not have to abandon the middle to provide it.

Two consequences follow.

The first is that showing outside is cheaper in a back three than in a back four, which makes it the stronger default in that structure even for sides that would show inside with four at the back. The cover-travel test still applies, but it is easier to pass, because the wide centre-back begins closer to the channel and has less ground to make up.

The second is that showing inside is more expensive, and this is the counterintuitive part. Inside-forcing in a back three directs the attacker toward three central defenders, which sounds ideal and is not, because the wing-back who showed him inside has now left the flank and the opposing side's own wing-back is arriving into it unopposed. A back three defends the middle well and the transitions between middle and flank badly, and inside-forcing attacks exactly that seam.

The third consideration is specific to the wing-back himself. He starts higher than a full-back does, which means he is frequently arriving into the duel while recovering rather than holding a set position, and a recovering defender cannot reliably show a direction at all. The realistic instruction for a wing-back who is recovering is delay only, with the wide centre-back taking the directional responsibility, and that pairing has to be rehearsed because it inverts the usual hierarchy.

### Does the answer change in a back three?

Yes. Showing outside becomes cheaper, because the wide centre-back covers the channel by design rather than by travelling. Showing inside becomes more expensive, because the wing-back who forced him inside has vacated the flank and the opposing wing-back arrives into it unopposed.

## Why the debate never resolves

A short note on why this question generates more heat than any other in defensive coaching, because understanding the disagreement helps a coach avoid being drawn into it.

The first reason is that both sides of the argument are describing real experience. A coach who has spent a career in amateur football has watched inside-forcing fail repeatedly and has concluded it is wrong. A coach who has worked in a well-drilled professional environment has watched it work and has concluded the opposite. Both are generalising correctly from their own data and incorrectly to everyone else's.

The second is that the instruction is highly visible and its consequences are not. Everybody in a stadium can see which way a full-back showed an attacker. Almost nobody can see whether the covering centre-back was in a position to make that instruction viable, because they are watching the ball. The visible half of the decision therefore absorbs all the credit and all the blame.

The third is that the outcome is noisy. A correct instruction produces a goal occasionally and an incorrect one prevents a goal occasionally, and a single fixture contains far too few wide duels to distinguish the two. Coaches who evaluate the instruction by results over one or two matches are reading noise, and reading noise produces confident conclusions in random directions.

The fourth is that nobody states their assumptions. Most of the online argument consists of people who mean different things by showing inside, applying their conclusions to different levels of football, with different personnel in mind, and arguing as though they disagree about football when they disagree about context. Fixing the definitions, as this article did at the start, removes most of the dispute before it begins.
## Auditing your own instruction over a season

The decision is made once and then either holds or decays, and decay is the normal outcome. Four records make the decay visible before it costs a match, and none of them requires equipment.

The first is the shoulder record. Once a month, watch one flank for a single sustained period of defending and note which shoulder your full-back takes before the attacker receives. If it has drifted from the instruction, the drift happened gradually and nobody noticed, which is how every defensive habit degrades.

The second is the delivery band record described earlier: where the crosses your side concedes actually come from. A rising proportion from the byline band against a stable opponent profile is the clearest signal that the cover-travel relationship has stopped working, and it usually precedes the goal that announces it by several fixtures.

The third is the central entry record, meaning how often an opponent turns into the central area from a wide position with space. If your instruction is to show outside and this number is rising, your full-backs are overcommitting to the touchline side, which is the standard execution failure of the outside instruction and is corrected by distance rather than by angle.

The fourth is the pairing record, which is qualitative rather than numerical. Once a month, watch the covering centre-back rather than the ball for one defending period and ask whether his first step when the ball goes wide matches what the full-back was told. Mismatches between the two are the most expensive defensive error a side can make and the least visible, because both players are individually doing something reasonable.

The value of keeping these across a season rather than checking them once is that the decision this article resolves is not a one-time choice. Personnel change, block heights drift, and a centre-back who passed the cover-travel test in preseason may not pass it in February. A coach who re-runs the test twice a season is making an informed decision twice; a coach who ran it once is relying on a measurement that has expired.

### How often should a coach revisit the inside or outside decision?

At least twice a season, because the cover-travel test measures current personnel at a current block height and both change. Re-run it when a centre-back is replaced, when the block height changes deliberately, and whenever byline deliveries start rising against a stable opponent profile.

## A worked example from first principles

To show the framework doing work rather than describing it, here is a complete decision made from scratch for an ordinary side.

Take a semi-professional team training twice a week, playing a 4-4-2 with a mid-block, whose right centre-back is quick and whose left centre-back is positional and slow. Their next opponent has a left-footed attacker on the right flank and a right-footed attacker on the left, and puts three players into the box on crosses with a genuine aerial target.

Run the cover-travel test on both sides. The right centre-back, covering the left flank, passes. The left centre-back, covering the right flank, fails. That single result determines most of the plan and it is specific to this side rather than to any principle.

On the side where cover travels, both options are open. The attacker there is right-footed on the left, so showing outside sends him to the byline on his stronger foot against a dangerous box, which is the concession the opponent wants. Showing inside sends him onto his weaker foot into a mid-block that is reasonably compact, with cover available. Verdict: show inside on that flank, with the near midfielder told to occupy the vacated area.

On the side where cover fails, showing inside is unavailable, because the promise cannot be kept. Showing outside sends the left-footed attacker to the byline on his weaker foot, which degrades the delivery substantially, which is fortunate but not the reason for the decision. Verdict: show outside on that flank, drop the block five metres on that side to shorten the covering distance, and fix the near-post responsibility to a named player because the opponent's box is dangerous.

Two flanks, two different instructions, both derived from the same test, neither derived from what the opponent's coach believes and neither copied from a professional side on television. The failure signal chosen for the match is the delivery band on the right flank, because that is the side where the plan is under most stress. If three deliveries arrive from the byline band in the first twenty minutes, the block on that side drops again and the full-back switches to delay only.

That is what the framework produces: two specific instructions, one measurement, one watched signal, and one prepared adjustment. It takes ten minutes on a Monday and it is more useful than any amount of general defensive coaching, because it is about this defence rather than about defending.

The final point is the one that makes the whole framework worth installing. Every instruction in this article is free. Changing a shoulder position, dropping a block five metres, telling a midfielder to hold the corner of the penalty area, agreeing a one-word call, and choosing which signal to watch cost nothing except the decision to make them. They are available to a coach with no budget, no analyst, and no video, and they produce a measurable change in where an opponent gets to deliver the ball from. Very little else in coaching offers that ratio.
## The debate register

Six positions dominate discussion of this question. Each gets a fair statement and a verdict, and the last one is partly conceded.

The first is that you always show a player down the line. The verdict is that this is the correct default at most levels and is stated as a universal rule when it is a conditional one. It is right for amateur, school, and youth football, right for most mid-level sides, and right for professional sides without a stepping centre-back. It is wrong for a compact deep block with support in place, and it is wrong on the flank where an inverted attacker's weaker foot points toward the byline. A default is not a law, and treating it as one produces coaches who cannot explain why their instruction fails against specific opponents.

The second is that showing inside is modern orthodoxy. The verdict is that this substantially overstates how widespread it is and misidentifies why it exists where it does. Inside-forcing at elite level is not a fashion; it is a plan that requires one specific player profile, a front-foot centre-back who steps aggressively into the half-space. Sides possessing that player use it, sides without it mostly do not, and coaches copying it from broadcast football without the player are copying an outcome rather than a method.

The third is that it depends entirely on the opponent's weaker foot. The verdict is that footedness is a tiebreaker applied after the structural variables, not a determinant applied before them. It degrades the final action rather than preventing the take-on, the gap between feet at elite level is smaller than reputations suggest, and every player has spent a career being shown onto his weaker side, so his routes from there are rehearsed. A coach deciding on footedness alone is deciding on the fifth most important variable.

The fourth is that the whole idea is outdated because wide players are two-footed now. The verdict is that the premise is exaggerated and the conclusion does not follow from it. Genuinely two-footed wide players remain a minority, and where one is faced the decision does not disappear; it defaults entirely to the cover-travel test and the block height, with the opponent removed from the calculation. A decision that has to be made without reference to the opponent is still a decision, and making it badly still concedes goals.

The fifth is that amateur sides should not think about it at all. The verdict is that this is wrong in a specific and costly way. Amateur sides should not think about the nuance, and they very much should install the default, because showing outside is the instruction that makes the absence of reliable support survivable. The advice to ignore the question entirely produces defenders with no positional principle at all, which is worse than a simple principle applied everywhere.

The sixth is that the instruction matters less than the defender's individual quality. The verdict is that this is partly correct and deserves the concession. A very good defender executing the wrong instruction will outperform a poor defender executing the right one, and the variance between individual defenders is larger than the variance between the two instructions. Where the position fails is in the inference. Individual quality is slow and expensive to change; the instruction is free and can be changed on Tuesday. A coach who accepts that quality dominates and therefore stops thinking about the instruction has declined the only improvement actually available to him.

## Training the decision

The instruction is a behaviour, and behaviours are installed by constraint rather than by explanation. Four coaching points carry the work, and the full session architecture belongs to the training articles in this series rather than here.

The first point is that the shoulder position must be corrected statically before it is attempted at speed. Ask a full-back to walk to a stationary attacker and stop where he thinks he should be, then move him physically to the correct shoulder and let him see what routes are open from each position. Two minutes, permanent effect, almost never done.

The second is that the exercise must be scored on route rather than on possession. If the constraint is that the defender may not attempt to win the ball and the score is where the attacker ended up, the behaviour changes within one session. If the score is duels won, the old behaviour returns immediately under competitive pressure.

The third is that cover must be present in the exercise from the beginning, because the instruction is a pair. A one-on-one exercise cannot teach a decision whose correctness depends on a second defender, and running it as a one-on-one teaches the full-back that he is alone, which is exactly the belief that produces diving in.

The fourth is that both flanks must be trained separately, because the geometry is asymmetric and a defender who has only practised on one side has learned a mirror image that will not transfer.

The failure that ruins installation is running it live and competitive from the start. A live duel returns the defender to his instincts, the old behaviour reasserts itself, and the coach concludes the instruction does not work. Constraint first, competition later, in that order and not the other one.
## The amateur coach's version

A coach with one session a week and a squad that changes every Saturday needs the compressed version, and it is short enough to deliver in a minute.

The instruction is: show him outside, on both flanks, in every fixture, until you have a reason not to.

The reason this is the right advice rather than a simplification is that showing outside is the only one of the two instructions that survives the absence of reliable support. A full-back who shows outside and is beaten has conceded a wide position, which the rest of the team can survive without doing anything clever. A full-back who shows inside and is beaten has conceded the middle of the pitch, which requires somebody to be in the right place and somebody usually is not.

Three additions make the default work rather than merely exist.

The first is the distance instruction, which matters more than the direction at this level: stay close enough that he cannot look up, far enough that you can turn with him. Most amateur goals conceded from wide areas come from a defender diving in, not from a defender showing the wrong way.

The second is the midfielder's screen: when the ball goes wide, the nearest central midfielder stands on the corner of the penalty area and does not go and help. This is the hardest instruction to get obeyed and the one that prevents the goal type that decides most amateur matches, which is the pull-back to an unmarked runner at the edge of the box.

The third is the near-post assignment: one named player is responsible for the near post on every cross. Not a zone, not an understanding, a name.

What an amateur coach should not attempt is the inside option, the flank asymmetry, or the game-state switches. All three require coordination that a side training once a week cannot maintain, and a half-installed scheme is worse than a simple one because players hesitate between two answers. Hesitation loses more goals than a suboptimal instruction ever will.

### Which choice suits amateur football?

Showing outside, on both flanks, in almost every fixture. It is the only instruction that survives unreliable support, because a full-back beaten on the outside concedes a wide position rather than the middle of the pitch, and the rest of the team can absorb that without doing anything clever.

## Verdict

The real answer to show him inside or outside is that the question is being asked about the wrong thing. It is not a question about the opponent, and the habit of answering it by scouting his weaker foot is why so many sides get it wrong while feeling well prepared. It is a question about your own defence, and specifically about one measurable capability: whether your covering centre-back can arrive in the wide area before the delivery.

That is the cover-travel test, and it is the deciding factor in every context in this article. If he travels, you have both options and the other variables choose between them. If he does not, showing outside with a lower block is the coherent plan and showing inside is a plan that will concede central entries. A coach who has not run the test with his real players at his real block height does not have the information the decision requires, and no amount of opposition analysis substitutes for it.

Around that central test, four verdicts hold. Elite sides with a stepping centre-back can show inside and often should. Professional sides with a positional centre-back should show outside and build the box for the delivery. Mid-level sides should show outside as a default with a rehearsed late-game exception. Amateur, school, and youth sides should show outside always, and should not be taught the nuance until the default is reliable.

Three refinements survive compression and are worth carrying. The geometry is asymmetric, so the instruction depends on the attacker's footedness relative to the flank rather than on the flank itself, and a side can correctly show outside on one side and inside on the other. Against a genuinely two-footed opponent the calculation loses the opponent entirely and defaults to your own structure, with the defender's advantage coming from distance rather than angle. And the decision should change with the game state in three specific situations, most urgently when a side goes down to ten players, when showing outside becomes the only coherent option available.

The teaching version is one sentence and it survives all the way to senior football: stand so the touchline is on his far side and let him run toward it, because the touchline is a teammate who never moves.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Should you show a wide opponent inside or outside?

Outside, in the large majority of contexts, and the exception is narrower than the online debate suggests. Showing outside is correct for amateur, school, and youth football, correct for most mid-level sides, and correct for professional sides whose covering centre-back is positional rather than mobile. Showing inside is genuinely correct in one situation: a compact block with a quick front-foot centre-back who steps into the half-space before the attacker's second touch, supported by a midfielder who occupies the vacated area. The deciding variable is not the opponent's weaker foot but whether your own covering centre-back can arrive in the wide area before the delivery. If he can, both options are open and the block height and the opponent's box occupation choose between them. If he cannot, showing outside with a lower block is the only coherent plan.

### Q: When is forcing an opponent down the line the right choice?

When your covering centre-back cannot travel wide quickly, when your block is high with a large space behind it, when your support is unrehearsed or unreliable, or when the opponent's box occupation is thin enough that the delivery is survivable. It is also the correct default at every level below fully professional football, for a structural reason rather than a tactical one: it is the only one of the two instructions that survives the absence of a second defender. A full-back beaten on the outside has conceded a wide position, which the rest of the team can absorb without doing anything clever. A full-back beaten after showing inside has conceded the middle of the pitch, which requires somebody to be in exactly the right place.

### Q: When is forcing an opponent inside the right choice?

When four conditions hold together. You need a quick front-foot centre-back who will step into the half-space before the attacker's second touch, because the instruction is a promise that the central route will be closed and somebody has to close it. You need a block that is genuinely compact rather than merely deep, so the attacker arrives into traffic rather than into space between the lines. You need rehearsed support arriving within about a second, which means training frequency sufficient to maintain a coordinated behaviour. And you benefit most when the opponent's box occupation is dangerous enough that conceding deliveries is the more expensive of the two concessions. Remove any one of the four and the instruction becomes an invitation rather than a trap.

### Q: Does showing an opponent onto his weaker foot always work?

No, and treating it as the primary consideration is the most common error in this decision. Footedness is a tiebreaker applied after the structural variables rather than a determinant applied before them, and it is weaker evidence than most coaches assume for three reasons. The weaker foot governs the quality of the final action rather than the outcome of the take-on, so a player can still beat you in that direction and then adjust. The gap between a strong and a weaker foot at elite level is frequently smaller than the gap between a good and a poor defensive structure. And every wide player has spent an entire career being shown onto his weaker side, so the routes he takes from there are practised rather than improvised.

### Q: What does forcing inside cost your midfield?

It commits a player permanently to closing the central route, and that player is usually the one you need higher and facing forward when possession turns over. Whoever takes the job, the near centre-back stepping or the central midfielder dropping, cannot leave the position, because leaving it converts the instruction into an invitation. There is a second cost that is less obvious and more expensive: a midfielder responsible for the inside route cannot simultaneously screen the edge of the penalty area, which makes the pull-back and the shot from the top of the box the opponent's cheapest route. Sides that install inside-forcing and then concede repeatedly from the edge of the area have paid that cost without ever pricing it.

### Q: What does forcing outside cost your box defending?

It converts the contest from a wide duel into a penalty-area contest, which is a good trade only if the box is genuinely organised: a named player responsible for the near post, a screen holding the edge, far-side defenders watching runners rather than the ball, and a goalkeeper with a stated rule about which deliveries he attacks. Without that organisation it is a bad trade. There are two further costs usually accepted without being counted. The side spends more of the match defending inside its own area, which suppresses counter-attacking. And blocked crosses become corners, so the number of set pieces conceded rises, which has to be prepared for rather than resented.

### Q: Which choice suits a side with a slow centre-back?

Showing outside, with a lower block. The instinct runs the other way, on the reasoning that the outside route requires a chase he cannot make, and that instinct leads to the wrong conclusion. The same lack of speed that stops him travelling wide also stops him stepping into the half-space with the urgency that inside-forcing requires, so a slow centre-back is not a stepping centre-back and inside-forcing without one concedes the central area rather than trapping it. Dropping the block instead shortens every distance he must cover and puts him inside the box before the delivery arrives. The alternative worth considering is changing who covers, using a mobile holding midfielder as the wide cover behind a beaten full-back.

### Q: Which choice suits a side that defends deep?

Showing inside, provided the block is genuinely compact rather than merely deep. A compact deep block fills the central area with bodies, so an attacker shown inside from a wide position arrives into traffic with no shooting lane, no passing lane, and no dribbling route. The outside route in a deep block hands over the byline position repeatedly. The condition is essential: a deep defensive line with a midfield that has not dropped with it produces space between the lines, and showing inside into that space is the most damaging instruction available. The side also has to accept that turnovers will happen centrally and near its own goal, which is dangerous if the recovery is imperfect and excellent if it is clean.

### Q: Should the decision change with the scoreline?

Yes, in three states, and most sides fail to change it because telling four players at once mid-match is awkward. Protecting a narrow lead late favours showing inside, because the opponent has committed extra bodies forward, so the central area now contains more of your players relative to theirs while the box contains an extra attacker. Chasing a match favours showing outside, for attacking reasons rather than defensive ones: you need your full-backs available and you want turnovers in wide areas where the opponent's counter faces the touchline. Going down to ten players should switch to showing outside automatically, because the player who was going to close the central route no longer exists in the structure.

### Q: Should the decision change depending on which flank the ball is on?

Yes, and this is the most commonly missed point in the whole question. The geometry is not symmetric, and the correct instruction depends on the attacker's footedness relative to the flank rather than on the flank itself. A right-footed attacker on the right shown outside travels toward the byline on his stronger foot; a left-footed attacker on the right shown outside travels there on his weaker one, which degrades his delivery severely. That means a side can correctly show outside on one flank and inside on the other in the same fixture, which requires both full-backs to be briefed separately and the centre-backs to know which is which. Cover mobility usually differs between your two centre-backs as well.

### Q: How do you communicate the choice across a back four?

State it as a pair rather than as a single instruction, because telling the full-back to show outside without telling the centre-back he is travelling produces one defender doing the right thing alone. Give it per flank, since the geometry is asymmetric and each full-back needs to know what the other has been told. Agree a one-word in-match call for each direction, and make the covering centre-back the caller rather than the full-back, because his availability is what determines which instruction is safe, which is the most valuable communication change most sides can make and costs nothing. Add a separate handover call for when the attacker enters the half-space, spoken by the player taking responsibility rather than the one giving it up.

### Q: Which choice suits amateur football?

Showing outside, on both flanks, in almost every fixture, until there is a specific reason not to. The justification is robustness rather than optimality. At amateur level the second defender arrives late, arrives in the wrong place, or does not arrive, and that is a consequence of limited training time rather than of understanding, so no amount of instruction fixes it quickly. Showing outside survives the absence of support in a way showing inside does not. There is a developmental reason too: defenders taught to show outside learn to use the touchline as a defensive tool, which is durable and transfers upward, while defenders taught to show inside learn to rely on help that does not come.

### Q: Does the answer change against a two footed opponent?

The opponent drops out of the calculation entirely, which is uncomfortable for coaches used to deciding by scouting. With no meaningful weaker side available, the choice cannot be made by reference to him, so it defaults completely to the cover-travel test and the block height. If your centre-back travels and your block is deep, show inside; if he does not travel or the block is high, show outside, and there is no third consideration. The technique also changes: the defender's advantage has to come from distance rather than angle, holding roughly a stride and a half without pre-loading weight to either side. That produces fewer clean wins and far fewer catastrophic losses. Pressing his supply also stops being optional and becomes the primary plan.

### Q: How do you know your choice was wrong during a fixture?

Five signals, and a coach should choose one per flank before kick-off rather than trying to watch everything. Deliveries arriving from the byline band with nobody near the crosser means cover is not travelling in live conditions whatever training suggested, and the correction is to drop the block rather than to demand more effort. The attacker turning cleanly into central space rather than into traffic means inside-forcing is unsupported and that flank should switch immediately. The full-back and midfielder occupying the same space while the attacker passes between them means they disagree about who is closing. Repeated shots from the top of the area means the screen has been abandoned. The opponent switching flanks repeatedly means they have read the instruction.

### Q: Should a defender ever change the decision on his own?

In three situations only, and a coach should name them in advance so the changes are permitted rather than improvised. When the covering defender is visibly absent, dragged across or on the ground, the defender should default to showing outside regardless of instruction, because showing inside with no support is worse than any alternative. When the opponent has repeatedly refused the offered route and accepted the price of the other one, three repetitions is enough evidence to change the angle without waiting for a stoppage. And in the closing minutes with a lead to protect, steering the ball toward a corner flag is correct whatever the pre-match plan said. Being beaten once, spotting a weaker foot, or reacting to a shout are not reasons.

### Q: What is the simplest rule to teach a young defender here?

Stand so the touchline is on his far side, and let him run toward it. That single sentence works because it converts an abstract idea about relative route pricing into a spatial one a child can hold: the white line is a teammate who never moves, and the job is to keep the attacker running toward it. The mental model stays correct into senior football, which is why it is worth installing first rather than replacing later. The layers arrive in order after it: distance around eleven or twelve, the idea of cover around thirteen or fourteen, the inside option not before fifteen or sixteen, and the footedness tiebreaker last of all rather than first.
