## The Verdict First: A Sentence That Behaves Like an Epitaph

The Prince's closing speech in Romeo and Juliet is six lines long, and the verdict of this reading is that those six lines do three kinds of work at once: they sentence a city, they bury two children, and they convert everything the audience has watched into a story that Verona will go on telling. "A glooming peace this morning with it brings" (5.3.305) opens the speech, and "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3.309-310) closes the play. Between those poles the speech performs the most compressed act of government in the tragedy. It declares a peace, adjourns a court, divides a population into the pardoned and the punished, and then, in its final couplet, stops governing altogether and starts commemorating. The couplet is a verdict disguised as an epitaph, and the disguise is the meaning: the Prince can close the feud's case only by admitting that the case has become a tale.

![The Prince delivering the closing speech over the bodies in the Capulet tomb - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-12.webp)

Every earlier speech of Prince Escalus has been an instrument of rule, an edict in Act 1, a sentence in Act 3, and each one failed to rule anything. The closing speech succeeds precisely where the instruments failed, because it abandons command for record. Where the edict threatened, "Your lives shall pay the forfeit of the peace" (1.1.94-95), the closing speech promises only talk, "more talk of these sad things" (5.3.307), and where the sentence in Act 3 divided one offender from the city, the closing speech divides the whole city and then dissolves the division into a single shared noun, woe. The argument of the pages that follow is that the couplet's formal features, its slant rhymes, its overflowing final line, its placing of Juliet's name before Romeo's, are not ornaments on that transformation. They are the transformation, enacted in sound. Quotations throughout follow the text of René Weis's Arden Third Series edition, and line references follow that edition's numbering, which differs by a line or two from other editions at several points in the scene.

The claim will be tested against the speech's own words first, then against the frame the Chorus built at the play's opening, then against the critics who have treated the ending as formal completion, as visionary transfiguration, and as an editorial problem. The reading is set out line by line in a table, because a six-line speech earns the slow treatment: each line is short enough to quote entire and dense enough to carry a paragraph.

One clarification belongs at the threshold, since the speech is often misdescribed as a lament. A lament is spoken by the bereaved over the body, and the play contains great ones, the household's cries over Juliet's counterfeit corpse in Act 4 among them. The closing speech's speaker is not bereaved in that register, whatever his losses, and its grammar is not the grammar of lament, which runs on apostrophe, repetition, and the second person addressed to the dead. The Prince's six lines contain no apostrophe, no repetition beyond the balanced somes, and no address to the dead at all. The lines are built from statements, one imperative of dismissal, and a concluding comparison. Classifying the speech correctly matters because the misclassification flatters it into the wrong competition, against Juliet's laments and Romeo's farewells, where its plainness looks like failure. In its own competition, against edicts and sentences and the closing speeches of the other tragedies, the plainness is the achievement, and the pages that follow judge it there.

## What Are the Last Lines of the Play? Six Lines After a Long Scene

Act 5 Scene 3 is the longest scene in the play, a scene of arrivals, discoveries, accusations, and explanations, and its full movement belongs to [the tomb scene and the double suicide that fills it](/2023/12/08/act-5-scene-3-tomb-double-suicide/). The closing speech is the still point at the end of that movement. By the time the Prince speaks, the stage holds the bodies of Romeo, Juliet, and Paris, the fathers have clasped hands over the corpses of their heirs, Montague has vowed a statue of Juliet "in pure gold" (5.3.299), and Capulet has answered with a statue of Romeo and the bleakest compliment in the play, "Poor sacrifices of our enmity" (5.3.304). The reconciliation those speeches enact is treated in full in the account of [the final reconciliation of the fathers and the golden statues they vow](/2023/12/15/final-reconciliation-golden-statues/). What remains for the Prince is not reconciliation, which the fathers have performed without him, but ratification and record. His six lines are all that stand between the handshake and the emptying stage.

The shape of the six lines matters. They are not a speech of grief in the manner of the laments in Act 4, nor a speech of command in the manner of the edict. They fall into three movements of two lines each. The first movement is weather and hour: a peace arrives with the morning, and the sun refuses its office. The second movement is procedure: the company is dismissed to further talk, and the future is divided between pardon and punishment. The third movement is the couplet itself, which abandons weather and procedure alike for a superlative claim about a story. A judge who begins with the sky and ends with literature has travelled a long way in six lines, and the travelling is the speech's plot. Each movement surrenders a portion of the Prince's authority. The weather will not obey him. The sentences are postponed beyond the play's edge. The story, once named, belongs to everyone who repeats it, which is to say it no longer belongs to him at all.

### How are the six lines of the closing speech grouped by their rhymes?

The six lines group as a sestet of three pairs, each pair bound by an approximate rhyme rather than an exact one: brings answers things, head gestures toward punished, and woe leans against Romeo. No pair chimes cleanly. The grouping promises couplet architecture while withholding the acoustic satisfaction couplets usually deliver, a withholding examined in its own section below.

The brevity also deserves its weight. The Friar's narrative, delivered minutes earlier in stage time, sprawls across more than forty lines of explanation, beginning with a promise of brevity, "my short date of breath / Is not so long as is a tedious tale" (5.3.229-230), and then telling the tale at length anyway. The Prince's closing speech keeps the promise the Friar broke. Six lines follow forty because authority at the end of this play speaks in proportion to its failure: the more completely the state understands what happened, the less it has to say. Romeo's own final speech, the long farewell over Juliet's body earlier in the scene, runs to more than forty lines as well, and its answering relation to the Prince's six is a single comparative point here, not a second subject: the lover who caused the ending talks at length, and the ruler who must absorb it has nearly stopped talking.

## The Stage Picture: A Full Court Addressed by the Shortest Speech

Count the witnesses. When the Prince begins the closing speech, the churchyard and tomb hold the largest assembly of the play's final movement: the two fathers and Lady Capulet, the Friar under guard, Balthasar with his master's letter, Paris's page, the Chief Watchman and his men, and the three bodies. Nearly every surviving strand of the plot has a representative standing in earshot, and several of the silent figures have spoken some of the scene's most consequential sentences, the page who fetched the Watch, the servant who carried the false report to Mantua. The closing speech is addressed to all of them and to none of them individually. After a scene built from interrogation, in which the Prince has demanded accounts from the Friar, the page, and Balthasar in turn, the final speech asks nothing. It is the only speech in the scene that is pure pronouncement, and its refusal to interrogate marks the exact point where investigation ends and record begins.

The bodies discipline the rhetoric. A speaker pronouncing a superlative about a story while the story's evidence lies unburied at his feet is under a discipline that no study performance can reproduce, and the discipline explains the speech's famous restraint. There is no apostrophe in the six lines, no address to the dead, no turning aside to the corpses, although the play has trained its audience to expect apostrophe at every crisis of feeling, from Romeo at the tomb's mouth to Juliet over the vial. The Prince does not speak to the dead at all. He speaks about them, in the third person and finally in a possessive, and the grammatical distance is the sound of office reasserting itself after five acts in which every private voice has been allowed its aria. The lovers' world ran on address, vows exchanged, stars defied, daggers greeted. The state's world, restored in the closing speech, runs on reference: these sad things, this story. The tragedy ends when the pronouns change.

The exit completes the picture. The stage directions of the early texts give the company a collective departure, and editors have traditionally marked a general exeunt on or near the final line, so that the play's last visual event is not a tableau of grief but a dispersal, men filing away to talk in another place. The contrast with the play's opening could hardly be sharper. The tragedy began with a crowd gathering, servants swelling into masters, masters into a prince, the street filling until authority arrived to thin it. It ends with a crowd dispersing at authority's word, the churchyard emptying until nothing remains but the tomb and its contents. The closing speech is the hinge of that reversal, and its brevity is the measure of how little pressure the hinge requires: one imperative, "Go hence," achieves what three acts of edicts could not, a Verona that moves when the Prince speaks. The assembly also fixes the speech's social range in a way no summary should blur: the words are heard by a servant who carried a letter, a page who fetched the Watch, a friar under guard, two fathers, Lady Capulet fresh from her daughter's tomb, and the officers who will dig the graves, and the speech grades none of them, addressing a city entire in the hearing of its whole surviving cross-section.

Read as a single instrument, the table shows a speech contracting. The first two lines face outward, toward sky and hour. The middle two face forward, toward proceedings the audience will never see. The last two face backward, toward the action completed, and reclassify it. Closure in this speech is therefore not one gesture but a sequence of three different kinds of ending, meteorological, legal, and literary, laid end to end so that the failure of each hands the task to the next. The peace cannot close the play because the sun contradicts it. The law cannot close the play because its sentences are deferred. The story closes the play because a story, unlike a peace or a sentence, is finished precisely by being handed over to retelling.

Two rows of the table reward a slower look than the rest. The fourth row, the balanced pardon line, is the only line of the six that could be spoken by the Prince of Act 1 without revision, pure antithetical statecraft, and its presence in the middle of the speech is a fossil, the old voice embedded in the new, proving that the closing speech's transformation is a layering rather than a replacement: the magistrate has not been exchanged for a poet, he has been outlasted by his own instruments one by one until the poet's instrument is the only one left in his hand. And the sixth row's overflow, the eleventh syllable, gains a further shade from the table's arrangement: it is the single row in which the line's content, a name, exceeds the line's form, so that the reading's last entry records the exact point where the tragedy's human material proves larger than the verse built to carry it. A table is a humble instrument, but for a speech of six lines it is the right one, because the speech itself is a kind of table, an accounting, and the reading merely extends the speech's own method to the speech.

## Glooming Peace: A First Line That Refuses to Celebrate

"A glooming peace" is a small oxymoron, and the play has been preparing the ear for oxymoron since Romeo's first lovesick catalogue. Peace is the noun the whole tragedy has been demanding, the condition the edict tried to enforce and the feud kept refusing, and when peace finally arrives the Prince cannot let it stand unqualified. The participle "glooming" darkens it at the moment of announcement. Glooming is dawn language, the word for light that has not yet decided to be light, and it catches the exact hour: the scene ends at daybreak, with a peace that has come too late to be morning in any festive sense. The word also carries mood inside weather. A glooming peace is a sullen peace, a peace with a face like the fathers' faces, achieved and joyless in the same breath.

The syntax sharpens the refusal. The Prince does not say that the morning brings a glooming peace, though the sense runs that way; he says "A glooming peace this morning with it brings," fronting the peace and stranding the verb, so that the sentence itself seems to carry its object heavily, the way the Watch has carried bodies. Peace is cargo in this line, something the morning hauls into the churchyard, and cargo is weighed, not celebrated. Then the second line completes the thought with the sun's refusal, "The sun for sorrow will not show his head" (5.3.306). In a play where the sun has been Juliet's metaphor and the lovers' clock, the closing speech retires the image into mourning. The heavens file no objection to the peace, but they decline to illuminate it. A government proclamation normally claims the endorsement of the order of things; this one records the order of things withholding endorsement.

### What does the Prince mean by "these sad things" in the third line of the closing speech?

The phrase is a pointing gesture rather than a description: "these sad things" gathers the bodies, the broken marriage plans, the exiled son, and the feud's whole cost into one deictic sweep, because itemizing them belongs to the offstage inquiry the line announces. The vagueness is procedural tact, postponing every particular to the promised talk.

The sun's line holds one more refinement. The sun is given a motive, sorrow, and a small refusal of office, the withholding of a head, at exactly the point in the play where the great personifications retire. Death, who has been bridegroom, paramour, and keeper through five acts of figure, receives no mention in the closing speech; nature receives one modest emotion and withdraws. The distribution is a scale model of the tragedy's ending in the world of things: the large fictions dissolve, the small facts remain, and a sun that will not show its head is the largest statement the disenchanted scene permits itself. The Prince, who never trafficked in personification even at his most rhetorical, lets the cosmos grieve in a subordinate clause and returns to business, and the restraint reads, after five acts of magnificent figure, as the first fully adult sentence in the play.

Against this opening, set the play's other mornings. The tragedy opened on a morning already armed, servants biting thumbs before the sun had climbed, and the Friar's great dawn speech in Act 2 weighed the same hour and found the earth holding both womb and grave. The closing speech's morning is the third term of the pattern, a dawn that brings neither brawl nor philosophy but an inventory. That is why the first line's weather matters to the verdict: a peace announced as glooming has already confessed its price, and a speaker who confesses the price in his first line has earned the right to pronounce the superlative in his last.

## Edict, Sentence, Epitaph: The Education of a Voice

The closing speech is the third term of a sequence that spans the play, and its meaning depends on hearing all three terms together. The Prince speaks in set form three times, and each speech is shorter in its claims than the one before. In Act 1 he enters over a drawn-weapon brawl and speaks as pure prohibition: "Rebellious subjects, enemies to peace" (1.1.77) is his first address to the city, a vocative that defines the citizens by their offence, and the speech that follows is a statute delivered at sword's point, complete with the recollection of three prior breaches and the tariff for a fourth. The voice is magnificent and useless. Within hours of stage time the feud it forbids has produced a masked intrusion, a duel challenge, and a marriage plot, none of which the law sees, because the law's instrument can only address the feud when the feud performs itself in the street.

In Act 3 the same voice returns diminished, and the diminution is audible in its syntax. The sentence on Romeo is delivered under pressure from both houses, and it arrives hedged with legal reasoning, the Prince rehearsing the claims of justice and kinship before pronouncing a penalty, exile, that satisfies neither: "And for that offence / Immediately we do exile him hence" (3.1.185-186). Where the edict spoke in absolutes, the sentence speaks in compromises, and the Prince knows the compromise is compromised, defending it in advance with the proverb about mercy murdering by pardoning. A ruler explaining his sentence while he gives it has already lost the authority the sentence needs. The consequences run exactly as the proverb feared, though by the opposite route: the pardoning element in the sentence, the spared life, returns to Verona and kills Paris, while the punishing element, the exile, kills Romeo and Juliet by separating the husband from the plan.

The closing speech completes the sequence by abandoning both registers. No prohibition, because there is nothing left to prohibit that prohibition could save. No sentence, because every candidate for the dock carries a share the speech has already confessed for its own speaker. What remains is the third register, epitaph, the voice that records. The education traced across the three speeches is the tragedy's political plot in miniature, and it runs against the usual direction of such plots: authority does not grow from weakness into strength but from total claim into true account, arriving at power only when it stops performing power and starts keeping the record straight. The Prince who could not keep the peace becomes, in six lines, the one officer of Verona whose account of the catastrophe the audience accepts without correction. No character in the play contradicts the closing speech, qualifies it, or survives it by a single line. For a ruler whose every previous utterance was answered by events with a refutation, that uncontested six lines are a kind of coronation, held over graves.

## Some Shall Be Pardoned: The Sentence That Sentences No One

The second movement of the speech is a dismissal and a division. "Go hence, to have more talk of these sad things; / Some shall be pardoned, and some punished" (5.3.307-308). The imperative "Go hence" is the only command in the closing speech, and it commands departure, not obedience: the Prince who once filled a street with threats now uses his imperative voice to empty a churchyard. Everything judicial is pushed beyond the frame. The talk will happen elsewhere, the pardons and punishments will fall on persons unnamed, and the play ends with its legal business conspicuously unfinished. Set against the edict of Act 1, where the Prince named the crime, the place, and the penalty in advance, the closing speech's vagueness is a confession. Law that announces its outcomes before the offence has learned, at ruinous cost, to announce nothing but a process.

The division itself is built as a perfect balance: "Some shall be pardoned, and some punished." Some against some, the passive verbs matched, the alliteration of pardoned and punished locking the alternatives together as two pans of one scale. The balance is beautiful and empty, and the emptiness is diagnostic. In Act 3 the Prince attempted a midpoint of exactly this kind, banishing Romeo where justice demanded death, and defended the midpoint with a proverb, "Mercy but murders, pardoning those that kill" (3.1.196). That midpoint killed Mercutio's kinsman Paris by chain of consequence and brought the banished man back to a tomb. The closing speech repeats the balanced structure with the content drained out, as if the form of equity survived while its application had become impossible. Who stands in the dock? The Friar, whose plot miscarried; the Nurse, who counselled a second marriage; the fathers, whose enmity armed every hand; the Prince himself, as his own earlier lines admit. A sentence that must somehow weigh all of these cannot be pronounced in a churchyard at dawn, so the speech pronounces its shape instead, and adjourns.

### Why does the closing speech address its dismissal to the whole company rather than to the two fathers alone?

Because the failure being adjourned belongs to the whole city, not to two households only: servants carried the feud into the street, the Prince's kinsmen fought and died inside it, the Friar plotted within it, and the Watch arrived too late. "Go hence" dismisses a population, and the pardon that follows must sort a population as well.

The deferral looks bolder still against the play's source. In Arthur Brooke's Romeus and Juliet of 1562, the poem from which the story descends, the authorities distribute the aftermath with brisk completeness: the Nurse is banished for concealing the marriage, the Friar withdraws to a hermitage outside Verona and dies there within a few years, and the apothecary who sold the poison is hanged. Brooke's Verona closes its accounts name by name, and the poem's moralizing frame approves the accounting. Shakespeare, who followed Brooke's plot with unusual fidelity elsewhere, reached the distribution scene and declined to distribute. The closing speech keeps Brooke's structure, a prince summing up over the bodies, and removes its content, every individual sentence the source supplied. The refusal is among the most consequential single changes between the poem and the play, because it transfers the work of judgment from the magistrate to the audience, who leave the theatre still holding the unsorted names. The pardoned and the punished remain a form with the names left blank, and every reader since has filled the blanks privately, which is to say the adjournment never actually ends.

There is also mercy in the deferral, and the speech knows it. A verdict pronounced over fresh bodies hardens into vengeance, as Verona's whole history demonstrates. By sending the company away to talk, the Prince replaces the feud's favourite instrument, the immediate answering blow, with the one instrument the feud never used, deliberation after the fact. The closing speech is the first act of Veronese government conducted at the speed of grief rather than the speed of insult. That slowness is the peace's best credential, better than the statues, better than the handshake, and it is purchased by leaving the story, rather than any defendant, to receive the final emphasis.

## The Evidence Under the Speech: A Letter, a Confession, a Page

A verdict is only as good as its evidence, and the closing speech rests on the most documentary foundation of any speech in the play. By the time the Prince pronounces on the story, the scene has assembled a small archive. The Friar has given a sworn narrative covering the marriage, the potion, the letter, and the tomb. Paris's page has testified to the fight in the churchyard. Balthasar has accounted for the report that drew Romeo from Mantua. And presiding over the whole archive is a piece of paper: Romeo's letter to his father, carried by Balthasar, opened and read by the Prince himself, who certifies its corroboration in the scene's driest and most important sentence, "This letter doth make good the Friar's words" (5.3.287). The story the couplet calls unmatched in woe is therefore not the Prince's impression. It is a verified file, confession checked against document, document checked against corpses.

That foundation explains the couplet's otherwise startling confidence. A superlative pronounced on rumour would be rhetoric; pronounced over an archive, it becomes a finding. The play has been saturated from its first act with failed documents and miscarried messages, a challenge delivered by letter that Tybalt sends and Romeo never answers in time, an invitation list carried by an illiterate servant into the wrong hands, and above all the Friar's letter, the one text whose delivery would have prevented everything, stopped by plague and quarantine in a sealed house. Against that history of textual failure, the ending assembles the first successful transmission of the tragedy: a letter that arrives, is read by the right person, and is believed. The closing speech's authority is bibliographic before it is emotional. Verona's ruler finally governs by reading.

The archive has one more implication for the couplet's famous word. A "story" is what an archive becomes when a community agrees to carry it, and the Prince's six lines perform the agreement. The Friar supplied the facts, the letter supplied the proof, and the closing speech supplies the form in which both will be remembered, a form with a title embedded in it, the story of Juliet and her Romeo. Every later act of the tale's survival, the statues, the fathers' jointure, the play itself in the hands of its audience, descends from that act of filing. The couplet is the archive's label, written by the only man in Verona with the standing to label it, and labels outlast archives.

## The Judge Inside the Dock: A Confession Before the Sentence

The closing speech cannot be read apart from the short, fierce address that precedes the fathers' reconciliation, the speech in which the Prince turns from investigator to defendant. "Where be these enemies? Capulet! Montague! / See, what a scourge is laid upon your hate, / That heaven finds means to kill your joys with love! / And I, for winking at your discords too, / Have lost a brace of kinsmen. All are punished" (5.3.291-295). This is the porch through which the closing speech must be entered. The judge who will presently divide the city into pardoned and punished has already entered his own name in the second column. "Winking" is the precise word for his offence: not corruption, not alliance with either house, but the deliberate closing of an eye, authority choosing not to see what its edicts forbade. The closing speech's refusal to name the punished is partly explained here. A judge inside the dock does not point.

The brace of kinsmen gives the confession its arithmetic. The Prince has lost Mercutio in Act 3 and Paris in Act 5, one kinsman to the feud's street violence and one to its marriage politics, and his own household's tally of the dead is therefore second only to the great houses themselves. "All are punished" (5.3.295) is the one-line draft of which the closing speech is the fair copy. Both speeches divide the world without remainder; both refuse the comfort of a single villain. The difference is temperature. The scourge speech burns. The closing speech, spoken after the Friar's long exculpation and the fathers' handshake, has cooled into record, and the cooling is the transition the whole scene exists to perform, from a city that answers death with accusation to a city that answers death with an account.

One word in the scourge speech vanishes entirely from the closing speech, and the vanishing is instructive: heaven. The scourge speech reads the catastrophe theologically, "heaven finds means to kill your joys with love" (5.3.293), heaven as the feud's true antagonist, working through the love it permitted. The closing speech contains no heaven, no god, no providence, no scourge. Its agents are a morning, a sun in mourning dress, an adjourned company, and a story. Between the two speeches the Prince's explanatory world contracts from the cosmic to the civic, and the contraction matches the Friar's failure, since the play's one professional interpreter of heaven has just finished explaining how his heaven-guided plan miscarried on a quarantined letter. The closing speech draws the conclusion the scene has earned: whatever heaven did, the record Verona can use is human, a peace to keep, an inquiry to hold, and a story to rank. The couplet's superlative is a secular judgment, delivered by a magistrate who has stopped appealing upward.

Read in that light, the closing speech's famous gentleness is not softness. The Prince speaks last and speaks briefly because every other voice in the scene has exhausted its genre: the lovers have spoken love and farewell, the Friar has spoken evidence, the fathers have spoken bargain. What remains is the genre none of them can perform, the official summary, and the official summary of this catastrophe cannot be a triumph. It can only be a peace described as glooming and a story described as unmatched in woe.

## Never Was a Story: The Superlative and the Frame

The couplet begins with a causal particle: "For never was a story of more woe." The "For" matters. The couplet is not an appendage of feeling after the business concludes; it presents itself as the reason for the business, the ground on which pardon, punishment, and peace all rest. Because the story is what it is, the city must go hence and talk. Grammar makes the tale the premise of the government. Then comes the superlative, "never," a word a careful judge should not be able to afford. The Prince has heard one tale, the Friar's, assembled from a letter, a potion, and a tomb. On that evidence he pronounces a universal negative about all stories whatsoever, and the pronouncement is legally worthless and poetically exact. The superlative is the sound of a mind passing the boundary of its office. Inside the office, men are pardoned or punished in degrees. Outside it, a story is the saddest there is, without degree.

The word "story" completes the frame that [the Chorus and the question of who speaks the play's frame](/2023/03/17/chorus-in-romeo-and-juliet-analysis/) opened in the Prologue. The Chorus began by confiscating the ending, announcing "a pair of star-crossed lovers" who "take their life" (Prologue 5-6) and describing the whole action as "the fearful passage of their death-marked love" (Prologue 9), then asked for patience on the promise that performance would mend the summary's gaps: "The which, if you with patient ears attend, / What here shall miss, our toil shall strive to mend" (Prologue 12-14). Note the pronoun problem the Chorus solved and the Prince inherits. The Chorus addressed an audience outside the fiction; the Prince speaks inside it, to mourners, and yet his word "story" reaches through them to the same audience the Chorus addressed. The mourners on stage have not lived a story; they have lived a catastrophe. Only the audience has watched a story, and the Prince's last couplet is spoken, by a kind of licensed trespass, over the heads of the court to the house.

That trespass is the frame closing. The Prologue promised that the summary would be mended by the performance; the couplet confirms that the performance has become a summary again, a thing that can be carried away and retold, which is exactly what the Prince orders when he sends the company off to talk. The play thus ends by predicting its own afterlife as a tale Verona tells, and the prediction has been fulfilled by every retelling since, including the play's own title page tradition, which has always sold the events as a story of love and death rather than as a case of Verona versus its households. The couplet is the moment the tragedy names its own genre, and the naming is done by the character least entitled to literary judgment and most entitled to legal judgment, a crossing of offices that this reading takes to be deliberate.

The second Chorus deserves a word here, because the frame has three panels, not two. The chorus that opens Act 2 closes its sonnet by promising that the lovers, armed with passion, will find the means to meet, "But passion lends them power, time means, to meet" (2.0.13), a couplet of confident machinery, all levers and provision. Set the three frame couplets in a row and the play's whole theory of itself appears. The first Chorus foretells the deaths and asks for patience. The second foretells the meeting and promises power. The Prince, inheriting the frame without the Chorus's licence, foretells nothing at all and instead performs the one speech act left when every prediction has been spent: he appraises. The frame begins in prophecy, passes through confidence, and ends in appraisal, and the descent from foretelling to appraising is the exact shape of the audience's own journey across the two hours, from knowing the ending, to hoping against the knowledge, to judging the whole. The closing couplet is where the frame hands the audience its final job, and the job is the one the Prince is doing as he speaks: deciding what the story was.

## Juliet and Her Romeo: The Order of the Names

"Than this of Juliet and her Romeo." The play is called Romeo and Juliet, in that order, on its title pages and in the mouths of centuries, and its last line reverses the billing. The brief for this article asks why Juliet is named first, and asks that the possible significances be engaged without overreading. The honest answer has three layers, and the layers must be kept distinct, because critics who merge them claim more than the line can hold.

The first layer is mechanical. The couplet's rhyme word is "woe," and the answering line must end on a sound that leans against it. "Romeo," with its long falling close, supplies that answering sound; "Juliet," ending on a stopped consonant, cannot. If a name must stand last for the rhyme, the other name stands first by arithmetic, and Juliet's priority may be nothing more than the residue of Romeo's necessity. Any reading that skips this layer and proceeds straight to meaning is building on sand, and the mechanical layer also explains the possessive without strain: "her Romeo" is the natural grammar of a line that has spent its first name and needs to attach the second economically.

The second layer is choice inside the mechanism. The rhyme word "woe" was itself chosen. A poet determined to end "Romeo and Juliet" could have rhymed the couplet on another word entirely and set the names in title order; the line as written is one solution among many, and its constraints were accepted, not suffered. Within the accepted solution, moreover, the possessive does quiet work that arithmetic does not require. Romeo is not merely named second; he is named as hers, grammatically possessed by the girl who, in the tomb, performed the tragedy's final deliberate act. Juliet wakes, reasons, refuses rescue, and dies by her own hand on a decision made in full knowledge, while Romeo dies on a false report, mid-error. The last line's grammar awards the story to the one who understood it. "This of Juliet and her Romeo" reads, at this layer, as a finding of fact about whose tragedy the events finally were.

The third layer is the caution. Nothing in the scene marks the order with emphasis, no character remarks on it, and the Prince is distributing praise to no one; he is closing a record. The reversal may register most strongly for an audience that arrives carrying the title in its ears, which is to say the effect is real but retrospective, a resonance rather than a signal. This reading's verdict holds the three layers together: the order is mechanically necessary, meaningfully available, and critically dangerous to overstate. What can be said without overstatement is that Shakespeare let the mechanical solution stand when other solutions existed, and that the standing solution happens to give the last grammatical word about the lovers to Juliet's priority and Romeo's belonging. In a play where Juliet is denied almost every public form of precedence, where her marriage is secret, her funeral mistaken, and her statue commissioned by her husband's father, the epitaph's quiet reordering is the only public honour the text pays her in her own right. The couplet pays it without announcing it, which is how epitaphs work.

One more witness belongs in the record, the book itself. The play reached print under a title that bills the lovers in the other order: the second quarto of 1599, the fullest early text, announces "The Most Excellent and Lamentable Tragedy of Romeo and Juliet," and Arthur Brooke's poem of 1562, the story's great English source, had earlier presented "Romeus and Juliet" in the same sequence. For the audience that first held the play as a printed object, the male name led everywhere, on the title page, in the source, in the Prologue's own grammar, where the lovers are introduced as a pair and the son takes grammatical precedence in household after household. The final line of the play reverses the billing of the book that contains it. Whether the reversal was weighed or merely allowed, it stands as the text's last editorial act upon its own title, and a reader who finishes the play and closes the cover has been given the story twice under two different orders of precedence, the title's order for the buying and the couplet's order for the remembering.

### What does the small word "this" do in "this of Juliet and her Romeo"?

The demonstrative points. "This" story is the one whose evidence lies on the stage floor, the bodies still visible as the words are spoken, and the pointing ties the tale to the tomb so firmly that "story" cannot float free into sentiment. The couplet commemorates at arm's length from the corpses, and "this" is the arm.

## The Rhyme That Almost Rhymes: Closure by Position

Return to the sound of the six lines, because the speech's rhymes tell the same story as its syntax. Early Shakespeare closes scenes and speeches with rhyming couplets that click, and the click is an acoustic full stop, harmony performing finality. The closing speech reaches for that instrument and finds it out of tune. Brings and things slant toward each other across the intervening line without meeting. Head and punished share no music at all. Woe and Romeo lean together, the long o of woe opening into the three syllables of the name, near enough to register as kin and too far to register as chime. Three pairs, three approximations. The speech is dressed in the uniform of closure while its sounds confess that closure is approximate, and the confession is perfectly judged: a peace founded on two fresh graves and a deferred inquiry should not rhyme cleanly, and a dramatist who made it rhyme cleanly would be lying in form what the Prince refuses to lie about in content.

The middle pair repays its own attention, because it is the pair where rhyme fails outright, and what fails there is not accidental in its placing. "Head" and "punished" close the second and fourth lines, the lines of the sun's refusal and the law's division, the two lines in which the speech reports things that do not happen: the sun does not show, the sentences are not pronounced. A rhyme is a small event of arrival, one sound reaching its appointed partner, and the speech withholds that event exactly where its content is non-arrival, while granting approximate arrival to the pairs that concern what does occur, the peace that comes with the morning and the story that stands complete. The pattern is too well placed to be mere Tudor casualness about rhyme, a casualness this dramatist shows often enough elsewhere to make the defence available. Availability is not compulsion, however, and the reading offered here does not need the pattern to be provable, only legible: a listener who feels the middle of the speech hang unresolved while its ends lean together has heard, in miniature, the structure of the whole ending, a peace framed, a grief framed, and at the centre a justice that never quite lands. Form is doing criticism of content inside six lines, which is why the speech rewards the slow treatment a table imposes and why excerpting the couplet alone, the universal habit, amputates the very structure that makes the couplet mean.

The final line's metre extends the confession. "Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" runs to eleven syllables, the extra syllable lodged in the trisyllabic name, so that the play's last line is longer than the measure that has governed its verse. The count depends on hearing the name at its full verse value, Ro-me-o, three syllables, as the play's verse habitually requires it: Romeo's name is one of the metrical constants of the tragedy, scanned long in the mouths of enemies and lovers alike, and the closing line simply applies the constant one final time, with the consequence that the epitaph cannot be made to fit the line length that five acts of pentameter have trained the ear to expect. An actor cannot hurry the name into two syllables without vandalizing the word the whole city has been saying for two hours, and so the overflow is performed afresh at every performance, the name taking its full time while the measure waits. Every other line of the speech fits the pentameter frame; the epitaph line bursts it by one syllable, and the bursting syllable belongs to Romeo. The name will not fit the state's measure, and so the measure stretches. Read against the first movement's tidy weather lines, the overflow enacts the whole relationship between the lovers and Verona's forms: the forms hold everything except the thing itself, which exceeds them by a fraction and breaks their symmetry at the final moment. An editor's evidence supports treating this as design rather than accident. Both early quartos, the compressed text of 1597 and the full text of 1599, end the play with the Prince's sentence and this couplet, while the matter around the ending shifts and contracts between them; the couplet is among the most stable elements of the most unstable scene in the play, the fixed point around which memorial reconstruction and revision alike arranged themselves. Weis's edition, printing the fuller second quarto, keeps the couplet's wording untouched by crux or conjecture. The seal was set early and never reset.

## The Biography of a Word: Peace, from Tybalt's Hatred to the Prince's Dawn

Track the noun "peace" through the play and the closing speech's first line becomes a homecoming. The word's first appearance in a mouth is Benvolio's, in the opening brawl, where peace is a plea: "I do but keep the peace. Put up thy sword, / Or manage it to part these men with me" (1.1.69-70). Tybalt's answer gives the word its defining early gloss: "What, drawn, and talk of peace? I hate the word, / As I hate hell, all Montagues, and thee" (1.1.71-72). Peace enters the play as the thing its most efficient killer hates by name, and the Prince's edict, arriving minutes later, can think of peace only as a possession to be forfeited, a bond posted against behaviour. Through the middle acts the word mostly vanishes, displaced by the vocabulary of love and feud alike, because peace in Verona is not a condition anyone experiences; it is a legal fiction maintained between brawls, the name of the intervals.

The closing speech returns the word to currency with its value changed. "A glooming peace" is peace as an achieved fact for the first time in the play, and the achievement is immediately priced by the adjective. The word has travelled from plea, through fiction, to fact, and the fact arrives damaged, which is the play's whole political wisdom in a single noun's itinerary. Notice, too, who is allowed to complete the word's journey. Benvolio, its first speaker, vanishes from the play after Act 3, his peacemaking unrewarded and unexplained. Tybalt, its hater, is in the tomb the peace is pronounced over. The Prince, who owned the word legally from the start, is the only speaker positioned to announce its arrival, and he announces it the way a survivor announces weather, without triumph, because everyone who made the word mean something opposed is dead or dismissed. The biography of "peace" is the biography of Verona in five acts, and the closing speech is the word's final entry, dated at dawn, signed by the magistrate, annotated by the gloom.

## Sad Things: The Plain Diction of the Closing Speech

Set the closing speech beside any speech of the lovers and the first fact is lexical poverty, a poverty that must be deliberate in a dramatist of Shakespeare's resources. The vocabulary of the six lines is drawn almost entirely from the plainest stock of the language: peace, morning, sun, sorrow, head, talk, things, story, woe. One Latinate pair, pardoned and punished, does the legal work, and one participial coinage, glooming, does the atmospheric work, and otherwise the speech moves in monosyllables like a man counting on his fingers. The lovers, by contrast, could not name a torch without building a cosmology around it. Juliet waiting for nightfall summons Phoebus's lodging, Phaethon's wagon, and the whole machinery of heaven in five lines. Romeo at the window turns a girl into the sun and her eyes into stars that would shame the lamps of heaven. The play's final speech turns the cosmos off. The sun appears one last time, in the closing speech's second line, and appears in order to withdraw, and after that withdrawal the diction never rises above the level of the churchyard floor again.

The word "things" is the register's lowest point and its most considered choice. "More talk of these sad things" (5.3.307) is a phrase of almost bureaucratic flatness, the vocabulary of a clerk adjourning a hearing, and placed where it is, two lines before the most famous couplet in the play, it performs a necessary descent. The speech must pass through flatness to earn its elevation; a speaker who began at the couplet's altitude would sound like a poet, and the Prince's whole claim on the audience's belief is that he is not one. Understatement is the credential of the verdict. When the superlative finally comes, it comes from a voice that has demonstrably declined every cheaper eloquence, the eloquence of the edict, the eloquence of the proverb, the eloquence of accusation in the scourge speech, and the flatness of "these sad things" is the receipt proving the decline.

Even "woe" belongs to the plain stock, an old, short, total word, the noun English reaches for when specification fails. The speech does not say whose woe, or what kind, or compared with what, until the comparison is made absolute by "never" and left unmeasured. A richer word would have analyzed the grief and thereby reduced it. The plain word leaves the grief whole, and the plainness completes the tragedy's largest verbal pattern: the feud's language was always grand and always empty, challenges, honours, and ancient grudges, while the language that finally tells the truth about the feud is six lines of near-monosyllables spoken by its chief magistrate at dawn.

## Grief Against Peace: Which Word the Speech Believes

Every reading of the ending must finally weigh the speech's two currencies against each other, and the fuller weighing of [the case for reading the ending as hopeful or hopeless](/2031/03/28/is-the-ending-hopeful-or-hopeless/) belongs to that question's own ground. The closing speech, taken alone, distributes its emphasis with unusual clarity. Peace is named once, in the first line, under a darkening adjective, as something the morning brings rather than something the city achieves. Woe is named once, in the last line, under a universal superlative, as the property of the story itself. Between those namings, grief is everywhere assumed and nowhere dwelt on: the sun sorrows, the things are sad, the talk will be of them. The speech believes in grief the way a climate is believed in, as the medium of everything, and believes in peace the way a treaty is believed in, as an instrument newly signed and untested. Final emphasis in verse is a matter of position, and position awards the speech to woe, since woe stands in the last line, carries the rhyme, and receives the overflowing name as its answering term.

Behind the speech stand the statues, and the statues are the fathers' rival answer to the same problem the speech solves, how the dead are to be carried into the city's future. The fathers answer in gold: figures raised, likenesses preserved, wealth converted into permanence, "in pure gold" (5.3.299). The Prince answers in words: no likeness, no metal, only a ranking of the story among stories. The play lets both answers stand, and the closing speech's silence about the statues, examined in the critics' section below, can be read as the state's tactful refusal to choose. Yet the sequel has chosen. The statues exist only inside the fiction, commissioned in a churchyard and never shown, while the couplet exists everywhere the play is read, the actual monument by which the lovers are carried into the future the fathers were trying to buy. Gold was the available technology of permanence in Verona; verse was the available technology in the theatre, and the theatre's technology won. The closing speech, knowingly or not, outbids the bargain it declines to mention, offering the lovers a memorial with a distribution no statue could match, two lines, memorized by strangers, in a possessive that gives the girl precedence.

Yet the balance is not a rout, and pretending otherwise flattens the speech. The peace, however glooming, is the speech's first word on the future, and the future is the one territory the feud never previously allowed the Prince to describe. Acts 1 and 3 gave him a present to police and a past to punish; only the closing speech gives him a future tense, "shall be pardoned," "shall be punished," talk that will happen, statues that will rise. The tragedy's last speech is the first speech in the play whose verbs lean forward, and that grammatical fact is the hope the ending actually contains, more solid than the statues' gold: Verona has acquired a future, even if the future's first appointment is an inquiry into the past. Grief has the last word, but government, for the first time, has the next one.

## The Ending in the Theatre: Rewritten Corpses and the Surviving Couplet

The couplet's authority has been tested in the hardest laboratory available, the stage, where adapters with commercial instincts have repeatedly tried to improve the ending and have repeatedly left the Prince's six lines standing. The most famous intervention is David Garrick's adaptation of 1748, which held the London stage for decades and reshaped the tomb scene to grant the lovers a final exchange, Romeo living long enough to recognize Juliet waking and to die in dialogue rather than in error. The alteration testifies to a genuine theatrical appetite: audiences wanted the lovers to meet once more with knowledge, and Garrick, the century's shrewdest man of the theatre, supplied the want. Yet even Garrick's version, which rewrote the play's most sacred scene and displaced Shakespeare's construction of the catastrophe, closed the evening under the government of the Prince's summary, because an ending in which everyone simply dies in the tomb is not an ending at all, only a stop. Some voice must convert the corpses into a conclusion, and the adapters who rewrote everything else discovered that the converting voice was the one part of the machinery they could not better.

The pattern repeated across the adaptation centuries is consistent enough to count as evidence. Endings have been softened, lovers have been granted reprieves in operatic and balletic retellings, Prokofiev's ballet famously began its life with a rescued ending before the tragic close was restored, and through every transaction the same transaction recurs: whatever is done to the deaths, the deaths must still be summed, and the summing falls to the Prince's lines or to a substitute so close in function that the substitution confesses its model. A line that survives its play's rewriting is load-bearing in a sense no library argument can match. The couplet is not the ending's decoration. It is the ending's permit to end, recognized as such even by the professionals most motivated to replace it.

There is a quieter theatrical fact as well. The final scene is long, crowded, and expensive in attention, and productions under pressure cut it, trimming the Watch, the page, the Friar's narrative. The closing speech is almost never among the cuts, although it is the scene's least dramatic material by any actor's measure, six lines of summary after the emotion has crested. Directors keep it because the alternative, a curtain on Juliet's body, strands the audience inside private grief with no civic hand to lead it out. The speech is the hand. Its survival in the cutting room, like its survival in the adaptation, is the stage's own adjudication of the question the critics argue in print, and the stage's verdict agrees with this reading's: the couplet seals, and nothing else in the text can be made to seal in its place.

## Go Hence: The Last Command and the Emptying Stage

The closing speech contains exactly one imperative, and the imperative deserves a section of its own because it is the point where the Prince's voice completes its long retreat from command into record. "Go hence" (5.3.307) orders movement, not conduct. It specifies no destination beyond away, no behaviour beyond departure, no penalty for disobedience, because none is imaginable. Set it against the corresponding imperative of the first edict, "Once more, on pain of death, all men depart" (1.1.101), and the whole education of the voice lies between the two orders. The Act 1 command is total in apparatus, pain of death attached, and temporary in effect: the street empties and refills, the feud merely changes rooms. The Act 5 command is bare of apparatus, two syllables and a direction, and permanent in effect: the stage empties and the play is over. Authority's instruments shrink as its efficacy grows, an inverse law the tragedy demonstrates at every level, in the Friar's increasingly desperate pharmacology, in Capulet's shrinking household, in Romeo's dwindling speeches as his purpose simplifies. The Prince's last command is obeyed because it asks for the one thing everyone present already desires, release from the place, and a government reduced to ordering what its subjects wish has at last synchronized itself with the city it governs.

The dismissal also performs the speech's kindest act of stage management. It releases the fathers from the obligation to perform grief before an audience of officials, sends the Friar to an inquiry rather than a scaffold, and gives the Watch, the page, and the servants the same instruction it gives the great, a democratic imperative under which the whole hierarchy of Verona walks out through one door at one speed. The tragedy's first crowd scene sorted men by rank as it assembled them, servants first and princes last. The last crowd movement unsorts them. Whatever the pardons and punishments later decide, the exit has already modelled the equality the catastrophe imposed, an equality of the dismissed, and the couplet that follows two lines later is spoken into the beginning of that movement, a seal pressed while the wax is already being carried away.

## Among the Tragedies: How Other Endings Close, and How This One Differs

Comparison with the endings of the other tragedies isolates what is singular in the Prince's close. Hamlet ends under new management: Fortinbras, a foreign soldier with no share in the court's corruption, orders the bodies borne "like a soldier" and closes the play with a military programme, rites of war and a volley. Macbeth ends with Malcolm distributing earldoms, recalling exiles, and scheduling a coronation, the state rebooting itself in a speech of pure administration. Antony and Cleopatra ends with Caesar arranging a state funeral and absorbing the lovers into imperial ceremony, "She shall be buried by her Antony, / No grave upon the earth shall clip in it / A pair so famous." In each case the closing speaker is a successor, a man whose authority the catastrophe has created or confirmed, and his speech faces forward into government, programmes, funerals, appointments. The dead are processed so that rule may resume.

Romeo and Juliet breaks the pattern at every joint. The closing speaker is not a successor but a survivor of his own failed regime, a ruler the catastrophe has indicted rather than installed. He announces no programme beyond talk, schedules no coronation, distributes no offices, and orders no funeral; the statues are the fathers' project, not the state's. Above all, where Fortinbras and Malcolm convert the dead into legitimacy for the new order, the Prince converts them into a story, an asset that belongs to no government and appreciates in no treasury. The singularity can be put as a rule: Shakespeare's other tragedies end when the state restarts; this one ends when the state gives up restarting and starts remembering. The difference follows from the catastrophe's shape. In the other plays the state was the victim or the prize of the action. In Verona the state was a bystander with a sword it never used in time, and a bystander's only honest closing genre is the record.

The comparison also steadies the couplet's superlative. Fortinbras calls Hamlet a soldier, Malcolm calls Macbeth a butcher and his queen a fiend, and those are verdicts of classification, placing the dead in categories the state keeps. The Prince's verdict is a ranking among stories, a category the state does not keep, borrowed from the Chorus's trade. Each closing speaker reaches for the strongest instrument his position affords. The Prince's position affords him nothing stronger than a tale told honestly, and the modesty of the instrument, set against the military and administrative instruments elsewhere, is the precise measure of how completely Verona's government has been reduced by the events it failed to govern.

## Levin, Knight, and Weis: What Kind of Ending Is This?

Three accounts of the ending frame the disagreement this article must adjudicate. Harry Levin, in the essay "Form and Formality in Romeo and Juliet," reads the play as a construction of patterned symmetries, in which speeches answer speeches and the frame completes itself: the Prologue's sonnet announces a design, and the closing couplet is the design's final term, the Chorus's confiscated ending returned to a character inside the action. On Levin's account the couplet's meaning is its position; it signifies by rhyming the play shut. G. Wilson Knight, in the Romeo and Juliet essay of The Wheel of Fire, reads in the opposite direction, from pattern to vision: the lovers' story, on Knight's account, asserts a radiance that the city's order can neither house nor extinguish, and the ending's function is transfiguration, the conversion of two corpses into an imperishable tale whose light judges the peace that survives them. Where Levin's couplet closes a form, Knight's couplet opens a legend. René Weis, editing the play for the Arden Third Series, supplies the third term, neither form nor vision but paper: the couplet's text is stable across the early editions, its rhymes approximate in every authoritative witness, its wording unemended by any editor of standing. The documentary record shows a seal that was always slightly imperfect, set in the play's earliest printings and never afterwards improved.

The disagreement is genuine because the accounts optimize for different evidence. Levin attends to architecture, and the architecture is undeniable: a play that begins with a Chorus summarizing the deaths ends with a prince summarizing them again, and the two summaries differ exactly as a promise differs from a discharge. Knight attends to effect, and the effect is undeniable as well: audiences do not leave the theatre discussing Verona's constitutional arrangements; the story is what survives, and the couplet itself predicts that survival. Weis attends to the witnesses, and the witnesses constrain both: a critic who calls the couplet a perfect formal click must explain why its rhymes do not click, and a critic who calls it pure transfiguration must explain why the transfiguring is done by the play's most bureaucratic speaker in the play's most procedural scene.

The adjudication this reading reaches is that Weis's evidence arbitrates in favour of a modified Levin. The couplet is a formal seal, as Levin holds, but a seal whose imperfection is part of its sealing: the slant rhymes and the eleventh syllable are the form registering strain, the design admitting, inside itself, that what it closes exceeded it. Knight's vision is then not wrong but downstream; the legend opens because the seal holds imperfectly, the way light leaves a tomb whose door is nearly shut. What the critics divide between them, the speech performs in sequence, and the sequence is the verdict: form first, vision through form, grief presiding over both.

A word should be said for the complaint none of the three quite voices, the complaint that the ending is cold, that six lines of magisterial summary are an anticlimax after the tomb's intensities, and that the play dies of administration before it dies of grief. The complaint has shadowed the final scene in performance for centuries and motivated the adapters who expanded the lovers' last meeting, on the theory that the emotional account had been left unsettled. Weis's evidence answers the complaint at its root. The coldness is in the earliest texts, not imposed by later taste; the scene was always going to end in record, because the play's subject was never only the lovers' feeling but the city's failure to house it. A warm ending, an ending that lingered in lament, would have acquitted Verona. The closing speech's chill is the temperature of a judgment the city has earned, and the couplet's superlative is the single degree of warmth the judgment allows itself, grief permitted at the exact moment grief becomes official.

Levin's formalism also clarifies a detail the other accounts leave idle, the causal "For" that opens the couplet. On the formal reading, the particle is the hinge of the whole design, the point where the frame's two directions of travel meet: everything in the play has been travelling forward from the Prologue's prophecy, and the couplet is where the action, arriving at the prophecy's destination, turns and justifies the proceedings it has caused. Sentence first, reason last, the legal order inverted into the poetic one. Knight's account, for its part, best explains the couplet's strange afterlife as a freestanding quotation, since a transfiguring sentence naturally detaches from its context and travels. The adjudication above stands, but it stands enriched: form explains the hinge, vision explains the travels, and the documents explain why neither the hinge nor the travels were accidents of transmission.

### How does the closing speech treat the handshake and the golden statues that precede it?

It ignores them. The fathers' bargain, the clasped hands, the promised statues, receives no mention, no ratification, no blessing from the Prince, whose speech begins with the weather as if the reconciliation were weather too, a condition to note rather than an act to confirm. The silence leaves the peace privately owned, two fathers' work the record declines to underwrite.

## An Epilogue in Disguise: The Speech at the Play's Threshold

Shakespeare's theatre had a settled instrument for the moment the closing speech occupies, the epilogue, and measuring the Prince's six lines against that instrument shows how completely the speech absorbs a job usually done from outside the fiction. Epilogues in the period step forward from the wreckage, drop character, and negotiate with the audience directly: Puck begs pardon for the shadows' offence and promises amendment, Prospero begs release by applause, Rosalind charges the women and men of the house by their love of the play. The epilogue's business is always the same trio of acts, apology, appraisal, and dismissal, the company sent home with a judgment placed in its hands. Romeo and Juliet has no epilogue, because it does not need one. The Prince performs all three acts without dropping character, and performs them better than a detached speaker could, because his apology is a confession with corpses attached, his appraisal carries the weight of office, and his dismissal empties not only the stage but the fiction itself.

The correspondence is nearly point for point. For apology, the epilogue's conventional plea for pardon, the speech substitutes the scourge speech's confession and the pardon it distributes in advance of any request. For appraisal, the epilogue's customary praise of the play just performed, the speech substitutes the superlative, a review in nine words from the one critic inside the story with standing to publish one. For dismissal, the epilogue's release of the audience, the speech substitutes "Go hence," an order given to the court that the audience obeys in sympathy, rising from its seats as the court rises from the churchyard. Even the Chorus, who might have returned to speak the epilogue proper, is honourably discharged by the arrangement: the frame's first speaker was an anonymous function, and the frame's last speaker is the city's chief magistrate, as if the play had spent its two hours promoting its own voice of summary from a hired presenter to the highest authority available, and the promotion is the tragedy's formal signature.

The disguise matters because it determines the couplet's tone. An epilogue may wink; the form licenses intimacy, even comedy, at the edge of the gravest matter. The Prince cannot wink, and the couplet's gravity, its refusal of every charm except balance and overflow, comes from its being epilogue material spoken under judicial discipline. The play thus ends with a threshold crossed in both directions at once: the audience is addressed as surely as in any epilogue, and never once acknowledged. The fourth wall holds to the final syllable, and behind it a magistrate files the story that the house will carry out into the street, which is where stories, as the couplet predicted, do their living.

## What the Closing Speech Leaves Standing

The stage empties on the speech's orders, and the play's last movement is an exit in obedience to a dismissal, the court filing out to talk elsewhere. Nothing in the closing speech is resolved except the one thing the Chorus promised at the start: the events have become a story, complete, superlative, and portable. Verona keeps the peace, provisionally and under a darkened sky. The audience keeps the story, permanently and by the Prince's own grammatical act, since it was the audience's patience that the frame requested and the audience's memory that the word "story" presupposes. The couplet's final service is that transfer of custody. "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3.309-310) is spoken by a ruler, minuted by no clerk, and preserved by everyone who heard it, which is to say that Shakespeare hands the record of Verona's failure to the only institution that has ever kept it faithfully, the retelling. The feud produced the deaths, the law produced the adjournment, and the couplet produced the play's immortality, all in six lines, of which the last two are the ones the world memorized. The seal holds, imperfectly, in slant rhyme and an extra syllable, and it holds because of the imperfection: a perfect couplet would have closed the story, and this one, straining at its own measure, sends the story out still sounding.

Consider, finally, what the speech refuses to do, since its refusals are the surest map of its design. It refuses to praise the lovers, offering no word for their beauty, their constancy, or their fame to come, although the statues behind the speech are already gilding all three. It refuses to curse the feud, although cursing was the dying Mercutio's mode and the mode the occasion invites. It refuses to absolve its own speaker beyond the confession already made, and it refuses to prophesy, although prophecy was the frame's first language and would cost the speaker nothing. Four refusals, each a door closed against a cheaper ending. What walks through the one door left open is the couplet, and the couplet's entire content is a comparison: this story, against all stories, for woe. The Prince ends his education by discovering that the last act of government over the dead is not to judge them, pardon them, or even mourn them in form, but to rank their story honestly among the stories, and to let the ranking stand as the record. Verona goes hence to talk. The talking has never stopped.

A last observation belongs to the play's last word. Tragedies are usually remembered by their final images, a volley, a coronation procession, a borne body, but this one ends on a proper name spoken in a possessive phrase, and the name is not the name in the title's first position. The word "Romeo" is the final sound of Romeo and Juliet, and it arrives as Juliet's property, inside her story, under her precedence, the hero grammatically enclosed by the heroine at the moment the state files its record. Every force in the play conspired to keep those two names apart, in separate houses, separate tombs of allegiance, separate futures. The closing couplet files them together, in one line, in one breath, in an order the feud would not have permitted and the title page did not predict. If the speech is a verdict, that filing is its operative clause, the one act the ending performs that no pardon and no statue could perform in its place: it joins the names in the public record, permanently, in the order of the tomb's own truth, the girl first because she finished the story, and the boy hers because, in the end, he was. Six lines, three movements, one couplet, and a name that will not fit its line: the closing speech is the smallest complete account the play gives of itself, an account that balances grief against government and finds, at the final audit, that the story outweighs them both.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch closing-couplet reading, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch closing-couplet reading is a line-by-line table of the Prince's six closing lines, recording for each line its closure work, its rhyme behaviour, and the order of the names in the final line. The pattern it exposes is contraction: the speech faces outward to the sky, then forward to deferred proceedings, then backward to the completed action, surrendering a portion of the Prince's authority at each stage until only the story remains, the single possession his office can still pronounce upon with complete confidence.

### Q: How many lines does the Prince's closing speech have, and why does its shortness matter at the end of so long a scene?

The closing speech has six lines, running from "A glooming peace this morning with it brings" to the couplet that ends the play. Its shortness matters because Act 5 Scene 3 is the play's longest scene, crowded with arrivals, discoveries, and the Friar's narrative of more than forty lines. Against that expansiveness, six lines perform exhausted authority: the state has understood everything and found almost nothing to say, and the proportion between the scene's length and the speech's brevity measures how completely explanation has failed to govern the event.

### Q: Why does the closing speech name peace before it names pardon and punishment?

Peace is named first because the speech orders its business by urgency rather than by legal rank: the city must first be told that the killing season is over, and only then that its accounts remain open. The sequence also protects the peace from the verdicts. Had pardon and punishment come first, the speech would have sounded like a sentence with a preface, and every listener would have waited for names. By fronting the peace and deferring the division, the Prince makes the ending a beginning, an adjournment into talk, rather than a final distribution of blame over fresh graves.

### Q: Why is Juliet named before Romeo in the play's final line?

Juliet is named first because the line's rhyme requires Romeo's name to stand last, where its long falling close can answer "woe," and because the resulting order is one the text had every chance to avoid and chose to keep. The mechanical cause comes first in honesty: "Juliet" ends on a stopped consonant and cannot carry the rhyme. Yet the rhyme word itself was chosen, so the order is a kept solution rather than an accident, and its effect is to hand the story to the lover who acted last and understood most, with Romeo held, fittingly, in her possessive, "her Romeo."

### Q: Does the final rhyme require Romeo's name to stand last in the line?

The rhyme effectively requires it, since "woe" needs an answering sound built on a long open vowel, and of the two names only "Romeo" supplies one. "Juliet" closes on a hard consonant that no slant rhyme could rescue, so a line ending in her name would abandon rhyme altogether at the play's most formal moment. The requirement is nevertheless a chosen one. A different rhyme word would have freed the order of the names entirely, which is why the mechanical explanation, though true, does not exhaust the question the order raises.

### Q: Why does no one answer the Prince's closing speech?

No one answers because the speech is built to end answerability: its only imperative dismisses the company, its sentences are postponed to a proceeding offstage, and its final couplet addresses a story rather than a person, leaving no interlocutor with standing to reply. Dramaturgically, the silence is the point. Every earlier princely speech in the play was answered, evaded, or disobeyed in the hearing of the audience. The closing speech is the first that receives the obedience of quiet, and the stage empties on it, the court filing out to the promised talk without a word of dissent.

### Q: Which two kinsmen has the Prince lost by the final scene, and how does that loss change his authority as judge?

The two kinsmen are Mercutio, killed by Tybalt in Act 3, and Paris, killed by Romeo in the churchyard in Act 5, and the Prince counts them himself: "I, for winking at your discords too, / Have lost a brace of kinsmen" (5.3.294-295). The loss changes his authority by implicating it. A judge who has buried two of his own house cannot perform clean detachment, and his closing speech behaves accordingly, dividing the city into pardoned and punished while conspicuously declining to exempt or to accuse himself beyond that one confession already entered.

### Q: What unfinished business does the closing speech leave to be settled offstage?

The speech leaves three pieces of business open. First, the inquiry itself: the company departs "to have more talk of these sad things," a fuller hearing at which the Friar's account and Romeo's letter will be weighed. Second, the division of the city into those who "shall be pardoned" and those who shall be "punished," announced without names. Third, the material settlement between the houses, the clasped hands and the golden statues, which the speech neither ratifies nor mentions. The play ends with government adjourned, deliberately, into a future the audience never sees.

### Q: What does the word "never" claim in "never was a story of more woe"?

The word "never" claims a universal negative across all stories, a superlative no evidence before the Prince could possibly support, since his knowledge of the case is hours old and secondhand. Its function is jurisdictional rather than factual. Inside his legal office, the Prince must deal in degrees, some pardoned and some punished. The word "never" marks the moment he steps outside that office into the role of memorialist, where absolutes are permitted. The claim is legally worthless and poetically exact, and the couplet's power depends on holding both descriptions at once.

### Q: Why does the Prince call the events a story of "woe" rather than a story of love?

The Prince calls it a story of woe because woe is the only description of the events that his office, and the evidence before him, entitles him to use: he is standing among bodies, conducting an inquiry, in a city whose peace the love itself could not keep. Love in the speech's world is the private fact the Friar's narrative reports; woe is the public fact the state must record. The word also answers the Prologue, which promised "the fearful passage of their death-marked love." The frame opens with love as the subject and closes with woe as the verdict, and the distance between the two nouns is the tragedy's whole argument.

### Q: Why do the line numbers of the closing couplet differ between editions of the play?

The numbers differ because editors lineate Act 5 Scene 3 from early texts that do not perfectly agree, the compressed first quarto of 1597 and the fuller second quarto of 1599, and because decisions about shared lines, prose, and stage directions earlier in the scene shift the count by a line or two before the speech begins. The couplet itself is stable in wording across the tradition; only its coordinates move. References in this article follow René Weis's Arden Third Series lineation, in which the closing speech occupies lines 305 to 310 of the scene.

### Q: How does the line "Some shall be pardoned, and some punished" balance its two halves, and what does the balance promise?

The line balances by strict symmetry: an indefinite "some" on each side, two passive verbs of matched weight, and the alliterating pair pardoned and punished locking the alternatives into one grammatical scale. Nothing tips. The balance promises equity, a settlement in which mercy and penalty will be distributed by measure rather than by feud, and it promises it precisely by withholding every name that would let a listener test the promise. The line is the form of justice preserved at the moment its content has become unpronounceable, an adjournment wearing the dress of a verdict.

### Q: What work does the word "morning" do in the closing speech's first line?

The word "morning" closes the play's clock. The tragedy began on a bright morning of brawling, ran through a single night of marriage, and compressed its catastrophe into a few days, so dawn at the tomb is both a literal hour and a structural rhyme with the opening. Morning is when Verona conducts its public business, markets, edicts, and inquiries, and by assigning the peace to "this morning" the Prince returns the lovers' story to civic time, the time of talk and record, after the long night in which private time governed everything and governed it fatally.

### Q: How does the closing speech answer the Prince's first edict in Act 1 Scene 1?

The closing speech answers the edict by reversing every one of its terms. The edict named the crime in advance and fixed the penalty, death, for a brawl not yet committed: "Your lives shall pay the forfeit of the peace" (1.1.95). The closing speech names no crime, fixes no penalty, and defers all outcomes to future talk. The edict trusted threat; the closing speech trusts record. Between them lies the whole education of Verona's government, purchased with six lives, and the final speech's vagueness, so unlike the young ruler's precision, is the diploma of that education.

### Q: Which of the play's dead does the closing speech never name, and what does the omission measure?

The closing speech names none of the dead at all, and among the unmentioned are Paris, who lies inside the tomb, Tybalt and Mercutio, whose quarrel began the killing, and Lady Montague, dead of grief offstage. The omission measures the speech's jurisdiction. An epitaph for the two lovers is the Prince's to pronounce; a roll of the feud's whole dead would turn his summary into a reckoning, reopening every account the adjournment exists to suspend. The couplet's exclusiveness is also its injustice, and productions that linger on Paris's body during the final lines make the omission visible.

### Q: How should an actor pace the closing speech so the couplet lands as a seal rather than a sigh?

The speech should be paced as procedure slowing into record: the weather lines delivered with a magistrate's steadiness, the dismissal and division at the tempo of business, and then a perceptible gear change before "For never was a story," where breath, not volume, should widen. The couplet fails when an actor begins it already elegiac, sighing from the first line and leaving the superlative nowhere to climb. Played as a verdict that discovers grief inside itself, the final line lands with the weight of a seal pressed into wax, formal, final, and warm from the hand that pressed it.

### Q: How did the closing couplet become the sentence most often quoted as the play's verdict on itself?

The couplet earned that position by being designed for extraction: it is self-contained, it carries its own context in the word "story," and it names both lovers, so an anthology can lift it entire without footnote or staging. School editions and collections of famous lines have accordingly carried it for centuries as the play's epitaph and advertisement in one, the two lines a reader meets before the play and remembers after it. Its grammar assists the fame, since a superlative about all stories invites quotation in a way that a balanced sentence about pardons and punishments, however fine, never could.

### Q: How does the closing couplet repay the bargain the Chorus struck with the audience in the Prologue?

The Chorus struck a bargain of patience: attend for two hours, and performance would mend what summary lacked, "What here shall miss, our toil shall strive to mend" (Prologue 13-14). The closing couplet repays the bargain by confirming that the mending happened. The Prologue's summary named the lovers and foretold their deaths in fourteen lines; the couplet, in two, certifies that the deaths have acquired the meanings the summary could not supply, grief, judgment, and a peace. The audience leaves holding exactly what the Chorus promised, a story whose outline was given away at the start and whose weight only the watching could earn.

### Q: Why does the last line of the play run to eleven syllables instead of ten?

The last line runs long because "Romeo" is a three-syllable name set in a line whose other words are almost all monosyllables, and the count, than, this, of, Juliet, and, her, Romeo, totals eleven syllables against the pentameter's ten. Every other line of the closing speech fits the measure. The overflow is small enough to speak without stumbling and large enough to register, the name exceeding the state's line by a single syllable at the final moment. Editors print the line as it stands in the early texts, uncorrected, and the excess has accordingly been part of the ending since the first printings.

### Q: How can an essay use the closing-couplet reading to argue that the ending is a verdict before it is a lament?

An essay can build the case from the table's order of business: the speech declares a peace, adjourns an inquiry, and divides a population before it permits itself a single superlative of grief, so lament arrives only after every act of government has been performed or formally deferred. The couplet's grammar supports the sequence, since its opening "For" presents the woe as the reason for the proceedings rather than a decoration upon them. Against the objection that the ending is pure elegy, the essay can set the speech's imperatives, futures, and balanced sentences, the working machinery of a court that happens, at the last, to speak in couplets.
