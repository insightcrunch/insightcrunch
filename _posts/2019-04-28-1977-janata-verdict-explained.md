The 1977 Janata election produced the most celebrated single result in the history of Indian democracy, and the celebration has concealed the two features that decided what followed. On the count recorded by the Election Commission of India in its Statistical Report on the General Elections to the Sixth Lok Sabha, the Janata Party won 295 seats against 154 for the Congress, Morarji Desai took office on 24 March 1977 as the first Prime Minister drawn from outside the Congress, and Indira Gandhi lost her own constituency of Rae Bareli to Raj Narain by 55,202 votes. Yet the same count describes two different countries voting on the same days. The Hindi belt expelled the Congress almost completely, the southern states kept the Congress in place, and the coalition assembled on the verdict dissolved within twenty-eight months, because the verdict had rejected the Emergency without endorsing any alternative architecture of government. The verdict was geographically uneven and coalitionally fragile, and both qualities were present in the returns before a single minister had been sworn in.

## The 1977 Janata Verdict in the Celebratory Reading

The celebratory reading has a precise early statement. Lloyd I. Rudolph and Susanne Hoeber Rudolph, writing in Foreign Affairs in 1977 under the title "India's Election: Backing into the Future", treated the result as proof that the Indian voter had internalised democratic norms deeply enough to punish a government that had suspended them. M. R. Masani, a founding figure of the Swatantra Party who had joined the anti-Emergency cause, called the outcome "India's Second Revolution" in Asian Affairs in 1977. Textbook treatments of Indian politics absorbed both formulations and standardised them: the election of March 1977 became the moment at which Indian democracy corrected itself, the electorate acting as the final constitutional safeguard after the courts, the press and Parliament had each been bent or silenced between June 1975 and March 1977.

The reading rests on evidence that no serious account can wave away. Indira Gandhi announced the election on 18 January 1977, released political prisoners in batches, and submitted the twenty-one months of [the Emergency that the 1977 verdict rejected](/2019/04/23/emergency-1975-1977-reanalysed/) to an electorate that had experienced censorship of newspapers, detention without trial under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act, a sterilisation drive conducted under quota pressure, and the demolition of working-class housing in Delhi. The opposition campaign, organised around Jayaprakash Narayan and the Janata alliance announced on 20 January 1977, told voters in terms recorded by the campaign literature of the period that the election would decide whether India kept democracy or accepted dictatorship. The voters answered in the terms offered. The Prime Minister lost her seat. Her son Sanjay Gandhi, who had held no elected office and had exercised authority through the Youth Congress and the sterilisation and demolition programmes, lost Amethi. A government that had amended the Constitution to place the Prime Minister's election beyond judicial review, through the Thirty-Ninth Amendment of August 1975, was removed by the instrument it had tried to insulate itself against.

### What did the Janata alliance promise the voter in 1977?

The Janata manifesto promised restoration before reconstruction: release of political prisoners, repeal of the Maintenance of Internal Security Act, an uncensored press, an independent judiciary, broadcasting freed from ministerial control, and constitutional amendment against another Emergency. Its economics promised decentralisation and village industry in the idiom Desai and Charan Singh shared, without a common programme beneath the pledges.

The celebratory reading is therefore correct about the direction of the verdict, and any rereading that denied the direction would falsify the record. Where the reading fails is in its treatment of the verdict as a uniform national act with a uniform national content. It moves from the size of the swing to the character of the mandate without passing through the geography of the returns, and it treats the government that the verdict installed as the verdict's natural expression, an institution whose rapid disintegration then appears as an unrelated misfortune of personalities. Both moves are conveniences. The returns, read at the level at which the Election Commission published them, state by state and constituency by constituency, show a rejection whose intensity varied by an order of magnitude between regions that had experienced the Emergency differently. The government, read through its own founding documents and internal correspondence, shows a coalition whose components had agreed on the enemy and on little else, whose twenty-eight months in office were spent discovering the disagreements that the campaign had deferred. A verdict can be morally unambiguous and analytically uneven at the same time, and March 1977 was such a verdict. The distinction that organises the evidence is threefold: direction, which was anti-Emergency and close to national; magnitude, which was concentrated where the Emergency had bitten hardest; and durability, which failed because a negative verdict confers no positive programme on its beneficiaries.

That distinction also disciplines the counter-claim. The strongest version of the celebratory reading does not deny the geography; it argues that geography is incidental, that a rejection registered everywhere in direction was a national rejection whatever its local intensity. The argument deserves its strongest form, because the Congress did lose ground in almost every state, and even in states the Congress retained, its vote contracted. The reply given by the evidence assembled in the sections that follow is that intensity is not incidental when it tracks a specific experience with a precision that the returns display. The Congress retained 92 of its 154 seats in four southern states and won not one seat across the 173 constituencies of Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Haryana, Punjab, Himachal Pradesh and Delhi. A national mood does not produce a map of that shape by accident. A geographically calibrated experience does, and the calibration is the finding.

## The 1977 Janata Election in the Election Commission's Count

The primary record is the Statistical Report on the General Elections to the Sixth Lok Sabha, published by the Election Commission of India in 1977, and every figure in the present article that concerns seats and votes is drawn from that report and attributed to it. Polling ran from 16 to 20 March 1977 across 542 single-member constituencies, two further members being nominated to represent the Anglo-Indian community. The registered electorate stood at 321,174,327, and turnout reached 60.49 percent, a rise of more than five percentage points over the 55.27 percent recorded in 1971, a fact that itself testifies against any reading of the verdict as apathetic or accidental. Voters turned out in larger proportions to deliver a smaller Congress. The machinery that registered the turnout ran on [the precondition framework for Indian elections](/2019/01/30/first-general-election-1951-1952-india/) established in 1951 and 1952: single-member constituencies, a symbol ballot designed for an electorate of mixed literacy, and a commission charged to count impartially whatever the count contained.

### Which document holds the official record of the 1977 results?

The Election Commission of India published the Statistical Report on the General Elections to the Sixth Lok Sabha in 1977, printing every constituency result, every candidate's votes, and the state and party summaries from which later accounts derive. Its tables are the primary source for the figures used in serious writing on the election.

The party summary in the Election Commission's Statistical Report gives the Janata Party 295 seats on 78,062,828 votes, 41.32 percent of the valid vote. The Congress, which had won 352 seats and 43.68 percent in 1971, fell to 154 seats on 65,211,589 votes, 34.52 percent, a loss of 198 seats against its 1971 position and the lowest seat total the party had recorded since independence. The Communist Party of India (Marxist) won 22 seats, the All India Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam 18, and the Communist Party of India 7. One figure in the party summary deserves slower attention than it usually receives. The Congress (Organisation), the party of the syndicate whose struggle with Indira Gandhi had produced [the 1969 split as structural pivot](/2019/03/26/1969-congress-split-decisive-moment/) of the whole period, and which had contested 1971 as the senior partner of a grand alliance, won three seats. The organisation that had selected prime ministers in 1964 and 1966 had been reduced, within a decade, to a parliamentary residue, and its leader Morarji Desai held the prime ministership only because the syndicate's remnant had dissolved into a larger vessel. The count thereby records two defeats, not one: the defeat of Indira Gandhi's Congress by the electorate, and the prior defeat of the old Congress machine by the logic of the merger it had been forced to join.

The human symbols of the count were counted at Rae Bareli and Amethi. At Rae Bareli, the Election Commission's constituency return records Raj Narain with 177,719 votes against 122,517 for Indira Gandhi, a margin of 55,202, reversing the same pairing's 1971 result in which Raj Narain had lost by 111,810 votes. A swing of that size in the Prime Minister's own constituency, a seat treated by her party as a family possession, measures the intensity of the rejection in the region where the Emergency's programmes had been administered with the greatest zeal. At Amethi, Sanjay Gandhi lost to Ravindra Pratap Singh of the Janata Party, and the defeat of a man who had never faced an electorate before 1977 closed the political career that the Emergency's critics had made the emblem of unaccountable power. Across the Hindi belt, ministers of the Emergency cabinet fell in sequence, and the counting of 20 and 21 March 1977 acquired the character of a roll call of the regime: Bansi Lal, the Defence Minister associated with the Emergency's enforcement machinery and with the Haryana sterilisation drive, lost at Bhiwani. The celebratory reading is entitled to these symbols. What the symbols cannot carry is the inference that the whole country voted as Rae Bareli voted, and the state tables in the same Statistical Report refute the inference on the facing pages.

The report's constituency ledgers run to hundreds of printed pages, and a reader checking a single result against a state summary can open the Commission's tables, circulated as scanned office documents, in a browser through an [online office file viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) and read the two records side by side.

## The Movement Behind the Merger: JP and the Years 1974 to 1976

The coalition that contested the election had a prehistory, and the prehistory explains both the coalition's moral capital and its organisational poverty. The anti-Congress mobilisation began as a student insurgency in Gujarat in the first weeks of 1974, the Nav Nirman movement against prices, corruption and the state government of Chimanbhai Patel, which dissolved the Gujarat assembly and forced the ministry's resignation before spreading, in March 1974, to Bihar, where students protesting prices, unemployment and corruption in the government of Abdul Ghafoor invited Jayaprakash Narayan out of a retirement that had lasted, in active terms, for two decades. JP accepted the leadership on conditions that defined the movement's character: it would remain non-violent, it would aim not at a change of ministry but at total revolution, sampoorna kranti, the phrase he pronounced at Patna on 5 June 1974, and it would build its organisation outside the party system, in student committees and people's committees that regarded the existing opposition parties as nearly as compromised as the ruling one. The movement's distrust of parties is the seed of everything that followed. The Janata Party was constructed in January 1977 by the very party machines the movement had disdained, under the movement's blessing, and the blessing was personal rather than institutional: it ran from JP to the electorate, not from any movement organisation to any party constitution.

The movement's escalation supplied the Emergency's occasion. Through the second half of 1974 and into 1975, the Bihar movement pressed for the state assembly's dissolution, the Congress government in Patna answered with arrests and lathi charges, and the confrontation nationalised itself as JP carried the campaign to Delhi and the opposition parties gathered, without merging, behind his platform. On 12 June 1975, the Allahabad High Court set aside Indira Gandhi's election, and the movement acquired its constitutional demand: the Prime Minister's resignation. On the evening of 25 June 1975, addressing the rally at Delhi's Ramlila Maidan that closed a day of national protest, JP called on the police and the armed forces to refuse orders they judged illegal and unconstitutional, a summons the government cited within hours as the justification for the Emergency proclaimed that night. The movement's leadership was arrested in the first sweep, JP among them, and the organisation the movement had built outside the parties proved, under detention and illegality, to have no second line. JP's health broke in custody; he was released on parole in November 1976 with his kidneys failing, and the movement as a movement did not resume. What survived into the election of 1977 was the movement's constituency, the students of the Sangharsh Vahini, the Sarvodaya workers, the socialist and Sangh cadres who had shared the jails, and its leader's unmatched standing as the man the regime had feared enough to imprison while he was dying by inches. The Janata campaign of 1977 ran on that inheritance. It also inherited the movement's structural defect, the absence of any organisation that could outlast the emotion of a crusade, and when the crusade ended at the swearing-in, the inheritance was spent. JP refused office, warned the government from his sickbed against the quarrels he read of in the newspapers he was again permitted to receive, and died on 8 October 1979, eleven weeks after the government his movement had made had fallen to the quarrels he had foreseen. The movement had supplied the verdict with its meaning. It had never supplied the government with a spine.

## The Campaign: Nine Weeks Between Announcement and Verdict

The election was announced on 18 January 1977, in a broadcast in which Indira Gandhi presented the decision as a return to the democratic discipline her government had interrupted, and the announcement caught every party, including her own, unprepared for a contest on a nine-week fuse. The opposition had to convert a prison generation into a campaign organisation at a week's notice. Leaders emerged from detention under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act with their parties' structures attenuated by twenty-one months of illegality or caution, their funds thin, their workers scattered. What the opposition possessed in place of organisation was a witness. Jayaprakash Narayan, seventy-four years old, his kidneys failing, on dialysis, released from detention in November 1976 on medical grounds, campaigned across the northern states as the living evidence of the regime's character and the movement's continuity. The meetings he addressed in February and March 1977, at Patna, at Bombay and climactically at the Ramlila Maidan in Delhi, drew crowds that contemporary correspondents numbered in the hundreds of thousands, and the Delhi meeting acquired a significance beyond its size: government servants attended in uniform-defying numbers, the fear that had policed attendance at opposition gatherings for twenty-one months visibly failed to operate, and the regime's own officials could read the failure in the crowd. Kuldip Nayar, the editor detained during the Emergency whose book The Judgement appeared in 1977 as an inside account of the period, recorded the campaign's atmosphere from within the fraternity that had endured the censorship: a press still formally restrained, an electorate educating itself through the BBC's Hindi broadcasts and through the word-of-mouth networks that censorship had inadvertently trained, and an opposition slogan, democracy or dictatorship, that required no printing press to circulate.

The Congress campaign displayed the costs of the informational closure that Dhanagare identified in the Uttar Pradesh result. A government that had censored the press for twenty-one months had also censored the channels through which a ruling party learns its own standing, and the assessments reaching the Prime Minister's house during February 1977, including the Intelligence Bureau's forecasts recorded in Inder Malhotra's account of the period, predicted a Congress victory. Candidate selection in the northern states was conducted through Sanjay Gandhi's Youth Congress network over the heads of the surviving district organisations, seating the emissaries of the Emergency's programmes before the very electorates that had experienced the programmes. Indira Gandhi campaigned with an energy her opponents acknowledged, addressing meetings across the country in defence of order, discipline and the Twenty-Point Programme, and the defence was coherent on its own terms: the trains ran, prices had been held, the strikes had stopped. The terms were the problem. Every claim of the campaign invited the voter to compare the Emergency's order against the Emergency's methods, and in the constituencies where the methods had been applied to bodies and houses, the comparison was the whole election. George Fernandes, held in prison in the Baroda dynamite case, contested Muzaffarpur from custody and won by one of the largest margins recorded in the election, a result that converted a prosecution into a plebiscite. The counting began on 20 March, and by the night of 20 and 21 March the trend lines from the northern counting halls had settled the national question, leaving only the scale to be tabulated.

## The Transfer of Power: 21 to 24 March 1977

The four days between the count and the swearing-in contain the period's most compressed evidence about what the verdict had and had not decided. The Emergency was revoked by proclamation on 21 March 1977, while the outgoing government still held office, an act that acknowledged the result before the result had finished arriving. On 22 March, Indira Gandhi tendered her resignation to the acting President, B. D. Jatti, President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed having died in office on 11 February, and she remained as caretaker Prime Minister while the Janata Parliamentary Party conducted the leadership sounding that Jayaprakash Narayan and Acharya Kripalani arbitrated. The interval was not free of the period's characteristic shadow. Christophe Jaffrelot, in India's First Dictatorship, published in 2020 and built on interviews with the officers and officials concerned, documents a meeting at which Sanjay Gandhi urged the Chief of the Army Staff, General T. N. Raina, to support a refusal of the verdict, arguing that three or four divisions disposed by district could hold the country while the party managed the rest. General Raina declined. The episode, which rests on the recollections Jaffrelot assembled and which the celebratory narrative rarely mentions, deserves its place in the record for what it reveals about the verdict's fragility at the moment of its birth: the transfer of power in March 1977 was peaceful because a soldier refused an invitation, because an acting President followed the forms, and because the defeated Prime Minister, after the invitation had failed, accepted the forms as well. Institutions that depend on refusals are institutions under repair, and the repair was the incoming government's stated purpose.

Morarji Desai was sworn in on 24 March 1977, and the cabinet he presented is the coalition's anatomy rendered as a list of portfolios. Charan Singh took the Home Ministry, the department of internal order, for the Lok Dal. Jagjivan Ram took Defence for the Congress for Democracy. Atal Bihari Vajpayee took External Affairs and Lal Krishna Advani took Information and Broadcasting for the Jana Sangh, the second appointment carrying an irony the press of the day savoured, the ministry that had administered censorship passing to a man the censorship had imprisoned. George Fernandes took Industry for the socialists. H. M. Patel, a former finance secretary of the old Indian Civil Service and a Desai confidant, took Finance. Raj Narain, the victor of Rae Bareli, took Health and Family Welfare, the ministry whose family planning programme he had prosecuted as a crime of the old regime and whose very name the new government altered from Family Planning to Family Welfare in the ministry's first weeks. Shanti Bhushan took Law. Every component of the merger sits visible in the allocation, holding a ministry that corresponded to its constituency's passion: order for the peasant proprietor's party, the microphone for the party of cadres, the armed forces for the defector whose credibility with the services was personal, industry for the socialist who intended to discipline the large industrial houses. The allocation was the merger's balance sheet, and a cabinet constructed as a balance sheet begins its life with every entry watching every other. The Lok Sabha that assembled in March 1977 had given the coalition 295 members of its own and the support of allies beyond them. The arithmetic was a landslide. The anatomy was a truce.

## The Hindi Belt Sweep: Reading the State-by-State Verdict Table

The geographic claim stands or falls on the state summaries of the Statistical Report, and those summaries are printed in full in the table that follows. The table is the State-by-State Verdict Table, compiled from the state tables of the Election Commission's Statistical Report on the General Elections to the Sixth Lok Sabha, and it prints every state that returned ten or more members in 1977, with the all-India total beneath. A reader who cites one document from the present article should cite this table, because the argument about unevenness is an argument about the distribution visible in its two central columns and about nothing else. Where the Janata column approaches the seat column and the Congress column approaches zero, the verdict was a rout. Where the columns reverse, the verdict was a retention, and the map divides between the two patterns with very little territory left in an intermediate condition.

Begin with the bloc in which the expulsion was total. Across Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Haryana, Punjab, Himachal Pradesh and Delhi, 173 constituencies voted, and the Congress won none of them. Uttar Pradesh, the state that had supplied the Congress with its parliamentary backbone for three decades and had returned Indira Gandhi herself, gave the Janata Party all 85 of its seats, the only occasion on which a single party has taken the entire Uttar Pradesh delegation. Bihar gave the Janata Party 52 of 54, the remaining two seats scattering to candidates outside both camps. Madhya Pradesh gave 37 of 40 and Rajasthan 24 of 25, the Congress surviving in each state through a single constituency. The pattern is not a swing in the ordinary electoral sense, in which a party loses ground across a region and concedes a majority of its seats. The pattern is an erasure, and it was registered by a party that had won 352 seats nationally six years earlier and had taken a majority of the Hindi belt seats in every election since 1952.

The scholarly literature on the northern result reached for structural explanation from the start. D. N. Dhanagare, in "Sixth Lok Sabha Election in Uttar Pradesh 1977: The End of the Congress Hegemony", published in Political Science Review in 1979, attributed the Uttar Pradesh wipeout to the conjunction of a united opposition, a Congress organisation worn down by factional exhaustion, and a ruling party that had lost control of the flow of information about its own standing because censorship had deprived the leadership of honest reporting during the Emergency. Dhanagare's account is valuable precisely because it refuses the vocabulary of spontaneous democratic awakening. On his evidence, the northern voter was not performing a constitutional ritual. The northern voter was answering specific injuries: the vasectomy camps conducted under targets set by district officials answerable to Sanjay Gandhi's programme, the arrests of local opposition workers under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act, the demolition drives that had levelled settlements in Delhi and in the towns of western Uttar Pradesh, and the firing on protesters at Muzaffarnagar in October 1976, where residents resisting the sterilisation drive were shot by police. The grievances had addresses, and the addresses were concentrated in the belt that voted the Congress out without remainder.

Two northern results complicate any simple north-against-south formula and deserve separate notice, since the honest version of the unevenness argument must carry them. In Punjab, the Congress also won nothing, but the beneficiary was principally the Shiromani Akali Dal, which took nine of thirteen seats on its own account and carried its separate quarrel with the Emergency, during which Akali workers had run a sustained civil disobedience campaign and filled the jails in large numbers. In West Bengal, the Congress collapsed to three seats of 42, and the principal beneficiary was the Left Front, whose parties took 23 seats; Mira Ganguly and Bangendu Ganguly, analysing the Bengal result in Political Science Review in 1979, traced the Congress collapse there to the record of Siddhartha Shankar Ray's government, to the state-directed violence against Communist cadres during the Emergency years, and to defections that had hollowed the party before polling began. Bengal and Punjab show that the anti-Congress verdict in the east and the northwest ran through regional vehicles with regional histories. The direction was shared. The machinery, the memories and the margins were local, and the returns record the difference.

## The Southern Retention: Ninety-Two Seats Below the Vindhyas

South of the Vindhyas, the Statistical Report records a different election. Andhra Pradesh returned 41 Congress members from 42 seats, conceding a single constituency to the Janata Party. Karnataka returned 26 Congress members from 28. Kerala returned eleven Congress members and gave the remaining nine seats to the parties allied with the Congress in the state's United Front, so that the Congress and its partners took all twenty Kerala seats between them and the Janata Party took none. Tamil Nadu returned fourteen Congress members, and the largest Tamil Nadu bloc, eighteen seats, went to the All India Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam of M. G. Ramachandran, which had fought the election in alliance with the Congress. The four states together supplied 92 of the 154 seats that the Congress retained, a figure printed in the Election Commission's state summaries and repeated in the brief of every subsequent account. A party that had been annihilated across 173 northern constituencies remained, in the south, the natural party of government.

The retention had institutional carriers, and naming them matters more than invoking a southern political temperament. In Andhra Pradesh, the Congress organisation under Chief Minister Jalagam Vengal Rao retained a district-level machinery that had administered the Emergency without the spectacular coercions of the northern drives, and the state's opposition had no equivalent of the Janata merger's northern breadth. In Karnataka, Devaraj Urs had built a coalition of backward classes, minorities and Dalits behind the Congress through land reform and reservation policies administered from 1972 onward, and that coalition held under Emergency conditions because the Urs government kept a measurable distance from the sterilisation quotas and from Sanjay Gandhi's parallel court. In Kerala, the Congress governed inside a coalition that included the Communist Party of India and the Muslim League, distributing the costs and credits of administration across partners and making a single-target verdict against one party structurally unavailable. In Tamil Nadu, the dismissal of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam government on 31 January 1976 had been an Emergency act directed at a regional rival, and the beneficiary of the resulting polarisation was not the Janata Party, which had no Tamil organisation worth the name, but Ramachandran's AIADMK, whose alliance with the Congress converted anti-DMK sentiment into seats inside the Congress column's wider camp. Where a regional vehicle existed that could absorb protest without crossing to Janata, as in Tamil Nadu and West Bengal and Punjab, the verdict's arithmetic changed again. The unevenness was therefore triple: between north and south, between regions with a single opposition channel and regions with several, and between states where the Emergency had been administered as spectacle and states where it had been administered as routine.

The southern results also delivered the verdict's most quietly consequential gift to the defeated party. Because 92 Congress members survived from the south, and because senior southern leaders retained their seats and their state governments, the Congress that entered opposition in March 1977 possessed a parliamentary rump large enough to remain the alternative government in waiting, an organisational skeleton in four functioning state units, and a claim to national character that a purely northern party of 62 members could not have sustained. The celebratory reading, concentrating on the drama of the northern rout, treats the 154 as a humiliation pure and simple. The number deserves a second description. The 154 were a bridgehead, geographically concentrated and institutionally intact, and the speed of the Congress recovery in the southern assembly elections of 1978, examined in a later section, was built on ground that the 1977 verdict had never taken. Geography decided not only how the Emergency was rejected but also how much of the rejected party survived its rejection.

The residual map beyond the two great patterns deserves a sentence of its own, because the exceptions mark the verdict's true perimeter. In Jammu and Kashmir, the Congress retained three of six seats in a state where the National Conference of Sheikh Abdullah, restored to office by the accord of 1975, commanded its own machinery and its own account with the Emergency. In the smaller states of the northeast and the west coast, in Assam's plains the Congress retained ten of fourteen seats, in Manipur both seats, and in Goa one of two, the verdict in each case filtered through local parties and local settlements that the national contest reached only in translation. The perimeter confirms the calibration rather than qualifying it. Where the Emergency had been administered as a distant file, the Congress survived as a party among parties. Where it had been administered as a bodily regime, the Congress ceased, for a season, to exist as a parliamentary presence at all.

## Why the Map Split: The Geography of Emergency Administration

The calibration argument requires a mechanism, and the mechanism is documented in the record of the Shah Commission, the commission of inquiry the Janata government appointed in May 1977 under Justice J. C. Shah, a former Chief Justice of India, to investigate Emergency excesses. The Emergency governed India through two distinct instruments, and the two instruments had different geographies. The first instrument was informational: pre-censorship of newspapers imposed from the night of 25 and 26 June 1975, the cutting of electricity to newspaper presses in Delhi, the merger of news agencies into the state-managed Samachar in February 1976, and the detention of journalists. That instrument fell on the whole country with something close to uniformity, and the resentment it generated among the literate and the politically attentive was likewise national in direction. The second instrument was physical, and the second instrument had an address. It consisted of the sterilisation drive, administered through quotas assigned to state governments and passed down to district officials, schoolteachers and health workers whose salaries and postings were tied to performance, and of the demolition drive, administered through municipal bodies in the name of beautification and slum clearance. The physical instrument fell where state governments competed to display zeal, and the competition was a northern competition.

The Shah Commission's state tables record the sterilisation drive's distribution with a precision that the election map appears to copy. The Commission recorded more than eight million sterilisations performed in the single year 1976 to 1977, roughly triple the number for the year preceding the Emergency, and the state columns show the programme's weight falling on Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Madhya Pradesh, Haryana, Rajasthan, Punjab, Himachal Pradesh and Delhi, the same bloc in which the Congress retained not one parliamentary seat. Haryana, whose Chief Minister Bansi Lal stood closest to Sanjay Gandhi, reported figures that exceeded its assigned targets by margins the Commission documented with bureaucratic horror. The demolition drive obeyed the same map. At Turkman Gate in Delhi in April 1976, the Delhi Development Authority under its vice-chairman Jagmohan demolished the homes of thousands of residents of the old city, and police fired on the residents who resisted, killing six by the official count that the neighbourhood disputed. At Muzaffarnagar in western Uttar Pradesh in October 1976, police fired on a crowd protesting the sterilisation campaign, and the town's dead entered the political memory of the region as evidence of what the programme meant at the point of delivery. A voter in Kerala or Andhra Pradesh had lived under censorship and detention law, and many had reason to resent both. A voter in Muzaffarnagar or at Turkman Gate had been governed through the body and through the house, and the March returns suggest that the Indian electorate priced the two experiences differently.

The direction and magnitude distinction absorbs this evidence without strain. Censorship, detention and the suspension of habeas corpus after the Supreme Court's judgment in ADM Jabalpur in April 1976 supplied the national direction of the verdict, the shared conviction across regions that the Emergency regime had to go. The sterilisation and demolition campaigns supplied the magnitude, the difference between losing a state narrowly and losing every seat in it, and the magnitude map is the Emergency administration's own map. Yogendra Yadav and Suhas Palshikar, writing on the long transformation of the Indian party system, have treated the late 1970s as the threshold of the social churn that later produced the Mandal upheaval, and the 1977 returns support the retrospective application of their framework: the northern countryside that Charan Singh's Bharatiya Lok Dal had organised among the middle peasantry delivered the Janata margin, and the social coalition visible in embryo in 1977, peasant proprietors mobilised against a Congress identified with urban administration and coercive targets, is the coalition whose assertion defined the following decade. The verdict was a national rejection pronounced at regionally different volumes, and the volumes were set by the geography of the thing rejected.

## The Capital as Constituency: Delhi's Seven Seats

One jurisdiction compresses the calibration argument into a single map, and the Election Commission's Delhi returns repay attention before the argument moves to the coalition's interior. Delhi returned seven members in 1977, and all seven went to the Janata Party: Atal Bihari Vajpayee at New Delhi, Sikander Bakht at Chandni Chowk, Kanwar Lal Gupta at Delhi Sadar, Vijay Kumar Malhotra at South Delhi, and Chaudhary Brahm Prakash, a former Chief Minister of the old Delhi state, at Outer Delhi, among the seven. The capital was the seat of the government that lost it. The voters of Delhi included the clerks, teachers, health workers and municipal employees through whose hands the Emergency's two physical programmes had been administered in the city itself, and the returns record their judgment on programmes they had been made to execute. No other result in the Statistical Report carries the same evidentiary charge, because no other electorate had watched the regime at work from inside its own offices.

Delhi had been governed during the Emergency as a demonstration site. The Delhi Development Authority, under its vice-chairman Jagmohan and answerable through the Lieutenant Governor to Sanjay Gandhi's extra-constitutional court, conducted the demolition programme that cleared settlements across the city, at Shahdara across the Yamuna, around the Jama Masjid at Turkman Gate, at Sarai Rohilla and in the colonies along the Ring Road, with the displaced residents transported to resettlement sites on the city's periphery, plots at Trilokpuri and Sultanpuri among them, frequently without the water, transport or title that the programme's literature promised. The sterilisation drive in Delhi operated through the municipal and health bureaucracies with quotas attached to salaries and promotions, and the city's government hospitals and family planning centres recorded a pace of operations in 1976 that the Shah Commission later examined file by file. The capital's Muslims, concentrated in the old city where the demolitions fell, the capital's poor, on whom both programmes fell, and the capital's salaried employees, through whom both programmes were administered, constituted an electorate that had experienced the Emergency as a system rather than as a proclamation. The seven results, uniform across the city's rich and poor constituencies alike, from New Delhi's officialdom to the resettlement colonies' own polling stations, measure what the system was worth to the people who had operated it. A government can survive the hatred of its victims and the contempt of its opponents. The Delhi returns record a third condition, the withdrawal of consent by its own instruments, and no government in the Indian record has survived that withdrawal once an election was permitted to register it.

The ethnographic record assembled a generation later confirms what the Delhi returns imply about the programme's incidence. Emma Tarlo, in Unsettling Memories, her 2003 study of the Emergency in Delhi's resettlement colonies, reconstructed from the recollections of the displaced how the demolition and sterilisation drives had fused in the experience of the city's poor into a single memory of the state arriving with lists: lists of houses to be cleared, lists of men to be taken for operations, the quotas of the second programme sometimes traded against exemptions from the first. Tarlo's informants remembered the Emergency not as a constitutional event but as the season in which the government acquired their addresses. The observation travels across the northern map. The Emergency's national instruments, censorship and detention law, required the citizen only to be silent. Its northern instruments required the citizen to present a body and a house for processing, and the processing generated the addresses, the camps, the certificates and the resentments that the constituency returns of March 1977 then tabulated with such uniformity.

## Five Parties Inside One: The Merger and Its Terms

The vehicle of the verdict had been assembled in nine weeks, and its assembly terms explain its lifespan. On 20 January 1977, two days after Indira Gandhi announced the election, four parties, the Congress (Organisation), the Bharatiya Jana Sangh, the Bharatiya Lok Dal and the Praja Socialist Party, announced that the opposition would fight under a single banner, the Janata alliance, with the Socialist Party adhering to the arrangement, a single agreed candidate against the Congress in each constituency, and a common minimum programme. The decision applied the arithmetic lesson of 1971, when a grand alliance of the same parties had contested separately in most seats, divided the anti-Congress vote constituency by constituency, and delivered Indira Gandhi 352 seats on a minority of the national vote. Jayaprakash Narayan, released from detention on health grounds and recognised by every component as the movement's moral head, blessed the merger and campaigned for it across the north despite failing kidneys, addressing the largest public meetings of the campaign. The Election Commission's arrangements forced one practical decision that stamped the alliance's character on the ballot itself.

### Which symbol did the Janata candidates fight the 1977 election under?

Janata candidates fought under the plough symbol of the Bharatiya Lok Dal, the farmer with a plough that Charan Singh's party had carried into earlier elections. A party created in January had no reserved symbol of its own, so the Election Commission allotted the Lok Dal's symbol to the alliance's agreed candidates across the country.

On 2 February 1977, the alliance acquired its fifth component from inside the ruling party. Jagjivan Ram, the senior-most Congress minister after the Prime Minister, a member of governments since 1946 and the Defence Minister of the 1971 war, resigned from the cabinet and the Congress, and founded the Congress for Democracy with Hemvati Nandan Bahuguna and Nandini Satpathy. The defection mattered beyond its arithmetic. Jagjivan Ram carried the largest Dalit following of any leader in the Congress system, and his departure told wavering Congress voters in Bihar and Uttar Pradesh that loyalty to the party no longer required loyalty to the Emergency. The Congress for Democracy fought the election in seat adjustment with the Janata alliance and merged into the Janata Party after the victory, completing the five-component structure that the founding documents of the Janata Party record: the Bharatiya Lok Dal of Charan Singh, the Jana Sangh of Atal Bihari Vajpayee and Lal Krishna Advani, the Congress (Organisation) of Morarji Desai, the socialist formations gathered around George Fernandes and Madhu Limaye, and the Congress for Democracy of Jagjivan Ram.

The leadership question exposed the arrangement's method within a week of the victory. Three men could claim the prime ministership: Desai, the senior claimant, defeated by Indira Gandhi in the Congress Parliamentary Party votes of 1966 and 1969 and carrying a decade of grievance; Charan Singh, whose Lok Dal had supplied the largest contingent of the alliance's northern members; and Jagjivan Ram, whose defection had broken the Congress's morale in the campaign's final month.

### Who settled the contest for the Janata leadership in March 1977?

Jayaprakash Narayan and Acharya J. B. Kripalani settled the contest by sounding the newly elected Janata members and reporting a majority for Morarji Desai. The Janata Parliamentary Party then elected Desai its leader on 24 March 1977, and President B. D. Jatti swore him in the same day, at eighty-one the oldest man to take the office.

The founding documents of the Janata Party, executed as the five components dissolved themselves into the single party in the weeks after the election, settled the merger's form and left its substance dangerously open. The party acquired a constitution, a national executive and a president, Chandra Shekhar, a Congress rebel who had been expelled by Indira Gandhi in 1975 and whose selection signalled that the new party intended to be more than the sum of its machines. What the documents did not settle can be listed from the disputes that later destroyed the government. No clause resolved whether members of the Jana Sangh component could retain membership of the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, the question the socialists would raise as the dual-membership controversy. No agreed economic programme stood beneath the manifesto's restoration pledges: the Lok Dal's agrarian populism, which treated the city as a parasite on the village, coexisted on paper with the Jana Sangh's urban trading constituency and the socialists' commitment to public ownership, and the documents papered over the conflict by scheduling it for later resolution. No succession rule and no binding arbitration mechanism governed disputes among the five leaderships, each of which retained its own funds, its own following and its own memory of having been a party. Christophe Jaffrelot, in The Hindu Nationalist Movement published in 1996, documented the Jana Sangh's entry into the Janata Party as a calculated acquisition of respectability by a movement that kept its cadre organisation, the Sangh, intact outside the merged party's discipline. The observation generalises. Every component entered the merger as a strategy and kept an exit in reserve, and a coalition whose members have kept their exits has agreed, in effect, to govern until the first question arrives on which the exits are worth using.

## Governing the Coalition: Restoration and Fracture, 1977 to 1978

The government's first year supplied the celebratory reading with its best institutional evidence, and the record should be stated at full strength. The Emergency was revoked by proclamation on 21 March 1977, before the new ministry had been formed. The Lok Sabha debates of March 1977 on the incoming government's agenda record a Parliament recovering its functions in public: members who had been detained questioned ministers who had been their jailers' political masters, and the house turned to the legislative programme of restoration. Censorship lapsed and was not renewed. The Maintenance of Internal Security Act, under which tens of thousands had been detained, was allowed to fall and was repealed in 1978. The Shah Commission began its sittings in 1977 and published its findings on Emergency excesses during 1978, converting allegation into an official record compiled through sworn testimony. The Constitution was then taken in hand.

### What did the Forty-Fourth Amendment change after the Emergency?

The Forty-Fourth Amendment, enacted by the Janata Parliament in 1978, replaced internal disturbance in the emergency clause with armed rebellion, placed Articles 20 and 21 beyond suspension during any emergency, required the cabinet's written advice for a proclamation, deleted the fundamental right to property, and restored judicial review powers the Forty-Second Amendment had curtailed.

Restoration, however, was the one task on which the five components agreed, and a government consumes its agreements. Beneath the constitutional programme, the coalition's second year was a sequence of fractures, each dated and each traceable to the unsettled terms of the merger. Morarji Desai and Charan Singh divided over the prosecution of Indira Gandhi: the Home Minister pressed for speed against the former Prime Minister and her son, and the Prime Minister insisted on procedure, and each man read the other's position as ambition. The government's attempt to arrest Indira Gandhi in October 1977 collapsed within a day when a Delhi magistrate discharged her for want of evidence, and the fiasco transferred public sympathy to the accused. On 30 June 1978, Desai forced Charan Singh and Raj Narain out of the cabinet after both had criticised his leadership in public, and the Lok Dal component spent six months in internal exile, negotiating its return. Charan Singh came back in January 1979 as Deputy Prime Minister and Finance Minister, restored because Desai's majority arithmetic required the Lok Dal's members, and the restoration taught every component the same lesson: the government's survival could be auctioned, and the price of a component's loyalty was a share of office renegotiated at each crisis.

The Congress, meanwhile, was rebuilding on the ground the verdict had left it, and the southern state elections of 1978 measured the recovery. The Congress split again at the start of 1978, and Indira Gandhi's faction, recognised as the Congress (I), contested the Karnataka assembly election of February 1978 and the Andhra Pradesh election of March 1978 under her leadership. The Congress (I) won both states, forming the government in Karnataka under Devaraj Urs and in Andhra Pradesh under Marri Chenna Reddy, results that the Election Commission's state reports record and that no reading of 1977 as a durable national repudiation can accommodate. In November 1978, Indira Gandhi returned to the Lok Sabha itself, winning the Chikmagalur by-election in Karnataka that the sitting Congress member D. B. Chandregowda had vacated for her, eighteen months after the electorate of Rae Bareli had expelled her. The Janata majority answered her return with its gravest unforced error. On 19 December 1978, the Lok Sabha, on a motion of breach of privilege and contempt arising from an obstruction charge of the Emergency period, expelled Indira Gandhi from the house and committed her to prison for the remainder of the session. The punishment, grossly disproportionate to the charge in the judgment of most contemporary reporting, returned the former Prime Minister to the condition in which her political method worked best: the prisoner of a government that appeared vindictive, campaigning from a cell's moral elevation against a coalition visibly consuming itself.

## The Shah Commission: The Verdict Converted Into a Record

The Janata government gave the verdict an archive. In May 1977, acting under the Commissions of Inquiry Act, the government appointed Justice J. C. Shah, a former Chief Justice of India, as a one-man commission to inquire into the facts and circumstances of the Emergency proclamation and into the excesses committed under its authority, and the commission's proceedings through 1977 and 1978 converted the election's grievances into sworn, cross-examined, officially printed findings. The scale of the record the commission assembled is itself a finding. Its reports counted the detentions effected under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act and the Defence and Internal Security of India Rules together at more than one hundred thousand persons, documented the sterilisation programme's quota machinery district by district, examined the demolition of Turkman Gate through the files of the Delhi Development Authority and the testimony of its officers, and traced the decision of 25 June 1975 to its source: a proclamation recommended to the President by the Prime Minister in the night, before the cabinet had met, on grounds the commission found inadequate to the constitutional standard the proclamation invoked. Two interim reports appeared in the spring of 1978 and the final report followed in August 1978, and together they supplied the prosecutions, the parliamentary debates and the special courts legislation of 1979 with an evidentiary spine that no party pamphlet could have carried.

The commission's sittings also staged the period's central confrontation in its purest form. Indira Gandhi, summoned as a witness, appeared before Justice Shah in January 1978 and declined to give evidence on oath, maintaining that her oath of office and the constitutional position of a former Prime Minister bound her to silence on the matters inquired into, and the commission proceeded to its findings on the documentary record without her testimony. Sanjay Gandhi likewise refused the commission his cooperation. The refusals were legally arguable and politically eloquent: the defendants of the Emergency's legality would not defend it under oath, and the commission's printed record, in which the files speak and the principal figures are silent, acquired the character of a judgment delivered in an empty chair. The afterlife of the reports completes the evidence for the fragility argument. The government that returned to office in January 1980 withdrew the Shah Commission's reports from official circulation, and for a generation the findings survived in the copies held by libraries and in quotation, a verdict's archive suppressed by the verdict's reversal. An election had been held, a commission had reported, a Parliament had legislated, and the whole apparatus of accountability rested, in the end, on whether the coalition that had commissioned it could hold itself together long enough for its record to acquire the protection of habit. The coalition could not, and the record went into the cupboard with the government.

## The Dual-Membership Question: The Merger's Unsettled Clause

Of the questions the founding documents had deferred, one carried a fuse. The Jana Sangh had dissolved itself into the Janata Party, but its members had not dissolved the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, the cadre organisation founded in 1925 from which the Jana Sangh had drawn its organisers since 1951, and thousands of Janata members accordingly held what the socialists named dual membership: allegiance to the Janata Party as citizens and allegiance to the Sangh as swayamsevaks. Madhu Limaye, the socialist theoretician who had helped negotiate the merger, raised the question from the party's first year and pressed it with a logic he stated in writing and in the party's councils. A party that claimed to be secular, Limaye argued, could not contain an organised bloc whose primary loyalty ran to a body outside the party's discipline, a body that the party could not question, audit or direct. The demand that followed was precise: Jana Sangh members entering Janata must choose, and the Sangh connection must be surrendered as the price of the merger's honesty.

The Jana Sangh's answer, given by Atal Bihari Vajpayee and Lal Krishna Advani in the party's forums and in their correspondence, deserves statement at the strength its holders gave it. The Sangh, they maintained, was a cultural and character-building organisation, not a political party; it contested no elections and issued no whips; membership of it conflicted with Janata membership no more than a socialist's membership of a trade union federation or a Gandhian's membership of a constructive-work trust conflicted with theirs, and no other component was being asked to dissolve its supporting organisations as a condition of a merger it had entered in good faith. The Jana Sangh had surrendered its name, its symbol and its independent existence, sacrifices no other component had matched in full, and a demand that singled out its members' private associations read, in Vajpayee's rendering, as a device manufactured by rivals to expel the best-organised component from a party whose other factions lacked its discipline. The internal correspondence of 1977 and 1978 on the dual-membership question, preserved in the successor archives of the Bharatiya Janata Party and in the Vajpayee papers, documents the hardening of both positions month by month: the memoranda grow more legalistic, the proposed formulas multiply (a Janata undertaking of loyalty, a Sangh clarification of its non-political character, a grandfather clause for existing members), and each formula dies on the same rock, that acceptance by one side would be recorded by the other as surrender.

The question's destructive power lay in its availability. Dual membership was not, between 1977 and 1979, the issue on which the government's daily survival turned; prices, drought relief, industrial unrest and the prosecution of the Gandhis consumed the cabinet's calendar. The question was, instead, a standing instrument, sharp enough to cut the party along its deepest seam and available to any component leader whose quarrel of the month required a principled costume. When Charan Singh's ambitions and Desai's resistance finally collided in the summer of 1979, the instrument was lying ready, and the socialists, whose grievance against Desai's economic conservatism and administrative rigidity had accumulated for two years, took it up. The party did not fall because its members disagreed about the Sangh. The party fell because its members disagreed about power, and the Sangh question was the form in which a disagreement about power could be made fatal. A merged party with a functioning arbitration mechanism might have contained the quarrel. The founding documents had provided none, and the absence that had been a detail of drafting in the spring of 1977 became, two years later, the mechanism of the government's end.

## The Verdict Repeated in the States: The Assembly Elections of 1977 and 1978

The parliamentary verdict was re-run within three months at the level of the state assemblies, and the repetition is the cleanest corroboration the unevenness argument possesses, because the assembly elections used fresh candidates, state issues and state organisations while reproducing the parliamentary map in its essentials. When the Janata government took office, nine state assemblies governed by the Congress were dissolved on the ground, stated by Home Minister Charan Singh in his letter of April 1977 to the chief ministers concerned, that the assemblies had lost the confidence of the electorate whose parliamentary verdict had been delivered in March. Fresh assembly elections followed in June 1977 across the north, and the Election Commission's state reports record the outcome state by state. In Uttar Pradesh, the Janata Party formed the government under Ram Naresh Yadav. In Bihar, the Janata government took office under Karpoori Thakur, the socialist leader whose reservation policy for backward classes in Bihar government employment, announced in 1978, prefigured the Mandal decade. In Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Haryana and Himachal Pradesh, Janata governments followed under Kailash Joshi, Bhairon Singh Shekhawat, Devi Lal and Shanta Kumar. In Orissa, the Janata Party formed the government under Nilamani Routray. In Punjab, the Akali Dal formed the government under Parkash Singh Badal in partnership with the Janata Party. The Congress lost, within a single season, the governments of the entire northern tier whose parliamentary seats it had already surrendered.

The two June results outside the Janata column repeat the parliamentary pattern of regional vehicles absorbing regional verdicts. In West Bengal, the assembly election of June 1977 brought the Left Front to power under Jyoti Basu, beginning a tenure that lasted thirty-four years, and the Bengal verdict thereby acquired the durability that the Janata verdict lacked: the Left had a programme, a cadre and a single chain of command, and its 1977 victory proved structural rather than situational. In Tamil Nadu, the AIADMK won the assembly election of June 1977 and M. G. Ramachandran became Chief Minister, converting his party's parliamentary performance into a state government that his personal following sustained for a decade. In Kerala, where the assembly election had accompanied the parliamentary poll in March 1977, the Congress-led United Front retained the state, and the Congress survived in the state government even as its national position collapsed. In Assam, where the assembly election fell in March 1978, the Janata Party formed its first government in the state under Golap Borbora, extending the parliamentary geography a year later in the one northeastern state with a substantial plains electorate. The assembly map of 1977 and 1978 is thus the parliamentary map with the contrast sharpened. Where the anti-Emergency verdict ran through a durable organisation, a Left Front in Bengal or a personal machine in Tamil Nadu or a coalition of record in Kerala, the verdict consolidated. Where it ran through the Janata merger, the verdict governed states whose governments soon replicated the centre's component quarrels, and the state Janata units, assembled from the same five ingredients, began their own cycles of factional litigation within two years.

The state results carry one further piece of evidence for the adjudication, and it concerns the Congress's southern bridgehead. Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka, the two southern states whose parliamentary delegations had kept the Congress alive, held their assembly elections in February and March 1978 rather than in the June 1977 round, and by then the Congress (I) had been founded and Indira Gandhi was campaigning in person. The Congress (I) victories in both states, recorded in the Election Commission's state reports, demonstrated that the southern retention of 1977 was not an artefact of a single bad campaign. The southern electorate, offered the same choice a second time after a year of Janata government in Delhi, returned the same answer. Durability, the quality the national verdict most conspicuously lacked, existed in 1977 and 1978 in exactly the places where the verdict had been least dramatic: in the Congress's southern retention, in the Left's Bengal consolidation and in the AIADMK's Tamil Nadu ascendancy. The drama and the durability stood in inverse proportion across the whole map, and any reading of the period that begins from the drama will misprice the durability every time.

## The Mandate Erodes: Restoration Without a Programme

A coalition assembled to restore a constitution must eventually govern an economy, and the Janata government's economic record supplied the second front on which the mandate eroded. The Industrial Policy Statement that George Fernandes laid before Parliament in December 1977 expressed the coalition's Gandhian and swadeshi instincts in concrete form: the list of products reserved for manufacture in the small-scale sector was expanded to more than eight hundred items, foreign capital was treated with a suspicion inherited from the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act regime, and the large industrial house was addressed in the language of restraint. The statement satisfied the socialist and Gandhian components and alarmed the trading and industrial constituencies that the Jana Sangh and the Congress (O) had brought into the coalition, and it settled nothing, because the coalition's agrarian wing measured policy by a different yardstick altogether. Charan Singh, returned to the cabinet in January 1979 as Finance Minister, presented the budget of February 1979 as an act of rural reparation: excise and customs burdens were shifted toward urban consumer goods, agricultural inputs were favoured, and the budget's reasoning, set out in Charan Singh's own statements, held that India's poverty would be answered in the village or not at all. Urban India read the budget as a tax on its consumption levied by a government that had promised relief from the Emergency's scarcities, and the urban reading entered the political vocabulary of the capital's press as evidence that the coalition contained two economic nations.

Events then taxed the coalition from outside. The second international oil shock of 1979 raised the import bill and pushed wholesale price inflation back into double digits during the government's final year, and the monsoon of 1979 failed across large parts of the country, producing a drought that the government's divided councils handled without a single commanding voice. Sugar, edible oil and kerosene shortages, the classic solvents of Indian governmental popularity, reappeared in the bazaars of the northern cities whose voters had delivered the verdict. None of this was the coalition's manufacture, and a fair account must record the inheritance: the Emergency had suppressed prices by fear and frozen wages by decree, and any elected successor would have governed through the release of both pressures. The political failure was of another order. A government with a programme converts external shocks into arguments for its continuation. A government without one experiences each shock as a fresh referendum on its existence, and the Janata government's components, competing for the inheritance they each expected, had no incentive to defend a collective record that none of them owned. By the middle of 1979 the question in Delhi's political conversation had shifted from the government's performance to its successor, and the shift itself shortened the time the government had left.

The prosecution programme, the coalition's last shared enterprise, displays the same erosion in miniature. The Special Courts Bill, passed by Parliament in May 1979 after two years of drafting and dispute, created courts to try the offences of the Emergency period against Indira Gandhi, Sanjay Gandhi and their officials, and the legislation was the government's answer to the charge that restoration had substituted rhetoric for reckoning. Its progress had been dogged by the coalition's internal division between the component that demanded exemplary punishment and the component, Morarji Desai's own, that insisted the former Prime Minister be met with evidence or not at all. The one completed prosecution of the period ran on a stranger track. In February 1979, a Delhi court convicted Sanjay Gandhi and the former Information Minister V. C. Shukla in the affair of the film Kissa Kursi Ka, a satire on the Emergency court whose prints and negatives government officers had seized and destroyed in 1977, and sentenced both men to imprisonment, from which bail pending appeal released them. The conviction of the Emergency's heir for burning a film, secured while the grand prosecutions for the Emergency itself remained unfiled, struck contemporaries as an allegory of the government's predicament: the coalition could punish the symbols of the old regime and could not assemble the unity, the evidence management or the time to judge the regime itself. Within five months of the special courts becoming law, the demand to scrap them had become the stated price of the support that kept Charan Singh in office for twenty-four days, and the prosecution programme ended not in verdicts but in a negotiation.

## July 1979: The Twenty-Eighth Month

The end, when it came, took five weeks and consumed the government, the party and the verdict's meaning in a single sequence. In the first days of July 1979, the socialist component forced the dual-membership question toward a decision inside the Janata Party's councils, demanding that members drawn from the Jana Sangh surrender their Sangh membership as the condition of the party's secular integrity. The Jana Sangh component refused, on the ground its leaders had maintained since 1977. Charan Singh, restored to the cabinet six months earlier as Deputy Prime Minister and Finance Minister, read the deadlock as his instrument. His Lok Dal following, together with the socialists and a stream of individual defectors, began resigning from the Janata Party and from the government in the second week of July, each resignation reducing Morarji Desai's majority in the Lok Sabha toward the no-confidence motion that Yeshwantrao Chavan, leader of the Congress (U), had tabled. On 15 July 1979, Desai resigned the prime ministership without facing the vote, the first Prime Minister of India to leave office by resignation, twenty-seven months and three weeks after taking it. The government that the verdict had installed had been ended, in the precise sense, by the verdict's own beneficiaries, acting through the merger's unsettled clause and in pursuit of the merger's unsettled succession.

President Neelam Sanjiva Reddy then performed the sequence's most consequential act of discretion. Jagjivan Ram, leader of the Janata Party's remaining parliamentary strength, staked his claim to form a government. The President instead invited Charan Singh, who had presented letters of support that included the outside support of the Congress (I), the party of Indira Gandhi, whose prosecution Charan Singh had demanded as Home Minister and whose leader he had pursued for two years. Charan Singh was sworn in as Prime Minister on 28 July 1979, heading a Janata (Secular) ministry sustained by the Congress (U) in the cabinet and by the Congress (I) from outside, on the President's condition that he prove his majority in the Lok Sabha before the end of August. The condition was never tested. The price of Congress (I) support, conveyed to the new Prime Minister, included the withdrawal of the prosecutions against Indira Gandhi and Sanjay Gandhi and the scrapping of the special courts that the Janata Parliament had legislated in May 1979 to try Emergency offences. Charan Singh refused the price. On 20 August 1979, a day before the confidence vote, Indira Gandhi withdrew her party's support, and Charan Singh resigned without having faced Parliament, the only Prime Minister of India of whom that is true. On his advice, the President dissolved the Lok Sabha on 22 August 1979 and ordered a mid-term election. Charan Singh remained as caretaker Prime Minister until 14 January 1980, when the election his resignation had made necessary returned the answer that closed the period: the Congress (I) won 353 seats, and Indira Gandhi took office again, thirty-four months after the electorate had expelled her.

The January 1980 figures, recorded in the Election Commission's Statistical Report on the General Elections to the Seventh Lok Sabha, complete the durability test. The Janata Party, which had won 295 seats in 1977, retained 31. The Lok Dal, Charan Singh's vehicle after the split, won 41. The Congress (I) took 353 seats on 42.69 percent of the vote, a tally one seat larger than the 352 with which Indira Gandhi had entered the Emergency decade in 1971, assembled this time without a war, without a Garibi Hatao insurgency and without any programme beyond the promise of a government that works, the slogan of her 1980 campaign. The electorate of January 1980 did not reverse the verdict of March 1977 on the Emergency; no party campaigned for the Emergency's return, and the Congress (I) itself disowned the period's excesses in terms. The electorate reversed the verdict's beneficiary, having inspected the alternative architecture for twenty-eight months and found a corridor of locked rooms where a government was supposed to be.

No reading of 1977 that stops at the verdict can survive the chronology of 1979, and the chronology is a matter of dated public record rather than interpretation. The coalition did not fall to a revived Congress assault, to a constitutional crisis imposed from outside, or to the electorate. The coalition fell to its own components, using the instruments its founding had left loaded, at the moment of their choosing, and the electorate, summoned to arbitrate the wreckage, restored the leader the verdict had been delivered against. The celebratory reading has no room for this sequence except as epilogue. The fragility reading treats the sequence as the verdict's own content unfolding on schedule: a mandate against something had installed a government of somebodies who agreed on nothing else, and the government lasted exactly as long as the agreement.

## Adjudication: Direction, Magnitude, Durability

The two readings can be stated as rival answers to the reader's question, what the 1977 election actually delivered. The celebratory reading, given canonical form by the Rudolphs in Foreign Affairs in 1977 and carried into the textbooks, answers that the election delivered democracy's self-correction: a people governed without consent for twenty-one months used the first available ballot to end the condition, and the institutions, Parliament, the courts, the press, resumed their places. The rereading defended in the present article answers that the election delivered a geographically uneven rejection of the Emergency, concentrated in the regions the Emergency had governed most coercively, and installed a coalition whose founding terms guaranteed its early collapse, so that the verdict's principal long-run product was the demonstration that India could remove a government without yet being able to replace one. Inder Malhotra, in Indira Gandhi, published in 1989, assembled the evidence on which the second answer largely rests: his account of the Janata years, written from the reporting of a journalist who had covered the coalition's councils, documents the component quarrels, the prosecution fiascos and the July 1979 manoeuvres in a detail that the self-correction narrative cannot digest, and his portrait of Indira Gandhi in opposition, patient, unrepentant and waiting on her enemies' arithmetic, supplies the mechanism by which a negative verdict converted itself into a restoration.

The founding generation's settlement frames the stakes of the choice between the readings. The republic that the Emergency suspended had been built by [the founding triangulation that the post-Emergency moment tested](/2018/12/17/patel-nehru-ambedkar-founding-triangulation/), Patel's administrative state, Nehru's parliamentary mass party and Ambedkar's constitutional text, and the celebratory reading is at bottom a claim that the settlement's deepest layer, the voter's commitment, held firm when every institutional layer had failed. The claim is moving and, in the dimension of direction, true. But the Nehruvian decades had also left the settlement with the weakness that [the Nehruvian-era institutional fragility framework](/2019/01/25/nehruvian-era-settlement-three-tensions/) identifies: the Congress System had substituted a dominant party's internal arbitration for the open competition of parties, so that when the dominant party's own leader suspended the constitution of the system, the system's defence had to be improvised from outside it, by a movement and a merger that had never governed. The improvisation won the election and lost the government, and both outcomes belong to the same analysis. The woman at the centre of the crisis understood the structure better than her opponents did. Her career, read as [Indira's four-phase office-rebuilding trajectory](/2019/03/21/indira-gandhi-personality-institution/), shows a politician who treated institutions as instruments to be rebuilt around a majority, and who recognised by 1978 that the Janata majority was a coalition of her enemies' separate pasts rather than a government with a future. Her refusal to plead, her cultivation of martyrdom through the December 1978 expulsion, her price exacted from Charan Singh and then withdrawn at the hour of maximum damage: each move priced the fragility that the rereading describes, and the market, in January 1980, cleared at her valuation.

The verdict on the verdict is therefore a compound sentence. The celebratory reading is right about what the Indian voter did in March 1977 and wrong about what the doing settled. Direction: national, anti-Emergency, morally legible in every region including the regions that retained the Congress, where the Congress vote still contracted. Magnitude: savage in the belt the Emergency had governed through the body and the bulldozer, mild where it had governed through the censor and the file, and the difference is written in the Election Commission's state tables in columns that no rhetoric about a uniform national awakening can redistribute. Durability: absent, because the mandate's beneficiaries were five parties with one shared memory and no shared state, and because the defeated party's southern bridgehead and the Left's Bengal consolidation gave the verdict's opponents, and its victim, firmer ground than the verdict gave its winners. The 1977 election remains the finest hour of the Indian electorate. The argument of the present article is that a finest hour is a unit of time, and that the government of India is not made of hours. What the verdict delivered was a rejection of permanent value and a government of twenty-eight months, and Indian politics after 1980, the era of coalitions assembled on programmes negotiated before elections rather than grievances shared during them, is the long attempt to supply the thing whose absence the Janata experiment had priced.

One deposit of the period survived the coalition that made it, and the adjudication must enter the credit before closing the account. The Forty-Fourth Amendment's emergency safeguards, the substitution of armed rebellion for internal disturbance as the ground of proclamation, the written advice of the cabinet as a precondition, and the immunity of Articles 20 and 21 from suspension, were not repealed when the Congress (I) returned in 1980 with the majority to repeal them, and they remain the constitutional law of the republic. The verdict also normalised the act at its centre. Before March 1977, no Indian general election had removed a government; the transfer of that month, and the second transfer of January 1980 that reversed it, established alternation as a habit of the system rather than a catastrophe within it, and every subsequent change of government in Delhi has executed the forms that 1977 proved could be executed. A coalition can squander its mandate and still leave the republic better defended than it found it, and the Janata government's constitutional residue is the part of the 1977 settlement that the fragility argument must carry as an asset on the losers' side of its own ledger. The fragility was the government's. The deposit was the verdict's, and the verdict was the electorate's, and the distinction between the two is the whole of the case.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the 1977 election about?

The election of March 1977 was a referendum on the Emergency, the twenty-one months from June 1975 during which Indira Gandhi's government had ruled under a proclamation that suspended elections, censored the press, detained opposition leaders under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act and administered a compulsory sterilisation drive. Indira Gandhi announced the election on 18 January 1977, and the opposition parties merged into the Janata alliance to fight it on a single question, whether India would keep democracy or accept dictatorship, in the campaign's own formulation. The Election Commission of India counted 542 constituencies in its Statistical Report on the General Elections to the Sixth Lok Sabha, and the returns answered the question by removing the Congress from office for the first time since independence. Beneath the single question, the election was also about the particular injuries of the Emergency in the regions that had suffered them, and the geography of the result, a Congress wiped out in the north and retained in the south, records that second subject in the returns. The opposition framed the choice in exactly those terms throughout the nine weeks of the campaign.

### Q: Who was the first non-Congress Prime Minister?

Morarji Desai, sworn in on 24 March 1977 at the age of eighty-one, was the first Prime Minister of India drawn from outside the Congress. Desai had spent his career inside the organisation he ended by defeating: a minister in Bombay state and at the centre, Finance Minister under Nehru and under Indira Gandhi, Deputy Prime Minister from 1967, and the syndicate's candidate in the leadership contests of 1966, which he lost to Indira Gandhi after Lal Bahadur Shastri's death, and of the succession politics that preceded the 1969 split. He left with the Congress (Organisation) in 1969, was detained during the Emergency, and emerged in 1977 as the senior claimant of the Janata alliance. His selection required arbitration: Jayaprakash Narayan and Acharya J. B. Kripalani sounded the newly elected Janata members and reported a majority for Desai over Charan Singh and Jagjivan Ram, and the Janata Parliamentary Party elected him leader on 24 March 1977. He resigned on 15 July 1979, the first Indian Prime Minister to leave office by resignation. He had contested the Congress leadership in 1966 and lost; the office reached him eleven years later through an opposition merger.

### Q: Why did the Janata Party collapse?

The Janata Party collapsed because the merger that created it in the weeks before the 1977 election had settled the coalition's form and none of its substance. Five components, the Bharatiya Lok Dal, the Jana Sangh, the Congress (Organisation), the socialists and the Congress for Democracy, dissolved into a single party to fight the Emergency, carrying incompatible programmes on the economy, incompatible social bases and three leaders who each believed the prime ministership was his by right. The founding documents provided no arbitration mechanism and left unresolved the question of dual membership, whether Jana Sangh members could retain their membership of the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh. Between 1977 and 1979 the coalition consumed its one shared purpose, restoration, and then quarrelled in sequence over the prosecution of Indira Gandhi, over Charan Singh's removal from the cabinet in June 1978 and his return as Deputy Prime Minister in January 1979, and over the dual-membership demand that the socialists forced to a crisis in July 1979. Desai resigned on 15 July 1979 as defections destroyed his majority, twenty-eight months after taking office.

### Q: Was the Emergency rejected nationally or regionally in 1977?

The Emergency was rejected nationally in direction and regionally in magnitude, and the Election Commission's Statistical Report on the General Elections to the Sixth Lok Sabha documents both halves of the answer. In direction, the Congress lost ground in almost every state, its national vote falling from 43.68 percent in 1971 to 34.52 percent in 1977, and its seats from 352 to 154. In magnitude, the rejection was concentrated where the Emergency's coercive programmes, the sterilisation quotas and the demolition drives, had been administered most zealously. Across Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Haryana, Punjab, Himachal Pradesh and Delhi, 173 constituencies, the Congress won not one seat. In the four southern states the Congress retained 92 of its 154 seats, winning 41 of 42 seats in Andhra Pradesh and 26 of 28 in Karnataka. The distinction matters because it explains the sequel: a verdict of uniform national intensity would have dissolved the Congress as an alternative government, whereas the verdict as delivered left the party a southern bridgehead from which it recovered within a year. Both halves of the answer stand in the same document, and later argument turns on which half explains the sequel.

### Q: What was the dual-membership controversy?

The dual-membership controversy was the dispute over whether members of the Janata Party who had entered it from the Bharatiya Jana Sangh could simultaneously retain membership of the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, the cadre organisation from which the Jana Sangh had drawn its organisers since 1951. The socialist component, led in the argument by Madhu Limaye, held that a party claiming to be secular could not contain an organised bloc whose loyalty ran to a body outside the party's discipline, and demanded that Jana Sangh members choose. The Jana Sangh's leaders, Atal Bihari Vajpayee and Lal Krishna Advani, answered that the Sangh was a cultural organisation that contested no elections, that membership of it conflicted with Janata membership no more than a socialist's trade union membership did, and that the demand singled out the component that had surrendered the most in the merger, its name, its symbol and its independent existence. The founding documents of the Janata Party had left the question open, no formula settled it during 1977 and 1978, and in July 1979 the demand became the instrument by which the socialists and Charan Singh's followers broke the party and the government.

### Q: Did Indira lose her own seat in 1977?

Indira Gandhi lost her own seat decisively. At Rae Bareli in Uttar Pradesh, the constituency she had held since 1967, the Election Commission's constituency return in the Statistical Report on the General Elections to the Sixth Lok Sabha records Raj Narain of the Janata Party with 177,719 votes against 122,517 for Indira Gandhi, a margin of 55,202 votes. The result reversed the pairing of 1971, when Raj Narain had contested the same seat against her and lost by 111,810 votes, and it carried a history: Raj Narain's election petition against the 1971 result had produced the Allahabad High Court judgment of 12 June 1975 that set aside her election, the judgment whose aftermath led to the Emergency's proclamation thirteen days later. Her son Sanjay Gandhi also lost, at Amethi, to Ravindra Pratap Singh of the Janata Party, in the first election Sanjay Gandhi had contested. A sitting Prime Minister's defeat in her own constituency remains, in the Indian record, a singular event, and it was registered in the state that the Emergency's programmes had governed most coercively.

### Q: Why did Charan Singh become Prime Minister?

Charan Singh became Prime Minister on 28 July 1979 because the manoeuvres that destroyed Morarji Desai's government left him holding the combination that President Neelam Sanjiva Reddy chose to invite. Desai resigned on 15 July 1979 after Charan Singh's Lok Dal followers and the socialists withdrew from the Janata Party over the dual-membership crisis, destroying the government's majority. Charan Singh, who had been forced out of Desai's cabinet as Home Minister in June 1978 and restored as Deputy Prime Minister and Finance Minister in January 1979, presented letters of support that included the Congress (U) and the outside support of Indira Gandhi's Congress (I), and Reddy appointed him on condition that he prove his majority in the Lok Sabha before the end of August. The premiership was thus a product of the collapse, and it carried the collapse's terms inside it: the Congress (I) support that made it possible was priced at the withdrawal of the prosecutions against the Gandhis and the scrapping of the special courts, and when Charan Singh refused the price, the support was withdrawn on 20 August 1979 and his government ended without facing Parliament.

### Q: How many seats did the Janata Party win in the 1977 election?

The Janata Party won 295 seats in the election of March 1977, on the count published by the Election Commission of India in its Statistical Report on the General Elections to the Sixth Lok Sabha. The party polled 78,062,828 votes, 41.32 percent of the valid vote, across the 542 constituencies in which elections were held, two further members being nominated to represent the Anglo-Indian community. The Congress won 154 seats on 34.52 percent of the vote, a fall from the 352 seats and 43.68 percent it had recorded in 1971. The Janata total was assembled overwhelmingly in the north: the party took all 85 seats in Uttar Pradesh, 52 of 54 in Bihar, 37 of 40 in Madhya Pradesh, 24 of 25 in Rajasthan, all ten in Haryana and all seven in Delhi, while winning a single seat in Andhra Pradesh, two in Karnataka and none in Kerala or Tamil Nadu. The Communist Party of India (Marxist) won 22 seats and the All India Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam 18, and allied and sympathetic parties beyond the Janata Party's own tally raised the anti-Congress strength in the house above the party's 295.

### Q: By what margin did Raj Narain defeat Indira Gandhi at Rae Bareli in 1977?

Raj Narain defeated Indira Gandhi at Rae Bareli by 55,202 votes, on the constituency return published by the Election Commission of India in its Statistical Report on the General Elections to the Sixth Lok Sabha. Raj Narain, the Janata Party candidate, polled 177,719 votes, 53.51 percent of the valid vote in the constituency, against 122,517 votes, 36.89 percent, for Indira Gandhi. The margin acquires its meaning from the constituency's history. Rae Bareli had returned Indira Gandhi in 1967 and again in 1971, when Raj Narain had contested against her as the candidate of the Samyukta Socialist Party and lost by 111,810 votes. His election petition against that result, filed in the Allahabad High Court, produced the judgment of 12 June 1975 that found Indira Gandhi guilty of corrupt electoral practices and set aside her election, the immediate occasion of the Emergency proclaimed on 25 June 1975. The 1977 contest was therefore a re-run of a litigation as much as of an election, and the constituency answered the second time by a margin that reversed the first by more than 167,000 votes across the two counts.

### Q: Which states returned no Congress member at all in 1977?

On the state summaries published by the Election Commission of India in its Statistical Report on the General Elections to the Sixth Lok Sabha, the Congress won no seat in Uttar Pradesh, where the Janata Party took all 85 seats; none in Bihar, where the Janata Party took 52 of 54; none in Haryana, where the Janata Party took all ten; none in Himachal Pradesh, where the Janata Party took all four; none in Delhi, where the Janata Party took all seven; and none in Punjab, where the Shiromani Akali Dal took nine of thirteen seats and the Janata Party three. The same summaries show the Congress surviving by single seats in the two other large Hindi belt states, one seat of forty in Madhya Pradesh and one of twenty-five in Rajasthan. The six shutout states together accounted for 173 constituencies, and their common characteristic was exposure to the Emergency's coercive administration at its most intense: the sterilisation drive's highest state quotas, the demolition campaigns of Delhi and the northern towns, and the detention machinery administered through the state governments that fell with the verdict. The pattern tracks the sterilisation map the Shah Commission later documented.

### Q: What share of the vote did the Congress win in 1977?

The Congress won 34.52 percent of the valid vote in the election of March 1977, on the count published by the Election Commission of India in its Statistical Report on the General Elections to the Sixth Lok Sabha, polling 65,211,589 votes. The figure represents a fall of just over nine percentage points from the 43.68 percent the party had won in 1971, and it converted, under the constituency system, into a loss of 198 seats against the party's 1971 tally of 352, leaving the Congress with 154. The Janata Party won 41.32 percent, a smaller share than the Congress had taken in its 1971 landslide, and the comparison locates the verdict's character: the Janata victory was a victory of distribution and of single candidatures, the anti-Congress vote having been gathered behind one agreed candidate in each constituency instead of dividing among rival opposition parties as in 1971. Turnout rose to 60.49 percent of a registered electorate of 321,174,327, against 55.27 percent in 1971, so the Congress's contraction was registered by an enlarged electorate rather than excused by an absent one.

### Q: How did Jagjivan Ram's Congress for Democracy change the 1977 contest?

Jagjivan Ram's resignation from Indira Gandhi's cabinet and his foundation of the Congress for Democracy on 2 February 1977 changed the contest in three documented ways. Ram was the senior-most figure to leave the ruling party: a minister in governments since 1946, the Defence Minister of the 1971 war, and the Congress leader with the largest following among the Scheduled Castes, who resigned with Hemvati Nandan Bahuguna and Nandini Satpathy after the election had been announced. His defection broke the appearance of inevitability on which the Congress campaign depended, signalling to wavering Congress voters, officials and candidates that loyalty to the party no longer required loyalty to the Emergency. His party carried a constituency the Janata components reached weakly, the Dalit electorate of Bihar and eastern Uttar Pradesh, into the opposition's column through a seat adjustment with the Janata alliance rather than a rival candidacy that would have divided the anti-Congress vote. And his standing made him a prime ministerial claimant in the leadership contest of March 1977, where Jayaprakash Narayan and Acharya Kripalani's sounding reported for Morarji Desai, after which the Congress for Democracy merged into the Janata Party and Ram became Defence Minister.

### Q: How did the forced sterilisation campaign shape the 1977 vote in the Hindi belt?

The sterilisation campaign shaped the Hindi belt vote by giving the Emergency a bodily address in the region's villages and towns, and the Election Commission's state summaries record the result: not one Congress seat survived across Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Haryana, Punjab, Himachal Pradesh and Delhi. The Shah Commission, appointed by the Janata government in May 1977 to inquire into Emergency excesses, documented the machinery: more than eight million sterilisations performed in 1976 to 1977, roughly triple the preceding year's figure, administered through quotas assigned to states and passed down to district officials, teachers and health workers whose salaries and postings depended on performance, with the highest pressures recorded in the northern states. The campaign's incidents entered the region's political memory as named events, the firing on protesters at Muzaffarnagar in October 1976 chief among them, and the opposition's campaign literature directed the grievance at Sanjay Gandhi, under whose programme the quotas had been driven. In the southern states, where state governments administered family planning without the same quota coercion, the Congress survived; the contrast between the two maps is the strongest single evidence that the verdict's magnitude tracked the campaign's geography.

### Q: How did Tamil Nadu vote in 1977?

Tamil Nadu voted in 1977 through a regional vehicle rather than through the Janata Party, and the Election Commission's state summary in the Statistical Report on the General Elections to the Sixth Lok Sabha records the shape of the result: the All India Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam of M. G. Ramachandran won eighteen of the state's 39 seats, the Congress won fourteen, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam won two, and the Janata Party won none. The AIADMK had fought the election in alliance with the Congress, so the anti-Emergency vote in Tamil Nadu had no Janata channel and the protest against the dismissal of the DMK's state government in January 1976 expressed itself through a Dravidian party allied to the ruling party in Delhi. The DMK, which had governed the state until its dismissal and had opposed the Emergency from office, collapsed to two seats, punished by an electorate that associated it with the corruption charges the Emergency's propaganda had amplified. In the assembly election of June 1977 the AIADMK won the state and Ramachandran became Chief Minister, so Tamil Nadu's verdict, delivered inside the Congress's wider camp at the centre, installed the state's most durable regional government of the period.

### Q: How did West Bengal vote in 1977?

West Bengal voted in 1977 against the Congress and for the Left, and the Election Commission's state summary in the Statistical Report on the General Elections to the Sixth Lok Sabha records the distribution: of the state's 42 seats, the parties of the Left Front took 23, the Janata Party took fifteen, and the Congress took three, its lowest Bengal tally of the post-independence period. The result reversed the state's Emergency history. Bengal's Congress government under Siddhartha Shankar Ray had administered the Emergency with a violence directed particularly at Communist cadres, which Mira Ganguly and Bangendu Ganguly, analysing the Bengal result in Political Science Review in 1979, placed at the head of the causes of the Congress collapse, alongside factional indiscipline and defections. The Janata Party's fifteen seats were won substantially in adjustment with the Left, and the durable consequence came in June 1977, when the Left Front won the state assembly election and Jyoti Basu became Chief Minister at the head of a government that held office for thirty-four years. Bengal thereby supplied the durability that the Janata verdict lacked: the state's anti-Congress verdict ran through a cadre party with a programme, and it consolidated instead of dissolving.

### Q: What happened in the Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh assembly elections of 1978?

In the Karnataka assembly election of February 1978 and the Andhra Pradesh assembly election of March 1978, the Congress (I) won both states, on the results recorded in the Election Commission's state reports, forming the government in Karnataka under Devaraj Urs and in Andhra Pradesh under Marri Chenna Reddy. The elections were the first major tests after the Janata government had held office for a year, and their timing gave them their significance. The Congress had split again at the start of 1978, and Indira Gandhi's faction, recognised as the Congress (I), contested both under her leadership while the Janata coalition in Delhi was entering its cycle of component quarrels. Victories in the two southern states that had supplied the largest blocs of her party's surviving parliamentary strength demonstrated that the anti-Indira sentiment of 1977 was not a durable national condition, and that the southern retention had been structural rather than accidental. Devaraj Urs's Karnataka coalition of backward classes, minorities and Dalits, assembled through land reform and reservation policies since 1972, held intact; the Congress (I) recovery in the south preceded Indira Gandhi's own return to the Lok Sabha at the Chikmagalur by-election of November 1978.

### Q: Why was Charan Singh removed as Home Minister in June 1978?

Charan Singh was removed from Morarji Desai's cabinet on 30 June 1978, together with Raj Narain, after a public quarrel over the leadership and direction of the Janata government that Desai treated as indiscipline incompatible with cabinet office. The quarrel had accumulated through the government's first year. As Home Minister, Charan Singh pressed for the rapid prosecution of Indira Gandhi and Sanjay Gandhi for Emergency offences, and read Desai's insistence on procedure and evidence as protection of the former Prime Minister; Desai read Charan Singh's public statements, which by the middle of 1978 included open criticism of the Prime Minister's leadership and hints of a separate political future for the Lok Dal, as the conduct of a rival rather than a colleague. The immediate occasion was the two ministers' refusal to withdraw their criticisms when Desai demanded retraction as the price of remaining in the cabinet. The removal solved nothing structurally: the Lok Dal's members remained necessary to the government's majority, Charan Singh spent six months as the coalition's internal opposition, and in January 1979 Desai restored him as Deputy Prime Minister and Finance Minister, at the price of teaching every component that the government's survival was negotiable.

### Q: What happened when the Janata majority expelled Indira Gandhi from Parliament in December 1978?

On 19 December 1978, the Lok Sabha, voting on a report of its Privileges Committee, expelled Indira Gandhi from the house and committed her to prison until the end of the session, on a charge of breach of privilege and contempt arising from her government's obstruction, during the Emergency, of a parliamentary inquiry into the Maruti affairs of her son Sanjay Gandhi. Indira Gandhi had returned to the Lok Sabha only weeks earlier, through the Chikmagalur by-election of November 1978, and the Janata majority's action converted the returning member into a prisoner of the government she had been expelled from office for misgoverning. She spent a week in Delhi's Tihar Jail before the session ended and released her. The political consequences ran against the majority's intention. The punishment, imposed for an offence contemporaries across the press treated as minor beside the Emergency's catalogue, revived the imagery of 1975 with the parties' positions reversed: the former Prime Minister campaigned from a cell against a government that appeared vindictive and frightened, her party organised protests in her name, and the episode entered the Congress (I) revival as the Janata period's most serviceable symbol of overreach.

### Q: What was the Chikmagalur by-election of November 1978, and why did it matter?

The Chikmagalur by-election of November 1978 was the contest in the Karnataka constituency of Chikmagalur, vacated by its sitting Congress member D. B. Chandregowda to make room for Indira Gandhi, which she won to return to the Lok Sabha twenty months after her defeat at Rae Bareli. The Janata Party contested the seat in earnest, fielding Veerendra Patil, a former Chief Minister of Karnataka, and the campaign became a national referendum in miniature on the former Prime Minister's rehabilitation: Indira Gandhi presented herself as the victim of a vendetta, citing the collapsed arrest attempt of October 1977 and the prosecutions that had produced no convictions, and the constituency's coffee-growing electorate, courted through the Congress (I) organisation that Devaraj Urs's Karnataka government sustained, returned her by a comfortable margin. The result mattered because it re-established Indira Gandhi as a parliamentary presence rather than a defendant in waiting, gave the Congress (I) a voice on the floor of the house its majority had to answer daily, and demonstrated in a counted vote that the verdict of 1977 had a half-life. Within weeks the Janata majority expelled her from the house it had just watched the electorate return her to.

### Q: Why did President Sanjiva Reddy dissolve the Lok Sabha in August 1979?

President Neelam Sanjiva Reddy dissolved the Lok Sabha on 22 August 1979, on the advice of the caretaker Prime Minister Charan Singh, after the sequence of July and August had exhausted every combination capable of commanding the house. Morarji Desai had resigned on 15 July 1979 as defections destroyed his majority. The President had then appointed Charan Singh, on evidence of support that included the outside backing of the Congress (I), with the condition that he prove his majority before the end of August. Indira Gandhi's party withdrew its support on 20 August, the day before the confidence vote, after Charan Singh refused its price, the withdrawal of the prosecutions against the Gandhis and the scrapping of the special courts. Charan Singh resigned without facing Parliament and advised dissolution, and the President, having satisfied himself that no other leader, including Jagjivan Ram of the Janata Party, could command a stable majority in a house elected for a verdict its beneficiaries had spent, accepted the advice and ordered the mid-term election that followed in January 1980. Charan Singh remained as caretaker Prime Minister until 14 January 1980.
