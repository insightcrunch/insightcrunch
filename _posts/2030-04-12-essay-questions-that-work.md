An essay question is the smallest complete theory of Romeo and Juliet that a student ever meets. Before a class has read a scene, the questions on the board have already decided what kind of object the play is: a story to be retold, a warehouse of themes to be inventoried, or an argument to be joined. The verdict of this article is stated at the outset and defended throughout. An essay question on the play works when it makes plot summary unusable and disagreement unavoidable. It does so by three means: it names a tension the play itself sustains and refuses to settle, it fixes the evidence on which the disagreement must be fought, and it installs a scale, a how far or a to what extent, in place of a destination. A question missing any one of the three collapses into a known failure mode. Without the tension it becomes a topic. Without the evidence demand it becomes an opinion poll. Without the scale it becomes a verdict looking for a scribe, and students oblige by writing the verdict's biography, which is the plot again, wearing a conclusion as a hat.

![An essay question on Romeo and Juliet framed as an argument rather than a topic - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-115.webp)

The play's own opening performs the design problem in fourteen lines. The Chorus announces the ending before the action begins: "From forth the fatal loins of these two foes / A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life" (Prologue.5-6, Folger Shakespeare Library edition, edited by Barbara A. Mowat and Paul Werstine, the edition used for quotation throughout this article). A text that spoils itself on purpose cannot be examined by asking what happens in it. Every candidate, every student, every reader already holds the outcome. What remains contestable is everything the announcement leaves open: whether the stars or the households did the killing, whether haste was the lovers' glory or their defect, whether the adults who arranged the marriage market or the children who fled it bear the charge. The examination of a self-spoiling play is therefore a pure case of question design. Where nothing can be asked about the destination, the whole weight of assessment falls on the route, and the route is built by the wording of the question. Bad wording yields the plot retold at walking pace. Good wording yields an argument about mechanism, responsibility, and degree. The difference between the two yields is not talent. It is design, and design can be specified, taught, and audited. That specification is the business of the pages that follow.

One preliminary fact about questions deserves recording, because it explains why the bad ones persist. Questions are inherited more often than they are written. A department's stock of prompts descends from previous heads of department, from textbook end-of-chapter apparatus, from revision guides, and from the setter's own schooling, and each act of inheritance skips the design audit that a fresh composition would invite. The topic label survives because it has always survived; nobody remembers a meeting at which it was adopted. The play suffers a particular version of this inertia because its reputation answers so many questions in advance: a setter who might interrogate a draft question on a less famous text waves the Romeo and Juliet prompt through on the strength of the play's familiarity, as if a text everyone half-knows could not be mis-examined. The opposite is true. The more familiar the play, the more pre-written answers the culture holds in stock, and the more exactly the question must be engineered to refuse them. Design on this play is not a refinement. It is the whole game, played against an opponent, received opinion, that has had four centuries to prepare its responses.

There is a further reason the question deserves this scrutiny, and it is the least comfortable: for most students, the questions are the reading. A school edition is mediated by its apparatus, a course by its worksheets, and a term's study of the play deposits, in the student's memory, not the five acts entire but the handful of scenes the questions sent the class back to, read in the light the questions supplied. A student examined on theme tours will remember, years later, a play about love and fate arranged in matching columns; a student examined on the adults' failures will remember a play about a city that killed its children by timetable and pride. The text does not change. The questions decide which of its possible forms survives in the reader, which is why the design of questions is not an assessment technicality downstream of the real teaching. It is the real teaching, in its most durable form, and the setter who treats it as paperwork has misunderstood the office in the one way that cannot be repaired by better marking.

## The Anatomy of a Working Question

A prompt that demands argument is a machine with three working parts, and each part can be inspected in the wording before any student writes a sentence. The first part is the tension: two forces inside the play, both documented, pulling against each other, such that honest readers land on different sides. Fate against choice is the play's founding tension, announced by the Chorus and itemised against itself by five acts of decisions. Admiration against alarm is the second: the lovers' speed is rendered in the play's most beautiful verse and punished by its plot. Adult guilt against adolescent recklessness is the third: the marriage market of Verona is a machine built by fathers, and the children who break it break themselves against it. A question that names one of these tensions inherits the play's own energy. A question that names a theme, love, hate, fate, conflict, inherits nothing but a filing category.

### What three parts must a prompt carry before it can demand an argument?

A prompt demands argument when it carries three parts in its wording: a named tension that the play sustains on both sides, a fixed evidence base on which the dispute must be conducted, and a scale, usually a how far or a to what extent, that forbids a one-word verdict and requires a measured judgement instead.

The second part is the evidence anchor, and it comes in two gauges. The narrow gauge fixes a passage: one speech, one scene, one exchange, small enough that every claim can be checked against lines the writer must actually handle. The wide gauge fixes the whole play and demands selection, which is itself an argumentative act, since the scenes a writer chooses confess the thesis before the thesis is stated. What a working question never does is leave the evidence base to habit, because habit has a known address: the balcony scene, the duel, the tomb, the five anthology moments that stand for the play in quotation banks. An unanchored question receives an answer assembled from the anthology, and the anthology answer is the summary in its most durable costume. The third part is the scale. The words how far do quiet but decisive work: they concede in advance that the proposition named in the question is partly true, and they ask for the measurement of the remainder. A scale converts a debating motion into an instrument. It also builds the essay's structure into the question, since a measured answer must move through concession, resistance, and judgement, which is the shape of an argument rather than the shape of a chronicle.

The scale carries its own counterfeit, and a setter's audit must catch it: the scale word bolted onto a destination. "To what extent is the Friar to blame for the tragedy?" wears the grammar of measurement while naming a verdict-shaped topic, since blame for the tragedy resolves, in the stock answer, into a tour of the Friar's decisions with a concluding percentage attached as decoration. The test is deletion: remove the scale phrase and ask whether the question's demands survive intact. If the essay the rubric imagines is unchanged, the scale was costume. A genuine scale changes what counts as an answer, because it obliges the writer to specify the unit of measurement, the respect in which blame is being weighed, intent, opportunity, consequence, and to defend the choice of unit against the alternatives. The Friar question repaired by that standard stops asking for a quantity of blame and starts asking for a theory of it: measured against Capulet, whose plan made the Friar's necessary; against the Nurse, whose defection stranded Juliet with no other counsellor; against the lovers themselves, whose secrecy chose the Friar as instrument. Measurement in a working question is always comparative, and the comparison is the argument.

Each part disciplines a different laziness. The tension defeats the inventory, the answer that lists instances of a theme as a shopper lists groceries. The anchor defeats the anthology, the answer that visits only the famous scenes. The scale defeats the obituary, the answer that narrates the lovers' lives from meeting to tomb and calls the narration a conclusion. A setter who can name which laziness a draft question still permits has a diagnostic instrument sharper than any taxonomy of verbs, and the failure anatomy that follows is organised by exactly that diagnostic.

The anatomy is best learned on a single specimen carried through its repairs. Begin with the staffroom standard, in this article's construction: "Discuss the role of fate in Romeo and Juliet." Every part is missing. The tension is gestured at, fate, but its opponent, the chain of human decisions, goes unnamed, so the essay can proceed as a one-sided exhibit list, stars, dreams, presentiments, the Friar's greater power, without ever meeting resistance. The anchor is absent, so the exhibits come from the anthology in performance order. The scale is absent, discuss being the verb that requests a tour, so the answer's conclusion can only restate the exhibits' drift. Repair the question in stages, and watch each part do its work. Name the opponent: "The Chorus blames the stars; the scenes itemise decisions. Discuss." The answer must at least alternate its evidence, though it may still alternate without judging. Fix the anchor at the hinge: "Starting with Romeo's cry that he is fortune's fool in Act 3, examine the play's distribution of blame." The evidence base narrows to the scene where both theories collide over Mercutio's body, and whole-play range must be argued outward from a fixed point. Install the scale: "How far does the play acquit the heavens?" The one-word answers, wholly and not at all, are both marked wrong by the grammar before the candidate begins, and the essay's necessary shape, concession, resistance, measurement, is the question's shape repeated in prose. Three repairs, three parts, and the specimen has crossed from topic to instrument. The transformation is not cosmetic. Each repair forecloses a documented failure and opens a documented excellence, and a setter who has performed the operation once on a specimen owns the diagnostic for every draft thereafter.

The anatomy also predicts the marking. A script's ceiling under a question is set by the parts the question carries: without a tension, the ceiling is the well-organised survey; without an anchor, the well-read anthology; without a scale, the fluent tour with its conclusion stapled on. Markers sense the ceilings without naming them, which is why the same script can be praised under one department's questions and found wanting under another's, and why arguments about standards so often dissolve, on inspection, into arguments about instruments. The setter who wants higher ceilings does not exhort the markers or the students. The ceilings are raised in the wording, before anyone writes, which is the cheapest place in the entire assessment system where quality can be bought.

## The Anatomy of a Question That Fails

The commonest failure is the topic label wearing a question's clothes. "Write about love in Romeo and Juliet" is not a question at all; it is a shelf heading. It names no tension, because love in the play is not one thing but a battlefield of incompatible versions, Mercutio's bawdy reduction of it, the Friar's warning against its violence, Juliet's contractual clarity about it, Romeo's serial devotion first to Rosaline and then to Juliet. It fixes no evidence, so the writer defaults to the anthology scenes in performance order. It installs no scale, so the answer's shape is given by the play's chronology rather than by any judgement, and chronology is summary's skeleton. The responses such a prompt harvests are famous in every staffroom: five paragraphs, one per act, each closing with a theme sentence bolted on like a nameplate. The student has not failed the question. The question has failed to ask anything, and the student has accurately answered the nothing that was asked, at length.

### Why does "Write about love in the play" collapse before a word is written?

The prompt collapses because it is a topic, not a question: it names a theme without a tension, fixes no passage or scene as the evidence base, and sets no scale for judgement. Deprived of all three, a conscientious writer can only proceed through the play in performance order, retelling the plot and attaching theme labels to each act.

The second failure is the plot summary in costume, the prompt that asks for a description and receives one, honestly. A question directing attention to what happens when Romeo returns to Verona, or to the events that lead to the deaths in the tomb, tests recall and sequence, and there are assessment purposes, at the bottom of a course, for which recall is the honest object. The design fault is not the existence of such questions but their disguise: set at the top of a course or in an examination hall, they certify memory as interpretation. The third failure is subtler and more respectable: the opinion poll. A prompt asking whether Romeo was right to kill Tybalt, with no evidence demand attached, invites a verdict untethered to the text's own machinery of honour, law, and haste. Students answer from temperament, and the marker receives temperament, graded. The play's treatment of the killing is a legal and ethical instrument of great precision: Mercutio dies cursing both houses, "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1.99), the Prince's edict has priced street violence at death since the first scene, and Romeo names his own passivity, his effeminate softening by love, as the killing's precondition. A question that ignores all of this machinery and asks for an opinion has discarded the play and kept the incident.

The fourth failure is the plural theme survey, the prompt that asks about the conflicts, the relationships, the ways in which a list of abstractions is presented. Plurality is the tell. A question that permits everything to be relevant guarantees that selection, the first act of argument, never occurs; the answer becomes a warehouse tour. The fifth failure is the most consequential for this play, because it teaches error: the presupposing question. A prompt that sends students to the balcony scene for a discussion of the lovers' first vows presupposes a balcony the text never names, and thereby installs, with an examination's authority, the very misconception the misconceptions scholarship catalogues and teachers labour to correct. The design lesson generalises beyond one architectural invention. Every question presupposes a play. Careless presupposition, a question that assumes the romance lasted months, that assumes the lovers are adults, that assumes the feud had a cause the text withholds, smuggles a misreading into the one document students are trained to trust without reserve. The study guides industrialise several of these failures at once, stocking theme-hunt prompts and model answers whose questions could be answered, and visibly have been answered, without the play open at all; [the study-guide industry and its distortions](/2030/04/19/study-guide-industry-distortions/) is the subject of a companion study in this series, and its bearing here is that the weakest questions in circulation are weak by commercial design, since a question that any summary can answer sells the summary.

The sixth failure is the most modern, and it is bred by the assessment system itself rather than by laziness: the technique hunt. Once mark schemes were understood to reward the naming of methods, a genre of prompt arose, in classrooms and in commercial revision material, that asks how Shakespeare uses language in a scene and accepts, as its answer, a procession of identified devices, a simile here, a caesura there, each admired for a sentence and abandoned. The device-spotting answer is the summary's mirror image: where the plot summary retells the events without the argument, the technique hunt retells the wording without the argument, and both are forms of coverage, the one across the play's actions, the other across its surface. Examiner reports across the boards return to the distinction with wearisome patience, rewarding the answer in which a method is traced to an effect and a judgement, and capping the answer in which methods are merely present. A question designs against the hunt by the fifth principle's demand for mechanism with consequences: not which devices a speech contains, but what the speech's machinery does to the scene that contains it, and what would be lost if the machinery ran differently. The Queen Mab speech is the standing test case, a passage whose devices are so conspicuous that any class can list them within minutes, and whose function, a friend performing brilliance to hold a room while the plot waits at the door, no list has ever touched.

A seventh failure completes the anatomy, and it is the subtlest because its products look like skills: the choreography answer. Where the technique hunt mistakes naming for analysis, the choreography answer mistakes routine for argument, marching each paragraph through the same sequence, a point, a quotation, an explanation, a link, whether the material warrants the sequence or not. The routine is taught with the best intentions, as scaffolding for writers who would otherwise produce nothing, and its defect as design is that a question which the routine fully satisfies has outsourced its thinking to the routine. The marker reads competence in the form and finds the judgement missing, because the routine contains no position at which judgement is required: no paragraph ever has to weigh, concede, or subordinate, the steps merely repeat. A question defeats choreography the way it defeats the hunt, by demanding a relation the routine cannot supply, a comparison whose second term differs from its first, a causal claim whose mechanism must be argued rather than asserted, a scale reading that the evidence must be arranged to earn. The setter's test is mechanical and worth stating plainly: if a paragraph template can be completed for the question without the writer deciding anything, the question has failed, however respectable its verb.

It is worth pausing on why the failures cluster so densely on this particular play, since the clustering is itself diagnostic. Romeo and Juliet is the rare set text that students have usually met before they meet it, through film, through quotation, through the culture's standing summary of itself, and every prior meeting supplies a pre-composed answer looking for a question loose enough to accept it. The topic label accepts the cultural answer entire. The plural survey accepts it in sections. The opinion poll accepts it as temperament. Only the fully assembled question, tension named, anchor fixed, scale installed, refuses the pre-composed answer at the door and requires the candidate to build a fresh one from the text under supervision. That is why the design standard for this play must be set higher than for a text the culture has left alone, and why the failure anatomy is not a counsel of perfection but a description of the minimum engineering the material demands.

Against these seven failures stands a body of documentary evidence about what strong questions look like when institutions build them under pressure: the examination rubric. The rubric is the one genre of question that must survive contact with hundreds of thousands of scripts and still discriminate among them, and its grammar, examined closely, turns out to be the anatomy of the working question written in institutional shorthand.

## The Mark Scheme Is a Design Document

Consider the most instructive single question set on this play in the period the series documents, the AQA GCSE question of June 2024, quoted from the board's published paper and mark scheme. The candidate is printed an extract, Juliet's soliloquy from Act 2 Scene 5, opening "The clock struck nine when I did send the Nurse" (2.5.1), and is set a single fused task: "Starting with this speech, explore how Shakespeare presents the difficulties faced by Juliet in Romeo and Juliet." Two subordinate instruction lines follow, directing the candidate to write about the presentation of those difficulties in the speech and in the play as a whole. The task carries thirty marks, with four further marks for spelling, punctuation and grammar. Every element of the wording earns its place, and the anatomy of the working question is visible in it part by part. "Starting with" fixes the narrow anchor and simultaneously refuses to make it a boundary: the extract is an origin, not a cage. "Explore" licenses a tentative, weighing movement rather than a verdict delivered in the first paragraph. "How Shakespeare presents" keeps the playwright, not the character, as the agent under analysis, so that Juliet is a construction to be examined rather than a person to be pitied, and the essay becomes an account of technique and design rather than a diary of sympathy. The theme noun, difficulties, is pitched at the level of generality that lets a strong candidate build a concept and a weaker candidate build a survey, which is the rubric's concession to the range of the candidature, priced and deliberate. The two instruction lines install the double gauge, speech and whole play, as a structural requirement rather than a hope.

### What does "how far" do inside a rubric that "discuss" cannot do?

The phrase installs measurement. "Discuss" requests a tour of a subject and accepts whatever order the material suggests, which is usually the plot's order. "How far" concedes that the named proposition holds partly, demands a judgement of degree, and thereby imposes the argumentative sequence of concession, resistance and verdict on every answer the rubric receives.

The variant grammar is equally deliberate. In the same June 2024 paper, the question on The Merchant of Venice asks candidates to explore how far Shakespeare presents Shylock as an isolated character, and in June 2025 the board set this play again, on an extract from Act 3 Scene 5, asking candidates to explore how Shakespeare presents relationships between parents and children. The how far form, where it appears, is the scale built into the rubric itself: the candidate is not asked whether the presentation holds but how far it holds, and the mark scheme's summit, the board's top level, rewards the response its descriptors call conceptualised, an answer organised by a governing idea about the text rather than by the text's sequence. The mark scheme of June 2024 instructs examiners that they are encouraged to reward any valid interpretations, a sentence that is the openness of the system stated in its own hand, and the same document architecture caps the opposite failure: the series' study of the GCSE records that OCR's June 2025 scheme caps at its middle levels the answers that never leave the printed extract, so that summary of the given passage, however fluent, has a priced ceiling. The whole apparatus, surveyed in [the play's place in the UK GCSE](/2030/03/08/romeo-and-juliet-uk-gcse/), amounts to a published theory of the working question: anchor narrow, range wide, reward the concept, cap the retelling.

One piece of the apparatus deserves separate notice, because it is where the design becomes social. A mark scheme is not applied by its authors in a vacuum; it is applied by thousands of examiners whose readings of borderline scripts must be converged before the marking of a national cohort can be trusted, and the boards maintain an elaborate standardisation machinery for the purpose, meetings and seed scripts through which the community agrees, in advance, what each level looks like on real paper. The GCSE study records the machinery's most telling feature: answers the scheme's authors did not anticipate are, in the board's own phrase, discussed and legislated for, which is to say the community extends its norms to cover the unforeseen rather than punishing the unforeseen for arriving. The feature matters to question design for a precise reason. A rubric can afford its openness, its invitation to any valid interpretation, only because a legislature sits behind it, empowered to rule on validity case by case; the classroom setter, who is a legislature of one, must either convene the department as a standing committee on validity or accept that an open question's marking will drift with the marker's week. The boards' example thus sets the classroom's homework in institutional form: openness scales only where the community that polices it is real, minuted, and empowered, and a department that wants open questions must first become, in miniature, a board.

The other boards write the same theory in different grammars, and the differences are a design seminar in themselves. Eduqas builds its extract task on audience response: "Look at how Romeo and Benvolio speak and behave here. How do you think an audience might respond to this part of the play? Refer closely to details from the extract to support your answer." The verbs are observational before they are analytical, and the destination is a reception to be reconstructed rather than a presentation to be anatomised. The essay task that follows in the same component restores the older proposition form: "Romeo and Juliet is a play about conflict. Write about some of the conflicts in the play and how Shakespeare presents them." Set beside AQA's fused instrument, the Eduqas pair shows its seams: the proposition is given, not earned, and the plural, some of the conflicts, licenses the warehouse tour that the fused question's architecture forbids. Edexcel divides the labour openly, quarantining an extract part devoted to language from an essay part that readmits context and whole-text range; in the summer 2025 series its extract presented Mercutio, the play's hardest test of whether language analysis can stay awake. OCR offers the candidate a structural choice, an extract-based task or a discursive one, and thereby moves the design decision partly into the candidate's hands. Four boards, four settlements between the same pressures: anchor and range, discipline and freedom, comparability and life.

Underneath the four forms sit the assessment objectives, and they are the system's deepest design document, because they state what a question on this play is for before any setter words one. The objectives fixed for the qualification, carried in each board's specification, weight the reading-and-response objectives at forty per cent each, reserve fifteen per cent for the comparison of texts, and five per cent for the technical accuracy of the candidate's own English. The proportions are a theory of the subject in numbers: interpretation and language analysis dominate, context is assessed only as it is woven into the response, and even spelling is given a small, explicit price so that no candidate can pretend it goes unpriced elsewhere. A rubric that asks a question the objectives cannot reward is a broken instrument, however elegant, and the objectives explain several features of the printed forms that puzzle outsiders: the insistence on presentation language, because the second objective names writers' methods; the absence of freestanding context questions, because no objective pays for context alone; the modest four-mark tariff for accuracy attached to the AQA task, because five per cent has to be collected somewhere. The classroom setter works under no statutory grid, but the discipline transfers: a question whose rewards have not been decided in advance is a question whose marking will be improvised, and improvisation at the point of marking is where unfairness enters a system that believed itself kind.

The American instrument makes the opposite settlement and is instructive for exactly that reason. The College Board's Advanced Placement Literature examination, in its 2024 free-response set, asks a third question that prints no passage at all. The prompt observes that many works of literature feature a character reluctant, unable, or resistant to decide, directs the candidate to choose a work of fiction in which a character delays or avoids making a decision, and requires an essay analysing how the impact of that indecision contributes to an interpretation of the work as a whole, closing with the standing warning: "Do not merely summarize the plot." The rubric's first point is awarded for a thesis that presents a defensible interpretation. Here the tension is supplied in the abstract, decision against delay, and the evidence base is the candidate's entire reading, chosen and carried in memory. The design wager is the mirror image of the GCSE's: where the board in England narrows the anchor to discipline the argument, the American instrument widens the anchor to the whole of a self-selected library and disciplines the argument by rubric instead, defensible interpretation or no point. Both systems agree on the enemy, named in the American rubric's closing warning with unusual bluntness. The enemy is summary. The instruments differ only in where they station the guard, at the question's wording or at the mark scheme's gate.

The documentary record of the boards' own series repays one more pass, because the sequence of questions set on this play reads as a design tradition arguing with itself in public. In June 2018 AQA took its extract from Act 3 Scene 5, the lark and nightingale parting with the Paris arrangement closing over it, the department's repertoire piece par excellence. In June 2023 the question asked how far Shakespeare presents Juliet's feelings towards Romeo, a feelings rubric that sits at the edge of the presentation grammar, since feelings invites the sympathy diary the agent wording elsewhere forbids, and the series reads, in sequence, as the board testing its own formula's boundaries. June 2024 broke the repertoire with the unrehearsed soliloquy and provoked the profession's fury, and June 2025 returned to Act 3 Scene 5 for relationships between parents and children. A question tradition, like a text tradition, is best read whole: stability of grammar, rotation of content, one experiment per cycle, and a retreat to the repertoire when the experiment draws blood. The same record disciplines a lazy inference a setter might draw from this article, that the fused AQA form is simply the best question and should be copied everywhere. Its own board does not copy it everywhere. The form is a settlement under specific conditions: a closed book, a paper of one hour and forty-five minutes shared between the Shakespeare task and a nineteenth-century novel, and a candidature of hundreds of thousands whose scripts must be marked alike. The question's tight architecture is the clock's child as much as the theory's. Transfer the rubric to a coursework essay with a week to write and its closures become cages; transfer an open coursework brief into the hall and its freedoms become lotteries. Question design is fitted design, and the document's first lesson is that fitness is local.

The record teaches a second lesson, about the half of the question that is not wording at all: the choice of passage. An extract-based rubric is two instruments fused, the printed passage and the verbal task, and the passage does design work the words cannot. A repertoire extract, the balcony scene, the parting at dawn, arrives in the hall accompanied by every commentary ever written on it, and the question set on it measures, in part, the candidate's access to that commentary; an unrehearsed extract strips the accompaniment away and measures the reading itself, at the cost of the anxiety the profession recorded in June 2024. Neither choice is a mistake; they are different measurements wearing the same uniform, and the experienced setter reads a board's series as an alternation between them, repertoire passages to certify the taught course, unrehearsed passages to audit it. The classroom setter commands the same dial and should turn it deliberately: early in a unit, passages the teaching has saturated, so that failure diagnoses the question rather than the coverage; late in the unit, a passage taught lightly or not at all, so that success certifies transfer rather than recall. What the setter must not do is turn the dial unknowingly, setting a repertoire question while imagining a test of reading, or an unrehearsed question while imagining a test of the course. Half of question design is choosing what the candidate will be looking at when the question is read, and that half is decided in silence, in the setter's study, long before the rubric's first verb is chosen.

## The Design Literature: Essential Questions and Problems

The examination boards did not invent their grammar in isolation. Behind the rubric stands a half-century of design literature on questions, and its findings converge on the anatomy already described, with one correction of emphasis that the literature insists on and the boards quietly embody. Grant Wiggins and Jay McTighe, in Understanding by Design (1998; expanded second edition 2005), gave the tradition its canonical term, the essential question: a question that, in their formulation, is not answerable with finality in a brief sentence, whose aim is to stimulate thought, provoke inquiry, and spark further questions. Their criteria for such questions read as a checklist against the failures catalogued above. An essential question has no single correct answer; it requires students to consider alternatives, weigh evidence, support a position, and justify it; it recurs, so that the same question can be met again at greater depth; and it points beyond the topic to the discipline's core perplexities. Fate or freedom, the standing question of the play's criticism, is an essential question wearing theatrical dress, and the boards' how far is the essential question compressed into an examination's forty minutes.

### Why is a theme noun too weak to carry a question by itself?

A theme noun names a filing category, not a dispute. Love, hate, fate and conflict sit in the play as settled containers, and a question built on the noun alone invites the writer to empty the container onto the page. Argument begins only when the noun is put under pressure by a second force or a scale.

John C. Bean's Engaging Ideas (1996; second edition 2011) supplies the complementary principle from writing studies: an assignment works when it is framed as a problem to be solved rather than as information to be displayed. Bean's distinction maps exactly onto the difference between the topic label and the working question. "Write about love" requests display; the writer's task is coverage, and coverage is organised by the material's own order, which for a play is the plot. A problem, by contrast, has a shape that display lacks: a gap between what is given and what must be decided, and the decider is the writer. The Friar states the play's problem-form in a single sentence at the wedding he is about to perform: "These violent delights have violent ends" (2.6.9). A prompt built on that sentence, asking how far the marriage scene is a warning its speaker proceeds to ignore, hands the writer a proposition the play itself utters and then tests for three acts, which is Bean's problem in its purest available form: the evidence is fixed, the tension is the speaker's self-refutation, and the scale is the distance between the sermon and the practice.

Bean's distinction also supplies the setter with a drafting procedure, since a topic can be converted into a problem by a small number of repeatable operations, and the operations are worth naming because they transfer to every text a department teaches. The first operation finds the play's internal disagreement about the topic, the point where the text argues with itself: on haste, the Friar preaches moderation and practises acceleration, Capulet counsels delay and then advances the wedding, Juliet warns against swearing by the moon and then builds a marriage in a night. The second operation attaches the disagreement to a passage where both sides are present in the same lines, so the evidence cannot be gathered from one camp alone. The third operation phrases the question so that settling the disagreement is the price of completion, by scale, by ranking, or by the counterfactual test of what would have to be different for the other side to win. A topic that survives the three operations has become a problem; a topic that cannot survive them, because the text holds no internal disagreement about it, was never an essay subject at all, only a comprehension exercise in formal dress, and the honest response is to retire it to the worksheet where it belongs. The procedure's value is diagnostic as much as generative: it explains, for any inherited question, which operation its author skipped, and the skipped operation is the failure the scripts will exhibit.

The cognitive research beneath the design literature explains why the collapse into summary is the default rather than a moral failing. Carl Bereiter and Marlene Scardamalia, in The Psychology of Written Composition (1987), distinguished two composing models: knowledge-telling, in which the writer retrieves content under a topic cue and writes it down in retrieval order, and knowledge-transforming, in which the act of writing is a dialectic between the content and the rhetorical problem, each reshaping the other. The topic label is a knowledge-telling machine: it cues retrieval, and retrieval order for a narrative is narrative order. The working question, tension, anchor, and scale assembled, is a knowledge-transforming machine, because its demands cannot be met by retrieval at all; no memory store holds a pre-computed answer to how far a presentation holds across a whole play, so the answer must be built. George Hillocks Jr.'s 1986 meta-analysis of composition research, Research on Written Composition, found the inquiry mode of instruction, structured work on problems and data, outperforming the presentational mode by a wide margin in measured effect on writing quality, and though Hillocks measured teaching rather than prompt wording, the kinship is direct: inquiry is what a well-built question compels and a topic forbids. The verb taxonomies of Benjamin Bloom's Taxonomy of Educational Objectives (1956), revised by Lorin Anderson and David Krathwohl in 2001, give the same gradient its staffroom shorthand, analyse and evaluate above understand and remember, and setters lean on the ladder when choosing between describe and explore. The ladder is a blunt instrument, and its bluntness deserves a paragraph of its own, because the commonest corruption of question design in schools is verb cosmetics: the describe task relabelled analyse, the retrieval exercise promoted by adjective into critical thinking. A verb governs nothing by itself. "Analyse the reasons for the lovers' deaths" can be, and daily is, answered by a list of reasons in plot order, because the question's satisfaction conditions, a set of causes, are retrievable without transformation; whereas "How far was the catastrophe avoidable?" contains no taxonomy verb at all and cannot be answered without judgement, since avoidability is not a fact stored anywhere in the text but a measurement the writer must construct from the text's near misses. The design literature's convergence survives the correction of its shorthand: across assessment theory, writing studies, and cognitive research, the question that works is the one whose satisfaction conditions cannot be met by telling what is already known in the order it is already known.

One further finding from the tradition disciplines the whole enterprise with a useful humility. Wiggins's backward design begins from the assessment, not from the activity: the question is written before the teaching, and the teaching is then built as preparation for the question's demands. Departments habitually run the sequence in reverse, teaching the play as it comes and composing questions in the final week from whatever the teaching happened to emphasise, which guarantees that the summative instrument measures the course's accidents rather than its intentions. The boards, whatever their faults, never run it in reverse: the rubric exists before the cohort is taught, and departments then teach toward the rubric's grammar, a washback the GCSE study documents in detail. The classroom setter who writes the unit's questions first, and lets them govern the selection of scenes, the order of reading, and the drafting practice, is not teaching to the test in the pejorative sense. The pejorative sense belongs to the opposite procedure, in which a question composed in haste at the end examines students on a design nobody, setter included, ever chose.

## Moves, Not Topics: Graff, Elbow and the Grammar of Argument

If the design literature explains what a working question must contain, writing studies explains what a working question must teach, because a prompt is also a syllabus in miniature: it shows students, often for the first time, what an argument in the discipline looks like. Gerald Graff and Cathy Birkenstein built the most influential account of that showing in They Say / I Say: The Moves That Matter in Academic Writing (Norton, 2006). Their claim is that academic writing is a conversation conducted through a finite set of recognisable moves, and that the moves can be made visible to novices through templates: the concession that stands its ground, "Though I concede that ______, I still insist that ______"; the reasoned dissent, "I disagree with X's view that ______ because ______"; the complication, in which a source is shown to overlook the consideration that changes the case. The relevance to question design is exact and has gone oddly unremarked. A well-built prompt is a template one level up. The how far rubric performs the concede-and-insist move on the candidate's behalf before the candidate writes a word: it concedes the proposition's partial truth in its own grammar, and it insists that the candidate locate the boundary of that truth. A topic label performs no move at all, which is why answers to topic labels so often contain none, five paragraphs of material arranged by the plot and a conclusion that announces the material has been arranged.

Graff's case rests on an earlier essay that a question designer should know, the argument of "Hidden Intellectualism", developed in Clueless in Academe (2003), that the argumentative intelligence students practise outside school, in sport, in music, in the adjudication of loyalties and reputations, is not the enemy of academic argument but its unacknowledged raw material, and that schools fail such students by refusing to build the bridge between the arguments they can already conduct and the arguments the syllabus demands. The template is the bridge's near end. Its relevance to the rubric is exact: a question that performs its move in public tells the student which of the arguments already mastered in the corridor is being requested in the hall, while a question that conceals its move behind a theme noun requests the performance while withholding the programme. The charge that templates flatten writing into formula mistakes the bridge for the destination; nobody crosses a bridge in order to live on it, and the mark scheme's upper levels, which reward the conceptualised response, are precisely the far bank, where the move has been internalised and the scaffolding can be kicked away. Design that omits the bridge does not produce originality. It produces a toll, collected from the students whose homes did not supply the crossing.

### Can a template teach argument, or does it only rehearse its costume?

A template teaches the choreography of argument, where claims stand in relation to concessions, objections and evidence, and choreography is not nothing: a novice who cannot yet find the positions cannot dance at all. What a template cannot supply is the judgement that fills the positions, which is why a working question pairs its frame with an evidence demand.

The counter-position in writing studies is Peter Elbow's, set out from Writing Without Teachers (1973) onward: exploratory, low-stakes writing, freewriting and the believing game, in which a reader first inhabits an interpretation generously before doubting it, is the engine by which genuine positions form. On Elbow's account, a curriculum that leads with the graded argumentative performance teaches students to ventriloquise positions they do not hold, and the polished five-paragraph performance is the ventriloquism's certificate. The disagreement between Graff and Elbow is real and is adjudicated here on the evidence of the instruments themselves. For the design of the question, Graff wins: a prompt that hides its argumentative frame, in the name of authenticity, privileges the students who absorbed the frame at home and abandons the rest to the topic label's collapse, whereas the rubric that performs its move in public, how far, explore, presents, gives every candidate the same visible target. For the process that surrounds the question, Elbow wins: a course in which every encounter with the play is a graded performance produces exactly the rehearsed, riskless essays the examiner reports lament, and the exploratory draft, ungraded and unconcluded, is where a student's actual position on the Friar's guilt or Juliet's haste can form before the template shapes it. The documented criticism of Graff and Birkenstein's book, voiced in Composition Forum's review of 2008, that the text by its own preface does not teach the logical principles of argument, marks the boundary of the template method precisely: templates teach the moves, not the logic, and a question setter who relies on the rubric's grammar alone will receive grammatically perfect evasions. The evidence demand is the logic's enforcement arm. Template plus anchor, frame plus text: the working question needs both parents.

The synthesis has one further consequence for the classroom that both traditions, taken separately, obscure. Graff's explicitness is usually defended as access, and Elbow's exploration as authenticity, but in question design the two are sequential phases of a single act of fairness. The exploratory phase is where a student's evidence is gathered and a position is allowed to be wrong in private; the templated phase is where the position is made answerable to an audience's norms. A question set too early, before exploration, examines students on positions they were never given the conditions to form, and the grades then rank prior cultural capital while claiming to rank reading. A course that never leaves the exploratory phase commits the opposite theft, graduating students who have held many positions and defended none. The examination, arriving at the sequence's end with its public grammar, is the templated phase institutionalised, which is why the fiercest disputes about its fairness are usually, on inspection, disputes about whether the exploratory phase was ever provided. The question cannot answer that dispute. It can only decline to disguise it.

## Does Openness Licence Anything? Fish and the Evidence Norms

Every defence of the open question meets the same objection in the staffroom: if the question has no correct answer, then any answer is as good as any other, and marking becomes taste. The objection deserves its strongest available answer, which literary theory supplied some decades before the GCSE existed. Stanley Fish, in Is There a Text in This Class? (1980), argued that interpretations are not produced by solitary readers confronting a self-interpreting text, nor invented freely by readers unconstrained; they are made possible by interpretive communities, bodies of readers who share the strategies, the evidence norms, and the sense of what counts as a move, within which alone a reading can be recognised as a reading at all. Fish is routinely misread as licensing anything. His argument does the opposite: it explains why, inside a functioning community, most conceivable answers are never offered, because they cannot be built from the evidence by the strategies the community recognises. The examination system is an interpretive community with its norms published. The mark scheme is the community's strategy book, printed and distributed to schools, and its instruction to reward any valid interpretation is immediately disciplined by the word valid, whose content the level descriptors spell out: validity is what survives contact with the text under the community's rules of evidence.

The play itself supplies the second bound, the text's recalcitrance. Readings of Romeo and Juliet are not equally buildable, because the play's causal machinery is unusually explicit and unusually uneven in what it supports. The Chorus offers two causal theories in a single sonnet, the stars in its sixth line and, three lines later, "the continuance of their parents' rage" (Prologue.9-10), and five acts then audition both theories against an itemised record of decisions, delays, and one quarantined letter. A fate reading must survive the apothecary's poverty, the Friar's arithmetic of forty-two hours, and Capulet's advancement of the wedding day; a choice reading must survive the Prologue's announcement and Romeo's presentiment before the feast. Neither survives intact, which is the play's design and the reason its central question has remained open under four centuries of professional attention. An open question on such a text does not licence anything. It licences the two or three answers the evidence can actually carry, and it exposes, with a clarity no closed question matches, the writer who attempts a fourth. The adjudication of Fish against his relativist caricature therefore runs: openness is a property of the question, discipline is a property of the evidence norms, and a working assessment needs both, the question open at the top and the norms closed at the base. Remove the norms and the staffroom objection wins. Remove the openness and the instrument measures obedience. The boards' settlement, open rubric over published mark scheme, is Fish's theory with a payroll.

The play's final scene offers the point in stage form. The Prince closes the play by distributing praise, pardon and punishment over the assembled houses, an act of public interpretation performed with sovereign authority, and the houses accept the distribution because the interpreter's standing, not the reading's novelty, is what settles the matter. Verona acquires, in that distribution, its official reading of the catastrophe: some pardoned, some punished, all of it legible as the community's negotiated settlement with a text of corpses that every survivor had read differently an hour earlier. The classroom's settlement is less bloody and structurally identical. Readings are argued, evidence is exchanged, and the community's norms decide which readings stand, with the teacher holding, provisionally and answerably, the Prince's office. A question designed inside that settlement does not fear divergence. It fears only the reading that cannot show its evidence, and it says so in its wording or in its scheme.

## Choice: How Much Is Fair?

The remaining design variable is choice itself, and the systems distribute it with revealing inconsistency. Inside the GCSE, choice lives mostly upstream: a school chooses its play, OCR offers the candidate a choice between an extract-based and a discursive task, and within a given play and series the prompt is single and compulsory. The AP instrument inverts the allocation, fixing the conceptual prompt for the entire cohort and throwing choice downward onto the work itself, which the candidate selects from a printed list or from private reading. Classroom practice, unconstrained by comparability, tends to generosity: a menu of questions, from which students pick the ground they fancy. Each allocation buys something and pays for it. The single compulsory prompt buys comparability, the property that lets a grade mean the same thing in different schools, and pays with the occasional misfit between a question's angle and a particular class's preparation, a misfit the profession protested loudly when AQA's June 2024 extract fell outside the rehearsed repertoire. The candidate's choice of work buys ownership and the chance to write from strength, and pays with the assessment literature's standing difficulty, that performances elicited by different prompts are not automatically the same measurement, a difficulty examination systems manage by rubric discipline and classroom systems mostly ignore.

### Why do boards ration the choice that classrooms give freely?

Boards ration choice because a grade is a comparison, and comparison requires that candidates face sufficiently similar demands; every additional option multiplies the routes by which an answer can be easier or harder than its neighbours. A classroom, whose judgements are formative rather than certifying, can afford the generosity that a certificate cannot.

The allocation of choice has a history inside the English system that explains its present shape. For most of the GCSE's first quarter-century, part of the literature grade rested on coursework, later on controlled assessment, in which students wrote at length, with the text open, on tasks their own teachers framed; choice, of task, of angle, of text focus, lived there, in the component the school controlled. The reformed qualification abolished that world at a stroke, as the GCSE study records, and moved the entire grade into closed examinations. The design consequence was a migration: everything the coursework task had carried, the framing decisions, the selection of evidence, the pacing, had to be rebuilt inside a rubric a stranger could set and a stranger could mark, and the modern fused question, with its extract hinge and its two instruction lines, is that rebuilding made visible. The classroom menu of questions, still common in school practice, is the coursework era's surviving custom, legitimate in formative settings where its costs are borne by teaching rather than by certification, and misleading only when its freedom is mistaken for the examination's, a confusion students pay for in the hall. Choice did not leave the system. It moved house, from the candidate's desk to the rubric's architecture, and the setter who understands the migration can decide, for each new question, which side of the wall its choices belong on.

The classroom menu conceals a further mechanism that its defenders rarely inspect: students choose, in practice, the question their preparation fits, which in a culture of pre-composed answers means the question is chosen by the stock answer rather than by the student. Offer a menu on which one option is the theme tour the revision industry has rehearsed and another is a scaled question nobody has modelled, and the menu functions as a sorting machine, sending the well-resourced toward the safe option their tutors stocked and the adventurous toward the instrument that punishes adventure at the grade boundary. Choice, unexamined, can amplify exactly the inequity it was introduced to relieve, which is why the boards' suspicion of it is not mere institutional timidity. The department that offers menus owes its students a harder discipline: every option engineered to the same standard of foreclosure, every option stripped of its stock answer, so that the choice being exercised is a choice of argument rather than a choice of preparation. A menu that passes that audit is rare, because the audit is laborious, and the rarity is the data.

The play's Chorus models the classroom settlement with unnerving aptness. Having announced the whole plot, it closes by delegating the remainder to the audience's labour: "What here shall miss, our toil shall strive to mend" (Prologue.14). The sonnet fixes the frame entire, the ending, the principals, the cause in dispute, and leaves the filling, the judgement of each scene as it arrives, to the hearers. That is the adjudicated position on choice in question design: the frame is not negotiable, because the frame is where comparability and discipline live, while the filling, the selection of scenes, the weighting of evidence, the side taken on the tension, belongs to the writer, because the filling is where the argument lives. A menu of wholly different questions dissolves the frame. A single question with a fixed verdict dissolves the filling. The working instrument, in the examination hall as in Verona's theatre, fixes the first and frees the second.

## Five Design Principles

The argument so far distils into five principles, each stated as a rule a setter can apply to a draft question on this play and each testable against the failures already catalogued.

**First principle: name a tension, not a topic.** The question should contain, in its own wording, the two forces whose contest the essay will judge: the stars against the households, haste as glory against haste as defect, the fathers' market against the children's flight from it. The series' standing study of [the play as a tragedy of fate or of character](/2022/02/04/romeo-and-juliet-fate-or-character-tragedy/) adjudicates the founding tension at full length; the question setter's task is smaller and prior, to ensure the tension survives compression into one sentence without resolving into a slogan. The test is mechanical: if the question can be answered by a student who holds only one side of the tension and has never noticed the other, the tension is not in the question, whatever the setter intended.

**Second principle: fix the evidence anchor, and say which gauge is in force.** A prompt should declare whether its evidence base is a passage, a scene-pair, or the whole play, because each gauge examines a different skill and fairness requires the candidate to know which. The narrow gauge has a distinguished local pedigree: [the Prologue close-reading lesson](/2030/03/01/close-read-prologue-in-class/) demonstrates how much disciplined argument fourteen lines can be made to yield when the anchor permits no drift. The wide gauge is not the narrow gauge's relaxation but its complement, since whole-play range converts selection into argument. Capulet's first answer to Paris shows the narrow gauge's power: "My child is yet a stranger in the world; / She hath not seen the change of fourteen years" (1.2.8-9) contains, inside two lines, the age question, the father's initial scruple, and the seed of his later reversal, and an essay confined to that exchange, properly interrogated, can reach the play's whole machinery of arranged marriage. What the principle forbids is the unmarked middle, the question whose scope is wherever the writer's memory happens to stop.

**Third principle: install a scale, never a destination.** The question's grammar should require a measured judgement: how far, to what extent, in what respects. A destination grammar, prove that, show that, discuss whether with a foreordained yes, converts the essay into advocacy for a verdict the setter has already reached, and students read the instruction correctly and supply the verdict with illustrations. The scale grammar's superiority is not politeness; it is that the play's own judgements are scaled. The Prince, closing the action, distributes blame across every party in a single sentence whose burden is that all are punished, a verdict of degree pronounced over a stage of bodies, and an essay question that demands a single culprit asks less of its writer than the play asks of its audience.

**Fourth principle: presuppose nothing the play does not contain.** The question is a factual document as well as an instrument, and its presuppositions carry an examination's authority into the student's memory. The balcony that the text never names, the romance of several weeks that the play's calendar refutes, the feud with a stated cause that the text withholds: each is a misconception waiting for a prompt to install it, and the misconceptions record shows how durable an installed error becomes once an authority has vouched for it. The setter's audit is simple and seldom performed: every noun phrase in the question should be checkable against the text as the text stands, in the edition the class is using, before the question is allowed to examine anyone else's accuracy.

**Fifth principle: make summary structurally useless.** The finished test of a draft question is adversarial: could a competent summary of the play, lightly reordered, satisfy it? If the answer is yes, the question is a summary in waiting, whatever its verbs promise. The reliable way to fail the summary is to demand a mechanism: not that an effect occurs, the lovers' language grows more shared, the violence escalates, the adults close ranks, but how the text manufactures the effect, line by line and decision by decision, and what the manufacture costs. Mechanism questions cannot be answered from a plot digest, because digests record outcomes and discard manufacture. They can barely be answered from the anthology scenes either, since mechanisms live in transitions, and transitions are what anthologies cut. A question that demands mechanism therefore does double duty: it bans the summary and it repatriates the neglected middle of the play, the connective scenes where the catastrophe is actually assembled.

The five principles compose into a single audit, and the audit is worth performing once in full on a realistic draft, because drafts fail in combination more often than in isolation. Take a question of a kind set in schools every term, constructed here for dissection: "Shakespeare presents the theme of conflict in many ways in Romeo and Juliet. Discuss." The first principle fails at once: conflict is a topic noun, and no opposing force is named, so the answer's organising idea must be imported or defaulted to chronology. The second fails silently: no gauge is declared, and the phrase in many ways actively instructs the writer to range without anchor, guaranteeing the anthology tour. The third fails in the verb: discuss requests the tour the second principle already licensed, and the proposition embedded in the first sentence, that the theme is presented in many ways, is a destination the essay can only illustrate. The fourth passes narrowly, the question presupposes nothing false, merely nothing at all, which is its own verdict. The fifth delivers the sentence: a competent summary of the play, reordered under clash headings, satisfies the question completely, and will receive, in most classrooms, a competent mark. Repair proceeds by the anatomy. Name the tension the theme conceals: Verona's violence is at once the feud's ancient inheritance and a series of fresh decisions by named men. Fix the anchor at the hinge where inheritance and decision collide, Mercutio's death scene, with whole-play range argued outward. Install the scale. The repaired question, in this article's construction, runs: "Starting with the fighting of Act 3 Scene 1, explore how far Shakespeare presents Verona's violence as an inheritance its characters could not escape rather than a choice they kept making." Every repair corresponds to a principle, every principle to a foreclosed failure, and the candidate who then writes a summary will be visibly, markably, answering a question that was not set. That visibility, the property by which an instrument exposes the substitution of a stock response, is the audit's final and sufficient test.

The bank is a set of constructions by this article, not a harvest of past papers; the documentary specimens examined above, from AQA, Eduqas and the College Board, are labelled as documents where they appear in the argument, and nothing in the table that follows borrows their authority. Three features of the bank deserve note. First, every question carries its scale or its mechanism in the wording: how far, what changes, where does the machinery turn. None can be answered by procession through the acts. Second, the evidence column is deliberately unglamorous in its specificity, scene numbers and day counts rather than theme words, because the anchor is the part of the question students are trained to skip and the part markers can verify. Third, the foreclosure column is the setter's confession: each question exists because a known failure, the slogan, the survey, the label, the recital, was observed in scripts and traced back to the wording that licensed it. A bank that cannot name the failure each question forecloses is a list of topics in waiting.

The bank's second use is calibration, and it deserves one concrete form, since a department's habits are invisible until an external object makes them cast a shadow. The department lays its own term's questions beside the bank's ten and audits the differences dimension by dimension: how many of its questions name a tension at all, how many fix an anchor narrower than the whole play, how many carry a scale, how many state their evidence demand in the wording rather than in the department's oral tradition. The exercise reliably produces a profile no self-description would have predicted, most departments discovering that the large majority of their questions share a single failure, usually the absent scale, occasionally the imported proposition, and the discovery is worth more than any individual repair because it converts question design from a private craft into a departmental standard with agreed terms. The fourth column earns its keep in the same exercise: for each of the department's own questions, the foreclosed failure must be named, and where none can be named, the question returns for repair with the diagnosis already written. Instruments improve the systems that submit to them; the bank asks nothing exotic, only that a department treat its questions as it teaches its students to treat quotations, as evidence to be examined rather than furniture to be inherited.

A final maintenance rule follows from the bank's own logic: questions decay. Any question in circulation long enough acquires a stock answer, because the commercial guides metabolise set questions into model responses and the models drain the question's foreclosure of force; a tension that once had to be argued becomes, after two cycles of the guides, a tension that can be recited with its conclusion attached. The decay is not a reason to abandon published questions, since the boards live with it permanently, rotating extracts and angles while holding the grammar constant. It is a reason to rotate on the same principle. The tension is the question's capital and survives rotation; the anchor is its clothing and does not. A department that re-anchors its standing questions each cycle, the fate argument moved from the Prologue to the apothecary, the haste argument moved from the wedding's advancement to the potion's timing, keeps the design while retiring the recitation, and the guides are left modelling answers to questions no longer being asked. Rotation, not novelty, is the setter's durable defence: the play's tensions are few and permanent, its scenes are many, and the product of the two is a question stock that no single cycle of the industry can exhaust.

## The Counter-Case: Closure Has Its Reasons

The verdict of this article is a design rule, and design rules meet their limits in real classrooms, so the counter-case must be given its full strength before the verdict is allowed to stand. Open questions strand writers. The student whose knowledge of the play is thin, whose academic English is still forming, whose confidence is a rumour, faces a how far question as a cliff face: the scale that liberates the strong writer paralyses the writer who cannot yet locate the proposition's truth at any point along it. Bean's problem principle contains its own governor, since a problem pitched beyond the solver's resources is not a problem but a wall, and Wiggins and McTighe never intended the essential question as a single-sitting instrument; it frames a unit, recurring across weeks, while the forty-minute examination answer is a different genre with different mercies. The boards' rubrics, read closely, are more closed than the open-question rhetoric admits: the extract is given, the theme noun is given, the two-part structure is given, and the candidate's freedom operates inside a frame built almost entirely of closures. The GCSE question, on this reading, is not an open question at all but a closed frame with an open centre, and its success, where it succeeds, is the success of the proportion.

Washback presses alongside. A question does not merely measure a course; over a school year it becomes the course, since departments rationally train the performance the instrument rewards, and the taught play contracts toward the question's terms as reliably as it contracts toward the examinable scenes. A setter therefore chooses, with every draft, what the play will be for the students who study under it: the setter of theme tours manufactures a year of theme tours, and the setter of tension, anchor and scale manufactures a year of measured argument. This is the heaviest responsibility in the design office and the one least often minuted, because its effects arrive disguised as student ability. The cohorts are not rising and falling. The questions are.

Comparability presses from the other side. A certificate is a promise that performances can be ranked across schools and series, and every increment of openness is an increment of marker judgement, which is to say of variance that the system must then spend its standardisation machinery to suppress. The proposition form, for all its deadness, marks fast and marks alike; the discursive question marks slowly and argues with itself. There is also the matter of teaching time: a department that trains students for open argument must spend weeks on the evidence base the argument consumes, and the contraction of the taught play toward its examinable scenes, documented in the GCSE study, is partly the rational response of schools purchasing argumentative depth with the currency of coverage.

The closure's reasons include a labour cost that design discussions omit. Open questions are marked by human beings, overwhelmingly serving teachers working as examiners, recruited, trained and standardised by the boards, and every increment of openness in the rubric is an increment of judgement demanded from that workforce at marking speed, in the evenings of a teaching term. The boards' published machinery, the standardisation meetings at which agreed scripts fix the standard before live marking begins, exists because the system knows what its openness costs and pays the price deliberately. A classroom setter enjoys the same freedom at a smaller scale and owes the same payment: every open question set is a promise to mark divergently, slowly, and against criteria the setter must be able to state, and the setter who cannot state the criteria has not designed an open question but an unmarked one. The counter-case therefore disciplines the verdict's enthusiasm at exactly the right joint. Openness is not a virtue to be maximised. It is a resource to be budgeted, spent where the tension is richest, and withheld where the machinery of fairness cannot afford it.

Sequencing is the design variable the single-question analysis misses, and a unit on the play is a sequence of questions before it is anything else. The play itself models the gradient. Its first Chorus poses the frame questions, who are these households, what is the grudge, how does the announced ending reframe every scene that follows it. Its second Chorus, heading Act 2, re-poses the inquiry at the hinge, contrasting the old desire dead in its bed with the new affection gasping to succeed it, and thereby converts the audience's question from what will happen to what the exchange of one love for another will cost. A well-built course follows the same gradient of gauges. Early questions run narrow: a single speech, a single exchange, the whole machinery of tension and evidence practised on fourteen lines or forty, where failure is cheap and correction immediate. Middle questions widen to scene-pairs and reversals, Capulet in Act 1 against Capulet in Act 3, the Friar's sermon against the Friar's plot. The summative question runs widest, whole-play range under a scale, because only at the end has the student assembled the evidence base a wide gauge honestly requires. Departments that invert the sequence, opening with the whole-play theme essay in the unit's second week, harvest the predictable crop, anthology answers from students who had read a fifth of the play, and then misread the harvest as a verdict on the students rather than on the sequence. The question bank in this article is ordered without regard to sequence; a course that adopts it should re-sort it by gauge, narrow to wide, which is the order in which its questions can be answered from knowledge actually in hand.

One clarification protects the counter-case from being misread as a defence of the topic label. The design rule governs the essay question, the instrument whose purpose is to elicit and rank written argument; a course contains other instruments with other purposes, and closure is frequently their correct form. The retrieval quiz that checks whether the class knows who killed Mercutio, the close exercise that parses a single speech's syntax, the timed paragraph that rehearses one move under pressure: each is closed by design and excellent in its place, because each measures a component that the essay will later spend. The failure catalogued in this article is not closure but confusion, the essay slot occupied by an instrument built for another purpose, a retrieval exercise wearing an essay's tariff. A department's question stock, audited whole, should show a deliberate ecology: closed instruments for the components, open instruments for the performances, and no instrument pretending to be the other. The boards model the ecology at national scale without advertising it, the accuracy marks and the extract's evidence work closing around the essay's open centre like a frame around a picture, each element sized to its function.

The adjudication therefore qualifies the verdict rather than reversing it. The working question is not the maximally open question. It is the question whose closures are structural, anchor, scale, evidence norms, and whose openness is located exactly where judgement lives, in the measurement of the tension. Closure about everything except the verdict; freedom about nothing except the verdict. The play, characteristically, has already staged the proportion: its Chorus closes everything, plot, principals, outcome, in fourteen lines, and leaves open the only thing worth five acts, the apportionment of blame, on which the Prince's closing sentence is itself merely the first, most authorised, and most contestable contribution.

## Verdict: The Question as a Small Theory of the Play

A setter of questions on Romeo and Juliet is, whether the office is acknowledged or not, a critic with a captive audience, because every question smuggles a reading of the play into the room inside its presuppositions and then examines students on their proximity to it. The dishonest forms of the office are the topic label, which smuggles the reading that the play is a warehouse; the plot question in interpretive dress, which smuggles the reading that the play is its events; and the presupposing question, which smuggles an error and calls the error the syllabus. The honest form is the question that shows its theory in its wording and invites the candidate to defeat it: a tension named in the open, an evidence base fixed where all can reach it, a scale that permits the answer the setter did not foresee. Such a question can be marked, because the evidence norms are public; it can be taught, because its anatomy is visible; and it can be beaten, because a candidate who measures the tension more finely than the setter did has done the thing the instrument exists to detect. The InsightCrunch question bank is offered in that spirit, as ten small theories of the play with their reasoning exposed, to be used, adapted, and where the evidence demands it, argued down. The play has survived four centuries of questions put to it by critics, examiners, and schoolrooms. The least a question can do, in return, is deserve the play.

The practical consequence, for any department that accepts the argument, is an audit rather than a conversion. The inherited stock of questions is exhumed, each specimen laid against the anatomy, tension, anchor, scale, evidence demand, foreclosed failure, and the stock is repaired or retired specimen by specimen, with the past papers of the boards kept on the table as the profession's reference instruments and the mark schemes read as what they are, the design documents of a rival and more disciplined office. Nothing in the audit requires new resources, new texts, or new doctrine. It requires only that the questions a school asks about the play be subjected to the standard the school applies to the answers: that they show their reasoning, cite their evidence, and survive disagreement. A school whose questions pass that standard has, whether it knows it or not, already taught the first lesson of the course, before a single scene is read, because the students will meet, in the instruments set before them, the exact intellectual conduct the instruments exist to reward. The question is the syllabus's first text. It should be written like one. The standard is not mysterious and the materials are not scarce: the boards publish their instruments and their reasoning every summer, the design literature has stated its findings for half a century, and the play itself, in its two Choruses, demonstrates the form, a frame announced in advance and a labour delegated to the audience under discipline. What has been scarce is only the habit of treating the question as an object of design at all. That habit, once acquired, does not stay in its corner. It spreads to the worksheets, the homework, the feedback, until the whole course is written the way the working question is written, with its tensions named, its evidence fixed, and its conclusions left honourably open.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the difference between an essay topic and an essay question?

A topic names a region of the play; a question names a dispute inside it. "Love in Romeo and Juliet" is a topic, and it can be answered by coverage, a tour of the region in the order the play supplies. "How far does the play present love as a discipline its characters keep failing?" is a question, because two defensible answers collide inside it and the evidence must arbitrate. The practical test for a setter is whether a student could begin writing without having decided anything. If the first paragraph can be composed in a state of perfect neutrality, the instrument in hand is a topic, whatever its punctuation claims, and the responses will be inventories wearing the costume of essays.

### Q: Why do prompts phrased as invitations to describe produce narrative instead of analysis?

A describe prompt sets retrieval as the task, and retrieval from a narrative source follows narrative order, so the essay inherits the plot's chronology as its structure. Bereiter and Scardamalia named the underlying process knowledge-telling: content is drawn from memory under a topic cue and set down in the order it arrives. Analysis requires a second operation, the testing of retrieved material against a claim, and nothing in the verb describe requests that operation. The remedy is not a grander verb but a different satisfaction condition: a scale to measure, a mechanism to trace, a tension to judge. Where the prompt demands one of those, retrieval alone visibly fails, and the writer is pushed into the transforming mode in which analysis lives.

### Q: Why should a prompt ask how Shakespeare presents an idea rather than what the idea is?

The first wording makes the playwright the object of study and the second makes the theme the object, and the difference decides the essay's contents. Asked what love is in the play, a writer collects the play's statements about love into a survey. Asked how Shakespeare presents love, the writer must attend to manufacture: who speaks, in what form, at what structural moment, with what consequences. Presentation questions recruit the sonnet shared between the lovers, the Friar's proverbial register, Mercutio's parody of the Petrarchan style, as evidence of differing weight, and they license the strongest move in school criticism, the observation that the play presents the same idea incompatibly in different mouths and lets the plot judge between the versions.

### Q: Why do the two instruction lines beneath a GCSE question carry half its design?

The headline rubric performs the question's rhetoric, but the two subordinate lines perform its architecture, dividing the task into the extract movement and the whole-play movement and thereby fixing the essay's skeleton before the candidate writes a word. AQA's June 2024 question on the difficulties faced by Juliet directs candidates to write about the presentation in the speech and in the play as a whole, and that division is the board's chief defence against the two priced failures of the system, the answer that never leaves the printed passage and the answer that never visits it. Setters working outside the examination hall routinely omit the equivalent lines, and then mark down, as structural weakness, essays whose shapelessness the question itself designed.

### Q: What can a mark scheme reward that the wording of a question cannot by itself secure?

A mark scheme can reward a quality of attention that no rubric wording can compel: the conceptualised response, in AQA's term for its top level, in which an answer is organised by a governing idea about the text rather than by the text's sequence. Wording can invite such a response, through a scale and a tension, and can refuse to reward its absence, through caps on answers confined to the extract, but the invitation is not the achievement, because a candidate may satisfy every instruction in a rubric while thinking in chronicle throughout. The mark scheme is therefore the question's second half, the place where the community's evidence norms are published, and a setter who drafts a question without drafting its reward structure has designed only the visible half of the instrument.

### Q: What is an essential question, and how does its work differ from an examination question's work?

Grant Wiggins and Jay McTighe defined the essential question as one that cannot be answered with finality in a brief sentence, that provokes inquiry, recurs across a course, and requires the weighing of evidence toward a justified position. Its work is curricular and slow: it frames a unit, gathers material over weeks, and is answered repeatedly at increasing depth. An examination question borrows the essential question's grammar, its openness and its scale, and compresses it into a single timed performance with a fixed evidence base and published norms. Confusing the two instruments damages both. A unit framed by an examination question teaches test procedure and calls it inquiry; an examination set with an unmediated essential question strands every candidate whose resources the frame was supposed to build.

### Q: Why do weaker writers need a frame around a question as much as stronger writers need freedom inside it?

A completely open question transfers the whole burden of design, selection, scope, and stance, onto the writer least equipped to carry it, and the predictable result is not freedom but retreat to the one structure already memorised, the plot. A frame, a given passage, a named theme, a two-part instruction, performs the design labour in advance and leaves the writer the judgement, which is the part worth examining. Stronger writers experience the same frame as a floor rather than a ceiling, because the scale inside it still permits any measured position. The boards' settlement, closed frame and open centre, is therefore an equity instrument as much as a measurement one: it equalises the starting conditions so that the variable under assessment is judgement rather than inherited familiarity with academic forms.

### Q: What does the warning "Do not merely summarize the plot" concede about examination design?

The warning, printed in the College Board's 2024 Advanced Placement Literature question on indecision, concedes that summary is the default failure of literary assessment, the response every instrument receives when its question can be satisfied by retelling. Its presence on the paper is an admission that the prompt, left to itself, does not fully prevent the failure it names: a candidate may choose a work, recall its story at length, and gesture at the concept, and the rubric must then do at the gate what the wording could not do on the page. The concession is instructive for setters at every level. Where a question can be answered by summary, summary will arrive, in quantity, and blaming candidates for delivering the response the instrument invited is a design error dressed as a standards sermon.

### Q: How does a printed extract change what a question can fairly demand of a candidate?

A printed extract equalises the evidence base at the moment of performance: every candidate confronts the same thirty lines, in the hall, without dependence on the accidents of revision or the quality of a school's quotation bank. That equality licenses demands that would be unfair across a whole text, in particular the demand for fine-grained attention to wording, since the words are present to be attended to. The GCSE's extract hinge also disciplines the setter, who must choose a passage dense enough to bear the question's weight; the profession's anger in June 2024, when AQA set a soliloquy outside the rehearsed repertoire, showed the system's dependence on extract predictability and, by reaction, the extract's power. Fairness and narrowness are the same property read from opposite ends.

### Q: Why can a question install a misconception before a unit of teaching begins?

A question is read by students as a factual document backed by institutional authority, and its presuppositions are absorbed before any teaching can qualify them. A prompt that directs attention to the balcony scene teaches, silently and with an examiner's imprimatur, that the play contains a balcony; a prompt that assumes a courtship of several weeks teaches a false calendar; a prompt that asks for the feud's cause teaches that the text supplies one. The misconceptions record assembled in this series shows how durable such installed errors are, surviving correction precisely because an authority vouched for them first. The setter's defence is an audit of presuppositions against the text, noun phrase by noun phrase, performed before the question is released to examine anyone else's accuracy.

### Q: What do Graff and Birkenstein's templates offer a setter of questions, beyond help for writers?

The templates in They Say / I Say (2006) are a catalogue of the moves by which academic arguments proceed, concession, dissent with reasons, complication, and a question setter can reverse-engineer them: every strong template implies a question whose answer would require that move. A prompt built to elicit the concede-and-insist move concedes a proposition's partial truth in its own wording and demands the boundary of that truth, which is the anatomy of the how far rubric. A prompt built to elicit complication names the standard account and asks what it overlooks. Read this way, Graff and Birkenstein's book is a prompt generator disguised as a style manual, and the setter who studies it acquires a repertoire of question shapes, each pre-fitted to an argumentative act the marker can recognise and reward.

### Q: Does an open question mean that any interpretation of the play can earn full marks?

No, and the examination system's own documents say so in terms. AQA's mark scheme for June 2024 instructs examiners to reward any valid interpretations, and the adjective carries the discipline: validity is tested against the text under the community's published rules of evidence, the level descriptors and caps that bound the mark scheme. Stanley Fish's account of interpretive communities explains the same boundary from theory: readings are produced within shared strategies, and a reading that cannot be built from the evidence by those strategies is not a bolder reading but an unavailable one. Openness governs the verdict, which may fall anywhere along the scale the question installs. It does not govern the evidence, where the play's itemised record of decisions, speeches, and stage events admits some constructions and refuses others outright.

### Q: Why do questions about the adults tend to produce stronger arguments than questions about the lovers?

Questions about the lovers attract the play's publicity, romance, sympathy, identification, and the answers arrive pre-written by the culture: beautiful, reckless, doomed. Questions about the adults attract the play's machinery, the marriage market, the edict and its non-enforcement, the advanced wedding date, the letter entrusted to a single friar, and machinery must be analysed rather than admired. The adult questions also have a structural advantage: the adults contradict themselves inside the text, Capulet the scrupulous father of Act 1 becoming the tyrant of Act 3, the Friar the preacher of moderation becoming the plot's chief gambler, so the tension a working question requires is documented in single characters and does not have to be assembled across the cast. Sympathy summarises; contradiction argues.

### Q: How can a teacher test a draft question before a class encounters it?

Three tests, each cheap and each decisive, expose most design faults in advance. The summary test asks whether a competent plot digest, lightly reordered, could satisfy the question; if it could, the instrument will harvest summaries. The slogan test asks whether the question can be answered from the play's reputation, star-crossed, forbidden love, tragic waste, without the text open; if it can, the culture will answer it and the student will transcribe. The presupposition test reads every noun phrase in the question against the text for silent falsehoods, balconies, calendars, causes. A draft that survives all three, with its tension named, its anchor fixed and its scale installed, has earned its place on the board. A draft that fails one has located its own repair.

### Q: Why do questions fixed on a single passage resist stock answers recycled from study guides?

A stock answer is built for portability: it must fit any question on its theme, so it is written at the level of generality at which all such questions blur together, themes, symbols, and a handful of anthology quotations. A passage-fixed question destroys the portability, because its evidence base is thirty specified lines whose wording the stock answer has never examined, and whose details, a tense change, a repeated pronoun, an image that turns against itself, will not bear the generalities parked on them. The mechanism demand compounds the resistance, since guides record what passages mean and discard how they work. A setter seeking insurance against the industry could do worse than the narrow gauge: one speech, forensically specified, with a question only its lines can answer.

### Q: Why does a question about haste have to fix the play's calendar before it can be answered honestly?

Haste is a ratio, speed against an available interval, and a question that leaves the interval vague invites essays measuring the lovers' pace against an imaginary courtship of months. The play's own calendar is severe: the action runs from Sunday to Thursday, the potion is measured at forty-two hours by its maker, and the wedding is advanced by a day at the catastrophe's hinge. A question that states the calendar in its wording, five days end to end, converts haste from an atmosphere into a measurable quantity and forces the disagreement where it belongs, onto whether compression was the tragedy or merely its accelerant. Without the fixed calendar the same question receives sermons about impulsiveness, a theme noun in a hurry, and the play's most precise structural device goes unexamined.

### Q: What separates a debatable question from a merely vague one?

A debatable question divides competent readers who share the same evidence; a vague question divides readers because each is answering a different question. The distinction is testable before any student writes: give the draft to two colleagues and ask each to state, in one sentence, the two strongest opposing answers. If both can do so, and the opposing answers genuinely collide over the same passages, the question is debatable and the collision is the essay's engine. If the colleagues produce answers about different subjects, or one cannot find a second side at all, the draft is vague or one-sided respectively, and no amount of imperative polish will save it. Debatability is a property of the play's tensions faithfully named; vagueness is the setter's indecision promoted into an instrument.

### Q: Why should a prompt's evidence demand be stated rather than left to custom?

Customary evidence demands are inherited unequally. Students formed in argumentative households arrive knowing that an essay on the play ranges across acts and cites the words on the page; students without that formation answer the question exactly as set, and a prompt silent about its evidence base marks the difference as if it were a difference in literary ability. Stating the demand, in this speech and in the play as a whole, from two scenes of the candidate's choosing, from the passage printed below, converts private knowledge of the genre into a public term of the task. Gerald Graff's case for making the moves of academic argument explicit applies to the setter with doubled force: the question is the one text every student in the room is guaranteed to read, and its silences are lessons too.

### Q: What does the InsightCrunch question bank record beside each question it lists?

The InsightCrunch question bank records four things for each of its ten questions: the question itself, labelled as this article's construction rather than as a borrowed document; the tension the question names, stated as the two forces a successful answer must hold together; the evidence it demands, specified by scene, speech, or structural feature rather than by theme; and the failure the question forecloses, the known collapse, slogan, survey, label, or recital, that its wording was designed to make impossible. The rationale columns are the bank's reason for existing. A question published naked teaches imitation of its surface; a question published with its reasoning exposed teaches the design judgement by which further questions can be built and faulty ones repaired.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch question bank to argue that a question works by banning summary rather than by inviting opinion?

An essay should treat the bank's foreclosure column as its evidence base, tracing three questions back to the failures each was built against: the fate question against the star-crossed slogan, the Friar question against the plot-device summary, the calendar question against the leisurely courtship that digests presuppose. For each, the essay can show that the wording does not merely encourage a better answer but withdraws the summary's licence, by fixing an evidence base the digest has not read, installing a scale the slogan cannot occupy, or demanding a mechanism that outcomes alone cannot supply. The contrast with the opinion poll completes the argument: where a question invites opinion, it receives temperament; where it bans summary, it receives judgement, because the writer has been left nothing else to do.
