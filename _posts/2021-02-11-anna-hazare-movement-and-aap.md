The Anna Hazare movement began as one man's fast at Jantar Mantar on 5 April 2011 and produced, within thirty-one months, a national ombudsman statute and a political party governing Delhi. That conversion of street presence into institutional form, repeated five times in dated sequence, is the subject of this article, and it is best read as citizen-renegotiation: citizens outside the legislature forcing the legislature to reopen terms it had left dormant for four decades.

## The Anna Hazare Movement and AAP as Citizen Renegotiation

The claim defended here is precise. Between November 2010 and January 2014, the Anna Hazare movement and the Aam Aadmi Party that emerged from it operated as citizen-renegotiation through five institutional moments, each one dated, documented and traceable in a primary record: a corruption dossier that a sitting government could not answer, a four-day fast that extracted a joint drafting committee, a twelve-day fast that extracted a parliamentary resolution, a party founding that converted a movement's personnel into an electoral machine, and a statute that entered into force on 16 January 2014 as the movement's institutional residue. The reading belongs to [the movements-as-citizen-renegotiation framework](/2021/01/09/indian-movements-citizen-renegotiation/) developed for this pillar, which treats a movement as renegotiation when it compels formal institutions to rewrite the terms on which citizens deal with the state, and it applies that framework to the most documented Indian movement of the twenty-first century's second decade.

Two rival readings compete for the same sequence, and both capture part of it. The anti-corruption reading treats April 2011 as an eruption of public anger against graft, powered by the 2G spectrum scandal and the Commonwealth Games contracts, and measures the movement by the ombudsman it demanded. The party-history reading treats the movement as a prologue, interesting chiefly because it launched Arvind Kejriwal and the Aam Aadmi Party into electoral politics. The citizen-renegotiation reading defended in these pages does not deny the anger or the party. It asks a different question: by what mechanism did a fast become a drafting committee, a drafting committee's failure become a parliamentary resolution, a resolution's inadequacy become a party, and a party's first government become the pressure under which a forty-five-year-old legislative file finally closed? The mechanism, not the mood, is the analytical object.

The documentary spine of the sequence is unusually firm. The Comptroller and Auditor General's report on the 2G spectrum allocation was tabled in Parliament on 16 November 2010. The Gazette notification constituting the Joint Drafting Committee for the Lokpal Bill is dated 8 April 2011. The sense-of-the-House resolution in both Houses of Parliament is recorded in the debates of 27 August 2011. The Aam Aadmi Party was formally launched on 26 November 2012 at the Constitution Club in New Delhi. The Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act received presidential assent on 1 January 2014 and commenced on 16 January 2014. A reading that follows these five documents in order can be checked against them, and the reading offered here invites exactly that check.

The movement also arrived with a pedigree inside the longer anti-corruption arc. [The anti-corruption politics of the Right to Information Act](/2020/12/12/2005-rti-anti-corruption-politics/) had already demonstrated, in the campaign that produced the 2005 statute, that a drafted bill carried by a citizens' campaign could move from village hearings to the statute book. Arvind Kejriwal had served his apprenticeship in that campaign, and Anna Hazare had campaigned for information rights in Maharashtra years before Delhi heard his name. The Jan Lokpal demand of 2011 was the second use of a proven instrument: draft the law outside Parliament, then make Parliament answer for not enacting it.

What distinguishes 2011 from 2005 is the completeness of the cycle. The information campaign won a statute and dispersed. The Hazare movement won a committee, lost a drafting war, won a resolution, lost a parliamentary winter, split on the question of power itself, founded a party, formed a government, resigned on the very bill that had started everything, and then watched the ombudsman statute pass in December 2013 under pressure that included a fresh Hazare fast at Ralegan Siddhi. Each stage renegotiated a different term of the citizen-state relationship: who drafts law, who answers grievances, who may enter elections, and what an ombudsman may touch. The five features traced below follow that cycle stage by stage.

The file the movement reopened had its own long documentary history, and the history explains why a draft bill, of all instruments, became a crowd's demand. The Administrative Reforms Commission, in its interim report of 1966, recommended a Lokpal for the Union and Lokayuktas for the states on the Scandinavian ombudsman model, to redress citizens' grievances against the administration including its political heads. The first Lokpal Bill followed in 1968 and passed the Lok Sabha in 1969 before lapsing in the Rajya Sabha on the lower house's dissolution. Successive governments reintroduced versions in 1971, 1977, 1985, 1989, 1996, 1998 and 2001, and every version lapsed, most without a serious debate, a record spanning Congress, Janata, National Front, United Front and National Democratic Alliance governments alike. The states, meanwhile, built the institution piecemeal: Maharashtra created the first Lokayukta in 1971, and Karnataka's 1984 statute produced the office from which Justice Hegde would strike in 2011. By April 2011 the Lokpal was therefore not an invention of the street but a forty-three-year-old parliamentary promise, drafted repeatedly by the state itself and repeatedly abandoned by it, which is the precise condition the citizen-renegotiation reading describes: a term of dealing between citizen and state that the state had written, signed in draft, and left unexecuted until citizens arrived with the draft in their hands and a date by which they required an answer.

## The Man from Ralegan Siddhi: What Hazare Brought to Delhi

Kisan Baburao Hazare, called Anna, elder brother, in his village, arrived in Delhi in 2011 with a record that his supporters cited and his critics discounted, and the record matters because the movement's form, the fast as an instrument, the village as a credential, the refusal of office, was assembled in Maharashtra over thirty-five years. Hazare returned to Ralegan Siddhi in Ahmednagar district after army service that included the 1965 war, and from 1975 he rebuilt the drought-prone village around watershed works, a rebuilt temple as the organising centre, prohibition of liquor enforced by collective sanction, and the removal of the grain bank and threshing practices that had bound poor households to moneylenders. The village won state and national recognition for the experiment, Hazare received the Padma Shri in 1990 and the Padma Bhushan in 1992, and the Maharashtra government of the day promoted Ralegan as a model village. The methods were debated from the start: prohibition and moral discipline enforced by community pressure sat uneasily with liberal accounts of individual choice, and Hazare's defenders and detractors in Maharashtra were arguing about the Ralegan mixture, development delivered through moral authority, decades before Delhi took sides on the same question.

The fast entered his repertoire as a weapon against state governments. In 2003 Hazare fasted in Mumbai against corruption in the Maharashtra government of the Congress and Nationalist Congress Party, naming four ministers; the campaign ended with a commission of inquiry under Justice P. B. Sawant and the resignation of a minister. He campaigned through the same years for a Maharashtra right to information law, pressing the state until Maharashtra enacted its statute in 2002 and strengthened the regime in 2003, groundwork that fed the national information campaign. The pattern that Delhi would see in 2011 was set in these Maharashtra episodes: a named grievance, a written draft or demand placed before the government, a deadline, a fast at a symbolic site, and a negotiated document at the end. Hazare's critics inside Maharashtra, including activists who had worked beside him, also recorded the pattern's costs: a preference for the dramatic deadline over the slow committee, an impatience with allies who disputed his draft, and a moral vocabulary that could turn disagreement into betrayal. Both halves of that record, the efficacy and the impatience, travelled to Jantar Mantar with him, and both halves explain what happened between April 2011 and September 2012 better than any account that begins the story in Delhi.

## Feature One: The Corruption Dossier That UPA-2 Could Not Answer

The second United Progressive Alliance government took office after the May 2009 general election with a stronger mandate than its predecessor, the coalition whose formation [the post-2004 UPA mandate](/2019/06/22/2004-upa-shock-verdict-explained/) had made possible. The Congress rose to 206 seats, coalition management eased, and the government's defenders expected a term of consolidation. Instead, the term's defining documents became audit reports. The sequence that destroyed the government's standing began on 14 November 2010, when Telecom Minister A. Raja resigned after days of parliamentary uproar over the 2G spectrum allocation of 2008, and deepened two days later when the Comptroller and Auditor General's report, signed by Vinod Rai, was tabled in Parliament on 16 November 2010 with a presumptive loss figure of Rs 1.76 lakh crore attached to licences issued at 2001 prices on a first-come-first-served basis.

### Why did corruption become the organising grievance of 2011?

Corruption became the organising grievance because three audit-grade scandals converged within one season, each carrying a number, a named minister and a documentary trail: the 2G spectrum allocation, the Commonwealth Games contracts and the coal block allocations. Price rise hurt households diffusely; the scandals gave anger an address, a file and a figure that television could repeat nightly.

The government's defence was stated at its strongest by Kapil Sibal, who took the telecom portfolio after Raja's resignation and argued at a January 2011 press conference that the presumptive loss was a computational error and that actual loss to the exchequer was zero. That argument had a real technical core: spectrum had been priced low as a matter of stated policy to spread tele-density, and a notional figure built on later auction prices was contestable. The defence failed politically for a reason the citizen-renegotiation reading makes visible. A presumptive-loss figure could be disputed as arithmetic, but the process facts underneath it, licences advanced in a queue, cut-off dates moved, and the same week's publication of the Niira Radia tapes, could not. The tapes, transcripts of intercepted 2008 and 2009 telephone conversations involving the lobbyist Niira Radia, journalists and corporate figures, were published by Outlook and Open magazines in November 2010 and placed the spectrum story inside a wider account of access, brokerage and influence at the summit of the state.

The season also produced an institutional embarrassment that no audit had to manufacture. In September 2010 the government appointed P. J. Thomas as Central Vigilance Commissioner, the head of the state's apex integrity body, although a charge sheet in the Kerala palmolein import case stood pending against him; the selection committee's own record carried Sushma Swaraj's dissent as Leader of the Opposition. On 3 March 2011, five weeks before the Jantar Mantar fast, the Supreme Court quashed the appointment, holding that the committee had failed to consider the pending case against the man chosen to police corruption in the administration. The sequence belongs to Feature One because it demonstrated, from within the state's own highest court, the vacancy at the centre of the anti-corruption architecture: the vigilance commissioner appointed under a cloud, the ombudsman unborn since 1968, the investigative agency administered by the department it might have to investigate. The Jan Lokpal draft's more extreme clauses read differently against that vacancy than against a working system, which is why the movement's audience kept growing through a spring in which the government won several of the arguments and lost the season.

The Commonwealth Games, held in Delhi from 3 to 14 October 2010 under an organising committee chaired by Suresh Kalmadi, supplied the second count. The Shunglu Committee, appointed by Prime Minister Manmohan Singh on 25 October 2010, and a separate Comptroller and Auditor General examination documented inflated contracts, delayed works and governance failures across the Games' preparation; Kalmadi was arrested by the Central Bureau of Investigation in April 2011. The coal block allocations, made through a screening-committee route between 2004 and 2009, supplied the third count on a slower fuse: the Comptroller and Auditor General's report on coal, tabled on 17 August 2012 with a presumptive gain figure of Rs 1.86 lakh crore for private allottees, extended the dossier after the movement's peak, and the delay itself became part of the story, since the file had been accumulating in plain sight while the first two scandals burned.

A fourth count ran through a state government and showed the dossier was not a Delhi monopoly. The Adarsh Housing Society scandal in Mumbai, a tower built on defence land in Colaba and allotted to officials, politicians and officers connected with its clearances, broke in October 2010 and forced the resignation of Maharashtra Chief Minister Ashok Chavan on 9 November 2010, a week before the 2G report was tabled. Hazare's Maharashtra had therefore already produced a chief ministerial resignation on a corruption charge before the national movement began, a sequence his supporters cited to argue that the demand of 2011 came from a decade of documented cases rather than a season of television, and his critics cited to argue the opposite, that Maharashtra's own anti-corruption politics, Hazare's included, had changed governments without changing the system the governments ran.

Parliament registered the pressure before any fast began. The winter session of 2010 was washed out by an opposition demand for a Joint Parliamentary Committee into the 2G allocation, a demand the government resisted through the session and conceded in February 2011. The concession matters for the reading defended here: by the time Anna Hazare announced his fast, the state's own institutions had already admitted, through the auditor's reports and the parliamentary committee, that the dossier was genuine. What they had not produced was a remedy with a date on it. UPA-2 had survived an earlier domestic crisis over a signature policy, [the 2008 nuclear deal's domestic crisis](/2020/10/18/2008-nuclear-deal-domestic-crisis/), by winning a trust vote; the corruption dossier was a different kind of challenge, because it could not be answered by arithmetic in the Lok Sabha. It required an institution, and the institution on the shelf, the Lokpal, had been pending since the first Lokpal Bill of 1968. Into that gap between an admitted dossier and an absent remedy stepped a seventy-three-year-old activist from Ralegan Siddhi whose own record of village-level transformation and earlier Maharashtra campaigns gave the demand a face that the opposition parties, compromised by their own state governments, could not supply.

The dossier's judicial count arrived while the movement was forming. The Supreme Court, monitoring the Central Bureau of Investigation's 2G inquiry through the winter of 2010 and 2011, asked on the record why action had not followed Subramanian Swamy's complaint to the Prime Minister, and on 2 February 2012 it cancelled all 122 licences issued in 2008, holding the allocation unconstitutional and arbitrary. The judgment postdates the movement's peak, yet it belongs to Feature One's logic: the presumptive-loss figure that Sibal had disputed as arithmetic was joined, in the Court's holding, by a finding that the process itself had been lawless, which is the finding the campaign had asserted from the street. Manmohan Singh's stated position ran on a separate track throughout. The Prime Minister maintained that coalition constraints and the stated policy of affordable telephony had governed the allocation, that he had acted on the material before him, and that he was prepared to appear before Parliament's Public Accounts Committee to say so. The citizen-renegotiation reading does not require a villain in this feature; it requires a documented gap between a dossier the state's own auditor and courts kept enlarging and a remedy the state's legislature kept deferring, and that gap, between November 2010 and April 2011, is a matter of dates.

## Feature Two: Five Days at Jantar Mantar and the Joint Drafting Committee

India Against Corruption, the platform that carried the campaign, took shape through 2010 as a loose coordination of activists, lawyers and former officials, and it street-tested its cause on 30 January 2011 with marches in Delhi and other cities against corruption and for a Jan Lokpal law. The drafting core had already done the legislative work: a Jan Lokpal Bill, prepared by a group that included the former law minister Shanti Bhushan, his son the advocate Prashant Bhushan, the former Supreme Court judge and Karnataka Lokayukta N. Santosh Hegde and Arvind Kejriwal, proposed an ombudsman with jurisdiction over the Prime Minister, ministers, members of Parliament and officials of every grade, with the Central Bureau of Investigation's anti-corruption wing folded into it, a citizens' charter backed by penalties, and Lokayuktas in the states created by the same statute. In March 2011 Hazare wrote to Prime Minister Manmohan Singh proposing a joint committee of officials and citizens to finalise the bill. The reply he received offered discussion without the committee's form: the government had constituted a Group of Ministers on corruption in January 2011 under A. K. Antony and pointed Hazare toward that channel, while the Prime Minister's letter of March 2011 held out talks on the bill without conceding either joint drafting or equal numbers. Hazare read the channel as a corridor to delay, since the Antony group's existence had not moved the Lokpal file in the two months since its constitution, and he announced an indefinite fast at Jantar Mantar from 5 April 2011.

The fast ran from 5 to 9 April 2011, ninety-six hours by the movement's own count, and it worked because it was built like a negotiation rather than a spectacle. Jantar Mantar, Delhi's designated protest ground near Parliament, filled with supporters whose composition Vinay Sitapati mapped in the Economic and Political Weekly of 23 July 2011: Gandhian activists who conferred legitimacy, urban professionals who brought energy and money, and legal activists who could walk a draft bill through a committee room. Candlelight vigils and solidarity fasts ran in other cities. The government opened talks within two days, with Kapil Sibal among the ministers meeting the campaign's intermediaries, and the sticking point narrowed to form: Hazare had demanded a committee chaired from the citizens' side; the settlement gave the chair to Finance Minister Pranab Mukherjee with Shanti Bhushan as co-chair, a compromise both sides could defend.

The Gazette notification of 8 April 2011, issued by the Ministry of Law and Justice, is the first feature's documentary residue and the movement's first institutional conquest. It constituted a Joint Drafting Committee of five ministers, Pranab Mukherjee, P. Chidambaram, M. Veerappa Moily, Kapil Sibal and Salman Khurshid, and five nominees of Anna Hazare: Hazare himself, Justice N. Santosh Hegde, Shanti Bhushan, Prashant Bhushan and Arvind Kejriwal, with Moily as convenor, and it directed the committee to complete its work by 30 June 2011. No Indian protest movement had previously been written into the state's own drafting machinery by gazette notification. Hazare ended the fast on the morning of 9 April 2011, with the government's commitment that a Lokpal Bill would be introduced in the monsoon session, and he attached a deadline of his own: passage by 15 August 2011, or the fast would resume. The renegotiation of April was therefore real but conditional, a place at the drafting table purchased with four days of fasting and repayable, if the table failed, in a second round.

The April fast also assembled the movement's public form, and the form deserves a paragraph of its own because it became the movement's signature. Supporters wore a white Gandhi cap printed with the words I am Anna, a device that multiplied the fasting man into every wearer and made the crowd legible to television as a single character repeated. Hindi film actors, yoga teachers and retired officials visited the pandal; the Art of Living network and other civic associations sent volunteers; schoolchildren were brought to see the old man who was not eating. The government's negotiators understood the arithmetic quickly enough: each day of the fast enlarged the audience that would judge the settlement, and the settlement's documentary form, a gazette notification rather than a press statement, was chosen because the audience had been promised a draft bill, not an assurance. When Hazare ended the fast on 9 April, the movement had demonstrated the full cycle in miniature, grievance, deadline, fast, document, and the summer's question was whether the cycle could survive contact with the document it had won.

## The Jan Lokpal Draft: A Citizens' Bill Read in Full

Because the summer's war was fought over clauses, the clauses deserve a reading of their own. The Jan Lokpal draft that the citizens' members carried into the committee proposed a Lokpal at the centre and Lokayuktas in every state under one statute, the state bodies created by the central law rather than left to state legislatures. The Lokpal itself was designed as a multi-member body whose chairperson and members would be chosen by a selection committee broader than the executive: the Prime Minister, the Leader of the Opposition, the Chief Justice of India and other senior judges, the Chief Election Commissioner and the Comptroller and Auditor General figured in the draft's selection architecture, with a search committee drawn from former holders of constitutional office screening names in public. Removal was placed behind a Supreme Court inquiry on a reference triggered by a petition of one hundred members of Parliament or a citizens' complaint screened by the Court, a design meant to make the ombudsman independent of the government of the day in tenure as well as appointment.

Jurisdiction was the draft's revolution and its controversy. The Prime Minister was covered, with the draft's later versions adding safeguards for the office; ministers and members of Parliament were covered for conduct outside their speech and vote on the floor, which the Constitution's Article 105 protects; the judiciary was covered in the early versions and then routed toward the judicial accountability legislation in the version the campaign defended in August; and officials of every grade, from the cabinet secretary to the village-level functionary whose delays filled citizens' lives, were brought inside one investigative scheme. The Central Bureau of Investigation's anti-corruption wing was to be merged into the Lokpal, ending the arrangement under which the agency that investigated ministers answered to a minister. Time limits ran through the text like a spine: preliminary inquiry in months, investigation within a year, trial in special courts within the following year, and dismissal as the presumptive penalty for a proved charge, with recovery of the loss caused to the exchequer from the convicted official's assets.

Two chapters carried the draft beyond an ombudsman for high office into the citizen's daily dealing with the state, and they explain the breadth of the April crowds better than the 2G figure alone. A citizens' charter chapter required every public authority to publish the services it owed, the officer responsible and the time within which the service would be delivered, with a monetary penalty on the responsible officer, recoverable from salary, where the deadline passed without cause, the penalty designed to convert the petty bribe, the payment for a ration card, a passport, a land record, from a private transaction into a documented default. A whistleblower chapter extended protection to officials and citizens who exposed corruption, answering the murders of whistleblowers that the information campaign had already counted, among them Satyendra Dubey, the engineer killed in 2003 after writing to the Prime Minister's Office about corruption in the national highway programme. The draft's critics, Roy and the National Campaign among them, aimed at exactly this comprehensiveness: a single body investigating the Prime Minister, disciplining the village clerk, supervising the police's investigative wing and fining defaulting officers was, in their argument, a parallel state with a saint's mandate and no electorate. The draft's defenders answered that each power answered a documented failure, the unprosecuted minister, the unpunished clerk, the captured agency, and that accountability for the Lokpal itself was supplied by the selection breadth, the removal inquiry and the draft's transparency duties. The drafting war of the summer was the collision of those two complete designs, and it could not be compromised clause by clause, because each side's clauses were load-bearing.

While the committee argued, the state-level version of the office staged a demonstration of what an ombudsman with jurisdiction and nerve could do. Justice N. Santosh Hegde, a member of the citizens' drafting team, was simultaneously serving as Karnataka's Lokayukta, and his final report on illegal mining in the state, submitted on 27 July 2011, indicted the sitting chief minister, B. S. Yeddyurappa of the Bharatiya Janata Party, in a mining lease scandal. Yeddyurappa resigned on 31 July 2011. The sequence cut across every partisan script of that summer: a member of Team Anna, wielding an existing Lokayukta's powers, had brought down a chief minister of the party that was supporting the movement in Delhi against a Congress government that was resisting the Lokpal. Hegde's report became the movement's favourite exhibit in the drafting war's public argument, proof offered in real time that the ombudsman design was neither utopian nor anti-politician in general, only dangerous to whoever held allocative power over mines, spectrum and land. It also quietly raised the design question the Act of 2013 would answer weakly: Hegde's Karnataka office rested on a state statute of 1984 with its own investigative wing, and the Jan Lokpal draft's insistence on investigative machinery under the ombudsman's own command was written by people who had watched that machinery work.

## The Drafting War of the Summer of 2011

The Joint Drafting Committee met nine times between 16 April and 21 June 2011, and the meetings failed on substance rather than procedure. The citizens' side held to the Jan Lokpal architecture: the Prime Minister within jurisdiction, the judiciary reachable through the same law, the Central Bureau of Investigation's anti-corruption wing merged into the Lokpal, officials of all grades covered, and a single statute creating state Lokayuktas. The ministers' side resisted each element in turn, offering a Prime Minister reachable only after leaving office, a judiciary left to a separate accountability bill then in preparation, an investigative agency kept under executive control, coverage concentrated on senior officials, and Lokayuktas left to state legislatures. By the final meetings the committee was producing two drafts rather than one, and the citizens' members recorded their dissent in a letter to the chair rather than a shared text.

### What did the government's Lokpal Bill of August 2011 propose?

The bill introduced in the Lok Sabha on 4 August 2011 kept the Prime Minister outside the Lokpal's jurisdiction, left the judiciary to separate legislation, kept the Central Bureau of Investigation under the government, covered senior officials while routing lower grades elsewhere, and left Lokayuktas to the states, a design the campaign promptly named the Jokepal.

The summer also tested the movement's boundaries. Baba Ramdev's separate fast at Ramlila Maidan from 4 June 2011, aimed at black money held abroad, ended in a midnight police eviction on the night of 4 and 5 June that the Supreme Court later held unlawful, and the episode showed the government rehearsing a harder response while the Hazare campaign, which had kept its distance from Ramdev's platform, watched the rehearsal. The ministers' public argument hardened in parallel. Kapil Sibal stated the parliamentary-supremacy case in its strongest form: laws in a constitutional democracy are made by elected legislatures through deliberation, and a drafting committee conceded under pressure could advise that process but could not replace it. Manish Tewari, speaking for the Congress, called the campaign's members unelected and unaccountable, a charge the campaign answered by pointing to the gazette notification the government itself had signed. Congress general secretary Digvijaya Singh alleged repeatedly that the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh stood behind the campaign, citing statements by Sangh-linked figures; Hazare rejected the allegation, and the Sangh's own statements welcomed an anti-corruption stir while denying that it was running one. The allegation and the denial both belong in the record, unresolved, because they framed the partisan contest over the movement's ownership that ran underneath the drafting contest.

The government sought political cover for its draft at an all-party meeting convened by the Prime Minister on 3 July 2011, and the meeting returned a divided brief: parties across the table endorsed a strong Lokpal in principle while reserving their positions on the Prime Minister's inclusion, the judiciary and the single-statute design for the states, leaving the ministers to draft alone with a mandate for strength and no agreement on content. The citizens' members read the meeting as confirmation that the political class would accept an ombudsman precisely to the extent that the ombudsman could not reach the political class, and the government's drafters read the same meeting as proof that no text could satisfy both Parliament and the street. Both readings were honest accounts of the same afternoon, and the six weeks between the meeting and the bill's introduction on 4 August were consumed by the drafting of a text that tried to be defensible in the House first and persuasive at Jantar Mantar second, an order of priorities that the August fast was designed to reverse.

A second citizens' position complicated the campaign's claim to speak for civil society as a whole. Aruna Roy, who had worked alongside Kejriwal in the information-rights campaign and led the National Campaign for People's Right to Information, refused the single-draft approach and argued for a basket of instruments instead: a Lokpal of measured powers, a judicial accountability law, a grievance redress statute, and whistleblower protection, each through the normal committee process. Her intervention matters to the adjudication later in this article, because it denied both combatants a monopoly: the government could not dismiss all drafting criticism as street pressure, and Team Anna could not claim that every alternative draft was a dilution. The Lok Sabha, meanwhile, sent the government's bill of 4 August 2011 to the Standing Committee on Personnel, Public Grievances, Law and Justice, chaired by Abhishek Manu Singhvi, within the week. Hazare read the referral as the burial he had predicted, and on the strength of his April warning he announced a second fast, at Ramlila Maidan, from 16 August 2011.

The committee's collapse had a subplot that damaged the citizens' side in public before the substance was ever argued. In April 2011 a compact disc surfaced purporting to record Shanti Bhushan, the committee's co-chair, in a conversation suggesting that a judge could be managed for a price. The Bhushans denied the conversation, produced forensic opinion calling the disc fabricated, and filed a police complaint; the government ordered its own forensic examination, whose reported findings differed from the family's, and the episode was never resolved to a common account. Its function in the sequence is documentary rather than evidentiary: the drafting war was fought over credibility as much as clauses, and each side accused the other of negotiating in bad faith, the ministers pointing to Hazare's public deadlines and threats as proof that the citizens sought a veto rather than a text, the citizens pointing to the ministers' dilution of every clause as proof that the committee had been conceded as theatre. When the monsoon session opened with the government's own bill rather than a joint text, the citizens' reading of the summer prevailed in the street, and the second fast was announced for the anniversary the calendar offered, the week of Independence Day, with Hazare demanding passage of a strong Lokpal by the end of August and warning that the fast this time would not be called off for a committee.

## Feature Three: Arrest, Tihar and the Twelve Days at Ramlila Maidan

The Delhi Police granted permission for the August fast at Jantar Mantar under conditions the campaign treated as a veto: a three-day limit, a capped gathering, and restrictions on the protest's form. Hazare refused the conditions, declared he would fast at JP Park and court arrest, and spent the night of 15 August 2011 at a flat in Mayur Vihar. On the morning of 16 August 2011, Delhi Police detained him under preventive provisions before the fast had begun, along with Kejriwal and other associates, and a magistrate remanded Hazare to seven days in Tihar Jail. The arrest converted a legislative dispute into a rights dispute within hours. Protests spread across Indian cities, Parliament adjourned in uproar, and by the evening of 16 August the government had ordered Hazare's release. He refused to leave Tihar until the fast was permitted at Ramlila Maidan without the original conditions, and for two days the state's most famous prisoner negotiated from his cell through intermediaries while crowds gathered outside the jail.

Hazare left Tihar on 19 August 2011, stopped at Rajghat, and took the fast to Ramlila Maidan, where it ran until 28 August. The ground carried its own citation: Jayaprakash Narayan had addressed his great rally there on 25 June 1975, hours before the Emergency's proclamation, and [the JP movement's total revolution](/2021/01/14/jp-movement-total-revolution/) supplied the comparison that supporters and sceptics alike reached for in 2011. The parallel was imperfect, as the later movement's own record shows, since the 2011 campaign sought a statute from a functioning Parliament rather than the resignation of a government, but the geography was deliberate and was read as deliberate by everyone who covered it.

The fast's audience was never confined to the ground. Solidarity protests ran through August in Mumbai, Bengaluru, Chennai, Hyderabad, Lucknow and dozens of smaller towns, resident welfare associations organised candle marches in Delhi's colonies, lawyers' bodies passed resolutions of support, and the Indian diaspora staged small demonstrations outside consulates, all of it feeding the negotiators' sense that the audience for the settlement was national and continuous rather than local and episodic. Parliament felt the same pressure in its own chamber, where the arrest was raised on the floor on 16 August itself and the government's spokesmen spent the day defending a detention that the government's own evening order then reversed. The reversal's speed is part of the feature's evidence: a state that had arrested a fasting man at dawn was, by night, asking him to accept release, and by the week's end was negotiating the venue of the fast it had tried to prevent. Whatever the movement's longer effects, August 2011 demonstrated a transfer of initiative so complete that the government's defenders, Sibal among them, spent the following months arguing that the transfer itself, rather than any clause of any bill, was the danger to be legislated against in the court of opinion.

### How large were the crowds at Ramlila Maidan?

No official count of the Ramlila Maidan gathering exists, and the figures in contention say as much about their holders as about the ground. Delhi Police estimates reported in the press ran to the tens of thousands at peak hours, organisers spoke of a lakh and more passing through across a day, and television framed the ground as a sea.

The negotiation ran on two tracks. One track was personal: Vilasrao Deshmukh, the former Maharashtra chief minister, carried messages between the Prime Minister and the fasting Hazare, and a letter from Manmohan Singh on 23 August 2011 appealed to Hazare to end the fast and offered a parliamentary debate on all the drafts in circulation, the government's, the Jan Lokpal text and the National Campaign version. The second track was procedural: an all-party meeting on 24 August 2011 produced a unanimous appeal to Hazare without conceding the bill, and Hazare answered through Deshmukh with three conditions for ending the fast, a citizens' charter with deadlines for public services, the lower bureaucracy brought under the Lokpal through an appropriate mechanism, and Lokayuktas established in the states. On 27 August 2011 both Houses debated the question in a special sitting. Pranab Mukherjee, opening for the government, warned that a distinction must hold between mobocracy and democracy and that the Constitution's procedures were the protection of the people's will rather than its obstacle. Sushma Swaraj, leading the Bharatiya Janata Party in the Lok Sabha, declared her party's broad agreement with Hazare's three conditions. Arun Jaitley, in the Rajya Sabha, defended parliamentary supremacy in law-making while conceding that the campaign had exposed a legislature that had left the Lokpal unenacted for over four decades. Sitaram Yechury of the Communist Party of India (Marxist) admitted that the crowds expressed a disgust at scam after scam that no party could wave away.

Both Houses then adopted, by voice vote, the sense-of-the-House resolution agreeing in principle to the three conditions and transmitting the debate to the Standing Committee. A copy of the resolution travelled to Ramlila Maidan, and on the morning of 28 August 2011 Hazare ended a fast that had run 288 hours from its Tihar beginning. The resolution was the movement's second institutional conquest and its most ambiguous one. It was not a statute, not a commitment to the Jan Lokpal text, and not enforceable; it was Parliament placing three principles on its own record under street pressure, which is either a modest thing or an extraordinary one depending on whether the standard is legislative output or constitutional practice. The citizen-renegotiation reading treats it as the second renegotiation: the terms on which a bill would be drafted had been publicly re-specified by the legislature itself, in session, in response to citizens assembled outside it.

Two mechanics of the Ramlila days repay attention because they became standard in the movement's later rounds. The first was mediation through Maharashtra. Bhaiyyuji Maharaj, a spiritual figure from Indore with access to both camps, carried proposals during the Tihar standoff, and Vilasrao Deshmukh's channel to the Prime Minister ran in parallel, which meant the fasting man's home state supplied the negotiation's trusted intermediaries while Delhi supplied its theatre. The second was the medical bulletin. A panel of doctors examined Hazare daily, the campaign released his weight and vital signs to the press, and the government's anxiety was calibrated in public to a falling number: by the fast's eleventh day he had lost over seven kilograms on the campaign's own reporting, and the risk that a seventy-four-year-old would collapse or die on live television disciplined the government's timetable more effectively than any slogan. The all-party meeting, the Prime Minister's letter and the special Saturday sitting of both Houses all landed in the fast's final four days, after the bulletins had made delay the most expensive option on the table. Parliament's resolution was therefore a negotiated instrument in the strict sense, drafted against a deadline set by a body, and the citizen-renegotiation reading counts that discipline, the conversion of one man's physiology into the legislature's timetable, as the movement's most original and most troubling invention, the point at which Roy's warning about coercion and Mukherjee's warning about mobocracy both found their evidence.

## The Long Parliamentary Winter and the Movement's Ebb

The Standing Committee's report, tabled on 9 December 2011, split the differences that the drafting committee had left open: the Prime Minister was brought within the Lokpal's reach under safeguards, the lower bureaucracy was routed through state-level and vigilance mechanisms rather than the central ombudsman's direct hand, the citizens' charter was hived off to a separate grievance redress bill, and the judiciary stayed outside. Hazare held a day-long protest at Jantar Mantar on 11 December 2011, sharing a platform with leaders of the Bharatiya Janata Party, the Left and other opposition parties in a rare common front, and served notice of a Mumbai fast to coincide with the winter session. The government introduced a revised Lokpal and Lokayuktas Bill in the Lok Sabha on 22 December 2011, and the House passed it late on 27 December 2011, on the same night that the companion constitutional amendment, which would have given the Lokpal constitutional status, fell short of the required two-thirds majority. In the Rajya Sabha on 29 December 2011, debate ran to midnight against a pile of moved amendments numbering over 180, and the House adjourned without a vote. The opposition called the adjournment an orchestrated escape; the government called it unfinished business. Both descriptions were compatible with the outcome, which was no statute.

The movement's own seams had shown during the autumn in two episodes that its chroniclers record without flinching. On 12 October 2011 Prashant Bhushan was assaulted in his Supreme Court chamber by young men who cited his remarks on Kashmir, where he had spoken of a referendum in the context of demilitarisation, and the campaign's response exposed its coalition's limits: Hazare publicly distanced the movement from the remark and considered whether the core committee itself should be dissolved, a step he announced and then withdrew within the month after a meeting at Ralegan Siddhi. The episode sits awkwardly in every reading. The movement's claim to speak for the citizen in general kept colliding with the views of its particular citizens, on Kashmir, on reservation, on the economy, and its method of handling the collisions, distance, silence, or the leader's personal ruling, anticipated the authority question that the party project would inherit in 2012 and again, inside the party, in 2015.

Hazare's Mumbai fast, held at the MMRDA ground in the Bandra Kurla Complex from 27 December 2011, ended on its second afternoon on medical advice, before a crowd a fraction of Ramlila's size, and it marked the movement's ebb in public. The causes were visible in the record rather than in any single failure: the demand had narrowed to clauses in a stalled bill, the fasting instrument had been used three times in a year with diminishing returns, and the campaign's electoral foray against the Congress in the February 2012 Uttar Pradesh assembly campaign produced no measurable verdict against the ruling party. A final campaign fast at Jantar Mantar, begun on 25 July 2012 by Kejriwal, Manish Sisodia and Gopal Rai and joined by Hazare on 29 July, closed on 3 August 2012 with the announcement that the movement would offer a political alternative. The announcement conceded the analytical point that the next section turns on: street renegotiation had twice extracted documents from the state, and twice the documents had stalled in the state's own machinery, so the renegotiation would have to be attempted from inside an elected house.

The autumn had already shown the campaign learning electoral arithmetic before the party question was settled. In the Hisar Lok Sabha by-election of October 2011, Team Anna campaigned explicitly against the Congress candidate on the ground that the ruling party had stalled the Jan Lokpal, and the Congress candidate finished third and forfeited his deposit in a seat the party had held. The result was claimed by the campaign as proof that the movement could move votes and discounted by the Congress as a by-election's local weather, and both readings fed the argument that split the movement eleven months later: Kejriwal's camp cited Hisar as evidence that voters were the movement's missing instrument, while Hazare's camp held that a campaign against one party in one seat was already a compromise of the andolan's standing above parties. The Standing Committee's hearings through the autumn of 2011 ran underneath the electoral skirmishing, taking evidence from the campaign, from the National Campaign for People's Right to Information and from the ministries, and the committee's December report, with its partial concessions and its separate treatment of the citizens' charter, satisfied none of the witnesses. The winter session's failure then completed the estrangement: by January 2012 the movement no longer believed the legislature would move without a different kind of pressure, and the legislature no longer believed the movement would accept any bill it could pass. The July 2012 fast and the August announcement were the formal recognition of that double disbelief.

## Feature Four: The Split of 19 September 2012 and the Founding of a Party

The political-alternative announcement forced the question the movement had deferred since April 2011: whether a campaign against the political class could enter elections without becoming what it opposed. Kejriwal's case, stated repeatedly through August and September 2012, ran in institutional terms. Fasts had produced a committee and a resolution; neither had produced a law; legislators answered to their parties rather than to the crowds outside; therefore the movement's own people had to contest, win seats, and carry the Jan Lokpal into an assembly themselves. He supported the argument with a survey the campaign ran through its own channels, whose published count reported that a large majority of respondents favoured forming a party, a figure that was the organisers' own tally and was treated as such by sceptics. Hazare's case ran in terms of the movement's moral form. An andolan, he held, derived its authority from standing outside the contest for office; a party would trade that authority for the compromises of candidature, funds and alliances; and his name and image were not to be used by any party, a stipulation he repeated whenever the question was put to him.

The principals stated their positions to each other formally on 19 September 2012 at the Constitution Club in New Delhi, where Hazare announced the separation. The division of the movement's assets followed the division of its convictions. Prashant Bhushan and Shanti Bhushan went with Kejriwal into the party project. Kiran Bedi, the former police officer who had been the campaign's most recognisable face after Hazare himself, held that the movement should remain non-political and stayed outside the party. Justice Santosh Hegde recorded his reservations about the electoral turn and kept his distance. The split was therefore not a quarrel over the Lokpal's clauses, on which the principals remained in agreement, but over the location of renegotiation itself: whether the citizen's leverage over the state was greatest outside the legislature's door, at it, or inside the chamber.

The separation's housekeeping was publicly untidy in a way that foreshadowed the party's later internal wars. The two camps disputed custody of the movement's name, its offices, its donor lists and its bank accounts through statements to the press through the autumn of 2012; Hazare wrote that the movement's name was being used for a politics he had not authorised, Kejriwal's camp replied that a movement belonged to its volunteers rather than to any single guardian, and the argument was never adjudicated anywhere except in the newspapers. The dispute is worth recording because it exposed a genuine constitutional question inside the movement, the same question the Jan Lokpal draft posed for the state: in whom does authority vest between elections, and by what procedure may it be reclaimed? The movement answered the state's version of the question with a draft bill. It never answered its own version, and the unanswered question followed the party through the expulsions of 2015 and beyond, a reminder that renegotiation is easier to demand than to institutionalise at home.

Kejriwal announced the party project publicly on 2 October 2012, Gandhi's birth anniversary, and the name Aam Aadmi Party, the Common Man's Party, was adopted at the party's national council meeting on 24 November 2012. The formal launch followed on 26 November 2012, Constitution Day, at the Constitution Club, with a written constitution, an internal Lokpal for party disputes, and Kejriwal as national convenor. The party's programme carried the movement's documents into electoral form: the Jan Lokpal as its first legislative promise, and, as its theory of rule, the argument of Kejriwal's book Swaraj, published in 2012, which held that power in India had been captured by a small secretariat class and had to be returned to gram sabhas in villages and mohalla sabhas in city neighbourhoods, assemblies of residents deciding local spending and holding local officials to account. The Election Commission allotted the party the broom as its symbol in 2013, an emblem chosen to say that the new party existed to sweep a dirty system clean. Hazare, for his part, reconstituted India Against Corruption under his own leadership in November 2012 and continued to insist that the movement and the party were separate things, a position he restated whenever the party used the movement's history in its campaigns. The fourth feature thus closes with the renegotiation changing instruments: the fast had been a way of addressing the state from outside; the party was a bid to hold a piece of the state and address the rest from within.

The party's pre-launch months showed the instrument being assembled in public. Through October and November 2012 Kejriwal held a series of press conferences levelling documented allegations against figures across the political spectrum: against Robert Vadra, the Congress president's son-in-law, over land transactions in Haryana; against the external affairs minister Salman Khurshid's family trust over disability aid in Uttar Pradesh; and against Bharatiya Janata Party president Nitin Gadkari over irrigation-linked land holdings in Maharashtra. Each allegation was answered by its target with denials and legal threats, none produced a prosecution in that season, and the sequence's function was positional rather than judicial: it announced that the new party would prosecute the whole political class in the court of publicity, sparing neither the government the movement had fought nor the opposition that had shared its platforms. Hazare watched the conferences from Ralegan Siddhi and repeated that the movement he led had made no such allegations and would contest no elections, so that by the launch on 26 November 2012 the Indian public was being asked to hold two related but distinct things in view at once, an andolan that disowned the party and a party that claimed the andolan's inheritance, and the voters of Delhi were the first jury asked to separate them.

## Delhi 2013: Twenty-Eight Seats and a Forty-Nine Day Government

The Aam Aadmi Party contested the Delhi assembly election of 4 December 2013 as a thirteen-month-old organisation against a Congress government that had held the capital for fifteen years and a Bharatiya Janata Party confident of the anti-incumbency. Its campaign ran on the movement's transferred assets: the Jan Lokpal as the headline promise, mohalla sabha consultations written into its manifesto process, and a candidate list built to advertise its difference from the established parties.

### How did AAP choose its candidates for the 2013 Delhi election?

The party published application forms, shortlisted applicants through a screening committee, ran background checks on criminal records and assets, posted shortlisted names for public objection from constituency residents, and rejected applicants against whom credible objections survived scrutiny, a procedure designed to make the ticket itself evidence for the party's founding claim about clean politics.

The campaign's final weeks supplied tests of the ticket's advertising. The party published separate manifestos for Delhi's constituencies alongside its city manifesto, an organisational labour no established party attempted, and the Bharatiya Janata Party answered the movement's moral framing by projecting Harsh Vardhan, a former Delhi health minister with a clean public record, as its chief ministerial face in October 2013. In November 2013 a sting recording circulated by a news portal purported to show several Aam Aadmi Party candidates agreeing to raise funds and settle disputes outside the law; the party called the footage doctored, filed complaints and asked the Election Commission and the courts to judge the raw tapes, and the episode closed without any candidate's nomination falling. The sting and the manifestos belong in the same paragraph because they were the campaign's argument and counter-argument in miniature: the party asked to be judged by its procedures, and its opponents answered that procedures filmed badly were merely procedures.

The results declared on 8 December 2013 produced a hung assembly and the movement's most compressed renegotiation. The Bharatiya Janata Party won 31 seats, the Aam Aadmi Party 28, the Congress 8, with the remaining three scattered. In the New Delhi constituency, Kejriwal defeated the three-term chief minister Sheila Dikshit by 25,864 votes, taking 44,269 votes to her 18,405. The Bharatiya Janata Party, short of a majority, declined to attempt a government, its legislature party resolving to sit in opposition rather than assemble the numbers by inducement, a refusal its leader Harsh Vardhan stated publicly within days of the result. Lieutenant Governor Najeeb Jung then invited Kejriwal, as leader of the second largest party, to explore a government, and Kejriwal asked for ten days to consult the party's volunteers and the city's voters, an interval his critics called indecision and his movement called the point of the exercise. The consultation ran through public meetings in the constituencies, a dedicated telephone line and message counts, and the party's published tally reported a clear majority of respondents in favour of accepting Congress support to form a government pledged to the eighteen-point programme it had placed before both national parties, on which the Congress had replied in writing while the Bharatiya Janata Party had not. Kejriwal announced acceptance on 23 December 2013, and the Congress conveyed its outside support through a letter from its Delhi leadership to the Lieutenant Governor. Kejriwal was sworn in on 28 December 2013 at Ramlila Maidan, the fast's ground turned into a swearing-in venue, at the head of a minority government dependent on the party the movement had spent two years prosecuting in public.

The forty-nine days that followed read in the record as the movement's programme attempted at administrative speed: a lifeline of free water up to twenty kilolitres a month for metered households, a halving of electricity tariffs for consumption up to four hundred units, a Comptroller and Auditor General audit of the three private distribution companies ordered over their resistance, and an anti-corruption helpline relaunched. The government also approved its Delhi Jan Lokpal Bill in cabinet on 3 February 2014 and sought to introduce it in the assembly session that opened on 13 February 2014. The Lieutenant Governor held that the bill, bearing on the capital's special constitutional position, required the Union government's prior recommendation under the Transaction of Business Rules before introduction; Kejriwal treated the requirement as the old veto in new dress and pressed the introduction regardless. On 14 February 2014 the assembly voted 42 to 27 against granting leave to introduce the bill, with Congress and Bharatiya Janata Party members voting the same way for differently stated reasons, and Kejriwal resigned that evening, recommending the assembly's dissolution. The resignation completed a symmetry the movement could hardly have scripted: the party born of a bill fell on the bill, in the capital, within seven weeks of taking office.

The forty-nine days also recorded the movement's governing style under stress, in episodes its critics cite as faithfully as its defenders cite the water and power decisions. Kejriwal publicly urged citizens to record officials demanding bribes on their telephones and send the recordings to the government, an instruction his opponents called an invitation to vigilantism and his supporters called the helpline's logic extended. Law Minister Somnath Bharti led a midnight operation in Khirki Extension in January 2014 against African nationals whom residents had accused of drug and sex offences, an episode the Delhi Police declined to execute as directed, the National Human Rights Commission later examined, and critics across parties held up as the movement's moralism curdling into majoritarian street power; the party defended Bharti as acting on residents' complaints against a genuine menace. Kejriwal himself sat on a dharna near Rail Bhavan on 20 and 21 January 2014, demanding action against police officers after a gang rape in the capital, and called the protest off after the Lieutenant Governor agreed that two officers would go on leave pending inquiry, a settlement his opponents read as theatre and his movement read as the first time a chief minister had picketed his own police. The episodes belong in the record because they complicate the citizen-renegotiation reading in a useful direction: renegotiation from inside office carried the movement's impatience with procedure into the state's own conduct, and Delhi spent the following year arguing about which half of the inheritance, the accountability instruments or the procedural impatience, the forty-nine days had really demonstrated.

## Feature Five, Part One: The Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act as Institutional Residue

The statute arrived through the same December, on a track that ran through Hazare's own renewed pressure. The bill had been reintroduced in the Rajya Sabha in May 2012 and examined by a select committee whose report came in November 2013. Hazare began an indefinite fast at Ralegan Siddhi on 10 December 2013, demanding the bill's passage in the winter session, and the session delivered. The Rajya Sabha passed the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Bill on 17 December 2013, and the Lok Sabha passed it with the upper house's amendments on 18 December 2013. Hazare ended his fast at Ralegan Siddhi on 18 December, calling the enacted law weaker than the Jan Lokpal draft yet a gain worth taking, while Kejriwal's party stated its verdict in the opposite register and called the Act a diluted instrument that left the investigative agencies where the movement had found them. President Pranab Mukherjee gave assent on 1 January 2014, and the Act, the first of 2014, commenced on 16 January 2014.

The Act's passage also closed a parallel judicial argument that had run through 2013 about the investigative agency at the system's centre. In May 2013, hearing the coal allocation case, a Supreme Court bench led by Justice R. M. Lodha described the Central Bureau of Investigation as a caged parrot speaking in its master's voice, after it emerged that the agency's draft status report had been shared with the law minister and officials of the government it was investigating; Law Minister Ashwani Kumar resigned on 10 May 2013. The phrase entered the Lokpal debate immediately, because the Jan Lokpal draft's demand to free the agency's anti-corruption wing from executive control, dismissed in 2011 as maximalist, had been restated by the Court in a metaphor no ministry could answer. The Act that passed in December nonetheless left the agency's structure largely where it stood, a compromise the movement's strategist cited in calling the statute diluted and the statute's defenders cited in calling it workable.

The enacted architecture is the fifth feature's content. The Lokpal is a chairperson and up to eight members, half of them judicial, chosen by a selection committee of the Prime Minister, the Speaker of the Lok Sabha, the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha, the Chief Justice of India or a nominated judge, and an eminent jurist. Its jurisdiction reaches the Prime Minister, with the exclusions the drafting war had foreshadowed: allegations touching international relations, external and internal security, public order, atomic energy and space sit outside it, and a complaint against a Prime Minister requires a full bench and a two-thirds majority to proceed, heard in camera. Ministers, members of Parliament and officials of all grades fall within the Act's scheme, with preliminary inquiry and investigation routed through the Central Vigilance Commission and the Central Bureau of Investigation under the Lokpal's direction in referred cases. A citizens' charter chapter obliges public authorities to publish their service commitments, and every state is directed to establish a Lokayukta within 365 days of commencement. The residue then displayed the gap between enactment and institution that the movement's own history predicted. The selection machinery stalled over the absence of a recognised Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha after 2014, a gap the government answered with an amendment process for the selection committee and a 2016 amendment on asset declarations, and the ombudsman's chair stayed empty through the Act's first five years. Hazare returned to his instrument twice in the interval. In March 2018 he fasted at Ramlila Maidan again, this time for the Lokpal's appointment and for farm demands, and ended the fast after a week on written assurances from the Union government conveyed through Maharashtra's chief minister. In January and February 2019 he fasted at Ralegan Siddhi on the same appointment question and ended that fast on 5 February 2019 after the Maharashtra government conveyed the centre's commitment to complete the selection. The selection committee met in March 2019, and Justice Pinaki Chandra Ghose, a retired Supreme Court judge then serving on the National Human Rights Commission, was named the country's first Lokpal on 19 March 2019 and sworn in by President Ram Nath Kovind on 23 March 2019, with four judicial and four non-judicial members appointed beside him. The appointment's timing, weeks after a fast by an eighty-one-year-old, was read by the movement's veterans as the template working one last time and by its critics as proof of the template's cost: an institution designed to stand above pressure had been staffed, at last, under it.

The December 2013 passage had a parliamentary texture that the speed of the session concealed. The Rajya Sabha's select committee had spent a year on the bill after its May 2012 introduction, and the upper house's debate on 17 December 2013 ran on an unusual cross-party consensus in which Arun Jaitley, leading the opposition in that house, argued for the bill's passage in the session while recording his party's reservations about its weaker clauses, and the Samajwadi Party, which had opposed the Lokpal design throughout on the ground that an unelected ombudsman would be turned against elected governments, dissented from the consensus in the debate. Hazare's fast at Ralegan Siddhi supplied the deadline discipline in the movement's established manner: he announced that the fast would end only with the bill's passage, his health bulletins ran on the news channels alongside the debate, and he broke the fast on 18 December with a public statement that the law, though short of the Jan Lokpal, had been won by the people's pressure and would be judged by the Lokpal it produced. The two principals of 2011 thus closed the legislative sequence from the positions the split had assigned them: the movement's founder outside the legislature with a fast, the movement's strategist inside a government with a resignation two months away, each still working the instrument he had chosen in September 2012.

## Feature Five, Part Two: From Four Seats to Two States

The party's post-2014 trajectory belongs to the brief's record and is stated here as that record carries it. In the general election of April and May 2014, the Aam Aadmi Party fielded over four hundred candidates and won four seats, all in Punjab, on roughly two percent of the national vote; Kejriwal, contesting Varanasi against Narendra Modi, lost by a margin of about 3.72 lakh votes. Read alone, the result suggested a movement that had mistaken a Delhi moment for a national one, and [the 2014 election inflection](/2019/07/03/2014-inflection-election-reanalysed/) stands in this series as the verdict that punished the overreach. Delhi then corrected the reading. In the fresh assembly election of 7 February 2015, the party won 67 of 70 seats on 54.3 percent of the vote, the Bharatiya Janata Party fell to three, and the Congress to none; Kejriwal took office again on 14 February 2015 at Ramlila Maidan, exactly one year after his resignation. The Delhi electorate confirmed the settlement on 8 February 2020, returning the party with 62 seats on about 53.6 percent of the vote against eight for the Bharatiya Janata Party. In Punjab on 10 March 2022, the party won 92 of 117 seats, and Bhagwant Mann was sworn in as chief minister on 16 March 2022, giving the movement's party charge of a full state a decade after the split that created it, a trajectory legible within [the Modi-era three-tension renegotiation](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/) as one of the era's durable counterweights. The 2022 Punjab result falls after this article's locked date and is included because the brief's evidence scope requires the trajectory to be carried to it.

The trajectory includes an internal rupture that the party-history reading must carry honestly. Within weeks of the February 2015 sweep, the party's founding tensions between Kejriwal's centralised campaign machine and the internal democracy promised in its constitution broke into public: Yogendra Yadav and Prashant Bhushan, two of the movement's most prominent faces, were removed from the party's political affairs committee in March 2015 and expelled in April 2015 after a national council meeting whose proceedings both camps described in irreconcilable terms, Yadav and Bhushan alleging a purge of dissent and the leadership alleging indiscipline and sabotage during the campaign. The episode matters to this article's argument because it re-ran, inside the party, the September 2012 argument about where authority in a movement may sit: the party created to renegotiate the citizen's terms with the state settled its own internal terms by the leader's arithmetic, and the expelled founders said so in exactly those words. The renegotiation reading survives the episode, but it is marked by it; the instrument built to carry citizens' pressure into institutions developed, in power, the same impatience with internal procedure that its opponents had always alleged against the fasts.

## The Movement and the Television Frame

No account of the five features is complete without the medium that carried them, because the renegotiation ran on a timetable set by twenty-four-hour news television. The channels broadcast the fasts continuously, placed their anchors at the pandals, ran the medical bulletins as breaking news and treated each negotiation round as a verdict, and the movement's strategists, Kejriwal foremost among them, managed the coverage as a primary theatre rather than a record of one. The government's counter-charge, stated by its spokesmen through 2011 and sharpened afterward, held that the coverage had converted a legislative question into a plebiscite conducted by camera, in which the complexity of a draft bill could not survive against the simplicity of a fasting face. Justice Markandey Katju, taking over as chairman of the Press Council of India in October 2011, gave the charge an institutional voice within weeks, criticising the electronic media's coverage of the movement and of public affairs generally as frivolous, divisive and bereft of the issues that affected the poor, and proposing a statutory regulator for the press, a proposal the press largely rejected.

The evenhanded record holds both halves. Television's frame demonstrably selected the movement's most telegenic elements, the cap, the slogans, the saintly faster, over its documentary core, the clauses, and Roy's critique drew much of its force from that selection. The same frame, however, carried the documentary core further than any pamphlet could have carried it: the words Jan Lokpal entered the national vocabulary through the coverage the critics deplored, and the government's own negotiators priced the coverage into every concession they made, as the choice of a gazette notification in April and a Saturday parliamentary sitting in August both attest. A renegotiation conducted in public requires a public medium, and the movement and the channels made each other in 2011, which is why the medium appears in this article as a condition of the five features rather than as their narrator.

## Three Readings of One Sequence

The anti-corruption reading has the strongest evidence at the sequence's start. Vinay Sitapati's dissection of the April fast in the Economic and Political Weekly of 23 July 2011 showed the movement working because three strands of the new middle classes, Gandhian moralists, globally exposed professionals and legal activists, each supplied what the others lacked, and what united the strands was a grievance against a state whose services they bought privately and whose corruption they met at every public counter. On this reading, the 2G figure and the Games contracts explain the crowds, and the Lokpal explains the demand. The reading is correct about the fuel. It struggles with the itinerary: anger alone does not explain why the movement's victories took the form of a gazette notification, a sense-of-the-House resolution and a select-committee report, the least combustible documents in Indian public life.

The party-history reading has the strongest evidence at the sequence's end. Pradeep K. Chhibber and Rahul Verma, in Ideology and Identity (2018), place the Aam Aadmi Party within a party system being reorganised around competing claims about the state's role, and Aakar Patel's columns on the post-2011 trajectory traced how the movement's moral capital was spent, saved and reinvested across a decade of elections. On this reading, 2011 matters because 2013, 2015, 2020 and 2022 followed from it, and the fasts were the founding myth of an unusually successful regional party. The reading is correct about the legacy. It struggles with the principals: Hazare's refusal of the party was as much a part of the sequence as Kejriwal's founding of it, and a party history cannot explain why the movement's statute arrived in December 2013 under pressure from the man who had rejected the party outright.

The citizen-renegotiation reading defended here holds what both rivals drop: the conversions. Each feature is a point at which citizens outside the state compelled the state to change the terms of dealing, and each conversion left a document that outlasted the crowd. The movement's international reception belongs to the record of its form. Time magazine's Person of the Year package for 2011, devoted to the protester as a global figure, counted the Delhi crowds among the year's defining street movements, and the comparison drawn in that season between Jantar Mantar, Tahrir Square and the Spanish indignados was made by the movement's admirers and its critics alike, the admirers to claim a democratic spring and the critics to warn that a legislature was being taught to answer squares rather than electors. The comparison flatters and misleads in equal measure, because the Delhi movement's distinctiveness was precisely its refusal of the square's usual demand: it asked not for a regime's fall but for a statute's passage, and it arrived carrying the statute's text. That pedantic, file-carrying quality, dismissed by Roy as the movement's fraudulence and celebrated by its volunteers as its seriousness, is the trait the citizen-renegotiation reading is built to explain.

The adjudication can be sharpened by putting four questions to all three readings in turn. Why did the explosion come in April 2011 rather than in 2009, when the allocation was already known? The anti-corruption reading answers best: the CAG report, the tapes and the Games converted a known allocation into a priced and televised dossier. Why did the movement's first victory take the form of a drafting committee rather than a resignation or an arrest? Only the renegotiation reading answers: the demand was a text, so the concession was a drafting table. Why did the movement split in September 2012 rather than dissolve? The party-history reading answers the party's half, and the renegotiation reading answers Hazare's: the partners disagreed about the location of leverage, not about the goal. Why did the statute pass in December 2013, in a session nobody had expected to deliver it? The renegotiation reading answers again: the legislature faced, simultaneously, a fasting founder at Ralegan Siddhi, a movement party taking office in Delhi on the bill's promise, and a general election five months away, and the three pressures were the movement's three instruments, fast, party and deadline, arriving together. A framing that answers two of the four questions is a partial truth; the reading that answers all four earns the sequence.

The map also marks the reading's debts to its neighbours in this series. The renegotiation frame at work here is kin to [the foreign-policy-as-domestic-politics framework](/2020/09/21/non-alignment-indian-domestic-politics/), which reads external decisions through domestic contests, and to [the basic structure doctrine](/2020/07/28/basic-structure-most-consequential-doctrine/), the series' standing example of a constitutional term renegotiated under pressure and then defended as settlement. One evidentiary warning closes the section. The movement's internal record, the correspondence among Hazare, Kejriwal, Kiran Bedi, Prashant Bhushan and Shanti Bhushan that produced the coordination of April 2011 and the rupture of September 2012, survives only partially, in the India Against Corruption archives and in private collections, and its thinness should discipline every reading, including this one. The craft of preserving such source material is a live concern for the reporters who covered the movement, and it is treated directly in the VaultBook account of [how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/), which follows the handling of sensitive documents from collection to archive.

## The Strongest Counter, Stated Fairly

The sharpest attack on the citizen-renegotiation reading came from inside the movement's own moment. Arundhati Roy, writing in The Hindu on 21 August 2011 under the title I'd Rather Not Be Anna, argued that the Jan Lokpal draft was a dangerous concentration of punitive power in an unaccountable body, that the campaign's funders and symbolism tied it to corporate and majoritarian interests, and that a movement chanting nationalist slogans behind a freshly minted saint was a coup in Gandhian dress rather than a democratic deepening. Anand Teltumbde and other Dalit critics pressed a companion charge: the movement's moral economy had nothing to say about caste, and its romance of a corruption-free order spoke for those who already held social power. Stated at full strength, the counter holds that the anti-corruption content was not the fuel of the sequence but its whole meaning, and that a reading organised around institutional conversion launders a politics that contemporaries had good reason to fear.

The counter lands on the draft, and the record concedes the point where the record must. The Jan Lokpal text of 2011 did propose sweeping jurisdiction with thin accountability for the ombudsman itself, and Parliament's refusal to enact it as drafted was a deliberative act, not merely an obstruction. What the counter does not dissolve is the mechanism the five features document. The movement's anti-corruption charge was real, and it operated, demonstrably, through renegotiation: the charge was converted into a drafting seat, the drafting failure into a parliamentary resolution, the resolution's weakness into a party, and the party's existence into the December 2013 endgame in which a fasting Hazare and a governing Kejriwal pressed the same legislature from opposite sides of its wall. A reading of mechanism is not a certificate of virtue, and the citizen-renegotiation frame claims the mechanism only. On the brief's named disagreement, the verdict of this article follows: the anti-corruption framing explains the energy, the party-history framing explains the inheritance, and the citizen-renegotiation framing explains the sequence, which is the thing the other two leave unexplained.

## What the Five Features Leave Standing

The residue can be audited without romance. A statute stands in force from 16 January 2014, with an ombudsman seated from 23 March 2019 and state Lokayuktas still uneven a decade after the Act directed their creation within a year. A party stands in government in Delhi across two full terms and in Punjab from March 2022, carrying into ordinary administration a programme, swaraj through mohalla sabhas, that began as a pamphlet written against the secretariat state. And a template stands available to later movements: draft the law yourselves, name the date, fast at the capital's designated ground, and make the legislature answer on the record. The farmer protests of 2020 and 2021, treated in the closing account of this pillar, ran that template at a scale the Anna Hazare movement never reached, against laws rather than for one, and the comparison between the two sequences is the pillar's final lesson in how citizens renegotiate when the formal channels stall: they build a document, a deadline and a crowd, and they refuse to leave the terms where they found them.

One further measure of the residue is the ombudsman's own docket since 2019, stated here in the qualitative terms its published reporting supports. The Lokpal's office received complaints running to the low thousands across its first years, the great bulk closed at the threshold for falling outside the Act's format or jurisdiction rather than after inquiry, and the institution's public footprint stayed small beside the movement-era imagination of it, a fact its defenders attribute to the deterrent value of a seated ombudsman and its critics to the design compromises of December 2013: no independent investigative wing of its own, dependence on the very agencies the movement had sought to free, and a selection process that had taken five years to convene. The argument reproduces, in miniature and in office, the argument of the drafting war, which suggests the correct closing judgment for the whole sequence. The Anna Hazare movement and the Aam Aadmi Party did not settle the terms between citizen and state; no single movement does. They renegotiated them, on the record, in five dated transactions whose documents survive, and they left the renegotiation open, as the farmer protests at Delhi's borders were demonstrating even as the sequence closed, for the next citizens willing to draft, to date and to fast.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: When did the Anna Hazare anti-corruption movement begin?

The movement's public beginning is conventionally dated to 5 April 2011, when Anna Hazare began his indefinite fast at Jantar Mantar in New Delhi demanding a Jan Lokpal law drafted jointly by citizens and government. Its build-up ran through 2010: India Against Corruption took shape as a coordinating platform that year, a citizens' draft of the Jan Lokpal Bill was prepared by a group including Shanti Bhushan, Prashant Bhushan, Justice Santosh Hegde and Arvind Kejriwal, and marches on 30 January 2011 tested the cause in Delhi and other cities. Hazare wrote to Prime Minister Manmohan Singh in March 2011 proposing a joint drafting committee, and announced the fast when the reply offered talks without the committee. The fast ended on 9 April 2011 with the Gazette notification of the Joint Drafting Committee. Hazare's earlier Maharashtra campaigns, against corrupt ministers in 2003 and for information rights before that, form the movement's prehistory, but the national movement as a dated sequence begins at Jantar Mantar in April 2011.

### Q: What is the Jan Lokpal Bill?

The Jan Lokpal Bill is the citizens' draft of an anti-corruption ombudsman law prepared in 2010 and 2011 by members of India Against Corruption, among them Shanti Bhushan, Prashant Bhushan, Justice N. Santosh Hegde and Arvind Kejriwal, and carried by Anna Hazare's movement as its central demand. It proposed a Lokpal for the Union and Lokayuktas for the states under one statute, selected by a broad committee beyond executive control, with jurisdiction over the Prime Minister, ministers, members of Parliament and officials of all grades. The Central Bureau of Investigation's anti-corruption wing was to merge into the Lokpal, investigations and trials were placed under time limits, and a citizens' charter chapter penalised officials whose services missed published deadlines, while a companion chapter protected whistleblowers. The draft was never enacted in its own form; the government's bills of 2011 diverged from it on the Prime Minister, the investigative agency and the states, and the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act of 2013 adopted a narrower architecture.

### Q: Who is Arvind Kejriwal?

Arvind Kejriwal, born in 1968, is an Indian Institute of Technology Kharagpur graduate who joined the Indian Revenue Service in 1995 and resigned in 2006 to work full time in public campaigns. Through the organisation Parivartan, founded in Delhi in 2000 with associates including Manish Sisodia, he worked on tax grievances and ration corruption, and he campaigned alongside Aruna Roy's network for the national Right to Information Act of 2005, receiving the Ramon Magsaysay Award for Emergent Leadership in 2006. In 2011 he was the principal strategist of India Against Corruption and a citizens' member of the Joint Drafting Committee for the Lokpal Bill. After the September 2012 split with Anna Hazare, he founded the Aam Aadmi Party, launched on 26 November 2012, became Delhi's chief minister on 28 December 2013, resigned after forty-nine days, and returned to the office in February 2015 after his party won 67 of 70 seats.

### Q: When was AAP founded?

The Aam Aadmi Party was formally launched on 26 November 2012, Constitution Day, at the Constitution Club in New Delhi, with Arvind Kejriwal as its national convenor, a written constitution and an internal Lokpal for party disputes. The name had been adopted two days earlier, on 24 November 2012, at the party's national council meeting. The launch closed a sequence that began when the July 2012 fast at Jantar Mantar ended on 3 August with the announcement of a political alternative, continued through the formal split between Kejriwal and Anna Hazare on 19 September 2012, and passed the public announcement of the party project on 2 October 2012. The Election Commission recognised the party and allotted it the broom symbol in 2013, and it contested its first election, the Delhi assembly poll of December 2013, thirteen months after the founding, winning 28 of 70 seats and forming a minority government on 28 December 2013.

### Q: What was the 5 April 2011 Jantar Mantar fast?

The fast of 5 to 9 April 2011 at Jantar Mantar, New Delhi, was Anna Hazare's first Delhi fast and the Anna Hazare movement's opening act. Hazare, then seventy-three, sat on an indefinite fast demanding that the Jan Lokpal Bill be drafted by a joint committee of ministers and citizens rather than by the government alone, a demand he had put to Prime Minister Manmohan Singh in a March 2011 letter. The fast, counted by the movement at ninety-six hours, drew crowds to the designated protest ground near Parliament, solidarity fasts and candle marches in other cities, and continuous television coverage. The government opened negotiations within two days and conceded the committee's form: a Gazette notification dated 8 April 2011 constituted a Joint Drafting Committee of five ministers chaired by Pranab Mukherjee and five citizens' nominees with Shanti Bhushan as co-chair, charged to finish by 30 June 2011. Hazare ended the fast on the morning of 9 April 2011, warning that it would resume if a strong bill was not passed by 15 August.

### Q: How did the Anna Hazare movement shape AAP?

The Anna Hazare movement shaped the Aam Aadmi Party in personnel, programme and method. Its founding leadership came from the movement's core: Arvind Kejriwal, Manish Sisodia, Prashant Bhushan and Gopal Rai carried the campaign's networks, donor lists and volunteer base into the party founded on 26 November 2012, after the September 2012 split in which Anna Hazare refused the electoral turn. Its programme was the movement's documents rewritten as a manifesto: the Jan Lokpal as the first legislative promise, and the decentralised self-rule of Kejriwal's 2012 book Swaraj, expressed through mohalla sabhas, as its theory of government. Its method, candidate screening in public, constituency manifestos, small-donor fundraising and the broom symbol's promise to sweep the system clean, was designed to keep the movement's moral contrast with the established parties visible in electoral form. The imprint also carried the movement's limits: a centralised leadership, an impatience with internal dissent that surfaced in the expulsions of 2015, and a Delhi-centred organisation that took a decade to win a second state.

### Q: What is the post-2013 Lokpal Act architectural content?

The Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, passed by the Rajya Sabha on 17 December 2013 and the Lok Sabha on 18 December 2013, assented to on 1 January 2014 and in force from 16 January 2014, creates a Lokpal of a chairperson and up to eight members, half of them judicial, chosen by a selection committee of the Prime Minister, the Speaker, the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha, the Chief Justice of India or a nominated judge and an eminent jurist. Its jurisdiction covers the Prime Minister with safeguards, since allegations touching security, external relations, public order, atomic energy and space are excluded and a complaint against a Prime Minister needs a full bench acting by two-thirds, in camera, alongside ministers, members of Parliament and officials of all grades, with inquiries routed through the Central Vigilance Commission and the Central Bureau of Investigation. The Act obliges public authorities to publish citizens' charters and directs every state to establish a Lokayukta within 365 days. The first Lokpal, Justice Pinaki Chandra Ghose, took office on 23 March 2019.

### Q: What did the Comptroller and Auditor General report on the 2G spectrum allocation in November 2010?

The Comptroller and Auditor General's report on the issue of licences and allocation of 2G spectrum, tabled in Parliament on 16 November 2010 under Vinod Rai, examined the 2008 allocation of 122 licences by the Department of Telecommunications under Minister A. Raja. It found that licences had been issued at 2001 prices on a first-come-first-served basis whose cut-off dates and queues were manipulated, that eligibility conditions were breached by several recipients, and that the exchequer had suffered a presumptive loss the report estimated at Rs 1.76 lakh crore by comparison with later benchmarks. The figure was presumptive rather than realised, and Telecom Minister Kapil Sibal argued in January 2011 that actual loss was zero because low spectrum prices were stated policy; the Supreme Court's judgment of 2 February 2012 nonetheless cancelled all 122 licences as unconstitutional and arbitrary. The report's tabling, two days after Raja's resignation, opened the corruption dossier on which the Anna Hazare movement built its 2011 campaign.

### Q: Who sat on the Joint Drafting Committee formed in April 2011?

The Joint Drafting Committee constituted by the Gazette notification of 8 April 2011 had ten members, five ministers of the Union government and five nominees of Anna Hazare. Finance Minister Pranab Mukherjee chaired it. The government side comprised Mukherjee, Home Minister P. Chidambaram, Law Minister M. Veerappa Moily, who served as convenor, Human Resource Development and Communications Minister Kapil Sibal, and Water Resources and Minority Affairs Minister Salman Khurshid. The citizens' side comprised Hazare himself, former Supreme Court judge and Karnataka Lokayukta Justice N. Santosh Hegde, former law minister Shanti Bhushan, who served as co-chair, his son the advocate Prashant Bhushan, and Arvind Kejriwal. The committee was charged to prepare the draft Lokpal Bill by 30 June 2011. It met nine times between 16 April and 21 June 2011 and broke down over the Prime Minister's inclusion, the judiciary, the Central Bureau of Investigation and the coverage of lower officials, producing rival drafts rather than a joint text.

### Q: What happened during Anna Hazare's arrest on 16 August 2011?

On the morning of 16 August 2011, before his announced fast at Ramlila Maidan had begun, Delhi Police detained Anna Hazare at a flat in Mayur Vihar where he had spent the night, after he refused permission conditions that limited the fast's duration and gathering. He was produced before a magistrate and remanded to seven days in Tihar Jail, while Arvind Kejriwal and other associates were also detained and released. The arrest, of a seventy-four-year-old who had committed no act beyond announcing a fast, triggered protests across Indian cities and uproar in Parliament, and by the evening the government ordered his release. Hazare refused to leave Tihar until the fast was permitted at Ramlila Maidan without the original conditions, negotiating from his cell through intermediaries for two days while crowds held vigil outside the jail. He walked out on 19 August 2011, visited Rajghat, and began the fast at Ramlila Maidan that ran until 28 August 2011.

### Q: What did Parliament agree on 27 August 2011 to end the Ramlila Maidan fast?

On 27 August 2011 both Houses of Parliament, in a special debate initiated by Finance Minister Pranab Mukherjee in the Lok Sabha, adopted by voice vote a sense-of-the-House resolution responding to Anna Hazare's three conditions for ending his Ramlila Maidan fast. The House agreed in principle to a citizens' charter guaranteeing time-bound public services, to bringing the lower bureaucracy under the Lokpal through an appropriate mechanism, and to Lokayuktas being established in the states, and it resolved to transmit the debate's proceedings to the Standing Committee then examining the Lokpal Bill. The Bharatiya Janata Party's Sushma Swaraj declared broad agreement with the three conditions in the Lok Sabha, and Arun Jaitley defended parliamentary supremacy in the Rajya Sabha while conceding the legislature's four decades of delay on the Lokpal. The resolution was an agreement in principle rather than a statute, and its text was carried to Ramlila Maidan that night; Hazare ended his 288-hour fast on the morning of 28 August 2011.

### Q: What objections did parliamentarians raise against drafting a law under the pressure of a fast?

The objection, stated most fully by Pranab Mukherjee in the Lok Sabha debate of 27 August 2011, held that a distinction must stand between mobocracy and democracy: law in a constitutional republic is made by elected representatives after deliberation, and a drafting process surrendered to a fasting crowd would injure the very institutions through which citizens rule themselves. Kapil Sibal had pressed the same case through the summer of 2011, arguing that a joint committee conceded under duress could advise Parliament but could not bind it, and Congress spokesman Manish Tewari carried the argument to the campaign's personnel, calling its members unelected and unaccountable. Arun Jaitley, supporting the August resolution, restated the supremacy point from the opposition benches while conceding the legislature's own delay. The campaign's answer, given by Kejriwal and the Bhushans, was that the citizens' draft claimed no authority beyond persuasion, that forty-three years of parliamentary custody had produced no Lokpal, and that the fast addressed the electorate that Parliament represented rather than Parliament itself.

### Q: Why did Anna Hazare and Arvind Kejriwal part ways in September 2012?

The split of 19 September 2012 turned on the movement's instrument, not its goal. Kejriwal argued that two years of fasting had extracted a drafting committee and a parliamentary resolution but no law, because legislators answered to their parties rather than to crowds, and that the movement's own people therefore had to contest elections and carry the Jan Lokpal into an assembly themselves; he cited a survey run through the campaign's channels whose published tally favoured forming a party. Hazare held that an andolan drew its authority from standing outside the contest for office, that a party would trade that authority for the compromises of candidates, funds and alliances, and that his name and image were not to be used by any party. After a formal meeting at the Constitution Club in New Delhi on 19 September 2012, Hazare announced the separation. He reconstituted India Against Corruption under his own leadership in November 2012, while Kejriwal launched the Aam Aadmi Party on 26 November 2012.

### Q: What argument does Arvind Kejriwal make in Swaraj?

Swaraj, published in 2012, is Arvind Kejriwal's statement of the political theory he carried from the Anna Hazare movement into the Aam Aadmi Party. Its argument holds that independence transferred power from a colonial administration to a small secretariat class of politicians and officials, leaving citizens as subjects who petition the state rather than principals who direct it. Against that settlement the book proposes direct and decentralised self-rule: gram sabhas in villages and mohalla sabhas in urban neighbourhoods, assemblies of all resident voters, should decide local development spending, select and supervise local officials, and hold the administration's local face to account between elections. Representative institutions remain, in the book's scheme, but their discretion is narrowed by the assemblies' control over funds, functionaries and the agenda of local grievances. The book became the Aam Aadmi Party's programme in embryo: mohalla sabhas were written into its Delhi manifestos, and its promise to return power to the neighbourhood supplied the positive doctrine behind the movement's negative demand for a Lokpal.

### Q: What was the outcome of the December 2013 Delhi assembly election?

The Delhi assembly election, voted on 4 December 2013 and counted on 8 December 2013, produced a hung house in a seventy-member assembly. The Bharatiya Janata Party won 31 seats, the debutant Aam Aadmi Party 28, the incumbent Congress 8, with the Shiromani Akali Dal, the Janata Dal (United) and an independent taking one each. The Congress, which had governed Delhi for fifteen years under Sheila Dikshit, lost its chief minister's own seat: in New Delhi, Arvind Kejriwal defeated Dikshit by 25,864 votes, polling 44,269 to her 18,405. The Bharatiya Janata Party, four short of a majority and declining to assemble numbers by inducement, chose opposition. Lieutenant Governor Najeeb Jung invited Kejriwal, whose party consulted its volunteers and the city's voters and announced acceptance on 23 December 2013 with the Congress's outside support. Kejriwal was sworn in as chief minister at Ramlila Maidan on 28 December 2013, heading a minority government that lasted forty-nine days.

### Q: Why did the first AAP government resign after 49 days?

The first Aam Aadmi Party government resigned on 14 February 2014, forty-nine days after taking office on 28 December 2013, over the introduction of its Delhi Jan Lokpal Bill in the assembly. The Kejriwal cabinet had approved the bill on 3 February 2014 and sought to introduce it in the session that opened on 13 February, holding the measure to be the promise on which the government existed. Lieutenant Governor Najeeb Jung held that Delhi's special constitutional position required the Union government's prior recommendation for such a bill under the Transaction of Business Rules, and Congress and Bharatiya Janata Party members combined on 14 February to vote 42 to 27 against granting leave to introduce it, the Congress withholding the practical cover its outside support had given. Kejriwal resigned the same evening, recommending the assembly's dissolution and fresh elections, and stated that a government unable to pass its founding bill had no claim to continue. Delhi passed under President's rule until the election of February 2015.

### Q: What happened in the February 2015 Delhi assembly election?

In the Delhi assembly election of 7 February 2015, counted on 10 February, the Aam Aadmi Party won 67 of 70 seats on 54.3 percent of the vote, the largest mandate in the capital's electoral history. The Bharatiya Janata Party, which had campaigned with former police officer and movement veteran Kiran Bedi as its chief ministerial face, fell to three seats; Bedi lost her own Krishna Nagar constituency. The Congress, Delhi's governing party for fifteen years until 2013, won none. The verdict reversed the party's national humiliation of May 2014, when it had taken four Lok Sabha seats, all in Punjab, and it was read across parties as the electorate's judgment on the forty-nine day government and its resignation: the voters who had watched Kejriwal fall on the Jan Lokpal Bill returned him with a majority that needed no outside support. He was sworn in at Ramlila Maidan on 14 February 2015, exactly one year after his resignation, and the government served the full term, winning again in 2020 with 62 seats.

### Q: How did AAP fare in the 2014 Lok Sabha election?

The Aam Aadmi Party contested the general election of April and May 2014 at national scale, fielding over four hundred candidates little more than a year after its founding, and won four seats on roughly two percent of the national vote. All four victories came in Punjab, where Bhagwant Mann in Sangrur, Dharamvir Gandhi in Patiala, Harinder Singh Khalsa in Fatehgarh Sahib and Sadhu Singh in Faridkot were elected, giving the party a parliamentary bridgehead in the state it would win in 2022. The Delhi gamble failed: the party lost all seven of the capital's seats months after forming its government there. Arvind Kejriwal contested Varanasi against the Bharatiya Janata Party's prime ministerial candidate Narendra Modi and lost by a margin of about 3.72 lakh votes. The result, declared on 16 May 2014, was widely read as a movement mistaking a metropolitan moment for a national one, a judgment the Delhi electorate revised in February 2015.

### Q: When was the first Lokpal appointed under the 2013 Act?

The first Lokpal under the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act 2013 was appointed more than five years after the Act commenced on 16 January 2014. The selection process had stalled over the absence of a recognised Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha after 2014, which the Act's selection committee required, and over litigation and amendment concerning the committee's composition. Anna Hazare fasted at Ramlila Maidan in March 2018 and at Ralegan Siddhi in January and February 2019 on the appointment question, ending the second fast on 5 February 2019 on the government's assurance that the selection would be completed. The selection committee met in March 2019, and Justice Pinaki Chandra Ghose, a retired Supreme Court judge then serving on the National Human Rights Commission, was named chairperson on 19 March 2019. President Ram Nath Kovind administered his oath at Rashtrapati Bhavan on 23 March 2019, alongside four judicial and four non-judicial members.

### Q: What are mohalla sabhas in the AAP programme?

Mohalla sabhas are neighbourhood assemblies in the programme the Aam Aadmi Party drew from Arvind Kejriwal's 2012 book Swaraj. A mohalla sabha convenes the voters of a defined urban neighbourhood, a few thousand residents, to decide local questions directly: which lanes, drains, parks and streetlights the local budget should fund, in what order, and whether the officials responsible for local services have performed. In the Swaraj scheme the sabha is not a consultative forum but the primary unit of self-rule, with the elected councillor and the municipal staff answerable to it between elections, on the model of the gram sabha that the book proposes for villages. The party wrote mohalla sabhas into its Delhi manifestos from 2013 onward, piloted funded sabhas in a group of constituencies during its second Delhi government, and presented the institution as the positive half of the Anna Hazare movement's inheritance, the machinery by which citizens would govern daily life rather than merely police its corruption.
