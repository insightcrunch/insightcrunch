Two founders sit with an adviser and ask which form their new venture should take, and the adviser opens a feature list. The list compares incorporation cost, annual filings, audit thresholds and a dozen other items, and it is nearly useless, because both forms on the table give the founders the one thing they think they are choosing between. A limited liability partnership and a private limited company both shield the personal assets of the people behind them. The Limited Liability Partnership Act 2008 was not enacted to sell that shield a second time. It was enacted because a large class of Indian businesses, professional firms above all, needed the shield without the machinery that company law bolts onto it, and because the ordinary partnership firm, the only alternative, exposed every partner to unlimited personal liability for the defaults of the others.

The choice between the two forms therefore turns on three questions that feature lists rarely ask in the right order. Will the business ever need to raise equity from outsiders who expect shares, a valuation and an exit? How will the tax system treat the profits when they reach the founders' hands under each form? And how much statutory compliance can the business actually carry, year after year, with the people it has? Get those three answers right and the form chooses itself. Get them wrong, and the founders discover the error at the worst possible moment: when an investor's term sheet arrives for a business that has no shares to issue, or when a dormant entity that nobody bothered to close has quietly accumulated years of per-day filing fees.

This article is the complete treatment of the statute that created the LLP form: what it built, how its parts work, where its liability shield stops, how conversion into it works and why conversion out of it barely does, what the 2021 amendment changed, and how the three deciding questions should be answered on real facts. The comparison it defends throughout is a narrow one. The LLP is a compliance and tax choice, not a liability choice, and a founder who picks it for the protection alone has answered a question that was never really open.

## Why India needed a fourth business form

Indian business law offered three vehicles for most of the twentieth century, and each left a gap. The sole proprietorship was simple and exposed its owner to everything. The partnership firm under the Indian Partnership Act 1932 was flexible and contractual, required no incorporation, and let the partners arrange their affairs almost as they pleased, but every partner was liable without limit for the firm's obligations and, more frighteningly, for the acts of the other partners done in the firm's name. The company, whether private or public, gave limited liability and a separate legal personality, but at the price of a dense statutory apparatus: boards, meetings, resolutions, statutory audit, registers, filings and a governance code written for enterprises with outside shareholders to protect. For a two-person professional practice or a small trading business, the company was armour designed for a much larger animal.

The gap was most acute for professionals. Accountants, advocates, architects, company secretaries and other regulated professionals organised themselves, by necessity, as partnership firms, because the rules of their professions and the structure of the Companies Act made incorporation an awkward or impermissible route for a practice. A chartered accountancy firm with offices in several cities could have dozens of partners, each personally exposed to the consequences of a negligent audit performed by a colleague in another city whom he had never met. The risk grew with the firm, which is to say it grew precisely with success. What the professions asked for, over decades, was a form in which the entity would answer for the firm's obligations, the individual would answer for his own conduct, and the internal arrangement between the participants could remain a matter of contract rather than company law.

The demand was recorded by a sequence of committees. The Abid Hussain Committee on small scale industries raised the case for a limited liability vehicle for small enterprises in the late 1990s. The decisive statement came from the Committee on Regulation of Private Companies and Partnerships chaired by Naresh Chandra, which reported in 2003 and recommended that Parliament enact a limited liability partnership law, aimed principally at professional firms and small businesses for whom the full company form was disproportionate. The Expert Committee on Company Law chaired by J. J. Irani, whose 2005 report shaped the thinking that later produced the Companies Act 2013, endorsed the same conclusion: a separate statute should create the form, sitting beside company law rather than inside it. Trade bodies had floated versions of the idea far earlier, and the model was not exotic. The United Kingdom had enacted its Limited Liability Partnerships Act in 2000, and Singapore followed with its own statute in 2005, and the Indian drafts drew on both.

The legislative route still took most of a decade. A Limited Liability Partnership Bill was introduced in the Rajya Sabha in December 2006 and examined at length, but it did not complete its passage. A fresh Bill was introduced in the Rajya Sabha in October 2008 and moved quickly: both Houses passed it before the end of that year, and it received the President's assent on 7 January 2009 as Act 6 of 2009, though it carries the year 2008 in its short title. The bulk of the Limited Liability Partnership Act 2008 was brought into force on 31 March 2009. The conversion provisions, sections 55 to 58 together with the Second, Third and Fourth Schedules, followed on 31 May 2009, and some machinery provisions tied to the Tribunal took effect later as that institution was constituted. The Act contains 81 sections and four Schedules, and it is administered by the Ministry of Corporate Affairs through the Registrars of Companies, who act as registrars for LLPs in their respective states.

Adoption outran the committee room's expectations. The form was designed with professional practices and small enterprises in view, and both came, but so did a far wider population: family businesses converting out of partnership firms, subsidiaries and joint ventures of companies that wanted a flexible internal constitution, start-ups that wanted incorporation without board machinery, and holding vehicles of every description. The Ministry of Corporate Affairs' monthly information bulletin counted 3,87,808 active limited liability partnerships as on 31 March 2025, a population far beyond the professional-firm niche the Naresh Chandra Committee had in mind. That divergence between the intended user and the actual user matters for reading the statute, because several of its design choices, the freedom of the internal contract, the absence of any capital discipline, the light audit net, make sense for the intended user and create the risks, examined later in this article, for everyone else.

## What the Limited Liability Partnership Act 2008 actually creates

Section 3 of the Act states the foundation in three propositions. A limited liability partnership is a body corporate formed and incorporated under the Act. It is a legal entity separate from its partners. And it has perpetual succession, so that a change in the persons who compose it does not touch its existence, its rights or its liabilities. Each proposition does work. Body corporate status means the LLP can own property, contract, sue and be sued in its own name. Separate personality means the debts of the entity are the entity's debts, and the shield discussed throughout this article is a consequence of personality rather than a freestanding concession. Perpetual succession means the death, retirement or insolvency of a partner is an internal event, recorded and filed, and not a dissolution, which is the single sharpest structural break with the ordinary partnership firm, where the departure of a partner can end the firm itself.

Section 4 then performs the amputation that most commentary gets wrong. Save as otherwise provided in the Act itself, the Indian Partnership Act 1932 does not apply to a limited liability partnership. The LLP is therefore not a species of partnership firm with a liability cap attached. It is a corporation that borrows the partnership's contractual interior. The law of partnership supplies none of its rules: not the implied authority doctrines, not the dissolution rules, not the duty frameworks of the 1932 Act. Where the LLP Act is silent, the answer must be found in the LLP agreement, in the Act's own Schedules, or in general law, and not in the statute that governs the firm down the corridor. Examination answers and incorporation websites that describe the LLP as a partnership registered under partnership law are describing a form that does not exist.

Registration is constitutive, not optional. Under section 11, two or more persons associated for carrying on a lawful business with a view to profit subscribe their names to an incorporation document, which is filed with the Registrar of the state in which the registered office is to be situated, together with a compliance statement by an advocate, a chartered accountant, a cost accountant or a company secretary engaged in the formation, or by a person named as a designated partner, that the Act's requirements have been met. The Registrar registers the document and issues a certificate of incorporation, and on registration the effects in section 14 follow: the LLP becomes a body corporate by the name in the certificate, capable of suing and being a party to proceedings, of acquiring and disposing of property, and of having a common seal if it chooses. The name must end with the words Limited Liability Partnership or the acronym LLP, which is the public signal that creditors are dealing with a limited fund. Every LLP carries a Limited Liability Partnership Identification Number, and its registered office, its partners and its designated partners are matters of public record, searchable in the same registry system that carries company records. For the full treatment of the company alternative against which this form competes, the pillar article on [the Companies Act 2013 complete guide](/2012/02/01/companies-act-2013-complete-guide/) sets out what the same founders would be buying if they chose the older vehicle.

### Can an LLP own property and sue in its own name?

Yes. On registration the LLP is a body corporate separate from its partners, so it holds property, opens accounts, contracts, sues and is sued in its own name, and none of that is disturbed when partners join or leave. The entity, not the shifting group of partners, is the person the law deals with throughout the LLP's life.

The internal geography of the Act repays a map before the detail. The opening chapters define the form and its participants. The middle of the statute deals with the partners' relations among themselves and with outsiders: the agreement, agency, liability, contribution. A financial chapter requires books of account, a statement of account and solvency, and an annual return. Conversion occupies its own chapter with a Schedule for each kind of predecessor. Compromise, arrangement and amalgamation with creditors and partners follow. Winding up has a short chapter of its own, and the closing chapters handle investigation, offences and penalties, fee and rule machinery, and the Registrar's power to strike a defunct LLP off the register. The First Schedule is part of the operative law, not an appendix: it supplies the default constitution for every LLP whose partners have not written their own.

## Who the partners are, and who carries the compliance

Section 5 opens the form widely. Any individual and any body corporate may be a partner in an LLP, so a company can sit inside an LLP as a partner, and LLPs can be nested inside group structures in ways the partnership firm never allowed. An individual is disqualified, under section 22's eligibility rules, only on narrow grounds: a person found to be of unsound mind by a court, an undischarged insolvent, and a person who has applied to be adjudicated insolvent while the application is pending. There is no ceiling on the number of partners, which distinguishes the form sharply from the partnership firm, whose numbers company law caps, and from the private company, whose membership is capped at two hundred. A professional firm of eighty partners fits inside the form as comfortably as a two-person consultancy.

Section 6 fixes the floor: every LLP must have at least two partners. The sanction for falling below the floor is one of the most instructive provisions in the Act, because it shows the legislature pricing the shield. If the number of partners drops below two and the LLP carries on business for more than six months in that reduced state, the sole remaining partner, if he knows that the business is being carried on with him alone, becomes personally liable for the obligations the LLP incurs during that period. The shield, in other words, is a facility granted to a plurality. A one-person enterprise wearing the LLP form is borrowing a protection the statute withdraws the moment the plurality disappears and stays disappeared.

Within the body of partners, the Act creates a smaller class that carries the statutory burden: the designated partners. Section 7 requires every LLP to have at least two designated partners who are individuals, and at least one of them must be resident in India. Where a partner is a body corporate, its nominee individuals act through this mechanism, so the compliance responsibility always lands on natural persons who can be identified, served and penalised. Residence was originally tested by a stay of at least one hundred and eighty-two days in India during the immediately preceding year; the 2021 amendment recast the test as a stay of not less than one hundred and twenty days during the financial year, a relaxation designed for a more mobile professional class. Every designated partner must obtain a Designated Partner Identification Number from the Central Government, the identity key that ties the individual to every filing he signs across every entity he serves.

Section 8 states what designation means. Unless the Act expressly provides otherwise, a designated partner is liable for all acts, matters and things that the LLP is required to do, and is liable to all penalties imposed on the LLP for any contravention of the Act. Designation is therefore not an honorific and not a rotating chairmanship. It is the point at which the entity's obligations acquire a human address. The other partners may be economically identical, sharing profits in the same proportions and voting on the same questions, but the designated partners are the ones whose names stand behind the annual return, the statement of account and solvency, the intimation of every change in the partnership, and the penalty notices when any of those are late or wrong. Founders choosing who should be designated are choosing who carries personal exposure for administrative failure, and the common practice of designating the most junior founder, or a spouse with no role in the business, misunderstands the provision in a way that the penalty notices eventually correct.

### What does a designated partner actually do?

A designated partner is the individual statutorily responsible for the LLP's compliance: filing the annual return and the statement of account and solvency, intimating changes in partners and the registered office, and answering for penalties when the LLP defaults. Every LLP needs at least two, individuals both, with at least one resident in India.

### How many partners can an LLP have?

An LLP must have at least two partners and the Act sets no maximum, so a professional firm with scores of partners and a two-person consultancy occupy the same form. The floor matters more than the ceiling: an LLP that trades for over six months with a single partner exposes that partner to personal liability for the period.

Changes inside the partnership are public events. A person may become a partner in accordance with the LLP agreement, may cease to be a partner in accordance with it or, failing any provision, by giving thirty days' notice of intention to resign, and ceases automatically on death or on being declared insolvent. Section 25 requires the LLP to intimate the Registrar of every admission and cessation, and of any change in a partner's name or address, within thirty days. The register is the state's memory of who stands behind the entity, and the filing discipline around changes is part of the price of the shield: an outsider dealing with the LLP is entitled to assume that the public record shows the real participants. A former partner, correspondingly, remains visible to the outside world until the cessation is filed, and the Act protects the former partner by allowing him to file the intimation himself where the LLP neglects to do it.

## The LLP agreement and the default code

If separate personality is the Act's skeleton, the LLP agreement is its nervous system. Section 23 provides that the mutual rights and duties of the partners, and the rights and duties of the LLP itself in relation to its partners, are governed by the agreement between them. The agreement, and every change to it, must be filed with the Registrar in the prescribed form, which makes the constitution of the business a registered document rather than a drawer contract. Almost nothing in the internal law of the LLP is mandatory. Profit shares, capital ratios, voting weights, admission terms, exit pricing, interest on capital, drawings, management roles and dispute routes are all matters for the document, and two LLPs in the same street can be governed by constitutions that share nothing except the statute that permits both.

The freedom is real but it has a floor, and the floor is the First Schedule. Section 23(4) provides that in the absence of an agreement on any matter, the mutual rights and duties are governed by the First Schedule's provisions on that matter. The Schedule's defaults are worth knowing precisely because so many small LLPs operate on them by accident. All partners are entitled to share equally in the capital and profits of the LLP, whatever they contributed. No partner is entitled to remuneration for acting in the business or management of the LLP. Every partner may take part in management. No partner may be introduced without the consent of all existing partners. Ordinary matters are decided by a majority in number of the partners, but the nature of the LLP's business cannot be changed without the consent of all. Every partner must render true accounts and full information. A partner who carries on a competing business without consent must account for its profits. And, critically, no majority can expel a partner unless the agreement itself confers that power, a default that has surprised many founders who assumed majority rule ran all the way down.

The practical teaching is that the First Schedule is a constitution for a partnership of equals who trust each other completely and expect never to disagree about money, work or departure. A business that matches that description can safely incorporate on the statutory form agreement and spend nothing on drafting. Almost no real business matches it for long. The agreement is also the document that fixes each partner's contribution, and contribution under this Act is a defined legal quantity, not an informal understanding. Section 32 provides that a partner's contribution may consist of tangible or intangible property, money, promissory notes, agreements to contribute cash or property, and contracts for services performed or to be performed, and section 33 makes the obligation to contribute enforceable in accordance with the agreement, with a creditor of the LLP who extended credit in reliance on a stated contribution able, in winding up, to enforce that obligation. What a partner promised to put in is therefore a creditor-facing fact, recorded in the agreement and the statement of account, and not a private arrangement that can be quietly revised when the business is failing.

## Liability: what the limit covers and where it stops

The liability scheme sits in sections 26 to 31, and it is best read as four distinct allocations. The first allocation concerns the entity. Under section 27, the LLP is liable to the full extent of its assets where a partner acts wrongfully in the course of its business or with its authority, and the obligations of the LLP, whether contractual or otherwise, are solely the obligations of the LLP. The entity is the primary bearer of everything the business does. The second allocation concerns the individual partner's exposure for the entity's acts: a partner is not personally liable, directly or indirectly, for an obligation of the LLP solely by reason of being a partner. The third allocation separates partners from each other. Sections 27 and 28 state the rule that defines the form for professional firms: an obligation of the LLP arising from a contract or otherwise is solely the LLP's obligation, and a partner bears no personal liability for the wrongful act or omission of another partner. The negligence of the audit partner in another city is the firm's problem and the entity's exposure; it is not a claim on the houses of the partners who had no part in the engagement. The fourth allocation preserves personal responsibility for one's own conduct: a partner remains liable for his own wrongful acts and omissions, and section 26 completes the architecture by making each partner an agent of the LLP for the purposes of its business, while expressly denying that a partner is the agent of the other partners.

Two provisions then fence the shield. Section 29 deals with holding out: a person who, by words or conduct, represents himself or knowingly permits himself to be represented as a partner in an LLP is liable to anyone who gives credit to the LLP on the faith of that representation, and the provision reaches the estate of a deceased partner whose name continues to be used. The register and the letterhead are therefore liability instruments, and an LLP that leaves a departed partner's name on its documents is manufacturing claimants. Section 30 is the fraud exception, and it is deliberately absolute. Where an act of the LLP is carried out with intent to defraud its creditors or any other person, or for any fraudulent purpose, the liability of the LLP and of every person who was knowingly a party to that conduct is unlimited for the debts and liabilities in question. The same section carries the Act's principal imprisonment provision, which as originally enacted punished the knowing party with imprisonment of up to two years together with a fine; the 2021 amendment raised that maximum to five years, the one place in the amendment where Parliament moved toward greater severity rather than less, because the decriminalisation programme was aimed at procedural default and not at dishonesty. Fraud in this statute is not a side note to the liability scheme. It is the scheme's stated boundary: the shield is granted to a business form, and is withdrawn from a fraud that merely wears the form.

Section 31 adds a mitigation device that few summaries notice. Where a partner provides useful information during an investigation of the LLP's affairs, the provision enables the penalty that would otherwise fall on him to be reduced, a whistle-blowing incentive built into the statute's enforcement chapter. Its presence is a clue to how the legislature imagined default inside LLPs being discovered: not usually by the Registrar's inspection, but by insiders with something to gain from candour. A related discipline applies to creditors at the point of failure. A partner who receives a distribution from the LLP knowing that the entity cannot thereafter pay its debts, or will be rendered unable to pay them, can be required in winding up to restore what he took. Limited liability under this Act is thus a rule about who bears the ordinary risks of the business. It has never been a rule that profits extracted on the edge of insolvency stay extracted.

### What does limited liability not protect a partner against?

It does not protect a partner against the consequences of his own wrongful acts, against unlimited liability where the business is carried on to defraud creditors, against personal liability after six months as a sole remaining partner, or against claims by someone who gave credit relying on his being held out as a partner.

## Books, accounts and the audit threshold

The financial provisions are short, and their shortness is the policy. Section 34 requires every LLP to maintain books of account on a cash or accrual basis, by the double entry system, at its registered office for each year of its existence, and to prepare a Statement of Account and Solvency for each financial year, signed by the designated partners, which must be filed with the Registrar within thirty days from the end of six months of the financial year, which for the standard March year-end means the end of October. The statement of account and solvency is the form's characteristic document: half accounts, half declaration. In it the designated partners state whether the LLP is able to pay its debts in full as they become due in the normal course of business, a solvency representation made on the public record every year by named individuals. A false comfort in that declaration is therefore personal in a way that an unsigned management account never is.

Audit is where the form's lightness is most concrete. A company must have its accounts audited regardless of size. An LLP's accounts must be audited only where the rules require it, and Rule 24 of the Limited Liability Partnership Rules 2009 sets the trigger: an LLP whose turnover in a financial year exceeds forty lakh rupees, or whose total contribution exceeds twenty-five lakh rupees, must appoint an auditor who is a chartered accountant in practice. Below both lines, the accounts are filed unaudited, on the designated partners' certification. The thresholds are deliberately low in absolute terms, because the legislature was not trying to exempt commerce from audit; it was trying to exempt the two-person professional practice and the genuinely small enterprise, for whom a statutory audit could cost more than the oversight was worth. The 2021 amendment added section 34A, empowering the Central Government to prescribe accounting and auditing standards for classes of LLPs in consultation with the National Financial Reporting Authority, a provision whose existence signals the direction of travel: as the form's population grows beyond its intended niche, the accounting discipline is being aligned, class by class, with the company regime it was designed to escape.

## The annual compliance calendar and the price of neglect

Strip the promotional literature away and an LLP's recurring statutory load is this. An annual return in the prescribed form, under section 35, must be filed within sixty days of the close of the financial year, which for a March year-end is the end of May; the return states the composition of the LLP, its partners and designated partners, and its contribution structure, and for larger LLPs it carries a certification requirement. The Statement of Account and Solvency follows by the end of October. The LLP agreement and every amendment to it must be filed. Changes in partners, designated partners, names and addresses, and the registered office must be intimated as they occur, generally within thirty days. A designated partner's know-your-customer record must be kept current under the identification-number regime. Outside the Act, the LLP files its income-tax return as a firm, and carries the ordinary registrations of any business, goods and services tax, professional tax and sectoral licences, which no choice of form avoids. That is the honest inventory. It is lighter than a company's calendar, which adds board meetings, annual general meetings, statutory registers, auditor appointment and rotation machinery, and a far longer list of event-based filings. It is not, as the incorporation websites imply, nothing.

What makes the LLP's calendar dangerous is not its length but its penalty design. The fee regime for delayed filing was built on an additional fee that accrued for every day of default, one hundred rupees a day for the filing in question under the fee schedule as originally framed, with no ceiling on the accumulation. A company that files late pays, and the meter largely stops being interesting. An LLP that stops filing simply keeps accruing. The consequence, discovered by a generation of founders, is the dormant LLP trap: an entity incorporated for a venture that never traded, or that traded and stopped, is left on the register because closing it requires effort, while its two annual filings go unmade year after year and the additional fees compound quietly in the background. By the time the founders return, perhaps because a due-diligence exercise or a new venture's background check has surfaced the old entity, the accumulated additional fee can exceed any sum the venture ever handled. The Ministry of Corporate Affairs recognised the scale of the problem in 2020 by running a settlement scheme that allowed defaulting LLPs to regularise their filings on concessional terms, an amnesty whose existence is the best evidence of how many entities the per-day design had caught. The 2021 and 2022 reforms moderated the fee burden for small LLPs and recast several defaults as civil penalties with fixed ceilings, but the structural lesson survives every reform: this form charges for neglect by the day, and an unused LLP is not a free option but a meter running.

The Registrar's backstop is section 75, which empowers the Registrar to strike a defunct LLP off the register where there is reasonable cause to believe it is not carrying on business or operations, following the prescribed process. Strike-off is the register's hygiene mechanism, not a founder's exit strategy: it does not extinguish liabilities already incurred, and designated partners who let an entity rot its way to strike-off carry the accrued penalties and the record of default with them. The contrast with the intended use is worth stating plainly. The legislature gave small businesses a form with two filings a year and a solvency declaration, in exchange for which it asked only that the filings actually happen. The form punishes the single failure it was designed around, the failure to attend to it at all.

## Conversion into an LLP, and the one-way door

Parliament expected the LLP's first population to arrive by conversion, and built a chapter for it. Sections 55 to 58, read with the Second, Third and Fourth Schedules, allow three kinds of predecessor to convert: a partnership firm, a private company, and an unlisted public company. A listed public company cannot convert, a boundary that keeps the form closed to enterprises with public shareholders to protect. The machinery is uniform across the three routes. The entity applies to the Registrar with the prescribed particulars of its partners, creditors and pending proceedings; on registration, the Registrar issues a certificate recording the conversion; and from that date the converted LLP is the same enterprise in a new legal skin. All property, tangible and intangible, all rights, liabilities and obligations of the predecessor vest in the LLP by operation of law, without any deed of assignment. Pending proceedings continue by or against the LLP. Existing agreements and contracts take effect as though the LLP had been the party. The predecessor firm is dissolved, and a converted company is deemed removed from the register of companies. Every partner of the firm, or every shareholder of the company, becomes a partner of the LLP on conversion, which is why conversion is a collective act requiring the consents the Schedules prescribe, and not a device by which a majority can drag a dissenting participant into a new form.

The tax system was fitted to the door so that conversion would not itself be a taxable event. The Income-tax Act treats the transfer of assets on conversion as exempt from capital gains tax where the statutory conditions are met: for a firm, broadly, that all its partners become partners of the LLP in the same proportions, that no consideration passes except the partners' interests and profit shares, and that the partners' aggregate profit-sharing does not fall below half for the period the exemption conditions prescribe; for a company, a stricter set of conditions including a ceiling on the company's turnover in the years before conversion, continuity of shareholding as partnership interest, and a bar on any shareholder receiving consideration beyond a share in the LLP. The conditions matter because they encode the policy: conversion relief exists for a genuine change of clothing by the same business, not for a sale dressed as a conversion. A business that fails a condition has not done something illegal; it has simply made a taxable transfer, and the difference between those two outcomes, on a business with appreciated assets, is large enough to decide whether conversion happens at all.

The asymmetry is in the return direction. The LLP Act contains no provision for converting an LLP back into a company or a firm. The Companies Act 2013 does contain a route by which an LLP may register itself as a company under its chapter on companies authorised to register under that Act, but the route is seldom travelled, sits awkwardly with the tax conditions that governed the journey in, and is treated by practitioners as close to a fresh start rather than a mirror-image conversion. Founders should read the choice of the LLP form with that asymmetry in view. Moving a firm or a company into an LLP is a designed, signposted, tax-managed step. Moving out again is an improvisation. This is the first concrete reason why the equity question, taken up in the decision section below, dominates the choice: a business that incorporates as an LLP and later needs share capital cannot simply re-paper itself, and usually ends up incorporating a new company and transferring the business into it, with the tax and contractual friction the conversion chapter was built to avoid.

## Investigation, winding up and the insolvency overlap

For a statute of 81 sections, the enforcement and exit machinery is compressed. The Central Government may appoint inspectors to investigate the affairs of an LLP, on the application of partners holding the prescribed stake or on the Registrar's report or in the public interest, and the investigation chapters adapt the familiar company-law model, with powers to examine partners and officers and to seize the initiative where records are being withheld. Compromise, arrangement and amalgamation have their own short provisions in sections 60 to 62, under which an LLP may compromise with its creditors or partners or amalgamate with another LLP with the Tribunal's sanction, a reminder that the legislature imagined LLPs growing, merging and rescuing each other, and not merely being born and dying.

Winding up is governed by sections 63 to 65. An LLP may be wound up voluntarily by its partners or by the Tribunal. Section 64 lists the circumstances in which the Tribunal may wind an LLP up: where the LLP decides by the prescribed majority that it be wound up by the Tribunal; where the number of partners falls below two for more than six months; where the LLP is unable to pay its debts; where it has acted against the interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India, the security of the state, friendly relations with foreign states, public order, decency or morality; where it has defaulted in filing the statement of account and solvency or the annual return for five consecutive financial years; and where the Tribunal is of the opinion that it is just and equitable to wind it up. Section 65 left the detailed winding-up procedure to rules, and the Limited Liability Partnership Winding Up Rules 2012 supply it. Note what the five-year filing default ground does: it connects the compliance chapter to the exit chapter, so that the dormant LLP that ignores its filings is not merely accruing fees but is, in the statute's own terms, an entity the Tribunal may terminate for the neglect itself.

Over this scheme the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016 has been laid, and the overlap is the least settled part of the LLP's legal environment. The Code's definition of a corporate person expressly includes a limited liability partnership, which means an LLP can be a corporate debtor: a financial creditor, an operational creditor or the LLP itself can set the corporate insolvency resolution process in motion before the National Company Law Tribunal, and liquidation under the Code is available where resolution fails. The Tribunal under the LLP Act is the same National Company Law Tribunal, so forum is not the difficulty. The difficulty is doctrinal: the LLP Act's own winding-up chapter was written for a world in which insolvency lived inside each incorporation statute, and the Code has since centralised insolvency for every corporate form, so practitioners must work out, point by point, which regime governs a failing LLP's debts, how the Code's moratorium and waterfall sit with the LLP agreement's internal allocation of losses, and what happens to the partners' contribution obligations inside a resolution process. Tribunal practice has admitted insolvency proceedings against LLPs and the broad proposition that the Code reaches the form is established; the detailed interface is still being written case by case, and any account that presents it as fully settled is ahead of the record. The pillar treatment of the Code itself, its resolution process and its liquidation waterfall, is in [the insolvency and bankruptcy code guide](/2012/04/01/insolvency-bankruptcy-code-2016-guide/), and the LLP reader should hold the two statutes together: the 2008 Act decides what the entity is, and the 2016 Code increasingly decides what happens when it cannot pay.

## The three questions that decide LLP or company

Everything so far has been description. The statute's real reader is making a decision, and the decision is governed by three factors, in a definite order. The first is external equity. The second is the tax treatment of distributed profit. The third is compliance capacity. Liability is not on the list, because both forms answer it the same way, and a decision procedure that begins with the factor that does not discriminate will end wherever the sales literature wants it to end.

| The deciding question | Where it points to an LLP | Where it points to a company | Where the general answer fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| Will the business raise external equity? | The capital will come from the partners themselves, from debt, or from a small circle content to hold partnership interests | Investors will expect shares, preference instruments, a valuation, employee stock options and an exit; that machinery exists only in a company | An LLP can admit an investor as a partner, but partnership interest is a negotiated contract stake, not a transferable security, and most institutional funds cannot or will not hold it |
| How are profits taxed on the way out? | The founders want profits taxed once, at the entity, with their shares of profit exempt in their hands and salary and interest to partners deductible within the tax limits | The founders are content to leave profits inside the business, where the concessional company rates can be lower than the LLP's flat rate, and will extract value on exit or by salary | The comparison reverses with the founders' extraction pattern, and it has shifted each time corporate rates and dividend taxation have been rewritten, so any fixed answer ages badly |
| How much compliance can the business carry? | Two annual filings, a threshold-based audit and no board or meeting machinery match a lean founding team | The business has, or will hire, the company secretarial capacity a company demands, and gains the credibility some lenders and enterprise customers attach to the form | The LLP's lighter calendar is enforced by per-day charges for delay, so a business that will neglect filings entirely is safer nowhere; it is merely charged differently |

The equity question is first because it is the only one that is close to irreversible. An LLP has no share capital. Its constitution knows partners and contribution, not shareholders and shares. It cannot issue equity, cannot create preference capital, cannot grant stock options to employees, and cannot offer an investor the standard venture instrument of compulsorily convertible preference shares with a liquidation preference and a board seat. An investor in an LLP must become a partner under the agreement, with whatever management and information rights the agreement negotiates, holding an interest that has no market, no standard transfer mechanism and no established valuation practice. Some angel investors and family offices will invest on those terms. Institutional venture and private equity funds, whose own documents and exit expectations are built around shares, generally will not, and a founder who tells himself that the right fund will make an exception is planning around an exception. If there is a realistic prospect that the business will raise priced equity rounds, the company form is not the conservative choice; it is the only choice, and choosing the LLP to save compliance cost in year one is buying a discount with a refinancing of the entire legal structure in year three.

The tax question is second because it is genuine but unstable, and must be answered on the founders' actual extraction pattern rather than on headline rates. An LLP is taxed as a firm. The Finance (No. 2) Act 2009 wrote the LLP into the Income-tax Act's definition of a firm and of a partner, and the result is a single layer of tax at the entity at a flat rate of thirty per cent, with an alternate minimum tax that can apply where the LLP claims specified deductions. The partner's share of the LLP's profit is then exempt in the partner's hands, while interest on capital and remuneration paid to partners are deductible expenses of the LLP within the limits the tax statute prescribes, and are taxed as income in the receiving partner's hands. A company is taxed at its own rate, which for domestic companies that surrender exemptions and deductions has been brought down to twenty-two per cent, and to fifteen per cent for qualifying new manufacturing companies, and the profit then faces tax again when it is distributed, since the abolition of dividend distribution tax moved the charge onto the shareholder's own income. The detailed law of both regimes is the territory of [the income tax act complete guide](/2012/07/01/income-tax-act-1961-complete-guide/), and the durable point for the form decision is structural. An LLP's profit, once taxed at the entity, reaches the founder clean. A company's profit is taxed more lightly while it stays inside and is taxed again when it leaves. A professional firm whose partners draw out substantially everything they earn each year therefore usually finds the LLP cheaper in total tax despite the higher headline rate. A growth company that retains its earnings to fund expansion usually finds the company's lower entity rate better, and its founders take their return as capital value on exit, where the LLP's exemption of profit share does nothing for them. Founders who quote the thirty per cent against the twenty-two per cent without asking where their money will actually sit have compared the wrong numbers.

The compliance question is third because it is the one founders feel daily but the one that should decide least. A company carries board meetings, minutes, statutory registers, an annual general meeting, auditor appointment with rotation for larger companies, and a filing calendar several times the LLP's length, and it needs a company secretary's habits even where it does not need a company secretary. An LLP carries two annual filings, threshold-based audit, and event filings. For a lean team, that difference is measured in professional fees and founder hours every single year, and it is the legitimate core of the LLP's appeal. But it must be priced honestly, with the per-day default charges of the previous section included: the LLP is cheaper for a business that files, and treacherous for a business that does not. The general guidance in this article is information about a statute and its working, not advice on any individual business, whose facts, sector licences and funding plans can reverse every one of these comparisons and should be tested before the incorporation document is filed.

### Why do professional firms choose the LLP form?

Because the form was designed for them. A firm of accountants, lawyers, architects or consultants can give every partner limited liability for colleagues' defaults, keep profit-sharing and management as matters of private contract, and avoid the board, meeting and share-capital machinery of a company, which fits a practice owned entirely by the people who work in it.

The professional case deserves a paragraph of its own, because it explains the form's shape. A professional practice is owned by its producers. It does not raise outside equity, because professional regulation in most fields forbids or frustrates outside ownership of a practice, and its capital needs are modest: premises, people, insurance and work in progress. Its profits are drawn out annually by the partners who earned them. Its catastrophic risk is a negligence claim against one partner's work. Every one of those facts points the same way under the three questions: no equity, full annual extraction, minimal compliance capacity beyond the practice's own administrators, and a desperate need for the statutory separation between one partner's default and another's assets. The LLP is not a compromise for such a firm. It is the precise instrument. The residual limits are regulatory rather than statutory: each profession's regulator controls who may practise and in what association, multidisciplinary partnerships that would combine, say, legal and accounting practice under one roof remain restricted in India, and the LLP form does not dissolve those restrictions. It gives the permitted practice a better vehicle; it does not widen what is permitted.

## Foreign money and the limits of the form

The LLP Act itself is nationality-blind: a body corporate partner can be Indian or foreign, and a foreign LLP can establish a place of business in India under the Act's provisions for foreign limited liability partnerships. The restrictions that matter live one level up, in the foreign exchange and investment policy framework, and they are the single most common reason the form fails a growing business. Foreign direct investment in LLPs was not permitted at all when the form was created. In 2011 the government opened the door partway, by press note, allowing foreign investment in LLPs operating in sectors where one hundred per cent foreign investment is permitted under the automatic route and where there are no foreign-investment-linked performance conditions attached to the sector. The conditions were telling. Investment had to come in cash through banking channels. An LLP with foreign investment could not make downstream investments of its own, a restriction relaxed only later and only within the same eligible-sector logic. External commercial borrowings were, and in substance remain, closed to LLPs, so a foreign-funded LLP cannot do what a foreign-funded company routinely does and borrow from its parent or from offshore lenders under the borrowing framework.

Each condition is defensible inside the policy that wrote it: the LLP was a new, lightly regulated form, its register thinner than the companies register, and the government was not prepared to let it become an unmonitored channel for foreign money into sensitive sectors. But the cumulative effect on a founder is simple and should be stated without varnish. A business that expects foreign venture or private equity money, foreign strategic investment in a sector with caps or approval conditions, or offshore debt, will find the LLP form closed or awkward at exactly the points where a company is routine. Founders with any realistic foreign-funding future belong in the company form from the first day, and the argument that they can convert later runs into the one-way door described in the conversion section. The restrictions are policy, not statute, and policy in this area changes by press note and consolidated circular; the durable statement is the structural one, that the LLP's access to foreign capital is conditional and derivative in a way the company's is not, and that the position must be re-verified against the investment policy in force whenever it is actually relied on.

## The 2021 amendment: decriminalisation and the small LLP

The Limited Liability Partnership (Amendment) Act 2021, Act 31 of 2021, which received the President's assent on 13 August 2021 and whose operative sections were brought into force on 1 April 2022, is the only substantial amendment the statute has received, and it changed the Act's temperament more than its structure. Its origins lie in the Company Law Committee constituted by the Ministry of Corporate Affairs in 2019, which was asked to review the offences under the LLP Act on the same philosophy the committee had applied to company law: that procedural and technical defaults by businesses should be met with civil penalties assessed inside the department, and that the criminal process, with its courts, its compounding applications and its theoretical jail terms for filing failures, should be reserved for fraud and deliberate wrongdoing. A statute whose register depended on voluntary filing by hundreds of thousands of small entities was being enforced, on paper, by offences; the committee's judgment was that this produced neither compliance nor convictions, only fear and arrears.

The amendment decriminalised twelve offences, converting provisions that had punished default with fines on conviction into civil penalties levied through an in-house adjudication mechanism. It inserted section 76A, under which the Registrar acts as adjudicating officer for penalties, following a hearing, with appeals lying to the Regional Director. It substituted section 39 so that offences punishable with fine alone can be compounded by the Regional Director rather than through the earlier, heavier route. It inserted section 67A, empowering the Central Government to designate special courts for the speedy trial of the offences that remain criminal, graded by the punishment they carry. It omitted section 73, which had penalised non-compliance with the Tribunal's directions in terms the new penalty architecture made redundant. And it moved in the opposite direction where it counted: the punishment for fraud under section 30 was raised from imprisonment of up to two years to imprisonment of up to five years, so that the same statute that stopped threatening founders with prosecution for a late return simultaneously announced that dishonesty would be treated more seriously than before. Read whole, the amendment is a sorting exercise. The defaults of the disorganised were repriced as fees for delay; the conduct of the dishonest was repriced as crime.

The amendment's second project was classification. Following the small company idea in company law, it inserted the definition of a small limited liability partnership in section 2(1)(ta): an LLP whose contribution does not exceed twenty-five lakh rupees, or such higher figure as may be prescribed up to a ceiling of five crore rupees, and whose turnover for the immediately preceding financial year does not exceed forty lakh rupees, or such higher figure as may be prescribed up to a ceiling of fifty crore rupees, together with any LLP meeting other prescribed requirements. Alongside the small LLP, the amendment recognised the start-up LLP, an LLP certified within the government's start-up recognition framework. The classification is not decorative. Penalties for a small or start-up LLP are set at half the amount otherwise leviable, subject to caps of one lakh rupees for the LLP and fifty thousand rupees for each designated partner, and the fee and adjudication architecture gives the class procedural concessions, on the reasoning that a two-person enterprise and a two-hundred-person enterprise should not face the same price for the same missed date. The amendment also rationalised the name provisions, substituting section 17 so that an LLP registered by a name too close to an existing LLP, company or trademark can be directed to change it, with power in the Central Government to allot a new name itself where the direction is ignored, rather than leaving the register polluted by lookalike names nobody could remove. And it recast the residence test for designated partners from one hundred and eighty-two days in the preceding year to one hundred and twenty days during the financial year, a small change that records how the professional class the form serves actually lives.

The small-business policy frame for all of this is the same one that produced the micro, small and medium enterprises legislation, and readers who want the wider statutory architecture for that sector will find it in [the MSME development act explained](/2020/04/15/msme-development-act-2006-explained/). What the 2021 amendment did not do is as significant as what it did. It did not introduce a statutory audit for all LLPs, did not disturb the agreement's contractual supremacy, did not touch the liability scheme except to sharpen the fraud offence, and did not create the missing conversion route back into a company. The legislature's verdict, a decade and more into the experiment, was that the form's design was right and its enforcement psychology was wrong. Whether the civil penalty regime actually produces the filings the criminal regime failed to produce is an implementation question the figures will answer over time; the design intention, at least, is coherent in a way the original offence-heavy scheme was not.

## The liability rules at work: three situations

Abstract liability rules mislead until they are run against facts, and the LLP's scheme is best understood through the three situations its readers actually face. Take first the trade debt. An LLP buys inventory on credit, the venture fails, and the supplier is owed a sum the entity cannot pay. The supplier's claim is against the LLP alone, to the extent of its assets. The partners' houses, savings and other businesses are outside the claim, not because of any kindness in the statute but because section 27 makes the obligation solely the LLP's. The supplier's protection was never the partners' wealth; it was the information on the register, the filed statement of account and solvency in which designated partners declared the entity able to pay its debts, and the contribution the partners stood committed to under the agreement, which the liquidator can call in. A supplier who extended large credit to a thinly contributed LLP without reading its filings has relied on a fund that never existed, and the Act's answer is that the register told him so.

Take second the professional negligence claim, the situation the form was built for. A partner of an accountancy LLP signs off on a negligent audit; a lender relies on it and loses money. The lender's claim lies against the LLP, whose assets and insurance answer for the wrongdoing of a partner acting in the course of its business, and against the negligent partner personally, because the statutory separation protects partners only from each other's defaults, never from their own. The remaining partners, including partners in other cities who never saw the file, are not personally liable. Contrast the partnership firm, where every partner would have stood exposed for the same act, and the design purpose of the entire statute stands revealed in a single comparison. Contrast also the company, where the negligent professional, if an employee or director, would generally leave the claim at the company's door and face personal exposure mainly through professional discipline or exceptional doctrines. The LLP's middle position is deliberate: the enterprise answers, the wrongdoer answers, the bystanders do not.

Take third the dishonest case, because it tests the boundary. The designated partners of a trading LLP take deposits and credit from suppliers while diverting the receipts to a second venture, intending all along that the LLP will be left with the debts. Section 30 removes the ceiling: the liability of the LLP and of every person knowingly party to carrying on the business with intent to defraud creditors is unlimited for those debts, and the criminal limb of the same section, strengthened in 2021, exposes the knowing parties to imprisonment of up to five years. The shield was a rule for allocating business risk among the honest. It was never drafted as an asset-protection scheme against the entity's own victims, and advisers who present incorporation as a way to trade through insolvency at creditors' expense are describing the one use the statute expressly refuses.

## The Registrar, the register and the public record

The administrative machinery is borrowed rather than built, which is part of the form's economy. The Registrars of Companies serve as Registrars of LLPs; the Ministry of Corporate Affairs administers the Act; filings are made electronically through the same portal family as company filings; and each LLP is identified by its Limited Liability Partnership Identification Number, which appears on its documents and correspondence. Section 36 gives any person the right, on payment of the prescribed fee, to inspect the documents kept by the Registrar in relation to an LLP, including the incorporation document, the names and particulars of partners and designated partners, and the filed annual returns and statements of account and solvency, and to obtain certified copies and extracts. The register is therefore not a formality but the form's central institution. Limited liability is a bargain in which the state withdraws the creditors' remedy against the partners' personal estates and substitutes disclosure: the public file is what a creditor gets instead of recourse. Every filing obligation in the Act is an instalment of that bargain, which is why the statute treats filing default not as paperwork failure but as the withdrawal of the very consideration for the shield.

The Central Government holds a reserve power in section 67 that deserves notice, because it makes the LLP Act deliberately incomplete. On notification, provisions of the Companies Act can be directed to apply to LLPs, with such adaptations and modifications as the notification specifies. The legislature knew it was creating a young form beside a mature code, and rather than guess which company-law solutions the LLP would need, it left a valve through which tested provisions could be imported as experience accumulated. The power has been used sparingly, and its existence is the answer to a family of questions about gaps in the LLP scheme: some gaps are oversights, but the statute contemplates that company law is the reservoir from which they may be filled, and the boundary between the two codes is administrative as well as conceptual. The fee provisions complete the machinery: fees are prescribed for incorporation, filing and inspection, and section 69, as substituted by the 2021 amendment, provides for additional fees for delayed filing, differentiated by class of LLP, of the kind the compliance section has already examined in operation.

Names and offices carry more legal weight than founders assume. The name of an LLP must end with Limited Liability Partnership or LLP, must not be undesirable, must not too nearly resemble the name of an existing LLP or company, and must not suggest, without basis, a connection with government or a regulated status. A name is a representation to every future creditor about who they are dealing with, and the 2021 substitution of section 17 tightened the correction mechanism: an LLP registered by a prohibited or lookalike name can be directed to change it, a trademark proprietor can invoke the process, and where the direction is ignored the government may itself allot a new name, which the LLP must then trade under. The registered office is the LLP's legal address for service and the place where its books and registers live; changes must be intimated, and an LLP that cannot be found at its registered office is not being elusive but non-compliant, since findability is again part of the creditors' bargain. None of this machinery is glamorous, and all of it is the substance of the form's credibility: a limited liability vehicle whose participants can be identified, located and read from a public file is a vehicle a stranger can safely lend to, and a vehicle that fails at those tasks is asking strangers to lend blind in exchange for a liability cap, a bargain no legislature intends to offer.

## Where the form came from, and what India changed

The limited liability partnership was invented for a crisis. The first statute of the kind was enacted in Texas in 1991, in the wreckage of the savings and loan failures, when the partners of law and accounting firms faced ruinous personal liability for the defaults of partners they barely knew, in firms whose scale had outgrown the assumption that partnership meant mutual knowledge. The reform that followed separated the two things partnership law had fused: a partner would answer for his own negligence, and the firm would answer with its assets, but the innocent partner's personal estate would no longer stand behind a colleague's error. The idea travelled to the United Kingdom, whose Limited Liability Partnerships Act 2000 made the LLP a body corporate, and to commercial jurisdictions across Asia and the Gulf. India received the idea through the committee process described earlier, and the Indian statute of 2008 follows the British corporate model rather than the American contractual one: the LLP is a body corporate with separate personality, not a partnership with a liability annexure.

One Indian choice deserves emphasis because it decides the tax analysis later. The United Kingdom taxes its LLPs transparently, looking through the entity to the members. India's Income-tax Act took the other fork: it classified the LLP with the partnership firm, taxing the entity at a flat rate and exempting the partner's share of profit in his hands, the same settlement Indian tax law had long applied to firms. The choice made the LLP immediately intelligible to every Indian tax practitioner, since it inherited the firm's entire tax jurisprudence on remuneration, interest on capital and profit shares, and it avoided the disruption of a look-through regime. It also fixed the comparison that the decision section works through: the LLP's tax outcome is the firm's tax outcome wearing corporate personality, and founders who model it as a cheap company, rather than as an incorporated firm, model it wrongly from the first line.

The neighbours of the form mark its boundaries. Below it sits the partnership firm: no incorporation, no separate personality, no filing with the Registrar of Companies, unlimited liability, and a constitution of pure contract, still the right answer for tiny, short-lived or family ventures that value invisibility and simplicity over protection and are honest about the risk they carry. Beside it sits the one person company, introduced by the Companies Act 2013 for the solo founder: full company form, separate personality, limited liability, one member, and the whole compliance calendar of a company in exchange for a share structure a single founder controls. Between them the LLP occupies the ground the others cannot reach: two or more owners, no outside equity, professional or closely held business, contractual interior, corporate exterior. A founder who can state which of those descriptions fits his venture has usually finished the decision; the three questions in the decision section are simply the disciplined way of reaching the same statement.

## Capital, drawings and the discipline companies have and LLPs do not

Company law surrounds capital with a protective doctrine: shares are issued for consideration under regulated procedures, capital cannot be returned to shareholders except through buy-back, reduction or winding up under supervision, and dividends can be paid only out of profits computed by statutory rules. The doctrine exists because a company's creditors lend against a fund the shareholders must not be free to drain. The LLP Act contains no equivalent doctrine, and the absence is a considered feature of the form. A partner's contribution is whatever the agreement says it is; it can be modest; it can, by agreement, be returned or adjusted as the partners arrange their affairs; profits are divided as the agreement directs and drawn as the agreement permits. The flexibility is why family businesses and professional firms find the form congenial: money moves in and out of the entity on contractual terms, without capital-reduction proceedings or distributable-profit computations.

The creditor's counterweight is thinner and founders should understand exactly how thin. It consists of the statement of account and solvency, in which designated partners declare annually that the LLP can pay its debts; the contribution obligation recorded in the agreement, enforceable in winding up where credit was extended in reliance on it; the restoration rule for distributions taken by a partner who knew the LLP could not then pay its debts; and the fraud provision waiting behind all of it. A company creditor has a capital fund protected by doctrine. An LLP creditor has a solvency declaration, a contributions ledger and the personal exposure of designated partners who signed falsely. Whether that exchange is acceptable depends on the credit decision being made, and sophisticated counterparties price it: banks lending to LLPs routinely take personal guarantees from the partners, which contractually restores the unlimited exposure the statute removed, for the debts that matter. The shield in practice is therefore strongest against the uncontracted claim, the negligence claimant and the trade creditor who did not bargain for a guarantee, and weakest where a lender was in a position to demand one. An honest account of the form must include that qualification, because a large share of the liability protection founders believe they have purchased has been signed away at the first bank facility, and the statute was never the instrument that gave it away.

## Investigation and the enforcement ladder

Between the annual filing regime and winding up, the Act keeps a middle tier of enforcement that operates when something inside an LLP appears to be wrong rather than merely late. The Central Government may appoint one or more competent persons as inspectors to investigate the affairs of an LLP, and the triggers track the company-law model: the Tribunal's direction, the application of partners holding not less than one-fifth of the total number of partners, the Registrar's report suggesting the affairs are being conducted with intent to defraud creditors or partners or for a fraudulent or unlawful purpose, or the government's own opinion that circumstances warrant investigation. An inspector can require the production of books and documents, examine partners, employees and agents on oath, and extend the investigation into related entities where the trail leads there. Partners and officers who obstruct, refuse information or destroy records commit offences under the Act, and the whistle-blowing provision in section 31, which allows a partner's penalty exposure to be mitigated where he gives useful information in such an investigation, belongs to this machinery: it exists to break the solidarity of a partnership that is being run for the benefit of some of its members against the others or against its creditors.

The findings of an inspector do not themselves decide anything, and the Act is careful about the sequence. The report may ground a prosecution where it discloses offences, may ground proceedings by the government for winding up where it discloses the statutory grounds, and may ground recovery proceedings in the LLP's name against those whose conduct the report condemns. Around this central mechanism sit the Act's offence provisions in their post-2021 shape: criminal liability retained for fraud, for false statements in incorporation and other material documents, for obstruction of inspectors and for the impersonation and holding-out offences that attack the register's credibility, and civil penalties for the long tail of filing and procedural defaults, assessed by the Registrar as adjudicating officer with the appeal to the Regional Director that section 76A provides. False statements deserve a word of their own, because the incorporation bargain depends on them being dangerous: a person who makes a statement in a document required under the Act knowing it to be false in a material particular faces imprisonment, and that threat is what stands behind every certificate a designated partner signs. The enforcement ladder, read whole, has three rungs in ascending order: fees and civil penalties for lateness and default; investigation and prosecution for falsity and fraud; and termination, by winding up or strike-off, for the entity that cannot or will not live inside the register. Each rung corresponds to a distinct failure, and the 2021 amendment's real achievement was to stop the ladder's users from treating every failure as the same one.

## Ending an LLP: dissolution, winding up and the struck-off register

An LLP does not end by neglect, and it does not end by the partners simply stopping. It ends by one of three legal routes, and the differences between them carry consequences the partners inherit personally if they choose wrongly. The first route is voluntary winding up under section 63, initiated by the LLP itself where its affairs can be wound up and its liabilities settled, conducted under the winding up rules with a liquidator, realisation of assets, payment of creditors and distribution of any surplus among the partners according to the agreement. The second route is winding up by the Tribunal under section 64, on the grounds already catalogued, including inability to pay debts and the just and equitable ground, which is the route a creditor or a locked-out partner will invoke when the voluntary route is being refused or abused. The third route is administrative: strike-off by the Registrar under section 75 where the LLP is defunct, whether on the Registrar's own motion after enquiry or on the LLP's application once its liabilities are extinguished and the prescribed declarations made. On dissolution following winding up, the entity ceases to exist, and the partners' exposure narrows to what the winding up has settled and what the law preserves against them, including the restoration and fraud liabilities already described.

Two practical truths govern exits. The first is that an LLP carrying unresolved tax assessments, open bank facilities or contingent liabilities cannot be honestly closed by paperwork alone, and partners who dissolve over the top of a contingent liability discover that the liability has survived the entity in the winding-up rules' preservation provisions. The second is that strike-off obtained while liabilities remain is a deferral, not an ending: the Act and the rules preserve the liability of the partners and designated partners as if the LLP had not been dissolved for the purposes of obligations incurred, and restoration of a struck-off LLP to the register is possible at the instance of creditors and others aggrieved within the prescribed period, reviving the entity for the purpose of being sued. The exit discipline is therefore the mirror of the entry discipline. Incorporation was cheap because the statute trusted the register; exit is formal because the register's readers, including creditors who lent on the faith of filed solvency statements, are entitled to a legible ending. Founders who budget for their venture should budget its funeral at the same time, and the cost of a clean voluntary winding up, while real, is a small fraction of the accumulated price of the unfiled alternative.

## What the courts have and have not settled

Measured against company law, the case law on the LLP Act is thin, and the honest treatment of the judicial record is to say which questions the thinness leaves open. Three areas matter most. The first is insolvency, already examined: the proposition that the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code reaches LLPs through its definition of corporate person is the settled part, and tribunals have entertained and admitted proceedings against LLPs on that footing; the unsettled part is the detailed junction between the Code's process and the LLP agreement's internal economics, which will be built decision by decision, and founders drafting agreements would be unwise to assume the Code will respect clauses written as if insolvency law did not exist. The second area is partner liability at the margins: the statute allocates liability with unusual clarity, but litigation will in time test its edges, including the reach of holding out where a former partner's name lingers in digital rather than paper form, the knowledge element of the fraud provision where one designated partner signs and another diverts, and the circumstances in which a court will look through the form at a partner conducting the LLP's business as a personal venture. On each of these the text gives strong indications and the case law gives few answers, and an article that reported a settled doctrine would be inventing one.

The third area is tax, where the litigation is real but belongs to the Income-tax Act rather than to this statute: disputes over the deductibility of partner remuneration and interest, over the conversion exemptions and their conditions, and over the alternate minimum tax's application to LLPs claiming development incentives, have been fought on the tax statute's text, with the LLP figuring as the taxpayer rather than as the subject. The pattern across all three areas is the same and it is instructive about the form's maturity. Where the LLP Act's text is mechanical, incorporation, filing, designation, conversion, it has generated administration rather than litigation. Where it meets older and larger bodies of law, insolvency, tax and professional regulation, the meeting points are where the development is happening, and they are developing in the ordinary common-law way: tribunal decision, appellate correction, and gradual settlement. A reader relying on any proposition in this territory should treat the structural statements in this article as the stable ground and the frontier questions as genuinely open, because on the present record that is what they are.

## The agreement drafted properly: what the boilerplate leaves out

Because the Act leaves the interior to contract, the quality of an LLP is largely the quality of its agreement, and the statutory incorporation form agreement, which many small LLPs adopt unchanged, answers almost none of the questions that eventually divide partners. A properly drafted agreement works through the points where the First Schedule's defaults and the partners' expectations diverge. It fixes contributions in money and in kind, states whether further capital can be called and by what majority, and records whether contribution bears interest. It separates profit share from remuneration, because the default rule that no partner is paid for acting in the business is the opposite of what working partners expect: salaries for the partners who run the venture, interest on capital for the partners who funded it, and profit shares for ownership are three different payments, and an agreement that does not distinguish them will distribute the year's profit by the Schedule's equality to partners who contributed unequally and worked unequally. It allocates management: which decisions the designated partners take alone, which need a majority, and which, borrowing the Schedule's own instinct, need unanimity because they change the deal itself, including admission of partners, change of business, sale of the undertaking and amendment of the agreement.

It then does the work founders least want to do and most need: it prices departure and death before either happens. It states how a retiring partner's interest is valued, by formula, by valuation, or by book value adjusted as specified; how the continuing partners pay for it and over what period; what happens to the outgoing partner's undrawn profits and capital; and whether a deceased partner's estate takes a payment or a continuing interest. It addresses expulsion with the precision section 24 and the Schedule demand, because a majority that believes it can expel and cannot will litigate its belief at the worst moment. It writes the restraints the partners actually intend, non-competition and non-solicitation on exit, in terms narrow enough to be enforceable. And it chooses the dispute route deliberately: arbitration, which keeps a professional firm's quarrels private and is the near-universal choice in practice, or the courts and tribunals, with the agreement's own internal escalation, negotiation, then mediation, then arbitration, written as steps rather than sentiments. The Registrar's file will hold the agreement and every amendment, so the document is semi-public within the inspection regime; partners who want genuine confidentiality for particular commercial terms handle them in side arrangements whose enforceability then depends on ordinary contract law rather than on the Act. None of this drafting is statutory. All of it is what the statute, by its silence, instructs the partners to supply.

## Conversion in practice: the firm that becomes an LLP

The busiest conversion route has been the partnership firm moving into the form, and its choreography shows the Schedules working as designed. The firm first settles its internal position: the partners who will carry into the LLP, the capital accounts as they will stand, the property the firm holds and the licences it trades under. The application to the Registrar carries the particulars the Second Schedule prescribes, including the firm's registration details where it is registered under the 1932 Act, its creditors and any pending litigation, because the Schedule makes the Registrar the guardian of the transition's honesty. On the certificate of conversion issuing, the firm dissolves by operation of the Schedule, its property vests in the LLP without conveyance, its debts become the LLP's debts, and its contracts and proceedings continue with the LLP substituted. The partners must then notify the conversion to the land and registration authorities, the tax authorities, the firm's bankers and licensing bodies within the periods the Schedule sets, because vesting by law does not update anyone's records by itself, and the practical failures of conversion are almost all failures of notification: a bank mandate in the firm's name, a shop licence in the firm's name, a lease the landlord was never told about. The tax exemption conditions examined earlier are tested at this point by the revenue authorities on the firm's facts, and a firm whose partners intend to change their ratios at the moment of conversion, or to bring in a new participant as part of the move, should expect the exemption to be contested, because the conditions are built to permit a change of clothing and to tax a change of ownership. Companies converting under the Third and Fourth Schedules follow the same architecture with the additional discipline their shareholder structure imposes: the conversion must carry the whole company, all its shareholders becoming partners, its creditors addressed, and a company with charges on its assets or with public shareholders outside the permitted classes cannot use the route at all.

## The decision applied: four ventures

A two-person design consultancy, owned by its founders, billing clients for the founders' own work, illustrates the form at its most natural. It will raise no equity; its clients buy the founders' skill and no investor will ever be offered a stake. Its profits are its founders' incomes, drawn out each year almost in full. Its compliance capacity is an accountant retained for filings. Every deciding factor points the same way: the LLP gives the founders protection against each other's errors and against the business's debts, taxes the profit once at the entity with their shares exempt, and asks for two filings a year. Incorporating as a company would buy them a share structure they will never use at the price of a compliance calendar they will resent annually. The only genuine condition is discipline: the consultancy is exactly the kind of small, confident enterprise that forgets the October filing, and its founders should build the calendar into the accountant's retainer on day one.

A software venture that intends to raise institutional capital is the mirror image. Its founders may have no revenue, no compliance burden worth mentioning and no professional licence in play, and the LLP would be cheaper for its first two years by a margin that does not matter. What matters is the term sheet in year three: a fund offering priced investment for preference shares, a liquidation preference, anti-dilution protection and an employee option pool. None of those instruments exists in the LLP's vocabulary. The founders would face the choice between refusing the money and rebuilding the venture inside a new company, transferring contracts, intellectual property and employees across, with the tax friction and the diligence questions that a mid-flight restructure invites. The venture belongs in a private limited company from incorporation, and the LLP's lower early cost is the most expensive saving available to it. The same logic governs, with less drama, any business whose credible plan includes an acquisition exit, since acquirers buy shares and share purchase agreements far more readily than they buy partnership interests and business transfers.

A family manufacturing business, owned by three siblings, funded by a bank facility and reinvesting most of its profit, sits between the poles and shows why the second question, tax on extraction, cannot be answered by slogan. The equity question is neutral: no outsider will invest. The compliance question favours the LLP mildly. The tax question turns on the drawings pattern. If the siblings draw most profits as income, the LLP's single layer of tax at the entity, with deductible remuneration and interest within the statutory limits, is attractive. If they retain profits to fund machinery, the company's concessional rate on retained earnings pulls the other way, and the bank's practice settles what the tax analysis leaves open, since the facility will be supported by the siblings' personal guarantees in either form, neutralising the liability difference for the debt that dominates the balance sheet. The honest answer for this venture is that the forms are close, the guarantee has already decided the liability question, and the choice should be made on the drawings pattern and on the family's succession intentions, since partnership interests under a drafted agreement can be arranged to pass within a family with a flexibility that share transfers, with their valuation and stamp consequences, do not always match.

A mid-sized chartered accountancy firm with forty partners closes the set, because it is the case the committees had in view. Its partners cannot practicably incorporate their practice as an ordinary company under the professional framework within which they work; its negligence exposure is the classic sections 27 and 28 case; its profits are drawn annually; and its internal constitution, profit points, admission of partners, retirement ages and the valuation of a departing partner's share, is exactly the contractual interior the Act leaves to the agreement. For this firm the LLP is not one option among several. It is the difference between a practice whose growth multiplies each partner's personal risk and a practice whose growth multiplies only its insurance bill, and the wave of professional firms that converted in the form's first years was the market delivering the verdict the Naresh Chandra Committee had predicted.

## Five beliefs about LLPs that the statute does not support

The first belief is that an LLP is a partnership with a new name, so partnership law governs it. Section 4 disposes of this: the Indian Partnership Act 1932 does not apply. The consequences are not pedantic. The implied agency rules, the dissolution on a partner's death, the fiduciary framework as the 1932 Act states it, none of these supply the LLP's law; the agreement, the Schedules and the 2008 Act itself do, and advice that reasons from firm law to LLP outcomes will be wrong at exactly the points where the two regimes differ, which are the points that matter.

The second belief is that an LLP has no compliance worth the name. The inventory in this article disposes of it: two annual filings, a solvency declaration signed by named individuals, event filings within thirty days, an audited statement of account above modest thresholds, and a fee regime that charges for delay by the day. The belief persists because incorporation agents sell the form by comparison with a company's calendar, and lighter-than-a-company has been misheard, at scale, as nothing-at-all. The dormant LLP population and the 2020 settlement scheme are the statistical footprint of the mishearing.

The third belief is that limited liability means the partners can never be reached. The statute disposes of it four times over: a partner answers for his own wrongful acts; every knowing party answers without limit where the business is carried on to defraud; a sole remaining partner who trades on past six months answers personally for that period; and a person who lets himself be held out as a partner answers to those who gave credit on the faith of it. Add the bank guarantee, which restores by contract what the statute removed, and the belief is not an approximation of the law but its opposite.

The fourth belief is that an LLP can take investment like a company if the documents are drafted cleverly enough. It cannot issue shares, options or convertible instruments, because the Act's capital concept is contribution under a contract, and no drafting converts a partnership interest into a security. Investors who appear to have invested in LLPs have become partners, with negotiated rights, or have lent money. The belief survives because the word investment covers both, and it dissolves the moment a founder asks the investor which instrument he expects to hold.

The fifth belief is that the tax answer is fixed and known: that the LLP is simply taxed better, or worse, than a company. The statute book disposes of it by having moved. Dividend taxation has been redesigned, company rates have been cut conditionally, and the alternate minimum tax has been adjusted, each change reordering the comparison for different extraction patterns. The durable truths are the structural ones set out in the decision section: LLP profit is taxed once at the entity and reaches the partner exempt; company profit is taxed at the entity at rates that reward retention and again on distribution. Any adviser who quotes a winner without asking how the founders take their money out is selling the belief, not the analysis.

## The case against the light form, stated fairly

A statute pillar owes its critics a hearing, and the LLP has serious ones. The regulatory critique runs as follows. The form grants the central privilege of incorporation, limited liability, in exchange for a disclosure and audit regime far thinner than the company's: no mandatory audit below thresholds a modest business crosses only slowly, no public financial statements of company-law depth, no governance organs, no meetings, no independent directors, and an internal constitution that is a private bargain. Where an LLP is a two-person practice, that thinness is proportionate. Where an LLP is a substantial trading enterprise with hundreds of employees and large borrowings, the same thinness looks like regulatory arbitrage: the enterprise has chosen the form precisely to escape the scrutiny its scale would attract as a company, and its creditors, employees and the revenue are the counterparties who bear the opacity. The critique gains force from the adoption pattern recorded earlier in this article: a form designed for professionals became a general-purpose small company substitute, and the law's thresholds, drawn for the intended user, govern the actual one.

The defence is equally concrete, and it begins by denying that the comparison set is the public company. For the businesses that actually use the form, the alternative was never a fully compliant private company with a company secretary and audited statements; it was the partnership firm or the proprietorship, forms with no incorporation, no register, no filings and unlimited liability. Measured against that baseline, the LLP is a large increase in disclosure: a public register, an annual return, a signed solvency statement, audited accounts above the thresholds, and designated partners personally answerable for the filings. The form did not drain the companies register of scrutinised entities; it drew unregistered businesses onto a register for the first time. The defence adds that scale is addressed by triggers rather than by form: audit thresholds, tax audit under the Income-tax Act at its own higher thresholds, sectoral regulation, lender-imposed audit and information covenants, and the fraud and investigation machinery all scale with the enterprise even where the incorporation statute does not. On the evidence of the register's growth, the legislature's wager, that a light form honestly administered collects more disclosure than a heavy form widely evaded, has substantially been borne out. The honest verdict sits between the two cases. The LLP is well designed for its intended and its actual core users; its genuine vulnerability is the substantial enterprise that uses the light regime as a cloak, and the correct policy response to that vulnerability is the direction the law has in fact been travelling: thresholds, standards under section 34A, and penalty reform, tightening the regime by size rather than abolishing the form's lightness for the small.

## The designated partner as the regime's hinge

Return, finally, to the office on which the whole light regime pivots. The designated partner is the legislature's answer to the obvious objection to a light form: if the state is not going to supervise the entity closely, someone identifiable must be supervisable instead. The office's duties are administrative, but its exposure is personal in ways founders systematically underprice. The designated partner signs the statement of account and solvency, and a solvency declaration made without reasonable grounds is not a filing error but a false statement on the public record. He carries the penalties for the LLP's defaults under section 8 unless the Act provides otherwise, which means that the per-day accruals and, after 2021, the civil penalties assessed against the entity have a named individual standing behind them in the adjudication. His identification number ties his record across entities, so a pattern of default follows the person and not merely the venture, and the annual know-your-customer obligation for that number keeps the tie current. And when the Registrar or an inspector comes to ask who was responsible for a decision, a filing or an omission, the designated partner is where the question lands first.

The practice that follows is unglamorous and worth stating as plainly as the statute states the office. Designate the partners who actually run the business and actually control its filings; an LLP that designates a figurehead has not transferred the risk but concealed it, and concealment converts administrative default into the false-statement territory where the Act's criminal provisions live. Keep the number of designated partners small enough that responsibility is real and large enough that the two-designated-partner floor survives a resignation, a death or a disqualification without a scramble. Diarise the office's year: the annual return by the end of May, the solvency statement by the end of October, the identification-number renewal in its season, and the thirty-day intimations whenever the partnership changes. The founders who treat the designated partner as the regime's hinge, rather than as a signature the incorporation agent requires, are the founders for whom the light form stays light.

## Incorporation in sequence: what the law actually asks

The incorporation sequence is short enough to state in full, and stating it exposes how much of the form's lightness is front-loaded into the agreement. The proposed partners first secure their identification: each designated partner obtains a Designated Partner Identification Number on the statutory application, with the identity and address proof the rules prescribe. The name is reserved through the Registrar's facility, tested against the undesirability and resemblance rules already described, and held for the prescribed period while the incorporation is completed. The incorporation document is then filed, stating the name, the proposed registered office address, the business to be carried on, and the particulars of the partners and designated partners, and carrying the compliance statement that the requirements of the Act and the rules have been met, made by an advocate, a company secretary, a chartered accountant or a cost accountant engaged in the formation, together with one of the designated partners. The Registrar, satisfied that the document complies, registers it and issues the certificate of incorporation, which is conclusive evidence that the LLP is incorporated by the name in it, and allots the identification number by which the entity is permanently known.

What founders routinely miss is the step after incorporation. Registration creates the body; the agreement gives it its constitution, and the agreement must be filed with the Registrar within thirty days of incorporation, with every later change to it filed within thirty days of the change. An LLP incorporated on the Registrar's standard-form agreement and never revisited is, in the eyes of the law, governed by the First Schedule's defaults, whoever drafted what in side correspondence. The sequence also explains the respective roles in the form's later life: the certificate is a one-time event, the agreement is a living document, and the two annual filings are the pulses by which the register knows the entity still lives. Measured against the incorporation of a company, with its memorandum and articles, its subscriber shares, its capital clauses and its first board, the LLP's sequence is genuinely shorter, but the steps it omits are not abolished. They are deferred into the agreement, and the quality of the incorporation is therefore the quality of the drafting that follows it.

## The LLP in ordinary business life

The form's behaviour under the ordinary accidents of business is determined less by the 2008 Act than by the rest of the statute book applying to the LLP as it applies to any enterprise, and a reader should know where the boundaries of this article's subject lie. As an employer, an LLP owes the same duties as any establishment: provident fund, insurance, gratuity and workplace legislation apply to it by their own terms, and the partnership form of the owners changes nothing for the employees. As a taxpayer it occupies the position described in the decision section, including registration, deduction of tax at source on its payments, and goods and services tax registration where its supplies cross the thresholds, exactly as a firm would. As a borrower it signs as a body corporate, offers its assets as security, and discovers, as noted earlier, that lenders convert its statutory shield into personal guarantees by contract. As a litigant it sues and is sued in its registered name, and the designated partners conduct the litigation for it. As a licence-holder it applies in its own name, and sectoral licences, from food safety to financial services, are granted or refused on the sector statute's criteria for the applicant's fitness, not on the incorporation form, though some regulated activities restrict which forms may hold the licence at all. The point of the catalogue is the form's foundational idea restated from the other side: incorporation statutes decide personality, liability and internal government, and then fall silent, leaving the enterprise to the general law. The LLP Act is unusually silent by design, and the silence is why the agreement, the tax code and the sectoral licence so often matter more to an LLP's daily life than the Act that created it.

## A map of the eighty-one sections

A statute pillar should leave its reader able to find anything in the Act without assistance, and the Act's arrangement rewards a single guided pass. The opening sections, sections 1 to 10, are the constitution of the form: commencement and definitions, including the definitions of the LLP, the partner, the designated partner, contribution and, since 2021, the small LLP; section 3's declaration of body corporate status; section 4's exclusion of partnership law; section 5's wide conception of who may be a partner, with eligibility governed by section 22; section 6's minimum of two partners; sections 7 and 8's designated partner scheme; section 9's rule for changes in designated partners; and section 10's punishment for contravention of the designated partner provisions. Sections 11 to 21 govern birth and identity: incorporation and its document, the effect of registration, the name provisions and their rectification, the registered office, and the publication of the name and of the entity's limited liability. Sections 22 to 26 are the interior constitution: eligibility to be a partner, the agreement and the First Schedule, cessation of partnership interest, registration of changes in partners, and the agency rule. Sections 27 to 33 are the liability and money core: the extent of the LLP's liability and of a partner's, holding out, fraud, whistle-blowing, and the two contribution provisions. Sections 34 to 35 are the disclosure code, accounts, the section 34A standards power and the annual return. Sections 36 to 41 complete the disclosure chapter: inspection of documents, the penalty for false statements, the Registrar's power to obtain information, compounding, and the enforcement of the duty to make returns. Section 42 gives a partner a transferable interest in the LLP; sections 43 to 54 carry the investigation chapter through to the inspector's report as evidence; sections 55 to 58 govern conversion from a firm, a private company and an unlisted public company; and section 59 provides for foreign limited liability partnerships. Sections 60 to 65 carry compromise, arrangement and reconstruction, and then winding up and dissolution. The closing chapter gathers the miscellany: the section 67 power to apply provisions of company law to LLPs, the special courts inserted as sections 67A to 67C, electronic filing, section 69's additional fee for delayed filing, the general penalties in section 74, strike-off of defunct LLPs under section 75, and the adjudication mechanism of section 76A inserted by the 2021 amendment, completing the enforcement picture. A reader who holds this map can locate any question the form raises in the correct chapter, which is most of what statutory literacy about the Act consists of.

## The verdict on the 2008 settlement

The Limited Liability Partnership Act 2008 made three wagers. It wagered that India's professional and closely held businesses wanted corporate personality without corporate government, and would come onto a public register to get it; the register's growth from a standing start to several lakh active entities, with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs counting 3,87,808 active LLPs as on 31 March 2025, is the evidence that the demand was real. It wagered that a contractual interior, default rules in a Schedule and a named class of designated partners carrying the compliance load, could substitute for the boardrooms and meetings of company law without leaving creditors defenceless; the absence of any systemic LLP scandal on the companies scale, and the register's survival of the form's first decade and a half, support the wager, with the dormant-filing population standing as its documented qualification rather than its refutation. And it wagered, in 2021, that civil penalties would collect the filings that criminal offences had merely threatened; that wager is the youngest of the three and the one whose returns are still coming in.

What the Act did not attempt is equally part of its record. It did not create an investable vehicle, and the founders who needed venture equity were left, correctly, to the Companies Act. It did not merge the LLP into the insolvency code's design from the start, and the junction between the two statutes is being worked out in litigation rather than in either text. It did not provide a way back, and the one-way door from company to LLP remains the sharpest practical warning the form carries. And it did not attempt to govern the professions it serves, leaving admission, discipline and the boundaries of practice to the regulators who already held them. A pillar article's closing judgment can therefore be precise. The LLP is a compliance choice dressed in the language of a liability choice: both it and the company limit liability, and the founder who chooses between them on that ground has not yet begun to choose. The choice is made by the equity the business will need, the tax path of the profit the founders will draw, and the filings the business will honestly sustain, and a statute that forces those three questions into the open, as this one does, has done more for its reader than a form that merely promises to be lighter. Readers preparing the corporate law cluster for examination will find the surrounding statutes organised in [the corporate law exam study guide](/2012/06/15/corporate-law-exam-study-guide/).

One closing word on keeping the record straight, in both senses. Founders who work through this decision should keep the documents the decision generates, the agreement and its amendments, the solvency statements, the conversion records and the professional correspondence that records why one form was chosen over another, in an organised private file of their own rather than scattered across inboxes and agents' offices. The same consideration leads some readers to keep their own statute notes and working papers in a private encrypted workspace, and the account of [how an office file viewer opens Excel, Word and PowerPoint files without the full office suite](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) describes that discipline of keeping working papers readable and to hand in practical terms. The LLP's register is public where the statute makes it public. The founder's reasoning, negotiations and advice are not, and keeping them that way is the final compliance the form quietly expects.

## Reading an LLP's public file before you trust it

The register's purpose is realised when someone reads it, and the file of an LLP, read in sequence, tells a disciplined story that founders should assume their serious counterparties will read. The incorporation document establishes the entity's birth, its registered office and its original partners. The filed agreement and its amendments reveal the constitution: how profits are shared, who manages, what the contribution obligations are, and, by the frequency and direction of amendments, how stable the founding bargain has been. The intimations of partner and designated partner changes draw the entity's personnel history; a file showing a rotating cast of designated partners, with resignations clustered around filing deadlines, is describing an entity whose compliance is being passed like a parcel, and the pattern is legible to anyone who looks. The annual returns give the snapshot of partners and solvency year by year, and the statements of account and solvency carry the substance: turnover, contribution, the balance of assets and liabilities, and the declaration itself, which an alert reader compares across years for the years it is missing altogether.

The absences speak. A gap in the statement filings of more than a year or two, in an entity still trading, tells a creditor that the entity is already inside the per-day charge regime and either cannot or will not regularise, and that the five-consecutive-years filing default ground for Tribunal winding up is accruing in the background. A contribution figure that has never changed since incorporation, in a business whose turnover has visibly grown, tells the reader that the entity trades on credit and guarantees rather than on partners' money, which is lawful and disclosed but changes the credit decision. An audit status inconsistent with the thresholds, an LLP above the audit line filing unaudited statements, signals a designated partner who signs without reading. None of this requires inside knowledge. It requires only the inspection right in section 36 and an hour, which is precisely the exchange the Act designed: privacy of the interior bargain, in return for a public file honest enough to price. Founders should read their own file as a creditor would once a year, because the file, and not the brochure, is what the form actually says to the world.

For the aspirant, the same file is the examination's favourite illustration of the statute's theory. Questions on this Act return persistently to the locations where the body's personality, the partners' contract and the Registrar's supervision meet: the effect of registration, the agency rule in section 26 and its asymmetry with firm law, the four exceptions to limited liability, the First Schedule's defaults and their displacement by agreement, the five-year filing default ground for winding up, and the 2021 amendment's conversion of offences into penalties. Each of those is a point where the Act's design can be stated as a reason rather than a rule, and the examiner rewards the reason. The LLP was created because partnership's risk and company's weight both failed the professional middle of Indian business; it governs itself by contract because its members are presumed to be the people in the room; it discloses because strangers must lend to it; and it designates partners because a light state needs a named throat, in the unlovely phrase, to stand behind the filings. A candidate who can say those four sentences and then prove them from the sections has understood the statute as its drafters understood it.

## The rules beneath the Act: how the regime is actually administered

The Act's eighty-one sections are the skeleton; the Limited Liability Partnership Rules 2009, amended repeatedly, are the working flesh, and a pillar account should say plainly that most of what a founder's adviser does lives in the rules rather than the statute. The rules prescribe the forms in which the Act's events are transacted: the incorporation document and its integrated filing, the intimation of partner and designated partner changes, the filing of the agreement and its amendments, the statement of account and solvency, the annual return, the application for conversion, and the applications for strike-off and winding up. They prescribe the fees, including the slab structure by contribution, and the additional fees for delay. They govern the manner of the Registrar's inspection facility, the maintenance of the register, the circumstances in which documents may be filed with additional fees during condonation and settlement exercises, and the detailed procedure for the adjudication of penalties that the 2021 amendment introduced. The 2022 amendments to the rules then operationalised the amended Act: revised forms for incorporation and for the reporting of significant beneficial ownership by partners, the mechanism by which the Central Government allots a fresh name to an LLP that defies a rectification direction, and the procedural detail of the Regional Director compounding and appellate routes.

Two administrative developments complete the picture. The first is the unification of identity: the designated partner identification number regime has been aligned with the director identification number regime under company law, so that an individual who holds a director identification number uses that number in the designated partner role, and the annual know-your-customer filing for the number has become the register's device for keeping its human map current. The second is the digitisation of the LLP's life cycle on the corporate affairs portal, where name reservation, incorporation, agreement filing, annual filings and strike-off applications are all electronic transactions, signed digitally by the designated partners. Digitisation changed the regime's character in one way founders feel and rarely name: defaults became computable. A paper regime discovers late filings slowly and punishes them episodically; a digital register knows the day a filing falls due, accrues the per-day fee automatically, and can identify the entire defaulting population for a settlement scheme or a strike-off drive in a single query. The harshness and the mercy of the modern LLP regime, the automatic accrual and the periodic amnesties alike, are products of that computability, and the 2021 shift to civil penalties is best understood as the legislature adjusting the law's temperament to the register's new ability to see everything.

## Perpetual succession at work

The phrase perpetual succession in section 3 is easy to read past, and it is one of the form's most valuable gifts in practice. A partnership firm, in law, is its partners in aggregate: when a partner dies, retires or is admitted, the firm is technically reconstituted, its contracts must be examined for continuity, its bank accounts and licences sit in names that have changed their legal meaning, and the dissolution rules of the 1932 Act hover over every change. An LLP suffers none of this. The body corporate persists unchanged in identity while its membership rotates beneath it. The contract the LLP signed binds the LLP the year after the signing partner retires. The lease, the bank facility, the government tender and the client engagement survive the death of a founding partner without novation, because the counterparty was always the entity and never the man. Bank mandates and authorised signatories change, as they would in a company; the obligations do not move at all.

The practical dividend appears at the moments businesses are least able to handle paperwork: succession. A family business held as an LLP can admit the next generation as partners, retire the founders in stages, and adjust profit shares and management rights by amending the agreement, while the entity's contracts, employees, licences and banking relationships continue untouched in the entity's name. The agreement's retirement and death provisions, discussed in the drafting section, do the legal work; the corporate form does the continuity work. Compare the firm, where the same generational transition is commonly handled by dissolving and reconstituting, with fresh registrations, fresh accounts and a fresh paper trail for every asset. Compare also the company, where succession is orderly but runs through share transmission, valuation at each transfer, and the stamp and tax consequences of moving equity. The LLP's succession is contractual and internal, and for the large class of Indian businesses whose deepest problem is surviving their founders, that quiet property of section 3 may be worth more than the liability shield that headlines the form. It is, fittingly, the property the name of the institution states first: the partnership continues, though the partners change.

## Before the incorporation document is filed

The decision this article has built can be run, in the end, in an afternoon, and the founders who run it well ask their questions in the statute's own order. They ask first whether any plausible future for the business involves outside equity, foreign investment in a capped or conditioned sector, offshore borrowing or an employee option pool, and if any answer is yes they stop: the company form is indicated, and the remaining questions are refinement. If the answers are no, they ask how the money leaves the business, because the tax comparison between the forms is a question about drawings and retention, and they model their own pattern, at their own income levels, under the rates and the dividend regime in force at the time, knowing the answer expires with the next redesign and the structure of the comparison does not. They then ask the compliance question with the failure case priced in: who owns the calendar, what the accountant's retainer actually covers, and what a year of neglect would cost under the per-day regime, including the effect on the designated partners who will sign. They ask, before any document is filed, what the agreement must contain for these particular partners: the separation of salary, interest and profit share, the valuation of a departing interest, the expulsion power, the restraints, and the dispute route, because incorporation is the cheapest moment in the venture's life to buy that drafting. And they ask the succession question the form rewards: how the entity is expected to outlive its founders, and whether the agreement's death and retirement clauses say so. A founding team that can answer those five questions in writing has understood the statute better than most of the pages that describe it, and is ready to file.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is a Limited Liability Partnership and why was it created?

A Limited Liability Partnership is a body corporate formed under the Limited Liability Partnership Act 2008, with a legal personality separate from its partners and perpetual succession. It combines the internal flexibility of a partnership, whose members arrange profit sharing and management by contract, with the limited liability of a company, so that a partner is not personally liable for the LLP's debts or for another partner's wrongful acts. It was created on the recommendation of committees including the Naresh Chandra Committee of 2003, because professional firms and closely held businesses needed protection from partners' individual defaults without carrying the governance and compliance weight of a company.

### Q: How is an LLP different from a private limited company?

The differences that decide the choice are three. An LLP has no share capital, so it cannot issue shares, preference instruments or employee stock options, and outside equity investors cannot take a conventional stake; a company is built for exactly that. Profit extraction differs: an LLP is taxed once as a firm and the partner's share of profit is exempt in his hands, while a company is taxed at its own rate and distributions to shareholders are taxed again in their hands. Compliance differs in scale: an LLP files an annual return and a statement of account and solvency each year, while a company carries board meetings, statutory registers, an annual general meeting and a far longer filing calendar.

### Q: Is a partner liable for another partner's wrongful act in an LLP?

No. Sections 27 and 28 of the Act provide that an LLP is liable for a partner's wrongful act or omission in the course of its business, but that a partner is not personally liable for another partner's wrongful act or omission. The wrongdoing partner remains liable for his own acts, and the LLP answers with its own assets. The separation does not hold where the business is carried on with intent to defraud creditors, in which case section 30 makes every person knowingly party to it liable without limit for the debts involved, alongside criminal punishment.

### Q: Does the Indian Partnership Act apply to an LLP?

No. Section 4 of the Limited Liability Partnership Act 2008 states expressly that the Indian Partnership Act 1932 does not apply to a limited liability partnership. An LLP is therefore not a species of partnership firm in law. Its partners' mutual rights and duties come from the LLP agreement and, where the agreement is silent, from the First Schedule to the Act, and the firm-law rules on matters such as dissolution on a partner's death do not govern it. Conversion of a registered firm into an LLP is provided for by the Act's Second Schedule, but the two forms are legally distinct before and after that conversion.

### Q: How many designated partners must an LLP have?

Every LLP must have at least two designated partners, and they must be individuals, with at least one of them resident in India. Where a partner is a body corporate, it acts through an individual nominee, who is treated as its designated partner. A designated partner must hold a Designated Partner Identification Number, and he is responsible for compliance with the Act's filing and procedural requirements and liable for the penalties imposed on the LLP for contraventions, unless the Act provides otherwise. The residence test, recast by the 2021 amendment, is a stay in India of not less than one hundred and twenty days during the financial year.

### Q: What did the LLP Amendment Act 2021 change?

The Limited Liability Partnership (Amendment) Act 2021, Act 31 of 2021, in force for its operative provisions from 1 April 2022, recast the Act's enforcement. It decriminalised twelve offences, converting them into civil penalties assessed by the Registrar as adjudicating officer under the new section 76A, with appeal to the Regional Director. It created the small LLP, defined by contribution up to twenty-five lakh rupees and turnover up to forty lakh rupees, with halved and capped penalties, recognised start-up LLPs, empowered special courts under section 67A, simplified compounding under section 39, and raised the imprisonment for fraud under section 30 from two years to five.

### Q: Can a company convert into an LLP?

Yes, within limits. Sections 56 and 57 of the Act, read with the Third and Fourth Schedules, permit a private company and an unlisted public company respectively to convert into an LLP, and section 58 governs the registration and effect of the conversion, vesting the company's property, rights and liabilities in the LLP by operation of law and dissolving the company. A listed public company cannot convert. The conversion requires the whole company to come across, with all its shareholders becoming partners of the LLP, and the capital gains exemption in the Income-tax Act depends on strict continuity conditions, including that the partners' aggregate share in profits remains at least fifty per cent for the prescribed period.

### Q: What compliance does an LLP actually face?

An LLP files two core documents every year: the annual return, in the prescribed form, within sixty days of the close of the financial year, and the statement of account and solvency, within thirty days from the end of six months of the financial year, declaring that the LLP can pay its debts. Its books must be audited where turnover exceeds forty lakh rupees or contribution exceeds twenty-five lakh rupees. Changes in partners, designated partners, the registered office and the agreement must be intimated within thirty days. Delay attracts additional fees that accrued, under the original scheme, at one hundred rupees per day without a ceiling, so the calendar is light but not optional.

### Q: Is any minimum capital required to incorporate an LLP?

No. The Act prescribes no minimum contribution for incorporation, and an LLP can be formed with whatever contribution the partners agree to record in the LLP agreement, in money, property, services or other benefit. Contribution matters in three practical ways rather than as an entry threshold. It fixes the partners' obligations, which a creditor can enforce in winding up where credit was given in reliance on a stated contribution. It feeds the statutory thresholds: contribution above twenty-five lakh rupees triggers the audit requirement and takes the LLP outside the small LLP definition. And it signals substance to lenders reading the public file.

### Q: Can a partnership firm convert into an LLP?

Yes. Section 55 of the Act, read with the Second Schedule, allows a firm, including a firm registered under the Indian Partnership Act 1932, to convert into an LLP. On registration of the conversion, the firm's property, rights, liabilities and obligations vest in the LLP by operation of law, pending proceedings continue against the LLP, and the firm stands dissolved and removed from the register of firms. The partners must notify the conversion to the taxation, banking and licensing authorities whose records name the firm. The capital gains exemption for the conversion depends on all partners of the firm becoming partners of the LLP in the same proportions.

### Q: Can an LLP receive foreign direct investment?

Yes, but only within the limits of the foreign investment policy, and those limits are the form's chief structural weakness. Foreign investment in an LLP is permitted in sectors where one hundred per cent foreign investment is allowed under the automatic route without foreign-investment-linked performance conditions. The investment must come in cash through banking channels, an LLP with foreign investment faces restrictions on making downstream investments of its own, and LLPs cannot raise external commercial borrowings. A venture expecting foreign institutional funding in a capped or approval-route sector, or offshore debt, will generally need the company form instead.

### Q: How is an LLP taxed compared with a company?

An LLP is taxed as a firm under the Income-tax Act: a flat rate of thirty per cent at the entity, with an alternate minimum tax where specified deductions are claimed, and the partner's share of profit is exempt in the partner's hands under section 10(2A). Interest on capital and remuneration to partners are deductible for the LLP within statutory limits and taxable in the partners' hands. A domestic company pays tax at concessional rates where it surrenders exemptions, twenty-two per cent under section 115BAA or fifteen per cent for qualifying new manufacturing companies, and its distributions are then taxed in the shareholders' hands.

### Q: Can an LLP raise venture capital or issue shares to investors?

An LLP cannot issue shares, because it has no share capital: its owners hold partnership interests under the LLP agreement, measured by contribution and profit share. It cannot create preference shares, convertible instruments or an employee stock option pool, which are the instruments venture capital is structured around. An investor can only become a partner, taking negotiated contractual rights in an illiquid interest, or lend to the LLP as a creditor. Institutional funds are therefore structurally unsuited to LLP investment, and a business with a realistic plan to raise priced equity rounds should incorporate as a company from the outset.

### Q: What happens if an LLP has no LLP agreement?

The Act supplies the constitution. Section 23(4) provides that where no agreement is made on a matter, the mutual rights and duties of the partners and of the LLP are governed by the First Schedule. The defaults include equal sharing of capital and profits, no remuneration for acting in the business, a right for every partner to take part in management, unanimity for admitting a partner or changing the nature of the business, majority decision on ordinary matters, access to true accounts, an obligation to account for profits of a competing business, and no power in a majority to expel a partner unless the agreement confers it.

### Q: Can insolvency proceedings be started against an LLP?

Yes. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016 defines a corporate person to include a limited liability partnership, so the corporate insolvency resolution process and liquidation under Part II of the Code can be commenced against an LLP before the National Company Law Tribunal, and tribunals have admitted such proceedings. This route runs alongside the LLP Act's own winding up provisions in sections 63 to 65, under which an LLP may be wound up voluntarily or by the Tribunal on grounds including inability to pay debts. The detailed interaction between the Code's process and the LLP agreement's internal loss sharing is a developing area of tribunal practice.

### Q: What is a small LLP under the LLP (Amendment) Act 2021?

A small limited liability partnership, defined in section 2(1)(ta) inserted by the 2021 amendment, is an LLP whose contribution does not exceed twenty-five lakh rupees, or a higher prescribed amount up to five crore rupees, and whose turnover in the immediately preceding financial year does not exceed forty lakh rupees, or a higher prescribed amount up to fifty crore rupees. The classification carries lighter consequences for default: penalties leviable on a small LLP, and on start-up LLPs, are set at half the ordinary amount, subject to caps of one lakh rupees for the LLP and fifty thousand rupees for each designated partner.

### Q: What happens if an LLP stops filing its annual returns?

The consequences accumulate rather than arrive at once. Additional fees accrue for each day of delay, a design that historically had no ceiling and caught dormant LLPs with liabilities far exceeding their activity. Under the amended regime the defaults also attract civil penalties assessed by the Registrar. If the default in filing the statement of account and solvency or the annual return continues for five consecutive financial years, it becomes a ground on which the Tribunal may order winding up under section 64. A defunct LLP may also be struck off the register by the Registrar under section 75, without extinguishing the partners' preserved liabilities.

### Q: Can a company be a partner in an LLP?

Yes. Section 5 of the Act permits any individual or body corporate to be a partner in an LLP, so a company, another LLP or a foreign company can hold a partnership interest. The exception is at the compliance layer: designated partners must be individuals, so a body corporate partner acts through an individual nominee who is treated as its designated partner, and at least two designated partners must be individuals with at least one resident in India. Under section 22, an individual cannot become a partner if he is of unsound mind by a court's finding, is an undischarged insolvent, or has a pending application for adjudication as an insolvent.

### Q: Can a partner leave an LLP, and what happens to their share?

Yes. Under section 24, a partner ceases to be a partner in accordance with the LLP agreement, or, where the agreement does not provide, by giving thirty days' notice of his intention to resign. A partner also ceases on death, on being declared of unsound mind by a competent court, or on being adjudicated insolvent. The outgoing partner, or the estate of a deceased partner, is entitled to the amounts the Act specifies: his capital contribution and his share of accumulated profits after deducting his share of losses, determined at the cessation date, unless the agreement provides a different settlement that the Act permits.

### Q: Can an LLP convert back into a private limited company?

The LLP Act contains no conversion route back: its conversion chapters run one way, from firm and company into LLP. The only path is the registration route under the Companies Act 2013, which permits an LLP, among other entities, to register as a company, in practice a company limited by shares. It is seldom used for an operating business, because it requires the company's constitutional documents, share allotment to the partners, fresh registrations with lenders, licensors and tax authorities, and carries none of the automatic vesting and tax neutrality that made conversion into the LLP attractive. Founders should treat the original choice as effectively permanent.
