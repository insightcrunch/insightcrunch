## The Arc of US Veterans Legislation and the Ratchet That Organizes the Field

US veterans legislation follows a rhythm so regular that the rhythm itself explains the field better than any single statute does. A war creates a cohort whose service interrupted schooling, careers, and household formation, and Congress answers with a readjustment package built around education, housing credit, and cash compensation for disability. A scandal or an administrative breakdown follows, as a benefit designed in haste meets a bureaucracy asked to deliver it at scale, and Congress answers with tighter administration, clearer eligibility rules, and stronger controls on the institutions that receive federal money. A court decision, a claims backlog, or a newly recognized condition then exposes a gap between the promise in the statute and the experience of the claimant, and Congress answers again, almost always by widening the promise rather than by narrowing it. Wars expand the beneficiary class. Scandals reform the machinery. Exposure of an unmet need adds a benefit, a presumption, or a category of eligibility. Retrenchment, by contrast, has no comparable place in the sequence, and that asymmetry is the organizing fact of the domain.

The claim can be stated in a form definite enough to test against the record assembled in the sections that follow. Benefits tied to service connected conditions operate as a one way ratchet with a scoring constraint. Once Congress recognizes a disability as connected to service, extends compensation to a class of survivors, or treats a condition as presumptively linked to a particular theater or exposure, later Congresses do not repeal the recognition, even when the fiscal cost grows far beyond the estimate attached at enactment. Political opposition therefore rarely decides outcomes in the manner familiar from tax or regulatory legislation, where organized interests line up on opposing sides and the losing side accepts a contraction of a program it opposed from the start. In veterans legislation the constituency for contraction is weak, diffuse, and reluctant to be recorded against claimants whose entitlement rests on service. The binding constraint is instead budget scoring: the projected cost of a proposal over the budget window, the offset demanded under congressional pay as you go practice, and the question of whether a benefit can be drafted narrowly enough to pass a cost estimate while broadly enough to satisfy the organizations pressing for it.

A master account of the domain has to hold two histories together at once. One history is substantive, a succession of benefit programs from readjustment allowances through disability compensation, pensions, health care, education assistance, home loan guaranty, insurance, and burial benefits, each layer added to earlier layers rather than substituted for them. The other history is formal, an account of how a body of law scattered across session laws, appropriations riders, executive orders, and regulatory pronouncements acquired a single code address that practitioners could cite. The formal history matters because veterans law is practiced as code law. The period before 1950 supplies the inheritance, the Korean era supplies the first full test of whether the Second World War model could be repeated under discipline, and the codification of 1958 supplies the container into which every later expansion was poured.

## The Inheritance Assembled Before the Korean War

The law in force when the Korean War began in June 1950 rested on an institutional settlement reached two decades earlier and on a single transformative statute enacted six years earlier. The institutional settlement came in 1930, when President Herbert Hoover signed Executive Order 5398 and the Veterans Administration began operation as an independent federal agency charged with consolidating and coordinating government activities affecting war veterans. Consolidation did not create new benefits. It created a single administrator, a single field structure, and a single point of political accountability, and it fixed the premise that veterans benefits formed one field of administration rather than a set of unrelated gratuities administered wherever an earlier statute happened to assign them.

On that institutional base the Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944, Public Law 78-346, signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on June 22, 1944, erected the substantive model that every later readjustment statute copied, revised, or reacted against. The 1944 act, drafted with the American Legion in a central role and carried through Congress as an omnibus measure, combined benefits that earlier practice had treated as separate subjects. Title II provided education and training, with tuition, fees, and related charges paid to the institution up to 500 dollars for an ordinary school year and a monthly subsistence allowance paid to the veteran, set at 50 dollars a month for a veteran without dependents and 75 dollars a month for a veteran with dependents in the original schedule. Title III provided guaranty of loans for homes, farms, and businesses. Eligibility for education rested on at least 90 days of service on or after September 16, 1940, and a discharge under conditions other than dishonorable, and entitlement ran for one year of education plus a period equal to the length of service, subject to a four year ceiling.

Three features of the 1944 settlement governed everything that followed. The first feature was universality within the cohort. Education assistance did not depend on disability, financial need, or prewar student status, because the theory of the act treated interrupted opportunity as the injury and restoration of opportunity as the remedy. The second feature was administrative decentralization joined to federal money. State approving agencies approved courses, institutions enrolled veterans and received payment, and the Veterans Administration supervised at a distance under statutory language that sharply limited federal control over educational institutions. The third feature was the absence of a code. The 1944 act amended and interacted with earlier statutes rather than replacing them, and it took its place in a body of veterans law that had accumulated since the Revolutionary era through individual enactments on pensions, compensation, insurance, hospitalization, soldiers homes, and burial, classified for convenience in an earlier edition of title 38 that carried no status as positive law.

The outbreak of war in Korea converted that inheritance from a closing chapter into a template under immediate pressure. North Korean forces crossed the 38th parallel on June 25, 1950, and President Harry S. Truman committed United States air, sea, and then ground forces under a United Nations mandate within days. Congress faced a choice between extending the 1944 act to the new cohort as written, with payment machinery that congressional investigators were at that moment criticizing, and writing a new statute that preserved the 1944 theory of readjustment while redesigning the machinery in light of the investigation record. Congress chose the second course, and the choice produced the first demonstration of the ratchet pattern in the postwar era: the benefit principle survived an abuse scandal intact while the payment design, the approval standards, and the cost controls were rebuilt around it.

## The Korean Era Education Statute Written Under Discipline

The Veterans' Readjustment Assistance Act of 1952, Public Law 82-550, approved on July 16, 1952, and codified in chapter 33 of title 38 before the repeal of that chapter in a later reorganization, governed readjustment for service on or after June 27, 1950. Eligibility tracked the 1944 pattern with adjustments to the new conflict. A veteran qualified after at least 90 days of active service during the Korean period, with discharge under conditions other than dishonorable, and a discharge for a service incurred injury or disability could qualify a veteran with shorter service. The period of qualifying service closed on January 31, 1955, the date treated as the termination of the Korean hostilities period for benefit purposes, so the statute defined its cohort by dates at both ends in the manner that became standard for era based legislation.

What the statute copied from the 1944 model was the architecture of readjustment. Education and training stood at the center, flanked by loan guaranty for homes, farms, and businesses and by unemployment compensation administered through the states on federal funds, and the stated purpose repeated the 1944 theory in nearly the same conceptual terms, namely vocational readjustment and restoration of educational opportunities lost through service during a national emergency. The veteran again chose the institution and the objective, subject to approval, and federal payment again followed enrollment rather than preceding it as a grant awarded in advance. Continuity of theory, however, coexisted with a redesign of nearly every mechanism through which money moved, and the redesign is the reason the 1952 act occupies a pivotal place in the field rather than a footnote position as a mere extension.

The central change concerned the direction of payment. Under the 1944 act the Veterans Administration paid tuition and fees directly to the educational institution, up to the statutory ceiling, and paid subsistence separately to the veteran. Under the 1952 act the government paid a single education and training allowance to the veteran alone, and the veteran paid tuition, fees, books, supplies, and living costs out of that allowance. For full time institutional training the monthly allowance reached 110 dollars for a veteran without dependents, 135 dollars for a veteran with one dependent, and 160 dollars for a veteran with more than one dependent, with lower rates for part time, cooperative, farm, and on the job training, a ceiling on the combined amount payable to apprentices and on the job trainees when wages were counted, reimbursement rather than allowance for correspondence study and for training pursued at less than half time, and payment of 75 percent of charges for flight training pursued as a standalone objective. The single allowance converted the veteran into the purchaser of education and removed the institution from a direct billing relationship with the Treasury for tuition.

The payment redesign answered a documented record rather than a hypothetical fear. A House select committee created in 1950 to investigate educational, training, and loan guaranty programs under the 1944 act, chaired by Representative Olin Teague of Texas and remembered as the Teague Committee, reported findings of overcharging, worthless courses, and schools organized to harvest federal tuition payments from enrolled veterans. The Congressional Research Service primer summarizes the committee conclusion in direct terms: direct payments to educational institutions had led to abuse. The 1952 act therefore layered controls that the 1944 act had lacked or had carried only in weaker form. A veteran had to declare an educational objective and could change that objective only under restricted conditions tied to satisfactory progress, aptitude, and normal progression. Courses of an avocational or recreational character, including bartending, dancing, photography, music, sports, and personal development subjects named in the statute, could not be pursued on the allowance. New enrollments faced an 85 percent rule under which a nonaccredited course below the college level at a private school lost approval when more than 85 percent of enrolled students had their charges paid through federal veterans programs, a test aimed at institutions that existed only because federal money sustained them. A two year rule barred approval of courses that an institution had not offered for at least two years, subject to exceptions for public institutions and for established institutions offering comparable instruction, a test aimed at schools created overnight to capture a cohort. Attendance, lessons completed, and satisfactory progress required certification by veteran and institution together, absence beyond stated limits stopped payment, and suspension of a program for more than twelve months required a waiver.

Entitlement arithmetic and the delimiting period completed the discipline. A veteran earned entitlement at one and one half times the duration of active duty in the qualifying period, capped at 36 months, a formula more restrictive than the 1944 structure under which four years of education could follow a shorter period of service, and the 36 month cap corresponded to four academic years of nine months each with summers excluded. A veteran could combine the 1952 entitlement with vocational rehabilitation or with unused entitlement under the 1944 act up to a combined ceiling of 48 months, preserving cumulative equity between cohorts without enlarging either program on its own terms. Use of the benefit was bounded at both ends. A program had to begin within two years after discharge or release, a period later extended to three years by Public Law 83-610 in 1954, and no allowance could be paid after seven years had passed from discharge or release or from the termination of hostilities, whichever date came earlier under the original text, a closing rule later extended by Public Law 84-7 in 1955 to eight years after discharge or release and to a fixed outer date of January 31, 1965. Congress lengthened the window twice as experience showed veterans losing entitlement through delay, ignorance of the benefit, or the press of immediate employment, and neither extension provoked a movement to shorten the window in compensation.

## The Codification That Gave the Field a Single Address

By the middle of the 1950s the substantive accumulation had outrun the form in which the law was published. A century and a half of pension acts, compensation acts, insurance acts, hospital acts, and readjustment acts, amended in fragments and construed through layers of administrative regulation, confronted any researcher with a retrieval problem before any question of interpretation could begin. Congress answered in two steps that are often compressed into one and that repay separation, because the first step changed the organization of the law in substance while the second step changed the legal status of the text in which the law would thereafter be found. The Veterans' Benefits Act of 1957, Public Law 85-56, approved on June 17, 1957, consolidated into one act the laws administered by the Veterans Administration relating to compensation, pension, hospitalization, and burial benefits, together with the laws governing administration of that system, and the preamble of the act stated the accompanying purposes of simplification and greater uniformity. Programs that had grown under separate enactments, with separate rate structures and separate vocabularies for comparable concepts, were restated in a single coordinated text in which definitions, eligibility conditions, and administrative provisions could be made consistent with one another.

The second step followed fourteen months later. Public Law 85-857, approved on September 2, 1958, as House bill 9700 in the 85th Congress and printed at 72 Statutes at Large 1105, bore the stated purpose of consolidating into one act all of the laws administered by the Veterans Administration. Its operative language did more than collect. The act provided that the laws relating to veterans benefits were revised, codified, and enacted as title 38 of the United States Code, under the title name Veterans' Benefits. Enactment as positive law is the legally significant phrase in that sentence. Most titles of the United States Code in the middle of the twentieth century were editorial compilations prepared by revisers, and under the rule codified at section 204 of title 1 such a compilation counted only as prima facie evidence of the law, so that a litigant who found a conflict between the Code and the Statutes at Large prevailed by producing the session law. A title enacted as positive law stands on a different footing. The Code text is itself the law, legal evidence of the law in the language courts use for positive law titles, and the prior session laws covered by the enactment were repealed and replaced by the codified text rather than merely summarized by it.

### Why do veterans lawyers cite title 38 instead of individual statutes?

Practitioners cite title 38 because Public Law 85-857 enacted the collected veterans statutes as positive law in a single code title in September 1958. A section number in that title identifies the operative rule directly, without tracing separate session laws, amendments, and repeals scattered across decades of Statutes at Large volumes.

The practical consequences explain the professional habit that the question records. After September 2, 1958, an amendment to veterans law took the form of an amendment to a numbered section of title 38, so the current text of a rule could be read in one place with editorial notes tracing each amendment to its public law. Citation by section also disciplined advocacy. A brief that cited a session law by public law number after 1958 invited the response that the session law had been repealed into the Code and that the codified section, as amended, controlled.

Precision about what codification did not do is as necessary as precision about what it did. Codification did not create the major benefits, because education assistance, compensation, pension, insurance, loan guaranty, and hospital care all predated 1958 as programs and entered the title as restatements of operating law. Codification did not freeze the law, because Congress amended title 38 continuously in the decades after 1958, adding chapters for new education programs, restructuring health care authorities, creating and later transforming judicial review of agency decisions, and revising claims procedure in response to litigation and backlog. Savings provisions in the 1958 act preserved rights that had accrued under the repealed laws, so no claimant lost a benefit by reason of the restatement alone. The governing presumption of a codification enacted in this form was continuity: language restated without a stated purpose of change carried its prior meaning into the Code, and a party arguing that the revisers had worked a substantive change by rearrangement or rewording bore the burden of showing that Congress had intended a change rather than a consolidation.

The two step settlement of 1957 and 1958 therefore closed the formative period of the field by giving US veterans legislation a durable grammar and a durable address at the same moment. The address, completed in September 1958, gathered the whole accumulation into title 38 as positive law, so that every later expansion, from Vietnam era education assistance through the Montgomery program, the Post 9/11 program, expanded presumptions, and restructured appeals, entered the statute book as an amendment to a known section or as a new chapter in a known title. The pattern that governs them was set in the fourteen years after the Second World War: a war produced a benefit, an investigation produced controls on the benefit rather than a repeal of it, and a codification produced the stable text into which the next benefit would be written when the next war, the next scandal, or the next exposed gap supplied the occasion.

## The Standing GI Bill and the Peacetime Force

The Veterans Readjustment Benefits Act of 1966, Public Law 89-358, signed by President Lyndon B. Johnson on March 3, 1966, changed the premise on which federal education benefits for veterans rested. The Servicemen Readjustment Act of 1944 had answered a single demobilization, and the Veterans Readjustment Assistance Act of 1952 had answered a second war in Korea. Each program had a conflict at its center, a defined period of qualifying service, and an expectation that eligibility would close as that generation moved through civilian life. The 1966 statute broke that pattern. Congress created an education benefit that attached to service itself rather than to a named war, and the benefit reached backward and forward at the same time. It covered veterans whose active duty fell after January 31, 1955, the closing date of the Korean conflict period, so that men and women who had served through a decade of peacetime duty, Cold War deployments, and advisory missions in Southeast Asia without a declared war acquired the same kind of readjustment entitlement that wartime veterans had held. The Congressional Research Service, in its primer on GI Bills enacted before 2008, recorded the consequence of that retroactive reach: because the 1966 benefits covered active duty service members from 1955 onward, no gap in education benefit eligibility opened after September 16, 1940, even though payment under the new program began only on June 1, 1966.

That design decision mattered for a force that no longer demobilized between wars. The United States maintained large standing forces through the nineteen fifties and nineteen sixties, rotated personnel through Europe and Asia, and escalated in Vietnam without a declaration of war and without a single mobilization moment. A benefit system built around the end of a war fitted that force poorly. Under the 1966 act, a service member who completed more than 180 days of continuous active duty and received a discharge other than dishonorable qualified for educational assistance, and a member discharged earlier for a service connected disability qualified as well. Entitlement accrued at the rate of one month of assistance for each month of active duty, up to a maximum of 36 months, a formula that Congress raised in 1968 to one and one half months of benefit for each month of service, with a full 36 months available after 18 months of service. The 1966 act also carried a first in the sequence of GI Bills, noted by the Congressional Research Service: benefits became available to members of the armed forces during active duty, rather than only after separation. Readjustment, in the statutory design, no longer waited for a discharge certificate. A serving member could begin a course of education with federal assistance while the service relationship continued, and a veteran could carry the same entitlement into civilian schooling, apprenticeship, and other approved training.

The benefit itself was deliberately modest, and that modesty shaped the politics of the next decade. Instead of paying tuition and fees directly to an institution, as the 1944 program had done, the 1966 act paid a monthly educational assistance allowance to the veteran, who then bore responsibility for tuition, fees, books, and living costs from that single payment. For a single veteran in full time institutional training, the initial rate was 100 dollars per month. Critics inside veterans organizations and inside Congress argued from enactment onward that the allowance covered books and minor fees rather than the full cost of attending college, and the rate history reads as a legislative admission of the point. Congress raised the single veteran rate to 130 dollars per month in 1967, to 175 dollars in 1970, to 220 dollars in 1972, and to 270 dollars in 1974. Supporters of higher rates treated the allowance level as the difference between a formal entitlement and a usable one. Administration officials who resisted larger increases treated the same figures as a budget question. Permanence settled eligibility. It settled nothing about generosity.

The permanence itself reorganized expectations inside the force. A peacetime enlistee in 1958 had entered service with no education entitlement waiting at the end of the enlistment, since the Korean program had closed to new service periods in 1955. After March 1966, that same enlistee held retroactive eligibility, and every later enlistee could plan around a benefit that existed independent of whether the enlistment coincided with combat. Education assistance became part of the standing terms of military service, usable in planning recruitment, retention, and transition, rather than an emergency measure improvised after a war ended. Vietnam era service then flowed into a structure that already existed, and Veterans Administration training reports recorded close to 1.8 million persons in training under the program by April 1975. The 1966 statute therefore occupies a hinge position: it preserved the machinery of readjustment from 1944 and 1952, converted that machinery into a continuing institution, and left to later Congresses the recurring fight over whether the monthly allowance bore any relation to the price of the education it was meant to buy.

## Sharing Medical Resources and Widening the Door to Care

The health legislation of 1970 requires a correction before description, because the label often attached to it promises more than the statute delivered. No act titled the Veterans Omnibus Health Care Act was enacted in 1970; the statute bearing the omnibus health care name belongs to 1976, as Public Law 94-581. The candidate public law number for 1970, Public Law 91-496, identifies a real and narrower measure. Enacted on October 22, 1970, from H.R. 9634, Public Law 91-496 amended title 38 of the United States Code in order, in its own enacted words, to improve and make more effective the Veterans Administration program of sharing specialized medical resources. The operative expansion was an expansion of treatment authority rather than of eligibility categories or hospital construction, and precision about that distinction preserves the shape of the period. Congress in 1970 did not rebuild the hospital system or open its doors to a new class of veterans. Congress made the existing system more porous, so that scarce specialized capacity could move between the Veterans Administration and outside medical institutions in both directions.

The sharing mechanism worked as follows in the statutory scheme that Public Law 91-496 strengthened. The Veterans Administration could enter agreements for the mutual use, or the exchange of use, of specialized medical facilities and services, regardless of whether the Administration was furnishing the service or receiving it. A veterans hospital with a specialized unit, equipment, or clinical expertise that community providers lacked could make that resource available under agreement, and a veterans hospital that lacked a specialized resource could obtain access to one held elsewhere rather than duplicate it at federal expense. The statute also adjusted the personnel machinery that made sharing workable. It authorized temporary full time appointments, for a period not exceeding one year, of persons who had completed a full course of nursing in a recognized school of nursing and were awaiting registration, and it permitted appointment of dentists of high academic and research standing on temporary terms without regard to certain licensure requirements that otherwise constrained federal appointment. Each provision addressed capacity rather than entitlement: nurses in the pipeline could be put to work before a registration formality cleared, eminent dentists could be brought into the system on flexible terms, and specialized facilities could be treated as shared regional assets instead of as property locked inside a single institution.

A companion measure enacted on the same day, October 22, 1970, carried the eligibility side of the 1970 health changes. Public Law 91-500 provided that older veterans would be deemed unable to defray the expenses of necessary hospital or domiciliary care, removing a means statement barrier that had stood between an aging veteran and admission for treatment of a nonservice connected condition. Section 2 of that same public law, as the amendment notes to title 38 record, extended the authority of the Administrator to furnish medical services to veterans permanently housebound or receiving pension or compensation based on need of regular aid and attendance, and it struck conditions that had limited such care to veterans suffering from one or more of six enumerated conditions or diseases. Read together, Public Laws 91-496 and 91-500 describe the actual 1970 settlement. Treatment authority widened through sharing agreements and staffing flexibility, access for a defined group of older and housebound veterans widened through a deeming rule and a broader outpatient authority, and the large questions of hospital capacity and general eligibility waited for later statutes, principally the Veterans Health Care Expansion Act of 1973, Public Law 93-82, which expanded outpatient care and created a health benefit program for certain dependents and survivors. Assigning the 1973 expansion to 1970 would flatter the earlier year and mislead a reader about when the system changed. The honest sequence is incremental: 1970 made the system share better and admit a vulnerable group more readily, while the war in Vietnam filled wards with younger veterans whose needs pressed against a capacity that Congress expanded in larger steps later in the decade.


## Employment Rights With an Education Increase Attached

The Vietnam Era Veterans Readjustment Assistance Act of 1974, Public Law 93-508, enacted on December 3, 1974, joined three policies that Congress had often pursued separately: a substantial increase in education allowances, a federal employment apparatus for veterans, and a duty imposed on federal contractors to act affirmatively in hiring veterans. The statute arrived over a presidential veto. President Gerald R. Ford vetoed the bill, H.R. 12628, on November 26, 1974, objecting in his veto message to the size of the benefit increase, to the extension of entitlement from 36 to 45 months for undergraduates, and to a new direct education loan program that he argued duplicated student aid administered elsewhere in the federal government. Congress enacted the bill over that veto on December 3, 1974, and the text at 88 Stat. 1578 became the governing settlement, covering increases under chapters 31, 34, and 35 of title 38, an education loan program, improvements in the Veterans Employment Service, increased employment of veterans by federal contractors, an action plan for disabled and Vietnam era veterans inside the federal government, and the codification and expansion of veterans reemployment rights.

The contractor provision became the durable legal core of the act, codified at section 4212 of title 38 and known by the act acronym, VEVRAA. In its original form, the affirmative action duty attached to federal contracts and subcontracts above 10,000 dollars, a threshold that later Congresses raised in steps, to 25,000 dollars in 1998 and to 100,000 dollars in the Jobs for Veterans Act, so any account of the 1974 duty must use the 1974 figure rather than a later one. A covered contractor had to take affirmative action to employ and advance in employment qualified Vietnam era veterans and disabled veterans, which meant the obligation reached recruitment, hiring, rates of pay, upgrading, and selection for training rather than stopping at a ban on discrimination. A ban alone would have left a contractor compliant while making no effort to find veteran applicants. The affirmative action formulation reversed the burden of initiative: the contractor had to act, and the federal government, through the Department of Labor and its contract compliance machinery, could ask what the contractor had done. The same statutory scheme required covered contractors to list employment openings with the local office of the state employment service, with exceptions for executive and top management positions, positions to be filled from within the organization, and positions lasting three days or less, and the employment service in turn owed veterans priority in referral to listed openings. Listing converted a general duty into a flow of concrete vacancies passing through a public office where veteran applicants were registered, which gave enforcement a paper trail and gave applicants a point of entry that did not depend on personal networks.

The public employment machinery received parallel attention inside the same statute. The act improved and expanded the provisions governing the Veterans Employment Service, the Department of Labor organization charged with veterans employment programs, and it provided for an action plan for the employment of disabled and Vietnam era veterans within the federal government itself, a recognition that the government could hardly impose hiring duties on its contractors while treating its own workforce as exempt from the effort. Administration of the wider employment system in title 38 ran through the Assistant Secretary of Labor for Veterans Employment, through state employment security agencies, and through specialists devoted to disabled veterans outreach, whose function was to meet the employment needs of veterans with particular attention to disabled veterans of the Vietnam era. Reemployment rights, the guarantee that a person who left a civilian job for military service could reclaim that job or a position of like seniority, status, and pay on return, were codified and expanded in the same act, completing a circle that ran from the job a service member left, through the jobs federal contractors had to open, to the public offices charged with matching veterans to work.

The education provisions of Public Law 93-508 were not an appendage, and contemporaries did not treat them as one. The act raised the monthly educational assistance allowance for a single veteran in full time institutional training from 220 dollars to 270 dollars, an increase of 22.7 percent, retroactive to September 1, 1974, with vocational rehabilitation subsistence allowances raised by 18.2 percent, from 220 dollars to 260 dollars for a veteran without dependents. Veterans Administration training reports from April 1975 attributed a period of rapid growth in chapter 34 training in part to that increase, alongside high unemployment among newly discharged veterans, and recorded the highest end of month trainee count since the 1966 program began. The act also extended undergraduate entitlement to 45 months, established the education loan program that President Ford had singled out for objection, expanded help for educationally disadvantaged veterans, and liberalized the veterans work study program. The legislative bargaining over the percentage illustrates the fiscal politics of the field. The Ford Administration proposed an increase near 8 percent, congressional proposals began near 18.6 percent, and veterans organizations including the American Legion held out for a figure near 23 percent before the conference settled at 22.7 percent. The near quarter increase in a single statute shows how adequacy arguments could prevail over restraint arguments when the affected constituency was returning veterans in a weak labor market, even against a veto grounded in inflation control. It also set the allowance baseline from which the next education experiment departed, since the contributory program created two years later asked new service members to accept a smaller and partly self funded benefit in place of the allowance structure that the 1974 act had just enlarged for the Vietnam generation.

## The Contributory Experiment and Its Diagnosis

The Veterans Education and Employment Assistance Act of 1976, Public Law 94-502, signed on October 15, 1976, ended the Vietnam era education program for new entrants and replaced it, in Title IV, with the Post Vietnam Era Veterans Educational Assistance Program, known by the acronym VEAP. Enrollment opened on January 1, 1977, and eligibility attached to persons who entered active duty after December 31, 1976. The break was generational by design. A person who entered service on December 30, 1976, entered under the chapter 34 program descended from 1966, with a monthly allowance paid by the government without any contribution from the service member. A person who entered days later entered under chapter 32, VEAP, in which the education benefit had to be partly purchased. Congress and the Department of Defense built VEAP for an all volunteer force, and the stated purposes in the legislation joined educational assistance to the needs of that force: providing assistance to persons entering the armed forces after 1976, helping those persons obtain an education, and helping the volunteer military attract qualified men and women. The contributory form was the policy wager. If a service member saved toward education during service, the reasoning ran, the member would value the benefit, plan for schooling, and arrive at separation with a fund matched generously enough to make the sacrifice worthwhile.

The matching arithmetic was precise in the statutory text. A participant agreed to a monthly deduction from military pay of not less than 50 dollars and not more than 75 dollars, in an amount divisible by five, deposited into the Post Vietnam Era Veterans Education Account in the Treasury. Personal contributions were capped at a maximum of 2,700 dollars. Each dollar contributed by the participant drew matching funds from the government at the rate of two dollars for each one dollar contributed, so a participant who contributed the full 2,700 dollars accumulated a total fund of 8,100 dollars. The Secretary concerned could add further contributions deemed necessary or appropriate to encourage persons to enter or remain in the armed forces, which gave the services a discretionary kicker for hard to fill specialties or retention problems. Entitlement ran for a maximum of 36 months, or the number of months for which contributions had been made, whichever was less, and benefits had to be used within ten years after discharge or release from active duty. A participant who disenrolled received a refund of personal contributions, while the government match was forfeited, a term that made enrollment reversible at the price of losing the matched share. On paper the two for one match looked generous, since few savings vehicles tripled a deposit. In practice the total fund divided across a course of education yielded modest monthly support, and the participant had to surrender pay in the present, during the lowest paid years of a military career, for a benefit whose value depended on completing a plan years in the future.

### Why did so few service members join the VEAP education program?

Participation stayed low because the design asked the lowest paid members of the force to fund one third of a modest benefit from monthly pay, offered a maximum pool that covered only part of college costs, and allowed disenrollment with a refund, which turned the account into reversible savings rather than committed educational entitlement for many enrollees.

The participation record, as the Congressional Research Service assembled it in its primer on veterans educational assistance programs, supports that diagnosis with unusual clarity. Between 1977 and 1982, fewer than 35 percent of eligible enlisted service members enrolled in VEAP, and officers enrolled at even lower rates. A related Congressional Research Service history recorded that only 20 to 25 percent of new recruits contributed to the program, and that about 40 percent of contributors had disenrolled to recoup a contribution that equaled 5 to 20 percent of after tax pay for some enlisted members. The refund figures carry the verdict further. Across the life of the program as the Congressional Research Service later tabulated it, more than twice as many enrollees disenrolled without receiving educational assistance as received educational assistance, over 700,000 individuals received disenrollment refunds in the decades after 1978, and about 250,000 individuals received VEAP training benefits. A program designed as an education entitlement functioned, for the majority of the people who touched it, as a short term savings account that they closed. The deduction competed with the immediate needs of junior enlisted households, the benefit ceiling of 8,100 dollars lost ground as tuition and living costs rose, and the absence of transferability meant a member who decided against college had no family use for the fund.

Congressional hearings in the early nineteen eighties, convened as the services reported difficulty recruiting and retaining a qualified volunteer force, treated VEAP as evidence in a larger argument about whether the contribution requirement itself was the defect. Witnesses urged elimination of the service member contribution on the ground that retention had not measurably improved under the contributory program. Defenders of the contributory idea could point to a principled claim, that a benefit partly funded by its beneficiary encouraged careful use and limited federal exposure, and that claim had genuine fiscal logic in a decade preoccupied with budget restraint. The empirical answer overwhelmed the principled one. A recruitment incentive that between two thirds and four fifths of its target population declined to join could not perform the function assigned to it, and an education program whose refund line outran its training line by a wide margin had failed on its own terms. By 1984 Congress had reached that conclusion and legislated a replacement built on different terms. The replacement belongs to the next stage of the sequence. The diagnosis that produced it can be stated without qualification: VEAP asked too much from the people with the least to spare, offered too little in return, and made withdrawal too easy, and a benefit with those three properties will be declined, deferred, and finally refunded rather than used.

## Counseling in the Community Rather Than the Hospital

The rebuilding of veterans institutions after the Vietnam War began with an admission about place. By the late 1970s the Veterans Administration operated one of the largest hospital systems in the federal government, yet Vietnam era veterans were not walking through its doors at the rates recorded for veterans of the Second World War and Korea. Department accounts identified the gap directly: Congress created the Vet Center program in part because Vietnam era veterans had sustained readjustment difficulties after returning from war and were not reaching conventional VA services. The statute that carried the remedy was the Veterans Health Care Amendments of 1979, Public Law 96-22, enacted on June 13, 1979. Its central innovation was not a hospital ward or a benefit payment. The statute instead created a counseling service placed in the community, staffed in large part by veterans of the same war, and defined around readjustment to civilian life rather than around a diagnosed illness or an adjudicated claim.

The problem the statute answered had been visible for years before Congress gave it a program. Men and women who had served in Vietnam described a recurring pattern of difficulty that surfaced after discharge rather than during service: broken sleep and intrusive recollection, estrangement from family, trouble holding employment, heavy drinking and drug use, anger that arrived without proportion, and a settled mistrust of the government institutions that had sent them to war and then processed their return. The psychiatrist Chaim Shatan gave one cluster of those experiences a name in 1972, writing of a post-Vietnam syndrome in an essay published that year. Clinicians sympathetic to the veterans, among them Shatan and Robert Jay Lifton, worked with veterans in informal discussion groups, often called rap groups, where the testimony of other veterans carried a weight that professional reassurance did not. The model embedded in those groups, peer speech before peer judgment, later became an organizing principle of the federal program.

Formal medicine, meanwhile, had no stable category for what the veterans described. The first edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, published by the American Psychiatric Association in 1952, had contained a diagnosis called gross stress reaction, framed as a short term response to severe trauma. The second edition, published in 1968, removed that category and substituted adjustment reaction to adult life, a rubric that treated the reaction as transient. A veteran presenting in a VA clinic in the middle 1970s therefore faced a vocabulary that scattered the presentation into depression, anxiety, character disorder, or substance abuse, and the absence of a unifying diagnosis mattered administratively as well as clinically, because VA treatment and disability compensation both ran through recognized conditions.

Congress legislated one year before psychiatry settled the terminology. The readjustment counseling provisions of Public Law 96-22 authorized the Administrator of Veterans Affairs to provide counseling to any veteran who had served on active duty during the Vietnam era and who requested it within the eligibility window the statute set, measured from discharge or from enactment. The counseling began with a general mental and psychological assessment directed at a functional question: whether the veteran had mental or psychological problems associated with readjustment to civilian life. If a VA physician or psychologist determined that mental health services were necessary to facilitate successful readjustment, the veteran received those services on an outpatient basis, together with necessary follow up care and, where readjustment required it, services for family members. Eligibility turned on service and request, not on a service connected disability rating. A veteran did not have to win a claim, or even file one, before talking to a counselor. The statute separated the act of seeking help from the act of proving entitlement, and that separation was the point.

In 1980, one year after the program was authorized, the American Psychiatric Association published the third edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual and included posttraumatic stress disorder as a formal diagnosis for the first time. The recognition, built on research concerning Vietnam veterans alongside Holocaust survivors and survivors of sexual violence, gave the symptom cluster a name that clinics could record, researchers could count, and the VA could compensate. The centers had opened under a statute written in the vocabulary of readjustment, a social and functional frame, and the two vocabularies coexisted inside the program through the 1980s. Readjustment counseling remained the statutory service, broad enough to cover employment difficulty, marital strain, and the ordinary disorientation of return, while posttraumatic stress disorder became the diagnosis through which the severest presentations were treated and, in the benefits system, rated.

### A Storefront Staffed by Veterans of the Same War

The physical form of the program expressed its theory. Vet Centers were established as small community based operations set apart from VA medical centers, often in storefront or small office premises on ordinary commercial streets, a setting the Department itself has contrasted with the sprawling campus of a VA hospital. Congressional testimony described the centers as small storefront operations with a staff of four to five people each, and testimony before the House Committee on Veterans Affairs identified a four person team of team leader, office manager, social worker, and psychologist as the original prototype dating to 1979. National Academies analysis of VA care later observed that the centers were, by design, not aligned under the management of local VA medical centers, an administrative separation that matched the physical one. A veteran could enter a Vet Center without entering the hospital system and without presenting a benefits file.

Staffing carried the deeper break with convention. From the founding period the program drew heavily on Vietnam veterans themselves, placing combat veterans inside the counseling room as counselors, outreach workers, and team leaders alongside psychologists and social workers with professional training. Department descriptions of the centers have repeatedly stressed that their staffs consisted in large part of war veterans, and that most counselors had served in a combat zone. The model answered the trust problem that the hospital system had failed to solve. A counselor who had served in the same war could not be dismissed as a bureaucrat guessing at an experience from outside it, and group counseling led by such a counselor reproduced, inside a federal program, the peer structure of the rap groups that veterans had built for themselves during the 1970s. Outreach, a core function of the program, depended on staff who knew where veterans gathered and why many of them avoided government buildings.

The result was an institution that sat inside the VA for funding and authority while standing outside its clinical hierarchy in setting, scale, and personnel. Administrators seeking integration could point to the anomaly of a counseling service insulated from the medical centers that treated the same patients, while defenders of the founding design answered that the insulation was the therapeutic instrument. Congress sided with permanence: an experiment framed around a single war generation became an established category of VA service, and eligibility extensions in later statutes carried readjustment counseling beyond the Vietnam cohort. The federal government had accepted that a class of war injury presented socially before it presented clinically, and it had built a deliberately unhospital place to meet it.


## An Education Benefit the Service Member Bought Into

The second movement rebuilt the education benefit around contribution, and it belongs in this account as a single dense pass, because a sibling article owns the statute in full. The Montgomery GI Bill was enacted in 1984 as Title VII of the Department of Defense Authorization Act, 1985, Public Law 98-525, signed on October 19, 1984. The program took its name from its House sponsor, Representative Gillespie V. "Sonny" Montgomery of Mississippi, the chairman of the House Committee on Veterans Affairs. The statute replaced the Veterans Educational Assistance Program for new entrants, a contributory arrangement from the late 1970s under which the government matched service member savings and which had drawn weak participation and weaker enthusiasm from the services. Where the older arrangement had asked soldiers to save toward a benefit of uncertain value, the 1984 design made the bargain automatic, uniform, and legible at the recruiting station.

The mechanics were stark. A service member entering active duty after June 30, 1985, was enrolled unless the member declined in writing, and pay was reduced by 100 dollars a month for the first 12 months of service, a total contribution of 1,200 dollars that was not refundable if the benefit went unused. In exchange the statute promised a basic educational assistance allowance paid for up to 36 months of approved education or training, set at 300 dollars a month for full time study in the case of a member who had completed three years of service, with a lower basic rate of 250 dollars a month for a member whose qualifying obligation was two years. The arithmetic was the argument. A contribution of 1,200 dollars bought a basic entitlement worth 10,800 dollars at the full rate. The federal contribution dwarfed the member contribution, while the member contribution gave the benefit the character of something earned and partly self financed rather than granted, and recruiters could describe the same numbers in a single sentence to a seventeen year old weighing enlistment.

Recruiting was not a side effect of the design but its stated theater of operation. The draft had ended in 1973, and the all volunteer force competed for high quality recruits against colleges and civilian employers in the open labor market. An education benefit payable after service converted enlistment into a financing plan for college. The statute reinforced that purpose by authorizing the individual services to pay supplemental educational assistance, additional amounts layered on the basic benefit and financed through the Department of Defense, for members who served longer terms or who held skills in which the services faced critical shortages. Those supplemental payments, known across the services as kickers and in the Army as the Army College Fund, let a service raise the education offer for a hard to fill specialty without raising the statutory benefit for every recruit. The basic benefit sat in chapter 30 of title 38, but its price, its supplements, and its purpose were set in the competition for volunteers.

The same enactment built a parallel program for the reserve components, the Montgomery GI Bill Selected Reserve program, carried in title 10 and later codified as chapter 1606 of that title. Eligibility rested on a six year obligation in the Selected Reserve together with completion of initial training and a high school credential, and the program paid a basic full time rate of 140 dollars a month at its creation. Its structural significance exceeded its rate: the 1984 reserve program was the first federal educational assistance program that did not require service in the active armed forces to qualify. Taken together, the two programs of 1984 fixed the template that governed entrants for a generation, education as an earned, partly prepaid, recruiting priced benefit, divided between an active duty program administered as a veterans benefit and a reserve program administered as a manpower tool.

## Cabinet Rank and a Court of Review

The third movement changed neither a counseling method nor a benefit rate. It changed the constitutional furniture around veterans policy, through two statutes enacted within weeks of each other in the autumn of 1988. One raised the administering agency into the Cabinet. The other subjected its benefits decisions to a judge.

### From Administration to Department

The Veterans Administration that Congress elevated in 1988 had been created in 1930, when an executive order of President Herbert Hoover consolidated the bureaus handling veterans pensions, hospitals, insurance, and soldiers homes into a single independent agency. By the 1980s the portfolio had outgrown the bounded frame of 1930. The agency ran a national hospital system, paid disability compensation and pensions, administered education benefits and home loan guarantees, and operated the national cemeteries, placing it among the largest establishments in the federal government. Its head, titled Administrator of Veterans Affairs, nevertheless sat outside the Cabinet. Veterans organizations pressed the elevation argument for years on that ground, contending that the size of the mission and of the constituency together merited a secretary at the table where administration policy was set.

### When did the VA become a cabinet department?

The Veterans Administration became the cabinet level Department of Veterans Affairs on March 15, 1989. President Ronald Reagan had signed the Department of Veterans Affairs Act on October 25, 1988, and the statute delayed cabinet status until that March date, when Edward J. Derwinski became the first Secretary.

The statute was the Department of Veterans Affairs Act, Public Law 100-527. The bill, introduced in the House by Representative Jack Brooks of Texas, passed the House by a vote of 399 to 17 and the Senate by a vote of 84 to 12, and President Ronald Reagan signed it on October 25, 1988, at a ceremony at Fort McNair in Washington. Opposition had come chiefly from within the President's own party, where members committed to halting the growth of the federal government resisted the creation of a fifteenth cabinet department on principle. Supporters answered that the act reorganized rather than expanded, since the new department would perform functions the Veterans Administration already performed, with the same programs, facilities, and workforce under a new title, and supporters made the constituency argument without embarrassment: veterans formed a large and organized share of the electorate, and a vote against elevation would be read by that constituency as a judgment about its standing.

The delay between signature and effect was itself a considered feature of the design. Because President Reagan signed the act in the final months of his presidency, the statute set the effective date of cabinet status at March 15, 1989, after the presidential transition, so that the incoming President, George H. W. Bush, would appoint the first secretary. President Bush appointed Edward J. Derwinski, a former member of the House from Illinois and a veteran of the Second World War, as Administrator of Veterans Affairs on March 1, 1989, and Derwinski became the first Secretary of Veterans Affairs on March 15, 1989, when the redesignation took effect. The act renamed the agency the Department of Veterans Affairs, placed a Secretary at its head, and transferred the functions of the old administration intact. What elevation supplied was rank: a secretary confirmed by the Senate, seated in the Cabinet, positioned to argue the department's budget against other department heads as an equal.

### A Court for Benefits Decisions

The second statute of 1988 cut in the opposite direction, distributing power away from the department at the moment the first statute was concentrating prestige in its head. The Veterans' Judicial Review Act, Public Law 100-687, enacted on November 18, 1988, created judicial review of individual veterans benefits decisions, a form of review that the veterans system had excluded for more than half a century. The chronology runs back to the Economy Act of 1933, Public Law 73-2, the Depression era statute that abolished the patchwork of earlier war based pension laws, authorized the President to rebuild the benefits system by executive order, and laid the foundation for the modern adjudication structure of regional offices and a Board of Veterans' Appeals. The no review rule that grew from that settlement, carried forward in the provision of title 38 that made the Administrator's decisions on questions of law and fact final and conclusive and beyond review by any court, left the Veterans Administration in a position that a later House committee described as splendid isolation: the one major federal administrative agency whose core decisions were expressly insulated from judicial oversight. A veteran denied disability compensation could appeal inside the agency, from a regional office to the Board, and the Board's word ended the matter. The merits of an individual claim, the finding that a condition was not service connected or that a rating percentage was correct, could not reach a court.

Reform pressure accumulated with the Vietnam generation's experience of the claims system. Defenders of the closed system, including successive administrators and the House Committee on Veterans Affairs in earlier decades, argued that review by judges would import lawyers, delay, and adversarial procedure into a paternal system designed to develop claims generously on the veteran's behalf. Advocates of review answered that a system deciding the livelihoods of disabled veterans could not rest permanently on the unreviewable judgment of the paying agency, and that other federal benefits systems, Social Security disability included, operated under judicial review without collapse. The 1988 act resolved the argument by building a specialist court rather than opening the general federal courts, conceding the principle of review while answering the expertise objection in institutional form.

The court the act created was the United States Court of Veterans Appeals, an independent court established under Article I of the Constitution, staffed by judges appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate for fixed terms, and renamed the United States Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims by a statute of 1998. The act gave the court exclusive jurisdiction to review decisions of the Board of Veterans' Appeals. Review proceeded on the record before the agency and the Board, without new fact finding by the court, and the court received power to affirm, modify, or reverse a Board decision or to remand the matter. The Secretary received no symmetrical right: the statute provided that the Administrator, and after elevation the Secretary, could not seek review of a Board decision at all, preserving the one way character of the appeal as a claimant's remedy. A claimant reaching the court had exhausted [the long administrative road through the VA claims process](/2019/06/15/va-claims-process-law/), and the function of the court began precisely where that road ended.

The standard of review the act wrote was borrowed from administrative law and calibrated to leave fact finding with the agency while policing legality from outside it. Under the scope of review provision, the court was required to hold unlawful and set aside Board decisions found to be arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law, contrary to constitutional right, in excess of statutory jurisdiction, or adopted without observance of procedure required by law. Findings of material fact, by contrast, were reviewed under a clearly erroneous standard, under which the court asked whether the Board's account of the evidence was plausible in light of the record rather than whether the court would have weighed the evidence the same way. The act coupled those standards with a duty that gave them practical bite, requiring the Board to supply an adequate statement of its reasons and bases, so that a reviewing judge could trace how the Board had moved from evidence to conclusion. The same statute revised the long standing restrictions on charging fees for representation in benefits cases, making paid legal representation economically possible in the new appellate forum.

The limits of the new review were as deliberate as the grant. The act stated in terms that the court may not review the schedule of ratings for disabilities, the table by which the department assigns percentage evaluations to impairments. A veteran could obtain review of how the schedule had been applied to the facts of an individual case, of whether the Board had followed governing law and procedure, and of whether its factual conclusions survived the record, but the content of the rating schedule itself remained a legislative and regulatory judgment outside the court's reach. The architecture therefore opened the individual decision while shielding the policy instruments that generated thousands of decisions at once. Congress had ended the finality rule of the 1933 settlement for the claimant standing before the Board, and had declined to hand the judiciary the department's rulebook along with the claimant's file.

Read together, the three movements describe a single arc. Care moved out of the hospital and into the neighborhood, under a vocabulary of readjustment that predated the diagnosis the work came to serve. The education benefit moved toward a prepaid contract between the volunteer and the force that recruited that volunteer. The administering agency moved upward into the first rank of executive departments, and its hardest decisions moved outward into a courtroom, where reasons had to be stated in a form an outsider could test.

## Presumptions, Reemployment Rights, and the Great Eligibility Settlement

Three statutes enacted between 1991 and 1996 settled questions that the veterans system had contested for decades, and each settled its question by building a machine rather than by declaring a final answer. The first machine was scientific: a standing arrangement under which an outside academy weighed evidence about herbicides and the Secretary of Veterans Affairs converted that weighing, under a statutory standard written into law, into a list of diseases presumed to be connected to service. The second machine was restorative: a reemployment code that treated an absence for uniformed service as if the absence had never interrupted a civilian career, and that backed the fiction with protections against discrimination and against discharge. The third machine was allocative, and it was the largest of the three: an enrollment system that sorted veterans into priority groups, attached a defined package of medical benefits to enrollment itself, and in doing so made possible a reorganization of veterans health care away from the hospital and toward the clinic. Benefits expanded, in disease after disease and in service after service, through administrative processes that Congress designed to keep expanding. The eligibility settlement of 1996, however, carried a condition inside it from the first day, because the same statute that opened the system to nearly every veteran also authorized the Secretary to close the door by priority group when appropriations fell short. The history of that condition, including the January 2003 suspension of enrollment for the highest income group, is the documented exception to any simple account of benefits moving in one direction only.

### The Herbicide Presumption and the Administrative List

The Agent Orange Act of 1991, Public Law 102-4, signed by President George H. W. Bush on February 6, 1991, grew out of a stalemate over evidence. Veterans who had served in the Republic of Vietnam during the Vietnam era reported diseases that they attributed to exposure to herbicides, among them Agent Orange and its dioxin contaminant, and for years each claim required an individual contest over causation that the available science rarely resolved cleanly. An earlier effort, the Veterans Dioxin and Radiation Exposure Compensation Standards Act of 1984, had produced a regulation that the Nehmer litigation exposed as inconsistent with its authorizing statute. Congress answered in 1991 by separating the scientific question from the benefits question and assigning each to a different institution under a different rule.

The scientific question went to the National Academy of Sciences. Section 3 of the 1991 act directed the Secretary to seek an agreement under which the Academy, an independent nonprofit scientific organization outside the federal government, would review and summarize the scientific evidence concerning the association between exposure to herbicides used in Vietnam and each disease suspected of such an association. For each disease the Academy was to determine whether a statistical association existed, taking account of the strength of the evidence and the appropriateness of the methods used to detect it, and to assess the increased risk of the disease among those exposed. The arrangement was periodic by design. The Academy was to report to the Secretary and to the House and Senate Committees on Veterans Affairs, and to conduct subsequent reviews at least every two years for a period of ten years from the first report, reassessing its determinations as new evidence appeared. The first comprehensive report, Veterans and Agent Orange, appeared in 1994 through the Institute of Medicine, and biennial updates followed. The benefits question stayed with the Secretary, but Congress wrote the standard that governed the answer, and the standard is the heart of the statute. The act created section 1116 of title 38, under which a listed disease becoming manifest in a veteran who served in Vietnam is considered to have been incurred in or aggravated by that service, notwithstanding the absence of any record of the disease during service, and under which service in Vietnam carried a presumption of exposure itself. For diseases beyond the ones Congress named in the statute, among them non-Hodgkin lymphoma, soft tissue sarcoma, and chloracne, the Secretary was directed to prescribe regulations providing a presumption of service connection whenever the Secretary determined, on the basis of sound medical and scientific evidence, that a positive association existed between exposure to an herbicide agent and the occurrence of a disease in humans. The statute then defined the decisive term. An association is considered positive if the credible evidence for the association is equal to or outweighs the credible evidence against it. Equipoise, in other words, was enough. The Secretary was required to take the Academy reports into account together with all other sound medical and scientific information available, and to weigh whether study results were statistically significant, capable of replication, and able to withstand peer review, but once the credible evidence stood in balance, the statute resolved the balance in favor of the presumption. Deadlines enforced the conversion. Within sixty days of receiving an Academy report the Secretary was to determine whether a presumption was warranted for each disease the report covered; a favorable determination led to proposed regulations, and an unfavorable one had to be published in the Federal Register with an explanation of its scientific basis.

The practical effect was to convert an evidentiary fight into an administrative list. Before 1991, causation was litigated claim by claim, veteran by veteran, disease by disease, and the outcome turned on records and expert contests that repeated the same scientific dispute thousands of times. After 1991, the dispute moved to a single forum with a fixed calendar: the Academy weighed the literature, the Secretary applied the positive association standard, and a disease either entered the regulations at title 38, Code of Federal Regulations, section 3.309(e), or it did not, with reasons published when it did not. A veteran whose disease appeared on the list no longer had to prove that herbicides caused the condition in an individual case; presence of the disease, qualifying service, and the presumption of exposure completed the chain. Supporters of the design, including the veterans service organizations that had pressed for the statute, treated the equipoise standard as a correction to a system that had placed the cost of scientific uncertainty on the people exposed in service. Skeptics of expansive presumptions treated the same standard as a decision to pay on a weaker finding than proof of causation. Both readings describe the same mechanism: Congress had chosen that unresolved scientific doubt about herbicide disease would be resolved for the claimant, and had built machinery to apply that choice repeatedly rather than once.


### Reemployment Rights and the Escalator Principle

The Uniformed Services Employment and Reemployment Rights Act of 1994, known as USERRA, Public Law 103-353, signed by President Bill Clinton on October 13, 1994, and codified at sections 4301 to 4335 of title 38, protected a different kind of continuity. The herbicide presumption protected a claim after service had ended and disease had appeared. USERRA protected the civilian life that service had interrupted, on the premise that a person who leaves a job for the uniformed services should return to the position that the uninterrupted career would have produced. The statute was a comprehensive rewrite of the Veterans Reemployment Rights law, and its lineage explains both its structure and the judicial doctrine at its center. Congress first conferred reemployment rights in the Selective Training and Service Act of 1940, extending the right a year later from draftees to voluntary enlistees, and the reemployment statute lived inside the draft laws for more than three decades. In 1974 the Vietnam Era Veterans Readjustment Assistance Act substantially amended the law, moved it into chapter 43 of title 38, and extended its reach to state and local governments for the first time, the federal government and private employers having been covered since 1940. USERRA replaced that accumulated structure in 1994 with a single modern code, but the central idea carried through unchanged from 1940.

That idea received its enduring formulation from the Supreme Court in Fishgold v. Sullivan Drydock and Repair Corporation, 328 U.S. 275, decided in 1946, the first case in which the Court construed the reemployment statute. The returning veteran, the Court explained in paraphrase of its holding, does not step back onto the seniority escalator at the point where the veteran stepped off; the veteran steps back on at the precise point that continuous employment would have reached. USERRA codified that escalator principle in sections 4313 and 4316. A person reemployed after service of more than ninety days is entitled to the position the person would have attained if employment had not been interrupted, or to a position of like seniority, status, and pay for which the person is qualified. Section 4316(a) extends the same continuity to seniority and to the rights and benefits determined by seniority, crediting the returning employee with the seniority held when service began plus the additional seniority and benefits that continuous employment would have added. The escalator runs in both directions: the guarantee is the position continuous employment would have produced, which does not insulate the veteran from a layoff that would have reached that position had the veteran never left.

The right itself is conditional, and USERRA states the conditions with precision. The person must have left a civilian position for service in the uniformed services, must have given the employer advance notice where the statute requires it, must have been released from service under conditions that are not disqualifying, and must report back or apply for reemployment within the period the statute sets for the length of service performed. The condition that does the most work in practice is the cumulative limit: the total period of service with respect to a particular employer relationship must generally not exceed five years. Congress then wrote exceptions around the limit for service that the individual did not truly choose or could not end. Service beyond five years is exempt from the count in defined circumstances: service the member could not conclude because release orders could not be obtained through no fault of the member, additional training certified as necessary by the Secretary concerned, retention on active duty by reason of war or national emergency, and service ordered in support of an operational or critical mission, together with specified categories of federal National Guard service. The structure is deliberate. Five years marks the outer bound of the sacrifice an individual employer is asked to absorb for voluntary extensions, while involuntary and mission driven service does not consume the allowance.

Reemployment standing alone would be easy to evade, so USERRA surrounded it with two further protections. Section 4311 prohibits discrimination: a person who is a member of, applies to be a member of, performs, has performed, applies to perform, or has an obligation to perform service in the uniformed services may not be denied initial employment, reemployment, retention in employment, promotion, or any benefit of employment on that basis, and an employer may not retaliate against a person who seeks to enforce the statute or assists in an investigation under it. Where service is a motivating factor in an adverse action, the employer bears the burden of showing that the action would have been taken in any event. Section 4316(c) then guards the restoration itself against immediate reversal by limiting discharge after reemployment: a person whose service exceeded one hundred eighty days may not be discharged except for cause within one year after reemployment, and a person whose service lasted more than thirty days but less than one hundred eighty one days receives the same for cause protection for one hundred eighty days. Taken together, the escalator, the discrimination ban, and the discharge protection answer the three ways a reemployment right can fail in practice: the veteran returns to a diminished position, the veteran is screened out or punished for serving at all, or the veteran is restored on paper and dismissed in fact. The 1940 statute had addressed the first failure; the 1994 rewrite gathered the answers to all three into one chapter, enforced through Department of Labor investigation and the federal courts.


### The Eligibility Reform and the Enrollment System

The Veterans Health Care Eligibility Reform Act of 1996, Public Law 104-262, signed by President Bill Clinton on October 9, 1996, was the centerpiece of the decade settlement because it redefined what eligibility for veterans health care meant. Under the rules that preceded the reform, eligibility was fragmented by category and by setting: hospital care stood on one footing, outpatient care on a narrower one, and many veterans qualified for inpatient treatment of conditions that the system had no clear authority to treat early and outside a hospital. The 1996 act replaced that patchwork with two linked devices. The first was a medical benefits package, a defined and uniform set of benefits that emphasized preventive and primary care alongside the full range of inpatient and outpatient services, available on the same terms to every enrolled veteran rather than assembled category by category. The second device was enrollment itself. Section 1705 of title 38 directed the Secretary to establish and operate a system for the enrollment of veterans, managed so that care provided to enrollees would be timely and acceptable in quality, and provided that beginning October 1, 1998, most veterans would have to be enrolled as a condition of receiving hospital and outpatient care. When enrollment opened on that date, the system sorted applicants into seven priority groups fixed by statute, ordered so that the strongest claims on the system came first.

The ordering of the original seven groups expressed the settlement in miniature. The highest groups belonged to veterans with service connected disabilities, ranked by severity of rating, with veterans rated fifty percent or more at the top, followed by those rated thirty or forty percent, and then a group joining former prisoners of war and Purple Heart recipients with veterans rated ten or twenty percent. A further group covered veterans receiving pension with aid and attendance or housebound benefits and veterans found catastrophically disabled. Below the disability groups came veterans unable to defray the expenses of necessary care under the means test in section 1722, then other veterans eligible under section 1710(a)(2), and finally the lowest of the seven, the veterans described in section 1710(a)(3), with no service connected disability and incomes above the means test, who agreed to make copayments. Two features of the design deserve emphasis. The benefit did not shrink as priority fell: the package was the same for the lowest group as for the highest, so priority governed admission rather than the content of care after admission. And the Secretary was required to decide each year which priority groups could enroll, in light of appropriations. The statute thus opened the system in principle to the whole veteran population while building the rationing mechanism into the same section that created the opening.

Eligibility reform also arrived at the moment of, and in support of, an organizational transformation that changed where veterans care happened. The Veterans Health Administration entered the mid 1990s as a system of largely autonomous hospitals, funded substantially on historical budgets and oriented toward inpatient beds. Under Dr. Kenneth W. Kizer, who became Under Secretary for Health in 1994 after directing the California Department of Health Services, the administration reorganized its facilities in 1995 into twenty two Veterans Integrated Service Networks, regional systems that replaced the earlier structure of regions and independent medical centers as the basic units of planning and budgeting. Network directors received authority to allocate resources inside their regions and accepted performance agreements tied to system goals, and funding formulas moved toward the number of patients served rather than the historical size of a facility. The clinical model moved with the structure. Hundreds of community based outpatient clinics extended care beyond the hospital campuses, and each enrolled veteran gained an assigned primary care provider; Kizer reported that the share of patients with an assigned primary care physician had risen, between 1994 and 1998, from roughly one in ten to more than eight in ten. None of that shift was administratively possible under the older eligibility rules alone, because a system authorized primarily for hospital care could not reorganize itself around outpatient visits. The 1996 act supplied the legal half of the change: a benefits package defined around preventive and primary care, furnished to a defined enrolled population whose numbers the networks could plan against and whose care could be capitated, measured, and compared. The eligibility settlement and the network reorganization were therefore one reform in two registers, statutory and managerial, each depending on the other.

### The Counter-Reading: Rationing Inside the Settlement

The strongest reading of the record from 1991 to 1996 is expansive: a presumption statute resolved scientific equipoise for claimants through a standing process, a reemployment statute gathered a half century of protection into a stronger code, and an eligibility statute converted a fragmented, hospital bound entitlement into a uniform package open in principle to every veteran. Against that arc, however, the settlement contains a documented counter-current, running through the priority system itself. Priority is a ranking of claims under scarcity. The highest groups held their places because their claims rested on service connected disability or inability to pay, the two grounds treated as obligations of the government rather than benefits subject to budget. The lowest groups held claims of a different character: honorable service, without a compensable disability and with income sufficient to pay for care elsewhere. For those veterans the 1996 act created not an entitlement to enrollment but a standing invitation, renewable each year at the Secretary discretion and conditioned on appropriations provided in advance. The fiscal restraint case for that design, made by Secretary Anthony J. Principi in defending the enrollment decisions that followed and by the Department's leadership in its budget testimony, was that a health system funded by annual discretionary spending cannot promise open ended access to a population of millions without regard to resources, and that protecting timely care for disabled and low income veterans required the legal capacity to close the lowest priority when money ran short. The expansion case, made by veterans service organizations including the Paralyzed Veterans of America and the Vietnam Veterans of America in congressional testimony, was that the promise of 1996 had been understood as a promise to veterans as such, and that sorting by income had converted a benefit of service into a benefit of need for everyone below the disability line.

### Were higher income veterans ever turned away from VA health care enrollment?

Yes. In January 2003 Secretary Anthony J. Principi suspended new enrollment for Priority Group 8, veterans without compensable service connected disabilities whose incomes exceeded VA means test thresholds. Veterans already enrolled kept their places. A partial relaxation effective in June 2009 admitted veterans whose incomes exceeded the thresholds by ten percent or less.

The suspension was not an accident of administration but an exercise of the authority the 1996 statute had created, applied to a group that the statute book had produced only months earlier. The Department of Veterans Affairs Health Care Programs Enhancement Act of 2001, Public Law 107-135, divided the old lowest group in two, effective October 2, 2002: a redefined Priority Group 7 for veterans whose incomes exceeded the national means test but fell below geographically adjusted thresholds drawn from housing law, and a new Priority Group 8 for veterans whose incomes exceeded both the national means test and the geographic means test for their area. Group 8 thus isolated, as a distinct statutory category, precisely the population whose claim was weakest under the settlement logic: no compensable service connected disability, and income above every threshold the system used. Demand had grown sharply after enrollment opened, and facilities reported waiting lists and appointments scheduled so far ahead that the timeliness the statute required could not be met. In a decision published in the Federal Register on January 17, 2003, at volume 68, pages 2670 to 2673, Secretary Anthony J. Principi determined that the department lacked sufficient resources to provide the medical benefits package to all veterans seeking enrollment, and suspended the enrollment of Priority Group 8 veterans who had not been enrolled as of that date. Veterans in Group 8 who were already enrolled retained their enrollment, and groups 1 to 7 remained open. The department defended the decision as a budget management measure necessary to protect the quality and timeliness of care for veterans in the higher priority categories, and Principi presented the suspension as preferable to allowing waiting lists to ration care invisibly for everyone. Carl Blake of the Paralyzed Veterans of America, testifying before the House Committee on Veterans Affairs, recorded that his organization and the co-authors of the Independent Budget had strongly opposed the decision and objected that a measure described as a one year moratorium remained in force for years afterward. His testimony estimated that as many as one and a half million Group 8 veterans stood to be denied enrollment by fiscal year 2008, a figure the Congressional Budget Office qualified in 2005 by finding that only about twenty percent of such veterans would in fact have used the care.

The relaxation, when it came, was partial and measured, which is what gives the episode its weight in any account of the direction of veterans benefits. Congress provided funds in the fiscal year 2009 appropriations, Public Law 110-329, to support expanded Group 8 enrollment, and regulations effective June 15, 2009, raised the effective income limits by admitting veterans whose incomes exceeded the national and geographic means test thresholds by ten percent or less, placing qualifying noncompensable zero percent service connected veterans and nonservice connected veterans into new subpriorities of Group 8. Secretary James B. Peake announced the planned reopening, estimating that about 265,000 previously ineligible veterans would become able to enroll. Veterans whose incomes exceeded the thresholds by more than ten percent remained outside the system. The episode therefore qualifies the ratchet claim rather than refuting it. Compensation presumptions and reemployment rights operated as entitlements in law and expanded by process; health care access for veterans without service connected conditions operated, by the deliberate design of 1996, as a managed program whose lowest tier could be and was closed by administrative decision for more than six years, and reopened only at the margin. The settlement gave the veterans system both of its modern characters at once: a benefits structure that grows by machinery, and an eligibility structure that rations by category, with the line drawn where service connected disability and low income end.

## The Claims System Matures and a New War Generation Enters the Statute Book

Between 2000 and 2010, Congress legislated for veterans in two registers at once. In the first, Congress repaired the machinery of the benefits system itself, defining how the Department of Veterans Affairs had to help a claimant build a record and deciding when a claimant could pay a lawyer to help instead. In the second, Congress wrote benefits for a generation whose service began on September 11, 2001, constructing an education benefit modeled in part on the program that followed the Second World War and a structured program of support for the family members who cared for seriously injured veterans at home. Read together, the four enactments show a system moving away from an older model, in which the Department presented itself as the claimant's helper and paid advocacy was treated as an intrusion, toward a model in which the duty to help was written as enforceable law, paid representation was admitted under regulation, and the household around the veteran became an object of legislation in its own right.

### The Duty to Assist Becomes Enforceable Law

The Veterans Claims Assistance Act of 2000, Public Law 106-475, was signed on November 9, 2000. Its stated purpose, carried in the text of the statute itself, was to reaffirm and clarify the duty of the Secretary of Veterans Affairs to assist claimants for benefits under the laws administered by the Secretary. That language of reaffirmation concealed a direct legislative reversal. In Morton v. West, 12 Vet. App. 477, decided on July 14, 1999, the United States Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims had held that the Department could not assist a claimant in developing a claim that was not well grounded. The well grounded claim doctrine had functioned as a threshold that a claimant had to cross before the machinery of assistance started to move. A claimant for service connection had to produce evidence of an existing disability, evidence of incurrence or aggravation in service, and medical evidence of a connection between the two, and only after that showing did the Department's obligation to help obtain further evidence arise. Morton carried the logic one step further by treating assistance for a claim that failed the threshold as assistance the Secretary lacked authority to provide at all.

The 2000 act dismantled that sequence. It eliminated the requirement that a claim be well grounded as a precondition of assistance, and it replaced the threshold with a set of affirmative duties that attached upon receipt of a complete or substantially complete application. The first duty was notice. Under the new section 5103 of title 38, the Secretary had to notify the claimant, and the claimant's representative if one existed, of information and medical or lay evidence not already provided that was necessary to substantiate the claim. The notice also had to divide responsibility between the parties, indicating which portion of the information and evidence the claimant was to provide and which portion the Secretary would attempt to obtain on the claimant's behalf. A companion provision required the Department to furnish application forms on request and to tell a claimant whose application was incomplete what information was necessary to complete it. Notice, under the statute, was therefore not a courtesy letter. It was a specification of the evidentiary record the claim required, issued before adjudication, and failures of notice became a ground on which the Board of Veterans' Appeals and the veterans court could return a case for further action.

The second duty concerned records. The new section 5103A required the Secretary to make reasonable efforts to assist a claimant in obtaining evidence necessary to substantiate the claim, and, as part of that assistance, to make reasonable efforts to obtain relevant records that the claimant adequately identified and authorized the Secretary to obtain. The duty reached private records as well as government records, and it carried its own procedural discipline. When the Secretary was unable to obtain records after reasonable efforts, the Secretary had to notify the claimant, identify the records that could not be obtained, explain the efforts made, and describe further action the Department would take on the claim. For records held by a federal department or agency, the efforts had to continue until the records were obtained unless it was reasonably certain that the records did not exist or that further efforts would be futile. For compensation claims, the statute named the core of the file expressly, directing the Department to obtain the claimant's service medical records and relevant records of treatment at facilities of the Department, together with other relevant records held by federal bodies. The statute set one boundary on the whole apparatus: the Secretary was not required to provide assistance where no reasonable possibility existed that the assistance would aid in substantiating the claim.

The third duty was medical. In a claim for disability compensation, the assistance required by section 5103A included providing a medical examination or obtaining a medical opinion when such an examination or opinion was necessary to make a decision on the claim. The statute tied that necessity to the state of the record, directing attention to whether the evidence indicated an existing disability or persistent or recurrent symptoms, whether the evidence suggested that the disability or symptoms could be associated with service, and whether the record contained sufficient competent medical evidence to decide the claim without the examination. That provision placed the familiar compensation and pension examination inside a statutory frame. Where the statutory conditions pointed toward an examination and none was provided, or where an examination was provided but did not answer the question the claim presented, the resulting decision rested on an incompletely developed record.

The effect on how claims were developed was structural. Before the 2000 act, the decisive contest in many cases had been fought at the threshold, over whether the claimant had assembled enough evidence, largely without departmental help, to make the claim plausible. After enactment, development itself became the legally supervised stage of the case. The Department had to say what evidence was missing, pursue records from custodians the claimant could not easily reach, tell the claimant when pursuit had failed, and obtain a medical opinion where the assembled evidence left diagnosis and connection unanswered. Denials issued on thin records became vulnerable on appeal, and remands for further development, for corrected notice, for records not sought, or for an adequate examination became a regular feature of appellate practice. The Government Accountability Office, reporting on implementation in 2002, recorded both the scale of the procedural change the Veterans Benefits Administration had undertaken and persistent problems with consistent compliance across regional offices. The act gave claimants enforceable procedural rights, and the price of those rights was a longer, more formal development process in which a case could cycle between a regional office and the Board on the adequacy of notice or of an examination before the merits were finally decided.

### Why were veterans not allowed to hire lawyers for their claims?

Paid counsel was effectively excluded because federal law capped the fee an attorney or agent could charge for a veterans claim at ten dollars, a sum too small to sustain a practice, while free representatives from veterans service organizations and the Department itself were presented as substitutes for lawyers in a nonadversarial process.

The cap had a precise and frequently garbled history. The first federal fee limitation for pension claims was five dollars per claim, enacted in the pension act of July 14, 1862, at the height of the Civil War pension system's expansion. Two years later, the statute of 1864 repealed the 1862 sections and substituted a maximum of ten dollars for making out and causing to be executed the declaration, the necessary affidavits, and the correspondence required to establish a claim before the Pension Office. Ten dollars was the sum the agent or attorney was to receive in full for all services in obtaining the pension, and it was not to be demanded or received until the pension had been obtained. The ten dollar figure, adjusted and recodified across the following century and carried into section 5904 of title 38 and its predecessors, remained the lawful maximum into the 1980s. Accounts that date the ten dollar cap itself to 1862 compress two statutes into one. The 1862 act supplied the five dollar limit and the criminal penalties for exceeding it; the 1864 act supplied the ten dollar limit that endured.

The stated rationale for the restriction rested on protection and on institutional design. In the account the Supreme Court accepted in Walters v. National Association of Radiation Survivors, 473 U.S. 305, decided in 1985, the fee limitation served a paternalistic interest in protecting veterans against extortion and improvident bargains with unscrupulous claim agents, a concern rooted in the Civil War era, when pension attorneys had built large practices on volume filing. The limitation also served an interest in economy and informality. The government argued, and the majority in Walters credited, the proposition that the benefits system had been designed to operate without retained counsel, with the Department and the recognized veterans service organizations assisting claimants without charge, and that introducing paid lawyers would make the process more adversarial and more expensive to administer. Justice Stevens, dissenting in Walters and joined by Justice Brennan and Justice Marshall, identified the same two interests and rejected their sufficiency, describing the first as paternalistic and the second as bureaucratic, and arguing that neither justified the restraint the ten dollar limit placed on a veteran's liberty to retain counsel of choice. The majority prevailed, and the constitutional challenge failed, which meant that any change had to come from Congress rather than from the courts.

Congress moved in two stages. The Veterans' Judicial Review Act of 1988, Public Law 100-687, which also opened a path to judicial review of benefits decisions, first broke the ten dollar regime by permitting fees for services provided after the Board of Veterans' Appeals had made a first final decision in the case, provided the attorney was retained within the period the statute allowed. That stage came very late in a case, after the claimant had already carried the claim through initial adjudication and a Board appeal without paid help. The Veterans Benefits, Health Care, and Information Technology Act of 2006, Public Law 109-461, signed on December 22, 2006, moved the starting point for paid representation to a much earlier moment in the process. Under section 101 of that act, an accredited attorney or agent could charge a fee for services provided after a claimant filed a notice of disagreement with a decision of the agency of original jurisdiction. The amendment took effect 180 days after enactment, on June 20, 2007, and applied to cases in which a notice of disagreement was filed on or after that date. The notice of disagreement is the filing by which a claimant who has received an adverse rating decision or other initial decision starts an appeal and requires the Department to prepare a statement of the case. Permitting fees from that point meant that paid counsel could shape the appellate record, identify the evidence to be developed on remand, and frame the issues for the Board, rather than arriving only after the Board had already ruled once against the claimant.

The 2006 act regulated the fees it admitted rather than leaving them to unrestricted contract. Fee agreements could be made contingent on the claim being resolved in a manner favorable to the claimant. For payment by the Department directly out of past due benefits, the total fee could not exceed 20 percent of the total amount of past due benefits awarded on the basis of the claim, and the statute provided that a fee not exceeding 20 percent of the past due amount awarded on a claim was to be presumed reasonable, subject to review and reduction by the Secretary where a fee was found excessive or unreasonable, with that review itself appealable to the Board. Past due benefits, the sum accumulated between the effective date of an award and its payment, supplied the fund from which the fee was withheld and paid. The same section built the gatekeeping regime that accompanied paid practice. The Secretary was directed to prescribe qualifications and standards for the accreditation of attorneys and agents, including requirements of good character and repute, competence to render claimants valuable service, and certification that the prescribed qualifications had been met. Accredited individuals had to report annually on their standing before courts, bars, and federal or state agencies, and the Secretary could not recognize an individual who had been suspended or disbarred and not reinstated. The claims process thus opened to the market for legal services a full procedural stage earlier than the 1988 act had allowed, and in exchange the practitioners who entered that market practiced under a federal accreditation system, a presumptive fee measure, and departmental review of the agreements they signed.

### Education Benefits Rebuilt for a New Service Generation

The Post-9/11 GI Bill arrived as Title V of the Supplemental Appropriations Act, 2008, Public Law 110-252, signed by President George W. Bush on June 30, 2008, and its benefits became payable on August 1, 2009, for service on or after September 11, 2001. Formally styled the Post-9/11 Veterans Educational Assistance Act of 2008, the title added a new chapter 33 to title 38 and replaced, for the post-2001 cohort that elected it, the flat monthly payment logic of the Montgomery GI Bill with a benefit built from the actual charges and living costs of the school attended. The Department paid tuition and fees directly to the institution, at the full benefit level up to the maximum amount of established charges regularly charged to in-state students for full time pursuit by the public institution of higher education in the state of enrollment with the highest such charges. Alongside tuition, the statute paid a monthly housing stipend equal to the monthly basic allowance for housing payable under title 37 for a member with dependents in pay grade E-5 residing in the military housing area encompassing the ZIP code of the institution, a design that tied the student's living support to the housing cost the armed forces themselves recognized for a junior enlisted household in the same locality. A third component addressed the smaller recurring costs of study through a yearly stipend for books, supplies, equipment, and other educational costs, set at 1,000 dollars at the full benefit level. Eligibility, entitlement length, and the detailed rate structure of that formula are traced in [a dedicated pillar account of the Post-9/11 GI Bill](/2019/03/01/post-911-gi-bill-2008-guide/), because the education statute carries a cluster of its own within the veterans domain.

Two features of the 2008 design marked its distance from earlier education programs. The first was transferability. The Secretary of Defense was authorized to permit a member who had served at least six years and who agreed to serve at least four additional years to transfer unused entitlement to a spouse or to children, converting an individual readjustment benefit into a family retention instrument allocated among dependents by months of entitlement. The second feature was gradation by service. The statute paid percentages of the maximum benefit according to aggregate active duty service after September 10, 2001, paying 100 percent at 36 months, and also at 30 continuous days where the individual was discharged because of a service connected disability, then 90 percent at 30 months, 80 percent at 24 months, 70 percent at 18 months, 60 percent at six months, and 50 percent at 90 days. Tuition support, the housing allowance, and the books stipend each scaled with the tier.

### Caregiving Enters the Benefit Structure

The Caregivers and Veterans Omnibus Health Services Act of 2010, Public Law 111-163, signed by President Barack Obama on May 5, 2010, legislated for a figure whom earlier veterans statutes had treated as background. Spouses, parents, and adult children had performed the daily labor of care for seriously injured veterans in every war generation, and that labor had been compensated, where it was compensated at all, only indirectly through the veteran's own compensation payments. Title I of the 2010 act added section 1720G to title 38 and directed the Secretary to establish a Program of Comprehensive Assistance for Family Caregivers. The statute also created a second, broader Program of General Caregiver Support Services for caregivers of veterans of all eras enrolled in the Department's health care system, and the distinction between the two programs carried the central political choice of the act.

Under the comprehensive program, a family caregiver approved as a provider of personal care services for an eligible veteran was to receive instruction, preparation, and training appropriate to the care to be provided, ongoing technical support, counseling, and lodging and subsistence when traveling with the veteran for treatment. A family caregiver designated as the primary provider received, in addition, mental health services as the Secretary determined appropriate, respite care of not less than 30 days annually, including care of the veteran for 24 hours per day commensurate with the care the caregiver provided, medical care through the Civilian Health and Medical Program of the Department of Veterans Affairs where the statutory conditions were met, and a monthly personal caregiver stipend. The stipend changed the legal character of family care. It treated caregiving by a family member as work the federal government would pay for directly, rather than as an unpaid incident of kinship that the veteran's compensation was expected to absorb. The Department opened applications on May 9, 2011, and the first stipend payments were issued in July 2011 to caregivers who had completed the required training, with payments made retroactive to the date of application.

Eligibility for the comprehensive program was deliberately narrow. An eligible veteran had to be a veteran, or a member of the armed forces undergoing medical discharge, who had a serious injury, including traumatic brain injury, psychological trauma, or another mental disorder, incurred or aggravated in the line of duty in the active military, naval, or air service on or after September 11, 2001, and who needed personal care services because of an inability to perform one or more activities of daily living, a need for supervision or protection based on symptoms or residuals of neurological or other impairment or injury, or other matters the Secretary considered appropriate. The date line in that definition excluded, from the stipend, the training linked benefits, and the caregiver health coverage of the comprehensive tier, every family caring for a veteran whose serious injury had been incurred before September 11, 2001, however severe the injury and however complete the caregiver's withdrawal from paid employment had been. Caregivers of veterans of earlier eras were routed to the general support services, which offered assistance without the stipend and the comprehensive package.

The politics of that limitation were argued in the open. Reporting by Tom Philpott for Military.com in 2014, summarizing the structure Congress had chosen in 2010, stated the fiscal rationale directly: Congress had opened the comprehensive program only to caregivers of veterans injured on or after September 11, 2001 in order to control costs, and advocates for the excluded families had hoped that a successful launch of a limited program would lead Congress to expand eligibility and end the inequity the limitation created. The Veterans of Foreign Wars, testifying before the House Committee on Veterans' Affairs through its associate legislative director Kayda Keleher, stated that the organization had strongly supported the 2010 act on the understanding that eligibility would later be expanded to veterans of all eras, and that the exclusion of caregivers of veterans of previous eras from the full complement of benefits implied that their service and sacrifices were not as significant, which the organization held to be wrong. The 2010 act itself kept the question before Congress by requiring the Department to report on the feasibility of expanding the comprehensive program. Within the statute as enacted in 2010, however, the line held where Congress had drawn it. A new war generation received a caregiver benefit built around stipend, training, respite, and mental health support, and the families of earlier war generations received general support and a feasibility study, a distribution defended on cost grounds by the limitation's supporters and accepted by allied veterans organizations only as a first stage.

## The Access Era and the Politics of Waiting

### The Phoenix Findings and the Fall of a Secretary

The access era opened with a failure of measurement that became a failure of trust. In April and May 2014, reports from the Phoenix Health Care System described schedulers who kept veterans off the official electronic waiting list while recording shorter waits than veterans had in fact endured. The Department of Veterans Affairs Office of Inspector General tested those reports. Its interim report, issued on May 28, 2014, substantiated the core allegation, finding that Phoenix schedulers had used unofficial lists and altered desired dates, that approximately 1,700 veterans seeking primary care had not been placed on the official electronic waiting list at all, and that those veterans were at risk of being forgotten or lost. Official Phoenix data showed an average wait of 24 days for a first primary care appointment. The inspector general calculated an average wait of 115 days. The interim report also described the manipulation as systemic and set in motion a nationwide audit. The final report of August 26, 2014, identified six veterans who had died after clinically significant delays and stated that investigators could not conclusively assert that an absence of timely care had caused those deaths. The substantiated scandal was one of falsified access and concealed delay, documented in the department's own records.

Secretary of Veterans Affairs Eric Shinseki apologized for the failures, initiated removal proceedings against senior leaders at Phoenix, and canceled performance awards tied to the discredited measures. On May 30, 2014, two days after the interim report appeared, President Barack Obama accepted Shinseki's resignation, stating that Shinseki had concluded that new leadership was required. The resignation converted an administrative audit into a legislative deadline, and the remedy that moved fastest was a statute that let some veterans step outside the system when the system could not see them in time or within a reasonable distance.

The Veterans Access, Choice, and Accountability Act of 2014, Public Law 113-146, signed by President Obama on August 7, 2014, answered the Phoenix findings with emergency resources, expanded authority to remove senior executives, and the Veterans Choice Program in section 101. Choice was designed as a temporary safety valve, and its eligibility rules translated the scandal into two administrable triggers. A veteran enrolled in VA health care qualified when the local VA facility could not schedule an appointment within 30 days of the clinically indicated date, or within 30 days of the date the veteran requested when no clinical date had been set. A veteran also qualified by distance, living more than 40 miles from a VA medical facility with a full time primary care provider, with further routes tied to travel by air, boat, or ferry, to an unusual or excessive travel burden, and to residence in a state or territory without a full service VA medical facility. The 30 day and 40 mile figures expressed the theory in numbers. Access was treated as a measurable entitlement condition, and when the department missed the standard, the veteran gained a funded option in the community. Every later access statute renegotiated the boundary between a trigger strict enough to control cost and a trigger loose enough to capture burdens that distance and a single wait figure measured poorly.

## Three Statutes in a Single Season of Repair

### Appeals Modernization and the End of the Legacy Cycle

The Veterans Appeals Improvement and Modernization Act of 2017, Public Law 115-55, signed on August 23, 2017, rebuilt the process by which a denied claimant challenged a benefits decision. Under the legacy system it replaced, a claimant filed a Notice of Disagreement, the agency prepared a Statement of the Case, the claimant perfected the appeal on VA Form 9, and the appeal was certified to the Board of Veterans' Appeals. Evidence could enter at multiple points, and a Board remand for further development returned the file to the regional office, after which a Supplemental Statement of the Case could send the same appeal back toward the Board. Cases cycled through remands for years. The 2017 statute replaced that single looping track with an election among defined lanes for decisions issued on or after its effective date of February 19, 2019. A claimant could seek Higher Level Review by a more senior adjudicator on the closed record, file a Supplemental Claim with new and relevant evidence, or appeal directly to the Board of Veterans' Appeals under dockets that traded a hearing and evidence submission against faster decision. Decisions were required to identify findings favorable to the claimant, and favorable findings bound later adjudicators absent clear and convincing evidence to the contrary. Legacy appeals continued under the older rules, with defined categories of legacy claimants permitted to opt into the modernized system. Congress traded an open ended cycle for bounded choices with deadlines, clearer notices, and preserved effective dates for claimants who acted within one year of a decision.

### Accountability, Discipline, and Protection for Disclosure

The Department of Veterans Affairs Accountability and Whistleblower Protection Act of 2017, Public Law 115-41, signed on June 23, 2017, addressed the personnel system from the other side. Managers had presided over falsified records, and employees who disclosed scheduling manipulation had described retaliation. The statute codified the Office of Accountability and Whistleblower Protection, headed by an Assistant Secretary, to receive disclosures, track retaliation allegations, and recommend disciplinary action. It required training on whistleblower protections, wrote protection into supervisory performance evaluation, and restricted discipline against an employee with a pending protected disclosure until specified procedural conditions had been satisfied. On discipline, it expedited the Secretary's authority to remove, demote, or suspend senior executives and other employees for misconduct or poor performance under a preponderance of the evidence standard, compressed notice and appeal periods, limited mitigation of a chosen penalty, and authorized recoupment of bonuses paid to employees later found to have engaged in qualifying misconduct. The pairing was deliberate. Speedier removal made accountability credible, and stronger disclosure protection made evidence of the next failure more likely to reach investigators.

### The Education Statute That Removed a Deadline

The Harry W. Colmery Veterans Educational Assistance Act of 2017, Public Law 115-48, signed on August 16, 2017, acquired its popular name, the Forever GI Bill, from one change in particular. The Post-9/11 program had imposed a delimiting date. A veteran generally had 15 years after the last discharge or release from active duty in which to use the entitlement, after which unused months expired. Section 105 removed that 15 year limitation for a defined cohort. The removal applied to veterans who left active duty on or after January 1, 2013, and to specified Fry Scholarship recipients whose eligibility arose on or after that date, including eligible spouses. Veterans whose service had ended before January 1, 2013, retained the delimiting date, so the statute drew a bright line rather than abolishing expiration for every holder at once. The boundary reflected the fiscal logic of the field. Removing a deadline converts a benefit with a predictable lapse rate into an open ended liability, and Congress accepted that conversion for newer entrants while declining to reopen the settled accounting for the older cohort. Around that change, the statute adjusted benefit levels, restored entitlement consumed at institutions that closed or lost approval, and expanded eligibility for certain reserve service. Each of the three 2017 statutes reduced a friction that rationed an earned benefit without a vote on the benefit itself, whether procedural cycling, managerial impunity, or a calendar deadline detached from educational need.


## Community Care Consolidated Under a Single Program

The VA MISSION Act of 2018, formally the John S. McCain III, Daniel K. Akaka, and Samuel R. Johnson VA Maintaining Internal Systems and Strengthening Integrated Outside Networks Act of 2018, Public Law 115-182, signed by President Donald Trump on June 6, 2018, replaced improvisation with consolidation. A veteran seeking care outside a VA facility could be routed through Choice or through a patchwork of older contract and fee basis authorities, each with distinct eligibility logic and payment plumbing. Section 101 established the Veterans Community Care Program as the single successor program, and the department launched it on June 6, 2019, the date on which Choice expired. VA remained the authorizer and payer, and community providers delivered the service. A reader tracing the operative replacement should consult [the detailed guide to the MISSION Act and its community care program](/2019/05/01/va-mission-act-2018-guide/) for the section by section account that a master history compresses. Community care was also available when VA did not offer the required service, when a veteran lived in a state or territory without a full service VA medical facility, when a veteran held grandfathered Choice distance eligibility, when the veteran and the referring clinician agreed that community care served the veteran's best medical interest, and when VA determined that a service line failed its own quality standards.

The access standards replaced the Choice triggers with a finer instrument, set by regulation under Secretary Robert Wilkie and announced in January 2019. Drive time displaced straight line mileage. A veteran qualified when the average drive to VA care exceeded 30 minutes for primary care, mental health care, and noninstitutional extended care services, or 60 minutes for specialty care. A veteran also qualified when VA could not furnish an appointment within 20 days of the requested date for primary care, mental health care, and noninstitutional extended care services, or within 28 days for specialty care, unless the veteran agreed to a later date. The shift answered a documented defect of the Choice era, in which a veteran separated from care by mountains, water, congestion, or poor roads could fall inside a radius and outside any practical access. Consolidation did not repeal rationing. It relocated rationing into standards that were more sensitive and more expensive to satisfy, because each tightening enlarged the population entitled to department funded care at community prices.

The same statute carried the caregiver expansion, and its staging showed Congress pricing an equity claim over time. The Program of Comprehensive Assistance for Family Caregivers, created in 2010, had limited its stipend, health coverage, respite, and training benefits to caregivers of veterans seriously injured in the line of duty on or after September 11, 2001. Section 161 of the MISSION Act ordered expansion to all eras and sequenced the cost. Expansion opened first, on October 1, 2020, to eligible veterans who had incurred or aggravated a serious injury in the line of duty on or before May 7, 1975, after the Secretary certified the information technology system required by the statute. The second stage followed on October 1, 2022, extending eligibility to veterans whose serious injury had been incurred or aggravated after May 7, 1975, and before September 11, 2001. Staging was the statute's method of conceding equal treatment across eras while spreading the scored cost and the application surge across fiscal years, a pattern that reached its fullest expression in the exposure legislation that closed the sequence.

## The Exposure Statute and the Scoring Constraint

### Why did the toxic exposure bill nearly fail in the Senate?

The bill nearly failed because Senator Pat Toomey objected that it reclassified existing veterans health spending from discretionary to mandatory, freeing room for unrelated discretionary spending. Supporters answered that the design prevented rationing through annual caps. Cloture failed on July 27, 2022, before passage followed on August 2, 2022.

The Sergeant First Class Heath Robinson Honoring our Promise to Address Comprehensive Toxics Act of 2022, Public Law 117-168, signed by President Joseph Biden on August 10, 2022, converted toxic exposure from a claims problem into a presumption system. Under the older model, a veteran bore the practical burden of connecting a condition to a burn pit, to herbicide spraying, or to another hazard of service, often decades after the exposure. The 2022 statute reversed the default. It added 23 burn pit and other toxic exposure related conditions to the list of service presumptions, expanded Agent Orange presumptions to added locations that included Thailand, Cambodia, Laos, Guam, American Samoa, and Johnston Atoll, and extended health care eligibility to defined categories of toxic exposed veterans, including more than 3.5 million post-2001 veterans identified in the Senate debate. A presumption removed the requirement that the individual veteran prove the causal link that Congress had decided service itself would supply, provided that service in a covered place and period and a diagnosis of a covered condition were established. The statute also established a formal process for evaluating exposures and recommending further presumptions, required toxic exposure screening for veterans enrolled in VA health care, and funded research, workforce, and facility capacity tied to the expected claims volume. A full anatomy of the presumption lists and eligibility categories belongs to [the dedicated treatment of the toxic exposure statute and its presumptive conditions](/2019/04/15/pact-act-2022-guide/), which owns that detail for the series.

The Camp Lejeune Justice Act of 2022, enacted as section 804 of Public Law 117-168, created a federal cause of action for harm caused by the water at Camp Lejeune, North Carolina. The action covered an individual, including a veteran or a legal representative, who had resided, worked, or otherwise been exposed, including exposure in utero, for not less than 30 days between August 1, 1953, and December 31, 1987, to water supplied by or on behalf of the United States at the base. The statute displaced the barrier that North Carolina's statute of repose had placed in the path of such claims, required the claimant first to present the claim under the exhaustion procedure of 28 U.S.C. section 2675, and made the United States District Court for the Eastern District of North Carolina the exclusive venue for suit, with causation shown by evidence sufficient to conclude that a causal relationship existed or was at least as likely as not. An action had to be brought within two years of enactment, or within 180 days after a claim was denied under the exhaustion procedure, and the cause of action was made the exclusive remedy for the covered harm, with punitive damages barred. The provision was exceptional because it treated a defined exposure cohort as tort plaintiffs against the United States rather than solely as benefit claimants against the department.

The near defeat of the statute in the summer of 2022 supplied the clearest evidence in the seventy year record for the proposition that scoring, rather than substantive opposition, operates as the binding constraint in veterans legislation. The Senate passed an earlier version on June 16, 2022, by 84 to 14. A constitutional defect then required the process to run again, because a revenue related provision had originated in the Senate in violation of the Origination Clause, and the House passed a corrected version on July 13, 2022, by 342 to 88. When the corrected bill returned to the Senate, cloture failed on July 27, 2022, by 55 to 42, five votes short of the 60 required, with 25 senators who had supported the June version voting against cloture. The Congressional Budget Office estimate most often cited in the debate placed the cost of the Senate passed package at 278.5 billion dollars over a decade, a figure that Senator Jon Tester, the chairman of the Senate Committee on Veterans' Affairs, and other supporters treated as the scored price of promises already incurred through service. Senator Pat Toomey of Pennsylvania, who led the objection, argued that the bill reclassified roughly 400 billion dollars of existing veterans health spending from the discretionary category, which Congress votes each year, to the mandatory category, which runs without annual action, and that moving that spending outside the discretionary caps would free an equivalent amount of room under those caps for unrelated discretionary spending over the decade. His amendment would have preserved that existing spending as discretionary while leaving the expansion itself intact, and Toomey stated on the floor that the amendment did not cap spending and did not reduce veterans spending. Supporters answered at equal specificity. Tester argued that the Toomey amendment would itself impose caps, because confining the Cost of War Toxic Exposures Fund created in section 805 to annual appropriations practice would subject projected exposure costs to yearly limits, and that capped funding for an entitlement population produces rationing when claims exceed the appropriation. Secretary of Veterans Affairs Denis McDonough made the same argument on July 31, 2022, stating that the amendment's estimates would set caps and could require the department to ration care. The dispute was between two theories of budget control, annual discretionary discipline on one side and entitlement style funding security on the other, and the history of the budget categories at stake reaches back to [the budget process statute that built the modern scoring framework](/2020/01/01/congressional-budget-act-1974-guide/). After six days of encampment by veterans and advocates at the Capitol, the Senate rejected the Toomey amendment and two companion amendments on August 2, 2022, and agreed to the House amendment by 86 to 11, Record Vote Number 280. A measure with 86 votes in final passage had come within five votes of procedural death over classification and scorekeeping, after its substantive expansion had already commanded 84 votes in the same chamber.


## Turning Points, Throughlines, and a Periodization of the Field

Four turning points organize the seventy years, and each changed the legal form of the veterans promise rather than merely enlarging its budget. Codification came first, as the Veterans' Benefits Act of 1957 and the 1958 enactment of title 38 as positive law gave the field a stable textual home. The second turning point was the pairing of 1988, when the Department of Veterans Affairs Act raised the Veterans Administration to a Cabinet department and the Veterans' Judicial Review Act opened Board decisions to review in an Article I court, ending the long statutory bar on judicial scrutiny of individual benefits determinations. A claimant who had petitioned an unreviewable administrator became a litigant with a record, a docket, and a court. The third turning point was the settlement of 1996, when the Veterans' Health Care Eligibility Reform Act ratified a post Cold War equilibrium administered under budget discipline, demonstrating that retrenchment in this field takes the form of calibration rather than repeal. The fourth turning point was the exposure turn, prepared by the Agent Orange Act of 1991 and completed by the toxic exposure statute of 2022, in which Congress displaced individualized proof of causation with presumptions tied to service in a place during a period.

Across those turning points, three throughlines recur. Expansions follow wars. The benefit structure enlarged after Korea, after Vietnam, after the Gulf War, and after the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan, each time with a lag measured in years or decades, because costs that present as disability, exposure illness, and educational need mature more slowly than the appropriations for the war itself. Reforms follow scandals. Managerial and procedural legislation clustered after documented failures, from claims backlogs to the Phoenix scheduling falsification of 2014, and the reform statutes targeted process, discipline, and access measurement rather than benefit levels, because scandal discredits administration without discrediting the underlying promise. Compensation ratchets while access is rationed. Disability compensation, dependency and indemnity compensation, and education entitlement moved in one direction across the whole period, and no Congress repealed a mature compensation entitlement held by a living cohort. Health care access, by contrast, was governed in every era by some rationing device, whether enrollment priority groups, facility capacity, distance and wait triggers, drive and wait standards, or staged eligibility dates. The asymmetry has a political logic. Compensation is an individual legal entitlement that a veteran can plead, appeal, and carry into court after 1988. Access is a service delivered from a finite physical system, and its rationing can be presented as administration even when its effect on the veteran equals denial.

A defensible periodization follows, dividing the seventy years into five periods defined by legal form rather than by presidential administration. Consolidation runs from 1950 to 1958. Expansion under administrative finality runs from 1958 to 1988, as Vietnam era benefits accumulated inside a system whose individual decisions remained largely beyond judicial review. The reviewable department runs from 1988 to 2001, opening with Cabinet status and judicial review and closing as a new generation of combat veterans entered the system after the attacks of September 11, 2001. The access era runs from 2001 to 2018, dominated by the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan, by the growth of community care from exception to program, and by the Phoenix scandal and the Choice and MISSION sequence. The exposure era opens at the end of the 2010s and reaches its defining enactment in the toxic exposure statute of 2022, in which presumption displaced individual causation proof at the largest scale in the field's history and the cost of that displacement was funded through mandatory classification. The scheme earns its place by the movement it clarifies, from a codified promise administered without review, to a reviewable entitlement, to a rationed service rebuilt around access metrics, to a presumption system whose fiscal design became its decisive battleground.

The claim that the whole record supports can be stated in the full form that the closing assessment of this article takes up and tests. Veterans legislation since 1950 operated as a one way ratchet with a scoring constraint. The ratchet describes the direction of entitlement. Compensation expanded, presumptions multiplied, deadlines fell for newer cohorts, and eligibility widened across eras, and movement in the opposite direction appeared as staging, delay, grandfathering, and calibration rather than as repeal of a vested benefit. The scoring constraint describes the location of genuine contest. Because substantive opposition to veterans benefits rarely sustained a majority in stated position, the decisive fights occurred over classification, caps, offsets, effective dates, and Congressional Budget Office estimates, over whether a cost would be counted as mandatory or discretionary, immediate or staged, inside or outside a cap. Phoenix produced an access statute because falsified waits made rationing visible, and the exposure statute nearly failed because its mandatory design made cost visible. The assessment that follows weighs that claim against the counterevidence in the full record, including episodes in which Congress accepted an unscored obligation or staged a cost rather than defeating it.

The record assembled in the statute sequence supports the claim in the strong form in which the opening section stated it. US veterans legislation operated as a one way ratchet with a scoring constraint, and that pairing is the organizing fact of the field because it explains both the direction of the law and the location of its real contests. Direction came first. A war created a cohort, a cohort acquired benefits, and benefits once attached to service connected conditions stayed attached. Codification in 1958 gave each expansion a permanent address in title 38, education programs succeeded one another by cohort without stripping an earlier cohort of an entitlement already earned, and presumptions converted contested questions of causation into standing administrative lists that grew by process rather than by repeated litigation.

The evidence for the ratchet is the absence of the opposite motion. No Congress in the sequence repealed a mature disability compensation entitlement, withdrew a presumption once granted to a living cohort, or closed an education program to the generation that had entered service under its terms. Change at the boundary took other forms. The contributory program of 1976 bound only new entrants. The education statute of 2017 removed a delimiting date only for veterans who had left service after a stated day, leaving the older cohort under the older rule. The caregiver program of 2010 drew an injury date line, and the community care statute of 2018 erased that line in stages spread across fiscal years. Staging, grandfathering, and cohort lines were the instruments by which Congress managed cost without recording a vote against a benefit already held.

Contest, where contest genuinely occurred, gathered around counting. The fate of the toxic exposure statute of 2022 made the pattern visible at full scale. The Senate had passed the substance by a wide margin, cloture then failed by five votes after Senator Pat Toomey objected that the bill reclassified existing health spending from discretionary to mandatory, and passage followed by 86 votes to 11 after Senator Jon Tester and Secretary Denis McDonough answered that annual caps on an entitlement population would produce rationing. Earlier fights carried the same shape at smaller scale, in the bargaining over the percentage of the 1974 education increase and in the staged certification that governed caregiver expansion. Opposition organized around estimates, classifications, offsets, and effective dates because opposition to the underlying benefit could rarely organize at all.

The rationing exception qualifies the claim and, properly read, strengthens it. Health care access for veterans without service connected disabilities was never placed on the same legal footing as compensation. The eligibility settlement of 1996 built priority groups into enrollment, authorized the Secretary to decide each year which groups appropriations could support, and supplied the authority under which Secretary Anthony J. Principi suspended new enrollment for the highest income group in January 2003. That suspension held for years, drew sustained opposition from the Paralyzed Veterans of America, and ended only in a partial reopening measured at ten percent above the means test thresholds. Compensation ratcheted because compensation is an individual entitlement pleaded claim by claim. Access was rationed because access is a service drawn from finite hospitals, clinics, and appropriations, and the statute of 1996 said so in its own text.

A reader following the field onward has a clear route. The statute table that follows this assessment serves as the map, with each enactment placed by year, public law, character, and the war or event that produced it. The sequence itself rewards reading in order, because each layer explains the machinery the next layer inherited. The dedicated accounts of the education program for the post 2001 cohort, of the community care consolidation, of the toxic exposure presumptions, and of the claims process supply the depth that a master history compresses, and the budget process account explains the scoring framework inside which every later fight was staged. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What veterans legislation has Congress passed since 1950?

Congress legislated in layers. The Korean GI Bill of 1952 extended the readjustment model to a second war, the acts of 1957 and 1958 consolidated the field into title 38, and the act of 1966 made education assistance a standing incident of service. Congress then widened health sharing in 1970, joined employment duties to education increases in 1974, tested a contributory program in 1976, created Vet Centers in 1979, and replaced the contributory design in 1984. Cabinet status and judicial review arrived in 1988, herbicide presumptions in 1991, reemployment protection in 1994, and enrollment based eligibility in 1996. A duty to assist followed in 2000, paid representation in 2006, a rebuilt education benefit in 2008, caregiver support in 2010, community access in 2014 and 2018, an appeals and accountability settlement in 2017, and toxic exposure presumptions in 2022.

### Q: When did veterans legislation make the VA a cabinet department?

The Veterans Administration became a cabinet department through the Department of Veterans Affairs Act, Public Law 100-527. President Ronald Reagan signed the act on October 25, 1988, at Fort McNair, after the House passed the bill by 399 votes to 17 and the Senate by 84 votes to 12. Cabinet status did not take effect on signature. The statute set March 15, 1989, as the effective date, after the presidential transition, so that the incoming President would appoint the first Secretary. President George H. W. Bush appointed Edward J. Derwinski as Administrator on March 1, 1989, and Derwinski became the first Secretary of Veterans Affairs on March 15, 1989, when the redesignation took effect and the renamed department took its seat in the Cabinet.

### Q: Does veterans legislation expand after wars or after scandals?

Wars and scandals produced different kinds of law. Wars expanded benefits, because each conflict created a cohort whose interrupted schooling, disability, or exposure Congress answered with a readjustment package, as after Korea, Vietnam, and the wars that followed the attacks of September 11, 2001. Scandals reformed administration, because scandal discredited machinery without discrediting the promise behind it. The Teague Committee investigation of abuse under the 1944 education program produced payment controls rather than repeal in 1952. The Phoenix scheduling falsification of 2014 produced an access statute, expanded removal authority, and later accountability legislation rather than any reduction in benefits. The field therefore grew on two tracks at once, entitlement widening after wars and procedure tightening after failures, with each track leaving the other intact.

### Q: What did the 1996 eligibility reform do in veterans legislation?

The Veterans Health Care Eligibility Reform Act of 1996, Public Law 104-262, signed on October 9, 1996, replaced fragmented eligibility categories with two linked devices. The first was a uniform medical benefits package, defined around preventive and primary care alongside inpatient and outpatient services, offered on the same terms to every enrolled veteran. The second was enrollment itself under section 1705 of title 38. From October 1, 1998, most veterans had to enroll to receive hospital and outpatient care, and applicants were sorted into seven statutory priority groups ordered by strength of claim, with service connected disability at the top. Priority governed admission rather than the content of care. The Secretary decided each year which groups appropriations could support, and the package gave legal support to the shift toward outpatient clinics and assigned primary care.

### Q: What is USERRA in veterans legislation?

USERRA is the Uniformed Services Employment and Reemployment Rights Act of 1994, Public Law 103-353, codified at sections 4301 to 4335 of title 38. The act protects the civilian career that uniformed service interrupts. Under the escalator principle drawn from Fishgold v. Sullivan Drydock and codified in sections 4313 and 4316, a returning service member steps back onto the seniority escalator at the point continuous employment would have reached, with seniority and seniority based benefits credited across the absence. Eligibility depends on notice, non disqualifying release, and timely return, and cumulative service with one employer generally may not exceed five years, subject to exceptions for involuntary and mission driven service. Section 4311 bars discrimination and retaliation based on service, and section 4316 guards restored employees against discharge without cause for a protected period.

### Q: Does veterans legislation ever cut benefits?

Cuts in the familiar sense, meaning repeal of a benefit held by a living cohort, are absent from the record for service connected compensation, and no Congress withdrew a granted presumption. Contraction took narrower forms. Congress replaced the chapter 34 education allowance with the less generous contributory VEAP program for new entrants only, leaving the earlier cohort untouched. Congress drew cohort lines, as when the education statute of 2017 removed a delimiting date only for veterans who had left service after a stated day. Health care access for veterans without service connected disabilities was rationed directly, most sharply when enrollment for the highest income priority group was suspended in 2003, a decision the Department defended as budget management and the Paralyzed Veterans of America opposed in congressional testimony.

### Q: What did the 1988 judicial review law add to veterans legislation?

The Veterans Judicial Review Act, Public Law 100-687, enacted on November 18, 1988, ended the rule under which decisions of the Administrator on veterans benefits were final and beyond review by any court. The act created the United States Court of Veterans Appeals, an independent Article I court later renamed the United States Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims, with exclusive jurisdiction to review decisions of the Board of Veterans Appeals. Review proceeded on the record before the agency, under standards that set aside decisions found arbitrary, capricious, or contrary to law, while findings of material fact were tested for clear error. The Board had to state adequate reasons and bases. The court could not review the rating schedule itself, and the Secretary received no right to appeal a Board decision.

### Q: What is the timeline of US veterans legislation?

The timeline divides into five periods defined by legal form. Consolidation ran from 1950 to 1958, closing with title 38 enacted as positive law. Expansion under administrative finality ran from 1958 to 1988, as the standing GI Bill, Vet Centers, and the Montgomery program accumulated inside a system whose decisions courts could not review. The reviewable department ran from 1988 to 2001, opening with cabinet status and judicial review. The access era ran from 2001 to 2018, dominated by two wars, the Phoenix scandal, and the Choice and MISSION sequence in community care. The exposure era opened at the end of the following decade and reached its defining enactment in 2022, when presumptions displaced individual proof of causation at the largest scale in the history of the field.

### Q: Did the Korean War have its own GI Bill?

Yes. The Korean War cohort received a dedicated statute, the Veterans Readjustment Assistance Act of 1952, Public Law 82-550, approved on July 16, 1952, for service on or after June 27, 1950. Eligibility required at least 90 days of active service and a discharge under conditions other than dishonorable, with shorter service qualifying after a discharge for a service incurred disability. The act copied the architecture of the 1944 program, with education at the center alongside loan guaranty and unemployment compensation, and redesigned the payment machinery. A single allowance went to the veteran alone, at 110 dollars a month for full time training without dependents, 135 dollars with one dependent, and 160 dollars with more than one. Entitlement accrued at one and one half times active duty, capped at 36 months.

### Q: What does title 38 of the United States Code contain?

Title 38 of the United States Code, titled Veterans Benefits, contains the codified law of federal veterans benefits as enacted as positive law by Public Law 85-857 in September 1958. Its chapters carry the substantive programs: disability compensation and pension, education assistance in successive chapters for successive cohorts, vocational rehabilitation in chapter 31, home loan guaranty, life insurance, and burial benefits. Other chapters and sections govern the machinery around those programs, including health care and enrollment in chapter 17 and section 1705, claims procedure and the duty to assist in sections 5103 and 5103A, representation and fees in section 5904, employment and reemployment rights in chapter 43, and the presumptions of service connection in section 1116. Amendments since 1958 entered the law as changes to numbered sections of the title.

### Q: What is the difference between compensation and pension in veterans law?

Compensation and pension answer different needs. Compensation is paid for disability or death connected to military service. Eligibility rests on service connection, severity is measured by a percentage rating under the rating schedule, and payment does not depend on the financial means of the veteran or survivor. Pension is a need based benefit. Eligibility rests on qualifying wartime service together with permanent and total disability that is not service connected, or on age, and payment is limited by income and net worth rules administered as a means test, the same test that section 1722 of title 38 applies in the health care system. Supplements for veterans who need regular aid and attendance, or who are housebound, attach in both systems, and the distinction between the two grounds much of the priority ordering in veterans health enrollment.

### Q: How did the Phoenix wait time scandal change veterans law?

The Phoenix scandal changed the law by converting access into a measurable statutory condition. The Office of Inspector General interim report of May 28, 2014, found that Phoenix schedulers had used unofficial lists and altered desired dates, that about 1,700 veterans seeking primary care had never been placed on the official electronic waiting list, and that the real average wait of 115 days dwarfed the official figure of 24 days. Secretary Eric Shinseki resigned on May 30, 2014. Congress answered with the Veterans Access, Choice, and Accountability Act, Public Law 113-146, which funded community care for veterans who could not obtain an appointment within 30 days or who lived more than 40 miles from a qualifying facility, and which expanded authority to remove senior executives. Later accountability and community care statutes rebuilt on that foundation.

### Q: What is the duty to assist in a veterans benefits claim?

The duty to assist is the obligation of the Secretary of Veterans Affairs to help a claimant build the record a claim requires, codified by the Veterans Claims Assistance Act of 2000, Public Law 106-475. The act abolished the well grounded claim threshold that Morton v. West had used to deny assistance, and attached the duty to a complete or substantially complete application. Under section 5103, the Department must notify the claimant of evidence needed to substantiate the claim and divide responsibility for obtaining it. Under section 5103A, the Department must make reasonable efforts to obtain identified records, must pursue federal records until the records are obtained or further effort is shown futile, and must provide a medical examination or opinion where the record makes one necessary to decide a compensation claim.

### Q: How are veterans health care priority groups assigned?

Priority groups are assigned at enrollment in the veterans health care system created by the eligibility reform of 1996 under section 1705 of title 38, which opened on October 1, 1998, with seven statutory groups. Assignment follows the strength of the claim on the system. Veterans with service connected disabilities stand highest, ranked by rating, with ratings of fifty percent or more at the top, followed by ratings of thirty or forty percent, and then a group joining former prisoners of war and Purple Heart recipients with ratings of ten or twenty percent. A further group covers veterans receiving pension with aid and attendance or housebound benefits and veterans found catastrophically disabled. Lower groups rest on the means test in section 1722, and the lowest group covers veterans without service connected disability and with income above the test who agree to copayments. The benefits package is identical across groups.

### Q: What do Vet Centers do and who runs them?

Vet Centers provide readjustment counseling outside the hospital system. The Veterans Health Care Amendments of 1979, Public Law 96-22, authorized counseling for Vietnam era veterans who requested it, beginning with a general mental and psychological assessment of readjustment to civilian life and continuing, where a clinician found services necessary, with outpatient mental health care, follow up care, and services for family members. Eligibility turned on service and request rather than on a disability rating, so no claim had to be won first. The centers operate as small community based offices run by the Department of Veterans Affairs, set apart from medical centers in place and, by design, in management. Staffs of about four or five people drew heavily on veterans of the same war as counselors, outreach workers, and team leaders alongside psychologists and social workers.

### Q: How did Congress respond to Agent Orange exposure claims?

Congress answered Agent Orange claims by replacing individual proof of causation with a presumption system in the Agent Orange Act of 1991, Public Law 102-4. Section 3 directed the Secretary to obtain recurring reviews of the scientific evidence from the National Academy of Sciences, which reported first in 1994 and at least every two years across the following decade. Section 1116 of title 38 then presumed both exposure during service in Vietnam and service connection for listed diseases. For diseases Congress had not named, the Secretary had to prescribe a presumption whenever a positive association existed, a term the statute defined to mean that credible evidence for the association equaled or outweighed credible evidence against it. Favorable diseases entered the regulations, and unfavorable determinations required published reasons in the Federal Register.

### Q: Why is budget scoring the main constraint on veterans legislation?

Budget scoring binds because substantive opposition rarely does. A proposal to widen compensation, add a presumption, or drop a deadline attracts broad stated support, so the decisive questions concern the Congressional Budget Office estimate, whether spending counts as mandatory or discretionary, whether an offset is demanded, and whether cost can be staged across fiscal years. The toxic exposure statute of 2022 supplied the clearest case. Its scored cost stood at 278.5 billion dollars over a decade, Senator Pat Toomey objected that reclassification of existing health spending would free room under discretionary caps, cloture failed once by five votes, and the measure then passed by 86 votes to 11. Senator Jon Tester and Secretary Denis McDonough framed the same design choice as protection against rationing through annual appropriations limits.

### Q: What did the Forever GI Bill change for newer veterans?

The Forever GI Bill is the Harry W. Colmery Veterans Educational Assistance Act of 2017, Public Law 115-48, and its signature change concerned time. The Post-9/11 program had imposed a delimiting date under which entitlement generally expired 15 years after the last discharge or release from active duty. Section 105 of the 2017 act removed that limitation for a defined cohort: veterans who left active duty on or after January 1, 2013, together with specified Fry Scholarship recipients whose eligibility arose on or after that date, including eligible spouses. Veterans whose service ended before that date kept the delimiting rule, so expiration ended for newer veterans rather than for every holder at once. The statute also adjusted benefit levels, restored entitlement consumed at institutions that closed or lost approval, and expanded eligibility for certain reserve service.

### Q: Who was the first Secretary of Veterans Affairs?

The first Secretary of Veterans Affairs was Edward J. Derwinski. The Department of Veterans Affairs Act, Public Law 100-527, signed by President Ronald Reagan on October 25, 1988, delayed cabinet status until March 15, 1989, so that the incoming President would name the first Secretary. President George H. W. Bush appointed Derwinski, a former member of the House of Representatives from Illinois and a veteran of the Second World War, as Administrator of Veterans Affairs on March 1, 1989. Derwinski became Secretary on March 15, 1989, when the redesignation took effect. The department he inherited performed the functions the Veterans Administration had already performed, running the hospital system, compensation and pension programs, education benefits, home loan guarantees, and national cemeteries under a Secretary seated in the Cabinet.

### Q: What role do veterans service organizations play in shaping veterans legislation?

Veterans service organizations shaped the field as drafters, witnesses, and representatives. The American Legion held a central drafting role in the Servicemen Readjustment Act of 1944, the model every later readjustment statute copied or revised. Organizations pressed Congress during the 1974 bargaining, where the Legion held out for an increase near 23 percent before the conference settled at 22.7 percent, and supporters of the Agent Orange Act treated its equipoise standard as a correction organizations had sought for years. Carl Blake of the Paralyzed Veterans of America testified against the 2003 enrollment suspension on behalf of his organization and the Independent Budget coauthors. Kayda Keleher of the Veterans of Foreign Wars testified that her organization had supported the caregiver act of 2010 as a first stage toward expansion to all eras. Inside claims, recognized organizations also supplied the free representation on which the system long relied.
