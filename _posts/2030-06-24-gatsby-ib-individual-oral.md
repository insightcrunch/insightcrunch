The IB individual oral is a strange examination. For ten minutes a student speaks about a global issue through extracts from two studied works, and then the examiner questions the speaker for five minutes more. The individual oral explores a global issue through extracts, and a Gatsby passage must serve a clear global issue. That sentence is the whole task in miniature, and most preparation fails because it ignores half of it. Students prepare the passage and hope the issue will attach itself somewhere during the talk, as if proximity were the same as argument.

The namable claim of this guide is that the oral is won or lost before the speaking begins, in the choice of the extract and in the explicit connection between that extract and the global issue. A passage in service of a global issue is the unit the task assesses. Not the best scene, not the most quotable forty lines, not the passage the student loved most in class, but the passage whose details, once analyzed, make the global issue more visible than it was before the extract was read. Everything that follows is a method for producing that kind of passage work: selecting an extract that carries an arguable claim about the question, and connecting every quotation to that claim so the listener hears one argument, not two topics standing side by side.

The complication deserves an honest statement, because it is the failure examiners hear most often and the one strong students commit without noticing. A student chooses the Plaza Hotel confrontation because it is dramatic, and the global issue is the abuse of power. The oral describes the confrontation in fine detail: Tom's interrogation, Gatsby's collapse, Daisy's retreat. The issue gets mentioned in the opening sentence and again in the closing one, like bookends. Between those mentions the passage and the question never touch. The analysis is competent, the passage is accurate, and the connection is decorative. The result is an oral about Gatsby with a global issue standing beside it, not an oral about a global issue seen through Gatsby. This happens because preparation usually proceeds by chapter: the student rereads, marks favorite scenes, and then tries to bolt an issue onto a passage chosen for its drama. The passage floats beside the issue instead of illuminating it, and no amount of fluent delivery closes that gap, because the gap was created at the selection stage. This guide argues that selection and connection are the two skills that separate the floating oral from the illuminating one, and that both are learnable through a disciplined method: test every candidate passage against the issue before committing, build each quotation into the claim, and rehearse the oral as an argument rather than a recital.

Turning an exam task into a usable method is the standard this series holds itself to, and the method here has five parts. First, which Gatsby passages survive the global-issue test, with the issues each one can genuinely support. Second, how a favorite scene becomes a working extract, with boundaries drawn and defended. Third, what illumination looks like at the sentence level, with floating and connected examples of the same passage side by side. Fourth, how the two extracts converse, how quotations work aloud, how to survive the five-minute questions, and which selection traps to avoid. Fifth, a week of drills that builds the two key skills directly. For the broader map of Gatsby across IB assessment, [the IB hub](/2030/06/17/gatsby-ib-english/) routes to this guide for the oral task itself; what follows stays inside the oral's own demands and does not repeat the general coverage there.

### Which Gatsby Scenes Survive the Global-Issue Test?

The novel offers many scenes students love, but the oral does not reward the beloved scene. It rewards the extract that carries an arguable claim about a global issue. Eight candidate passages get tested below against that standard, and only the ones whose details do analytical work survive, each matched to the issues it can genuinely support.

The valley of ashes in chapter two survives because its details are already doing the analysis. The ash-grey men who move dimly through the dust, the long blighted trains that crawl to a mysterious halt, and the enormous yellow spectacles of the Doctor T. J. Eckleburg billboard watching over it all give the speaker concrete images with arguable meanings attached. The global issue of environmental degradation caused by industrial progress finds a fitting extract here, because Fitzgerald ties the wasteland directly to the wealth that produced it: the ashes are the residue of the same economy that throws Gatsby's parties. A speaker can argue that the novel presents ecological ruin not as accident but as the landscape of inequality, where the poor breathe what the rich burn. The passage also supports a second issue, the dehumanization of labor, since the men in the valley are described as already half dissolved into their surroundings. Two genuine issues from one extract is the mark of a passage worth selecting, provided the speaker commits to one of them rather than gesturing at both.

The reunion in chapter five survives on the strength of its small objects. The defunct clock on the mantel, which Gatsby nearly knocks over and Nick catches, the rain that gives way to sunlight as the meeting turns, and the shirts heaped in a tumble of color until Daisy weeps into them, each of these is a detail a speaker can point to and unpack aloud. The global issue of materialism distorting intimate relationships fits this extract closely, because the scene stages desire expressed entirely through objects: Gatsby does not tell Daisy he loves her so much as he shows her what money bought. A speaker can argue that the passage presents love as something the novel's world can only speak about in the language of possessions, and that Daisy's tears at the shirts are the moment the dream touches reality and finds it is made of fabric. The clock supports the related claim that the past cannot be recovered by force of wealth, since time here is something Gatsby handles clumsily and nearly breaks. The passage is compact, its details are memorable without the book in hand, and its language rewards close attention, which is exactly what an extract needs to do under the pressure of being heard once.

The Plaza Hotel confrontation in chapter seven survives because it concentrates the novel's class conflict into one room. Tom's cross-examination of Gatsby, the revelation of the drugstore and bootlegging money, Daisy's panic and withdrawal, and Tom's final contemptuous dismissal as he sends the defeated party home together, all of this happens in a single stifling suite on the hottest day of the summer. The global issue of inherited privilege defending itself against new wealth finds its extract here, and the speaker's argument can be precise: the scene shows that class power in the novel is not about money but about the authority to decide whose money counts. Tom wins without being admirable, which is the scene's sharpest point, and a speaker who notices that the victor is the novel's least sympathetic figure has found an arguable claim about how power protects itself. The heat, the mint juleps, the closed room all contribute to an atmosphere of pressure that the speaker can connect to the question, since the confrontation is staged as an interrogation in which the old order holds every advantage.

The final pages of chapter nine survive as the novel's widest lens. Nick's meditation on the green light, the Dutch sailors' first sight of the new world, and the closing image of boats beating on against the current give the speaker language that already moves from the particular to the general. The global issue of nostalgia and the impossibility of recovering the past fits naturally, but the stronger move is to widen further: the passage supports an argument about the persistence of collective longing, the way whole societies fix their hopes on a receding object. A speaker can argue that Fitzgerald turns one man's reaching toward a light into an image of a national habit, and that the passage's power lies in making the reader complicit, since the meditation addresses anyone who has believed tomorrow would be better. The risk with this extract is its fame. Because the ending is the most quoted part of the novel, the speaker must analyze rather than admire, finding something in the language that the listener has not already heard praised. The passage rewards a speaker who notices the shift from past to present tense, the way the meditation suddenly includes the reader, because that shift is a formal choice the speaker can connect to the question's universality.

The first dinner at the Buchanans' in chapter one survives for a different reason: it shows prejudice as furniture. Tom's talk about the supremacist book he has been reading, Daisy's brittle cynicism, and the casual way the conversation moves past Tom's views without challenge give the speaker an extract about the normalization of bigotry in elite spaces. The global issue of prejudice and discrimination finds a precise match here, because the scene's point is not that Tom holds ugly views but that nobody at the table treats them as disqualifying. A speaker can argue that the passage presents bigotry as a social credential, something a wealthy man can voice at dinner the way he might discuss horses, and that the real indictment falls on the silence around him. Nick's narration matters here: he records the talk without intervening, and a speaker can connect that narrative passivity to the question by arguing that the novel shows how respectable people accommodate what they should confront. The extract is early in the novel, which means the speaker must establish context efficiently, but its compactness is an advantage, since the whole dinner scene runs only a few pages and the relevant lines are easy to bound.

The Dan Cody episode and Gatsby's boyhood schedule in chapter six survive as the novel's account of self-invention. The seventeen-year-old James Gatz writing out his daily regimen of exercise, study, and elocution, and the older Gatsby's service to the copper magnate Dan Cody, who gave him a taste of the wealthy world and then left him with nothing, give the speaker the material for an argument about social mobility and its limits. The global issue of inequality of opportunity fits this extract, because the passage shows reinvention as real labor: Gatsby did not merely dream himself rich but worked at the transformation with a discipline the schedule documents. A speaker can argue that the novel treats the self-made man as both admirable and doomed, since the same old order that Cody represented will later refuse Gatsby at every turn. The passage also supports an issue about the myth of meritocracy, because the schedule's earnest rationality is set against a world where Tom Buchanan's position was never earned at all. The speaker who pairs the schedule with Cody's betrayal has a before-and-after structure built into the extract itself, which makes the ten minutes easier to organize.

Myrtle's apartment party in chapter two survives as the novel's most concentrated staging of exploitation. The gathering in the overcrowded flat, Myrtle's performance of wealth she does not possess, Tom's casual violence when he breaks her nose, and the drunken guests who notice nothing, all of this gives the speaker an extract about the intersection of gender and class. The global issue of violence against women, or of exploitation at the intersection of gender and class, finds a fitting passage here, because the scene shows how Myrtle's vulnerability is produced by both her sex and her poverty at once. A speaker can argue that the passage presents her performance of upper-class manners as both her strategy and her trap, since the very airs that attract Tom are what make her disposable to him. Tom's violence is the scene's center, and a speaker can connect it to the question by noting how the narration moves past it: the party resumes, the guests keep drinking, and the novel's world absorbs the violence as routine. That absorption is the arguable claim, because it turns a single act into evidence of a structure.

George Wilson's garage and the eyes of Eckleburg in chapter eight survive as the novel's account of who pays. Wilson's grief turned into a misdirected moral mission, his fixation on the billboard's eyes as a watching god, and Gatsby's death in the swimming pool while the Buchanans retreat behind their money, give the speaker an extract about moral accountability and its absence. The global issue of justice denied to the powerless, or of scapegoating, fits this passage, because the novel's ending distributes consequences with perfect injustice: the guilty leave town, the innocent die. A speaker can argue that the passage presents a world in which moral language survives only among those it cannot protect, since Wilson is the one character who takes the idea of judgment seriously and it destroys him. The eyes of Eckleburg reward close attention here, because the speaker can trace how the symbol's meaning shifts from commercial indifference to a hollowed-out divinity, and connect that shift to the question by arguing that the novel shows conscience persisting as decoration in a world that has stopped listening to it.

### How Do You Phrase a Global Issue So a Passage Can Serve It?

The global issue is the oral's term, and most issues arrive as topics rather than arguments. A topic cannot be served by a passage; only a claim can. This block shows how to turn a broad topic into a phrased issue narrow enough that the choice of extract matters, with before-and-after phrasings drawn from the Gatsby passages above.

The task asks for a global issue, which in practice means a significant matter that crosses borders, phrased as a statement the extracts can be argued against. Students usually begin with a topic: wealth, power, the environment, gender. A topic is a field, and a field cannot be illuminated because it has no edges; any passage set anywhere in the field fits, which means no passage fits particularly well. The first discipline is to convert the topic into a claim about how the matter works, because the passage will be arguing the how, not merely displaying the what. Wealth becomes the claim that inherited wealth confers the authority to define whose money is legitimate. Power becomes the claim that privilege operates as procedure, the interrogation rather than the fortune. The environment becomes the claim that ecological ruin is the landscape inequality produces rather than an accident of industry. Each of these is arguable, which is the test: an intelligent listener could disagree, and the disagreement is what the extract's details will resolve.

The narrowing ladder is the practical tool. Start with the topic and ask what about it, three times, until the phrasing names a mechanism. Wealth, what about it? Its defense against newcomers. How does that defense work? Through the authority to interrogate and disqualify. The issue is now the claim that old money maintains itself by deciding whose wealth counts, and suddenly the Plaza extract is not merely relevant but exactly right, while the party chapters, which show wealth displayed rather than defended, are visibly worse choices. The ladder matters because it makes selection meaningful: with the broad topic any extract fits, and the speaker's choice proves nothing; with the narrowed claim the extract choice is itself an argument, and the examiner can see the thinking in the selection. Students should write their issue at each rung of the ladder and keep the page, because the discarded broader phrasings are what the teacher's questions will try to drag the oral back toward, and the speaker who knows the ladder can defend the rung.

Before-and-after examples make the ladder concrete. Before: the effects of materialism on relationships. After: the substitution of objects for emotional language in intimate life, so that love can only be proven through display. The before phrasing could be served by any scene containing money and affection; the after phrasing points straight at the shirts, where the substitution is staged in a single image, and away from scenes where money is merely present. Before: prejudice in society. After: the normalization of bigotry as drawing-room conversation among the elite, where the silence of respectable listeners is the mechanism. The before phrasing invites a tour of every unpleasant character; the after phrasing points at the first dinner scene, where Tom's talk passes unchallenged, and the speaker's claim is about the silence rather than the speech. Before: environmental damage. After: the presentation of the poor as becoming the waste the rich produce, so that ecological ruin and dehumanization are a single process. The before phrasing fits any description of a wasteland; the after phrasing demands the ash-grey men specifically, and the extract's boundaries draw themselves around the lines where that becoming is shown.

A further discipline is to check the phrasing against the second work early, because the issue must be servable by two extracts, not one. The narrowed claim that fits Gatsby perfectly may fit nothing else the student has studied, and discovering this after the Gatsby analysis is built wastes the preparation. The test is to write the one-sentence version of the question and ask what kind of passage in the second work could argue it: if the answer comes readily, a scene of interrogation, a dinner where something passes unchallenged, a landscape where the poor dissolve, then the phrasing is workable for the partnership. If the answer requires contortion, the issue is either too narrow, fitted only to Gatsby's particulars, or the second work is the wrong partner. The sweet spot is a claim specific enough to make extract choice matter and general enough to be arguable through two different texts, and finding it is genuinely difficult, which is why issue phrasing deserves its own preparation time rather than being settled in the margin of passage selection.

Two common phrasing failures deserve naming. The first is the moral judgment disguised as an issue: wealth corrupts people, power is abused by the rich. These are verdicts, not claims about mechanisms, and they make the oral preachy rather than analytical, because the extract is then used to condemn rather than to show. The fix is to convert the verdict into a mechanism: not that wealth corrupts, but how the novel presents corruption operating, through immunity, through interrogation, through the substitution of objects for feeling. The second failure is the issue that is really a theme of the novel wearing global clothes: the American Dream, the Jazz Age, the Roaring Twenties. These are period labels, and the task asks for an issue of broader significance. The fix is to ask what the theme is an instance of: the American Dream as an instance of the promises meritocracies make and break, which is arguable across borders and centuries, and which the Dan Cody episode can genuinely illuminate. The phrasing is where the oral's intellectual level is set, and the time spent there pays for itself in every later decision, because a well-phrased issue selects its own extracts, writes its own claims, and answers its own questions.

### How Does a Favorite Scene Become an Oral Extract?

A scene is what you remember; an extract is what you argue. Converting the first into the second means finding the forty lines where the global issue is most concentrated, marking where the passage begins and ends, and knowing why every line inside the boundary belongs. The work is surgical, and it follows a repeatable discipline.

The first move is to stop thinking in chapters and start thinking in lines. A student who has chosen the Plaza Hotel confrontation as the scene is really choosing several pages of material, and several pages are too many for the oral's economy. The extract needs to be roughly forty lines, which means the speaker must find the forty lines where the global issue burns hottest and cut everything else, however beloved. For the Plaza scene with the question of inherited privilege, the hottest lines are the interrogation itself: Tom pressing Gatsby about his past, the drugstore revelation, Daisy's panic, and Tom's dismissal. The arrival at the hotel and the departure afterward are scene-setting the speaker can summarize in one sentence of framing. The discipline is to ask of every candidate line whether it carries the issue or merely carries the story, and to cut the lines that only carry the story, because in the ten minutes every line must justify its place by serving the claim.

The second move is to defend the boundaries aloud. An extract begins where the issue enters the passage and ends where the passage stops feeding the issue, and the speaker should be able to say why the first line is the first and the last line is the last. This matters because the examiner's questions often probe the edges: why start here rather than a page earlier, what is lost by ending where you ended. A speaker who chose the reunion extract for the question of materialism distorting relationships might begin with Gatsby's arrival in the rain and end with Daisy's tears over the shirts, and the defense is ready: the rain establishes the emotional stakes, and the shirts are the moment desire becomes object, so everything between them is the argument's evidence and everything outside them is context the speaker can supply in framing. Boundaries that cannot be defended reveal a passage chosen by feeling rather than by testing, and the questions will find that weakness.

The third move is to read the extract with the question as the only lens. This is the reading students resist, because it feels reductive to read a rich passage as if it were about one thing. But the oral is reductive by design: it asks what the passage shows about the global issue, not what the passage shows in general. The student takes the forty lines and marks every detail that touches the issue, and separately marks every detail that does not, and then decides what the non-touching details are doing. Some of them are atmosphere that the speaker can acknowledge in a sentence. Some of them are distractions that the speaker should not mention at all. The valley of ashes extract for the environmental issue, for example, contains the detail of the trains stopping mysteriously, which serves the argument by suggesting a whole economy paused over the waste it created, and it contains small particulars of the roadside that do not, which the speaker can pass over without apology. The point of the marking is not to flatten the passage but to build the argument's skeleton, so that the oral has a spine of issue-bearing details with the rest of the passage as flesh the speaker need not dissect.

The fourth move is to write the extract's claim in one sentence before any rehearsal begins. The claim is the sentence the whole oral will prove, and it must name both the passage and the question in a single arguable statement. For the shirts scene it might be that Fitzgerald stages the reunion as a transaction in which love can only be expressed through displayed wealth, so that Daisy's tears mark the moment the dream discovers its own materiality. For the Plaza scene it might be that the confrontation shows inherited privilege winning not through superior wealth but through the authority to define whose wealth is legitimate. The test of the claim is whether an intelligent listener could disagree with it; if no one could disagree, the claim is a summary wearing an argument's clothes and the extract needs rethinking. The claim also tests the extract's fitness: if the sentence cannot be written without strain, if it keeps sliding into generalities or needing qualifications that the passage does not supply, the passage is probably floating and a different selection would serve better. Writing the claim early saves the student from discovering this in the exam room.

The fifth move is to check the extract against the second work before finalizing. The oral requires two works, and the Gatsby extract must leave room for the comparison rather than consuming the whole argument. A speaker who has built a magnificent forty-line analysis of the final pages for the nostalgia issue should ask whether the second work can add anything or whether Gatsby has already said everything. The best extracts are the ones that open a question the second work can answer differently, because the oral's energy comes from the conversation between the works. The Wilson extract for the justice issue, for example, pairs naturally with a second work that shows accountability functioning, or failing differently, which gives the speaker something to do in the comparative minutes beyond restating Gatsby. An extract that closes the question leaves the speaker with nothing to compare, and the oral sags in its second half no matter how strong the first half was. Selection is therefore a double test: the passage must serve the argument, and it must serve the partnership.

### What Does Illumination Look Like at the Sentence Level?

Two orals can use the same passage and earn different marks, because the passage is only half the work. Illumination happens when every quotation is tied to the global issue through an explicit claim; floating happens when the analysis describes the passage and the question waits beside it. The difference is visible sentence by sentence, and it can be taught.

Consider the shirts scene in chapter five, with the global issue of materialism distorting intimate relationships. A floating version sounds like this: Gatsby shows Daisy his house and then his clothes, heaping shirt after shirt in a tumble of color, and Daisy puts her head into them and weeps. The scene is famous for its excess, and it shows how much Gatsby has achieved since he was a poor officer. This version is accurate, the details are right, and the question is nowhere in it. The speaker has described the passage competently and left the global issue standing outside the description, waiting to be mentioned. A listener who missed the first minute would not know what issue this oral was about, because nothing in these sentences needs the issue to make sense. That is the signature of floating: the analysis would be identical if the global issue were different, which means the analysis is not about the question at all.

Now the illuminating version of the same passage: Gatsby does not tell Daisy he loves her; he shows her the shirts, because in the world the novel builds, love has no language except possession. Each shirt heaped on the table is an argument that his feeling is real because his wealth is real, and Daisy's tears are the moment the dream touches reality and finds it is made of fabric. The scene's pathos is that neither of them notices the substitution: he mistakes display for devotion, and she weeps at the display as if it were the devotion. Here every sentence serves the argument. The claim that love has no language except possession is an arguable statement about materialism distorting relationships, the shirts are the evidence for it, and the commentary explains how the evidence proves the claim. A listener who missed the first minute would still hear the question in these sentences, because the analysis cannot be separated from it. That inseparability is the test the speaker should apply to every paragraph: if the global issue were changed, would these sentences still work? If yes, they are floating.

The same contrast can be shown with the Plaza Hotel scene and the question of inherited privilege. The floating version runs: Tom confronts Gatsby about his past, reveals the bootlegging and the drugstores, and Daisy chooses Tom. Tom's victory shows that old money defeats new money in the novel. This is plot summary with a thesis-shaped ending, and the question of privilege is named only in the word old money. Nothing is said about how privilege works, which is what the issue asks for. The illuminating version runs: Tom wins the confrontation without being the better man, and that is the scene's point about privilege. His weapon is not wealth, since Gatsby has wealth, but the authority to interrogate: he asks the questions, demands the answers, and decides which answers count. The scene stages privilege as a procedure, a cross-examination in which the old order holds the gavel. Daisy's choice is not between two men but between the man who can be questioned and the man who does the questioning. Here the speaker has found the mechanism, the procedure of interrogation, and connected it to the question through the claim that privilege is the authority to define legitimacy. The difference between the versions is not knowledge; both speakers know the scene. The difference is that one speaker analyzed what happened and the other analyzed how it works, and the question lives in the how.

A third demonstration, shorter, uses the valley of ashes and the environmental issue. Floating: the valley of ashes is a wasteland between West Egg and New York, full of dust and ash-grey men, watched by the billboard eyes of Doctor Eckleburg. It symbolizes the moral decay of the Jazz Age. The last sentence is the giveaway: moral decay of the Jazz Age is a period label, not the global issue, and the speaker has swapped the assigned issue for a familiar theme. Illuminating: the ashes are the residue of the economy that throws Gatsby's parties, which means the novel presents environmental ruin as the landscape of inequality rather than as an accident. The ash-grey men are described as already half dissolved into their surroundings, so the passage argues that the poor do not merely live near the waste; they become it. The billboard's enormous spectacles watch without seeing, which is the passage's image of a society that has built a god out of advertising and then stopped expecting it to intervene. Each sentence here attaches a detail to the question through a verb of argument: presents, argues, is. The floating version's verbs were is and symbolizes, the verbs of labeling. The speaker who wants to check a paragraph can look at its verbs: labeling verbs float, arguing verbs illuminate.

The mechanism underneath these examples is the claim-detail-commentary unit, adapted for speech. The claim states what the detail shows about the question. The detail is the concrete particular from the extract, paraphrased or quoted briefly. The commentary explains the connection, the because that turns observation into argument. Floating orals are usually missing the third element: they have claims and details but the commentary is a restatement of the detail rather than an explanation of its relation to the question. The discipline that fixes this is to end every analytical sentence with a silent because, and to check that the sentence can actually supply one. Gatsby heaps the shirts because in his world love must be proven through objects. Tom interrogates Gatsby because privilege in the novel is the authority to demand accounts. The ash-grey men dissolve into the dust because the passage presents the poor as becoming the waste the rich produce. When the because cannot be supplied, the sentence is decoration, and the speaker should cut it or rebuild it before the oral, not during.

One more distinction matters, because students confuse eloquence with illumination. A beautifully phrased description of the green light can still float if its beauty serves admiration rather than argument. The oral does not reward the speaker's love of the language; it rewards the speaker's use of the language to make the issue visible. The test is always the same: does this sentence make the global issue clearer than it was before the sentence was spoken? If the sentence only makes the passage clearer, it is doing half the work. The students who master this distinction stop performing their reading and start arguing from it, and the examiner hears the difference within the first two minutes, because an illuminating oral has a forward motion that a floating one lacks: each sentence builds on the last toward the claim, while the floating oral circles its passage admiringly and never quite arrives.

### How Do the Two Extracts Converse in a Strong Oral?

The oral asks for two works, and students often prepare them as separate speeches joined by a transition sentence. A stronger method treats the extracts as witnesses answering the same question differently. The Gatsby passage below sets up that comparison, so the second work extends, complicates, or reframes what Gatsby shows rather than merely following it.

The weakest version of the two-work structure is the parallel summary: five minutes on Gatsby, a sentence like a similar pattern appears in the second work, and five minutes on the second work. The listener hears two orals, not one, and the global issue is the only thing they share. The examiner's questions will expose this immediately, because any question that asks how the works differ will find the speaker unprepared. The speaker prepared two analyses and no relationship between them, and the relationship is where the oral's comparative thinking is supposed to live. The fix is to plan the relationship before planning either analysis: decide what the Gatsby extract establishes and what the second extract does with that establishment.

Three relationships cover most strong orals. The first is extension, where the second work shows the same issue operating in a different context, and the comparison argues that the issue is genuinely global rather than local to one text. A Gatsby extract on materialism distorting relationships, the shirts scene, might be followed by a second work's scene of love expressed through a different kind of transaction, and the speaker argues that the mechanism is the same even though the objects differ. The second relationship is complication, where the second work troubles the claim the Gatsby extract seemed to prove. The Plaza scene argues that inherited privilege always wins, but a second work might show privilege failing or being refused, and the speaker argues that the issue is more contingent than Gatsby suggests. The third relationship is reframing, where the second work approaches the issue from the other side: the Wilson extract shows justice denied to the powerless, and a second work might show the powerful experiencing a different kind of moral emptiness, so that the speaker's final claim is larger than either extract alone. The speaker should name the relationship in the oral itself, in a sentence the listener can hold onto: where Gatsby shows the mechanism, the second work shows its limit, or a sentence with that shape.

The practical consequence is that the Gatsby extract should be chosen with the partnership in mind, not merely for its own strength. A passage that proves everything leaves the second work with nothing to do, and a passage that proves one sharp thing leaves the second work a clear job. The valley of ashes extract arguing that environmental ruin is the landscape of inequality pairs well with a second work that shows the same landscape from the perspective of those who profit from it, because the two perspectives complete each other. The reunion extract arguing that love speaks only through objects pairs well with a second work in which love resists that language, because the contrast sharpens both. Students often choose their two extracts independently and then discover in rehearsal that the second has nothing to add; the discipline is to choose the second extract for the job it will do in the argument, which means the job must be defined before the choosing.

Time allocation follows from the relationship. The oral's ten minutes are not two equal halves by law; they are whatever division serves the argument, and the speaker should decide the division in advance rather than letting it happen. A common working shape gives Gatsby roughly four minutes, the second work roughly three, and the explicit comparison roughly two, with the remaining minute for the opening frame and the closing consolidation. But the numbers matter less than the principle: the comparison is not the leftover time but a planned segment with its own claim. Students who run out of time usually spent too long establishing the Gatsby passage because they loved it, and the comparison got compressed into a final sentence. The rehearsal discipline is to time the segments separately, so the speaker knows which part is stealing from the others, and to cut from the passage analysis rather than from the comparison, because the comparison is where the oral proves it is about the question rather than about the books.

The transition between the works deserves a sentence of its own in the notes, written out in full rather than improvised. Improvised transitions drift toward summary: now I will talk about the second work. A written transition states the relationship: Gatsby shows privilege as the authority to interrogate, and the second work tests whether that authority holds when the interrogated refuses to answer, or whatever the actual relationship is. The sentence does argumentative work by telling the listener what to listen for, and it prevents the second half from feeling like a new beginning. For students who want to continue this line of study, [the comparative-essays companion](/2030/07/15/gatsby-comparative-essays/) is the linked next stop.

### What Work Do Quotations Do in the Ten Minutes?

In a written essay the quotation sits on the page for the reader to reread; in the oral it is heard once and must land immediately. That changes what a quotation is for. It is no longer decoration or proof by itself. It is the moment the speaker points to language and shows how that language carries the global issue.

The first consequence is that quotations in the oral must be short. A forty-word quotation read aloud is a paragraph the listener must hold in memory while the speaker analyzes it, and most listeners cannot. The effective oral quotation is a phrase, sometimes a single image: the tumble of color in the shirts, the enormous yellow spectacles, the boats beating on. The speaker introduces it, speaks it clearly, and then spends the analytical time on what the phrase does. Students who try to reproduce long passages from memory usually stumble, and the stumble costs more than the quotation was worth, because the listener stops hearing the argument and starts hearing the performance anxiety. The discipline is to choose three or four short, anchorable phrases from the extract during preparation, the ones that carry the most analytical weight per syllable, and to paraphrase everything else with confidence. A paraphrase the speaker controls beats a quotation the speaker fumbles, and the examiner is listening for analysis, not recitation.

The second consequence is that every quotation needs a frame before it and a claim after it. The frame tells the listener where in the extract the language occurs and what to listen for: when Nick describes the men of the valley as dimly moving through the dust, the verb choice matters. Then the quotation or paraphrase. Then the claim: the passage presents the workers as already half dissolved into the waste they tend, which makes the environmental issue a matter of bodies, not just landscape. Without the frame, the quotation arrives unannounced and the listener does not know which words matter. Without the claim, the quotation sits on the table like an exhibit nobody explains, and the speaker has floated: the language was presented, the issue was not illuminated. The frame-claim sandwich is the smallest complete unit of oral analysis, and a speaker who builds every quotation into that unit will never float for long, because the structure forces the connection.

The third consequence concerns the speaker's handling of figurative language, which is where Gatsby's extracts are richest and where speakers most often admire instead of argue. The green light, the eyes of Eckleburg, the ash-grey imagery, the shirts, each of these invites appreciation, and appreciation is the enemy of the ten minutes. The method is to translate every image into a claim about the question before speaking it aloud. The green light is not beautiful; it is the passage's way of giving longing an object, which lets the speaker argue that nostalgia in the novel is always directed at something receding. The eyes of Eckleburg are not striking; they are the passage's way of presenting moral witness as a commercial artifact, which lets the speaker argue that the novel shows conscience persisting as decoration. The translation must happen in preparation, not in performance, because under the pressure of speaking the speaker will default to admiration. Writing the translation next to each planned quotation in the notes, in plain argumentative prose, is the discipline that keeps the images working for the question.

A related discipline is the handling of narrative voice, because the Gatsby extract is always mediated by Nick, and the oral can use that mediation as evidence. Nick's judgments, his metaphors, his selective attention are all formal choices the speaker can connect to the question. When Nick calls the Buchanans careless people who smash things up, the speaker arguing about privilege can point out that the judgment comes from a participant, not an outsider, which makes the indictment more damaging: even the novel's insider cannot excuse what he has seen. When Nick describes Gatsby's parties with a catalogue of excess, the speaker arguing about materialism can note that the narration itself performs the accumulation it describes, piling detail on detail the way the host piles pleasures on guests. The oral rewards this kind of formal attention because the task asks about content and form together, and students who discuss only what happens in the extract are leaving half the task untouched. For a fuller method of breaking down what a quotation is doing, [the guide to analyzing Gatsby quotations](/2029/07/16/how-to-analyze-gatsby-quote/) works through the same close-reading moves in the written-essay context, and the moves transfer directly to speech.

The final consequence is about number: an extract can support only as many quotations as the speaker can connect, and the number is smaller than students think. Three well-framed, well-claimed phrases will carry an oral; eight rushed ones will sink it, because each unconnected quotation is a small float, a moment where the passage was displayed and the question was not served. The preparation test is to list the planned quotations and write the claim each one supports; any quotation whose claim duplicates another's should be cut, and any quotation without a claim should never have been listed. The oral is not a tour of the extract's best lines. It is an argument that uses the extract's language as evidence, and evidence is selected, not collected.

### What Should the Listener Hear in the Five-Minute Questions?

The oral continues after the ten minutes, when the teacher asks questions that probe the argument's weak points. Students who dread this phase usually prepared a speech, not a position. Preparation means anticipating the challenges your own claims invite and rehearsing answers that return to the extracts rather than drifting into generalities.

The questions will cluster around three targets. The first is the connection: the teacher will ask how a particular detail in the extract relates to the global issue, choosing the detail the speaker glossed over or the claim that sounded asserted rather than argued. A speaker who claimed the shirts show love expressed through objects may be asked why Daisy's tears matter to that claim rather than merely decorating it. The prepared answer returns to the extract: the tears are the moment the dream touches reality, which proves the substitution the speaker claimed, because she weeps at the objects as if they were the devotion. The unprepared answer drifts into generalities about materialism, and the drift reveals that the connection was never built at the level of the detail. The discipline is to rehearse the oral's three most assertive claims and, for each, to prepare the detail-level answer the question will demand.

The second target is the boundary: why this extract and not the lines around it, why this issue and not a neighboring one. These questions test selection, and they are the ones students fear most because they expose choices made by feeling. A speaker who used the Plaza scene for inherited privilege may be asked why the confrontation rather than the earlier party chapters, where class is also on display. The prepared answer defends the selection on the issue's terms: the parties show wealth displayed, but the confrontation shows privilege operating, the interrogation procedure by which the old order defends itself, and the question is about the operation of power rather than its display. The answer works because the speaker knows what the extract was chosen to prove and can name what neighboring passages would not prove. Students should rehearse this defense for their own extract before the oral, because the question is predictable and the answer is simply the selection rationale spoken aloud.

The third target is the comparison: how the two works differ in their treatment of the question, or what the second work adds that Gatsby does not. These questions test whether the oral was genuinely comparative or merely sequential, and they punish the parallel-summary structure described earlier. A speaker who argued extension, that both works show materialism distorting relationships through different objects, may be asked what is lost by treating the two contexts as the same. The prepared answer names the difference the speaker already built into the relationship: Gatsby's objects are aspirational, bought to win love, while the second work's objects are whatever its context supplies, and the comparison argues that the mechanism travels even though the goods do not. The key is that the answer deepens the comparative claim rather than retreating to separate summaries. Students should prepare one sentence that states the relationship between the works as an argument, because that sentence is the answer to every comparison question in compressed form.

A practical method for all three targets is the question bank. After the oral's structure is drafted, the student writes ten questions a skeptical teacher might ask, two on connection, two on selection, two on comparison, two on the global issue's scope, and two on details the speaker is least sure about. Then the student answers each aloud, in no more than a minute, returning to the extracts every time. The answers are not memorized; the moves are rehearsed, so that on the day the speaker has already practiced the shape of a good answer: acknowledge the challenge, return to the detail, restate the claim the detail supports. The bank also reveals the oral's real weak points, because the questions the student cannot answer are the paragraphs that need rebuilding before the exam, not during it.

One more preparation matters, and it is psychological. The questions are not an attack on the oral; they are the task's second half, the part where the speaker demonstrates that the argument survives pressure. Students who treat the five minutes as an interrogation tense up and defend everything, including claims they should qualify. The stronger posture is that of a speaker with a position: concede the small point when the teacher is right, and the concession strengthens the main claim by showing it does not depend on the detail. If the teacher notes that the valley of ashes also contains the roadside details that do not serve the environmental issue, the speaker can agree and redirect: those details are atmosphere, and the argument rests on the ash-grey men and the watching billboard, which is why the extract's boundaries were drawn where they were. The concession displays control, and control is what the questions are really testing: does the speaker own the argument, or was the argument only ever a script?

A fourth target deserves its own preparation, because it is the question students handle worst: the scope challenge, which asks whether the issue is genuinely global or merely local to the texts. The teacher might ask what the shirts scene has to do with anyone outside the novel's world, or whether the Plaza interrogation tells us anything about privilege beyond one fictional summer. The prepared answer does not inflate the passage's importance; it specifies the mechanism and argues its portability. The shirts scene shows love expressed through objects, and the mechanism, the substitution of display for devotion under the pressure of wealth, is recognizable wherever courtship and consumption meet. The Plaza scene shows privilege as the authority to interrogate, and that procedure is recognizable in any setting where one party sets the terms of legitimacy. The speaker should prepare one sentence per extract that states the mechanism in portable terms, because the scope question is really asking whether the analysis found something or merely described somewhere, and the portable mechanism is the proof that it found something.

### Which Selection Traps Sink Otherwise Strong Orals?

Strong students fail this task in predictable ways, and the failures cluster around selection. The passage is too famous to argue, too long to control, or too loosely tied to the question to illuminate it. Naming these traps in advance matters, because each one feels like good preparation right up until the oral begins.

The first trap is the famous passage, and the final pages of chapter nine are its purest form. Every examiner has heard the green light meditated upon many times, which means the speaker starts with a disadvantage: the listener's mind is already full of other people's readings. The trap is not that the passage is bad; the earlier section showed it can serve the nostalgia issue well. The trap is that the speaker, knowing the passage is admired, substitutes admiration for analysis and delivers the reading everyone expects. The defense against the trap is to find something in the passage the expected reading does not use: the tense shift in the closing meditation, the sudden inclusion of the reader, the specific mechanics of how the image widens from one man's hope to a collective condition. If the speaker cannot find such a detail, the honest move is to choose a less famous passage, because an original analysis of the Dan Cody episode will always outscore a competent repetition of what the examiner has heard about the ending. Fame is not a disqualification, but it is a tax, and the speaker must pay it in fresh observation.

The second trap is the montage extract, the forty lines that are really four scenes stitched together. A student who loves the parties, the reunion, and the Plaza confrontation may try to build an extract that touches all three, arguing that together they show the issue across the novel. The result is not an extract but a highlights reel, and the oral becomes a tour rather than an argument. The task asks for extracts, bounded passages, because boundedness is what makes close analysis possible: the speaker can attend to language, to sequence, to the way one sentence sets up the next. The montage abandons all of that for coverage, and coverage is the written essay's temptation, not the oral's strength. The discipline is one passage, one place in the novel, with the boundaries defended as described earlier. If the issue seems to need three scenes, the issue is probably too broad, or the speaker has not yet found the forty lines where it concentrates.

The third trap is the plot-turn extract, the passage chosen because something important happens in it rather than because something arguable is shown by it. Myrtle's death is the clearest example: it is the novel's most shocking event, and students choose the passage for its drama. But the death scene as written is brief and external, Nick hears about it more than he sees it, and there is little language in it to analyze. The speaker ends up narrating the event and its consequences, which is summary, not analysis, and the global issue gets attached to the event's importance rather than to any detail's meaning. The test that exposes this trap is the quotation test from the earlier section: list the phrases the extract offers for close analysis, and if the list is thin, the passage is a plot turn, not an oral extract. The stronger choice is usually the scene before or after the event, where the novel does its thinking: the apartment party before Myrtle's death, Wilson's garage after it.

The fourth trap is the issue-first selection, the mirror image of the passage-first floating described at the start. Here the student chooses the global issue with care, perhaps inequality of opportunity, and then hunts the novel for any passage that mentions class, settling on the first dinner scene because Tom is rich and Nick is not. The passage mentions the issue's vocabulary without embodying its mechanism, and the oral becomes a demonstration that the words appear in the text rather than an argument about what the text shows. The defense is the illumination test: can the speaker write the one-sentence claim naming what the passage argues about the question, and can an intelligent listener disagree? If the claim keeps coming out as a paraphrase, the passage is a keyword match, not a fit. The honest move is to keep hunting, because the novel is dense enough to reward the search: the Dan Cody episode serves the same issue far better than the dinner scene, since it shows the mechanism of mobility and its blocking rather than merely displaying wealth.

The fifth trap is the overloaded issue, where the global issue is phrased so broadly that any passage fits and none illuminates. An issue like the effects of wealth on society could be served by half the novel, which means the extract cannot be wrong and therefore cannot be right either; the selection proves nothing because everything would prove it. The task asks for a significant issue, but significance is not breadth. The discipline is to narrow the issue until the passage choice matters: not wealth's effects but the authority of inherited wealth to define legitimacy, not materialism but the substitution of objects for emotional language. The narrowed issue is what makes the Plaza extract better than the party extract for the privilege argument, and the narrowing is itself part of the preparation the oral rewards. Students should test their issue phrasing by asking whether a different passage would serve it equally well; if yes, the issue needs another round of narrowing before the extract is finalized.

### How Does Class Analysis Strengthen an Oral Extract?

Many global issues route through class, and Gatsby is among the most class-saturated texts a student can bring to the oral. The novel maps wealth onto geography, speech, and moral immunity with unusual precision. A student who can name those mechanisms turns a familiar extract into a sharp instrument for any issue touching inequality.

The geographical mechanism is the most visible and the easiest to use aloud. The novel's map is a class diagram: East Egg for inherited wealth, West Egg for new wealth, the valley of ashes for the poor, Manhattan for the place where the classes mix under controlled conditions. A speaker using the valley of ashes extract does not need to argue this from scratch; the passage supplies it, and the speaker's work is to connect the geography to the question explicitly. The ash-grey men live where the rich drive through on their way to pleasure, which means the passage presents poverty not as a separate world but as the corridor the wealthy must pass through and have trained themselves not to see. That trained not-seeing is the arguable claim, and it turns a description of landscape into an argument about how inequality reproduces itself through inattention. The speaker who names the mechanism, geography as class made visible, has a sentence that works for any extract set in any of the novel's places: the Plaza suite as neutral ground where old money stages its authority, the Buchanans' house as a fortress of inherited ease, Gatsby's mansion as a stage set built to impress across the water.

The speech mechanism is subtler and rewards the speaker who attends to how characters talk. Tom's speech is assertion: he declares, interrogates, and pronounces, and the novel gives him the language of someone accustomed to being believed. Gatsby's speech is performance: the repeated old sport, the carefully casual phrases, the sentences that try a little too hard, all mark a man speaking a class dialect he learned as an adult. Myrtle's speech at the apartment party shifts between the vernacular of the garage and the borrowed airs of the flat, and the strain of the shift is the passage's class argument in miniature. A speaker can use these differences to make an issue concrete: for the question of inequality of opportunity, the claim is that the novel presents class as audible, something carried in every sentence, so that mobility requires not just money but a new voice. The oral format favors this mechanism because the speaker can demonstrate it: a brief paraphrase of Tom's interrogative rhythm against Gatsby's performed ease lets the listener hear the class difference rather than merely being told about it.

The moral immunity mechanism is the novel's darkest class argument and the one most directly useful for issues about justice and power. The Buchanans retreat behind their money at the end, Nick says, into a vast carelessness, and the novel's plot confirms it: Tom's actions contribute to two deaths and he faces nothing, while Wilson and Gatsby, the poor man and the new-money man, die. A speaker using the chapter eight extract can argue that the passage presents accountability as a class privilege, something the wealthy can purchase exemption from, and that Wilson's misdirected vengeance is the only justice the novel's world permits the powerless. The mechanism also sharpens the Plaza extract: Tom's victory there is not just personal but structural, the old order's immunity operating in advance, before any crime has been committed. The speaker who can name moral immunity as the mechanism has found the thread that connects the novel's class analysis to global issues about corruption, impunity, and the unequal distribution of consequences, which are among the most productive issue families for this text.

These mechanisms matter for selection because they tell the speaker what to look for in a candidate passage. The test is not whether the passage contains rich people and poor people; most of the novel does. The test is whether the passage shows a mechanism operating: geography doing class work, speech betraying origins, immunity shielding the guilty. The valley of ashes passage shows all three at once, which is why it is such a strong extract. The dinner scene shows speech and immunity in compact form. The Dan Cody episode shows mobility attempted and blocked, the mechanism of the glass ceiling rendered as narrative. A student who has internalized the three mechanisms can walk through any forty lines of the novel and mark where the class work is happening, which turns passage selection from a hunt for famous scenes into a scan for mechanisms. For a deeper account of how wealth and class operate across the whole novel, [the analysis of wealth and class in Gatsby](/2024/05/13/wealth-class-great-gatsby/) traces these patterns chapter by chapter, and the oral speaker can treat it as background reading for whichever extract the mechanisms recommend.

The speaker's notes should carry the mechanisms in shorthand, because they are what the examiner listens for when the analysis moves from description to argument. A margin note that reads geography as class beside the valley extract, or interrogation as procedure beside the Plaza lines, is the difference between a speaker who notices details and a speaker who knows what the details are doing. The mechanisms also protect against the most common question-bank failure, the teacher asking what a detail has to do with the question: the answer is always the mechanism, stated plainly, with the detail as its instance. Prepared this way, the class analysis is not background knowledge but the oral's connective tissue, present in every paragraph without ever becoming a lecture.

### How Does a Week of Drills Build Selection and Connection?

The skills this oral rewards are trainable, but only by drills that isolate them. Reading the novel again is not a drill. What follows is a seven-day routine in which each session practices one move: choosing a passage, testing it against an issue, or speaking it aloud under the clock.

The first day is the passage hunt. The student takes the global issue as phrased and reads through the novel's key chapters with a single question in mind: where does this issue concentrate? The product of the day is not one extract but three candidates, each marked with its line boundaries and a one-sentence claim about what it argues. The discipline is to write the claim for each candidate before any affection for the passage is allowed to form, because the claim is the test and the passage must pass it. A student working on the privilege issue might emerge with the Plaza interrogation, the dinner scene's unchallenged bigotry, and the closing retreat behind money, each with its sentence. The day's work is done when three claims exist on paper, and the student should resist the urge to start rehearsing any of them; rehearsal before selection is how the floating oral gets built.

The second day is the illumination test. For each of the three candidates, the student writes the because sentences: three claims about the question, each supported by a detail from the extract and a because that connects them. The candidate whose because sentences come easily is the one where the passage genuinely serves the argument; the candidate whose sentences strain, reaching for generalities or repeating the detail in different words, is floating and should be cut. By the end of the day one extract remains, and the student also has the beginnings of the oral's analytical core, since the surviving because sentences are the paragraphs in embryo. The day's discipline is honesty: the point is to kill two candidates, not to rescue them, and the student who cannot kill a beloved passage on day two will be defending a floating oral on exam day.

The third day is the boundary defense. The student takes the surviving extract and writes the answers to the two boundary questions: why does the passage begin where it begins, and why does it end where it ends? The answers must be in the issue's terms, not the story's: the extract begins with the rain because the rain establishes the emotional stakes the materialism argument needs, not because that is where the scene starts. The student also trims the extract to its final length, cutting any line that does not serve the claim, and checks that the remaining lines can be covered in the time available. The day ends with a fixed extract, fixed boundaries, and a fixed claim, written on a single page that will become the oral's foundation.

The fourth day is the quotation drill. The student lists the three or four phrases from the extract that will be quoted or closely paraphrased, and for each writes the frame and the claim it will carry in performance. Then the student speaks each frame-phrase-claim unit aloud, timing it, until the unit lands cleanly without the notes. The discipline is to speak, not to write further: the oral is a spoken task, and units that read well but tangle the tongue need simpler phrasing. The student should also practice the paraphrases for the details that will not be quoted, because confident paraphrase is what keeps the oral moving between its anchor phrases.

The fifth day is the structure rehearsal. The student speaks the full ten minutes aloud, twice, with a timer, following the planned division: the opening frame, the Gatsby analysis, the second work, the explicit comparison, the closing consolidation. The first run will be too long; every first run is. The student cuts from the passage analysis, never from the comparison, and runs it again. The discipline is to keep the notes minimal during these runs, because the oral is spoken from understanding, not read from a script, and the student needs to discover which transitions require written help and which flow from the argument's logic. By the end of the day the student has a ten-minute oral that fits the clock and a clear sense of where the weak transitions are.

The sixth day is the question bank. The student writes the ten skeptical questions described earlier and answers each aloud in under a minute, returning to the extracts every time. The questions the student cannot answer become the evening's repair work: a paragraph rebuilt, a boundary redefended, a comparative sentence sharpened. The day's product is not just prepared answers but a tested argument, one that has already survived the challenges the teacher is most likely to raise. The student should also rehearse the concession move, agreeing with a small correction and redirecting to the main claim, because the questions reward ownership more than defensiveness.

The seventh day is the dress rehearsal and the rest. The student delivers the full oral once, with the five minutes of self-questioning after, exactly as the exam will run, and then stops. No more drilling, no more tweaking. The discipline of the final day is trust: the selection was tested on day two, the connection was built on days three through five, and the argument survived day six. Further work now would only introduce the kind of last-minute changes that unravel under pressure. The student reviews the single foundation page, the extract boundaries, the claim, and the comparative sentence, and then leaves the material alone. The routine's logic is cumulative: each day's product feeds the next, and the week converts the two abstract skills, selection and connection, into a concrete extract, a concrete claim, and a concrete performance that the examiner can actually hear.

The morning of the oral has its own small discipline, and it is mostly about what not to do. Do not rewrite the claim, do not swap a quotation, do not rephrase the issue at the last minute, because changes made under adrenaline have not passed the illumination test and will float. Read the foundation page once, slowly, and then speak the comparative sentence aloud one time, since it is the sentence most likely to tangle under pressure and the one the oral's second half depends on. Arrive with the notes skeletal but legible, the extract boundaries marked, and the anchor quotations verified against the text one final time. The preparation is over; the performance is the method, performed once more, and the student who trusts the week's work will hear the argument carrying itself within the first two minutes, the way a well-built structure carries weight without visible effort.

## The Verdict: Serve the Issue or Waste the Passage

The verdict is that the passage has no value in this oral except as an instrument of the global issue. A beloved scene that does not illuminate the claim is decoration, and decoration earns nothing. The students who accept this choose the extract for what it proves, not for how it feels, and their orals argue rather than display.

This position runs against the most natural instinct in literary study, which is to begin from love of the text. The instinct is not wrong about literature in general; it is wrong about this task in particular. The individual oral is not an occasion to show what Gatsby meant to the reader. It is an occasion to show what a passage of Gatsby can prove about a global issue, and the difference between those two occasions is the difference between the floating oral and the illuminating one. The method laid out above, the passage hunt, the illumination test, the boundary defense, the quotation drill, the question bank, is all machinery for enforcing that difference, and its severity is deliberate. Every shortcut the method forbids, choosing by affection, analyzing by admiration, comparing by sequence, is a shortcut that feels like preparation and functions as evasion.

The practical upshot is a rule the student can carry into the exam room on a single line: if a sentence does not serve the global issue, cut it. That rule governs selection, since the extract is chosen for what it proves; it governs analysis, since every quotation gets its frame and its claim; it governs the comparison, since the second work is chosen for the job it does in the argument; and it governs the questions, since every answer returns to the extracts. An oral built under that rule will not be the most wide-ranging, the most eloquent, or the most comprehensive. It will be the most argued, and the task rewards the argued oral because the task is itself an argument: the individual oral explores a global issue through extracts, and the Gatsby passage that serves that exploration clearly is the one that earns its ten minutes.

The verdict also settles the question students ask most anxiously, which is whether this method kills their enjoyment of the novel. It does not, but it does reorder it. The love of the text becomes the fuel rather than the steering: the student who loves the shirts scene will work harder on its because sentences, will defend its boundaries more fiercely, will find the fresher observation in it, precisely because the affection supplies the energy and the method supplies the direction. What the method kills is only the undisciplined love, the kind that wants the oral to be a tribute. A tribute is a fine thing to write in a journal and a poor thing to deliver in an examination, because the examination is not asking what the novel meant to the reader. The students who understand this stop feeling guilty about cutting their favorite lines from the extract, since the cut is not a rejection of the passage but a recognition of what the task is.

The verdict applies with equal force to the second work, and students should hear that clearly. The temptation is to apply the method rigorously to Gatsby and then choose the second extract casually, as if the comparison were the oral's decorative wing. It is not. The second extract must pass the same illumination test, defend the same boundaries, and carry the same kind of arguable claim, because the examiner's questions will probe it with the same skepticism. An oral with a superb Gatsby extract and a floating second extract is half an oral, and the comparison segment cannot rescue a partnership in which only one partner argues. The rule covers every sentence of the ten minutes without exception: if it does not serve the global issue, cut it, whichever work it came from.

## Studying the Individual Oral with the Companion Tools

Method improves through rehearsal, and rehearsal needs the right instruments. Gathering oral-ready passages demands the novel's text with annotations for checking details, and practicing the ten-minute structure demands a place to rehearse with feedback. Two companion tools divide the work exactly along those lines.

The first instrument is for gathering the extracts. The passage hunt, the boundary defense, and the quotation drill all depend on the student working with the novel's actual language rather than with remembered scenes, because the illumination test fails when the details are vague. The annotated text at [VaultBook's Great Gatsby study page](https://vaultbook.net/tools/great-gatsby-annotated-text.html) is where to gather oral-ready passages and evidence, since an annotated text lets the student verify the exact lines a candidate extract will use and check the details the because sentences depend on. The discipline is to do the selection work with the text open: mark the candidate boundaries in the forty-line range, confirm the phrases that will become the anchor quotations, and test each candidate's claim against the wording on the page rather than against memory. A student who selects from memory selects scenes; a student who selects from the text selects extracts, and the oral rewards the second.

The second instrument is for practicing the performance. The structure rehearsal, the question bank, and the dress rehearsal need a setting where the student can run the ten minutes, time the segments, and get feedback on whether the connection is actually audible to a listener. The essay and oral practice at [ReportMedic's Gatsby practice page](https://reportmedic.org/tools/great-gatsby-essay-practice.html) is where to practice the oral structure and get feedback on it, because feedback is what converts a rehearsed run into a lesson. The review should ask the questions the task asks: does every quotation serve the global issue, do the boundaries hold, does the comparison argue a relationship rather than sequence two summaries, and would a listener who missed the opening still hear the question in the analysis? Students should keep their because sentences and their comparative sentence in a notebook, not to memorize them word for word, but because the collection becomes a visible record of the connection muscle strengthening, and the weak sentences in the collection show exactly where the next drill should aim.

A final study discipline ties the two instruments together. After each full rehearsal, the student should do the float check: listen back, or have a partner listen, for any sentence that would still make sense if the global issue were changed, and cut or rebuild every such sentence before the next run. That single habit, repeated across the week's drills, attacks the exact failure the task punishes and builds the exact skill it rewards. The preparation arc is then complete: the annotated text supplies the verified extracts, the drills supply the selection and the connection, the practice with feedback supplies the performance, and the float check supplies the correction. The oral, when it arrives, is simply the method performed once more, ten minutes of argument with five minutes of defense, the passage in service of the question from the first sentence to the last.

The two instruments also serve the boundary defense directly, which is the part of preparation students most often skip. With the annotated text open, the student can count the candidate extract's lines, confirm that the anchor quotations are worded exactly as the novel has them, and check that no line inside the boundary is being misremembered. Misremembered details are a quiet hazard of the oral: the speaker who paraphrases a scene from memory sometimes imports a detail from the film version or from a neighboring chapter, and the examiner, who knows the text, hears the import instantly. Verifying against the text eliminates the hazard. The practice setting then tests whether the defended boundaries survive contact with the clock: a boundary that looked principled on paper sometimes proves too wide when spoken, because the speaker cannot cover every line in the time available, and the feedback from a timed run is what reveals the overreach. Selection verified against the text, performance verified against the clock, and connection verified by the float check: the three verifications together are the whole of preparation, and each has its instrument.

Build the question bank from the task's own logic rather than from guesswork. Every because sentence invites one follow-up: why this issue and not another, why this passage and not the next scene, what the counter-reading would say. Every boundary invites another: what was cut and why. Every comparison invites a third: what the pairing reveals that either text alone could not. Ten such questions, written from the student's own draft, cover most of what the five minutes can ask, and answering them aloud turns the defense from a hazard into a routine.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you use Gatsby in the IB individual oral?

You use it as one of the two works through which you explore a global issue, contributing an extract of roughly forty lines that genuinely illuminates the issue you have chosen. The method is to phrase the global issue as a specific claim, hunt the novel for the passage where that issue concentrates most densely, defend the extract's boundaries, and build every quotation into the argument through a frame and a claim. Preparation means testing candidate passages with because sentences and killing the ones that float. In performance, Gatsby occupies roughly four of the ten minutes, the second work roughly three, and the explicit comparison the rest, with the opening frame and closing consolidation taking the remainder. The novel's density of image and incident makes it a strong choice, but only the extract that serves the issue earns its place.

### Q: What does the individual oral demand?

It demands a ten-minute spoken argument about a global issue, explored through extracts from two studied works, followed by five minutes of questions from the teacher. The global issue must be significant and transnational, phrased as a statement rather than a topic, and the extracts, typically around forty lines each, must be analyzed for both content and form in service of that issue. The task rewards knowledge of the texts, analysis that connects detail to the issue, focus and organization across the ten minutes, and clear spoken language. What it does not reward is summary, admiration, or coverage: the speaker who narrates the plot or tours the novel's highlights is doing unrewarded work. The demand is for a controlled case in which every sentence serves the issue.

### Q: How must the passage illuminate the global issue?

It must do so at the level of the detail, not the level of the label. Illumination means that each quotation or closely paraphrased particular is tied to the issue through an explicit claim, so that a listener who missed the opening would still hear the issue in the analysis. The test is the because sentence: the speaker states what the detail shows about the issue and supplies the because that connects them. A passage that merely mentions the issue's vocabulary, or that gets the issue named in the introduction and the conclusion with description in between, is floating beside the issue rather than illuminating it. The passage must also be arguable, supporting a claim an intelligent listener could dispute, because the oral is an argument and the extract is its evidence.

### Q: How do you structure the oral?

A working structure gives the ten minutes five segments: a one-minute opening frame that states the global issue, introduces both works, and lands the central claim; roughly four minutes on the Gatsby extract, moving through its details in claim-detail-commentary units; roughly three minutes on the second work's extract; roughly two minutes of explicit comparison that argues the relationship between the works, whether extension, complication, or reframing; and a closing consolidation that restates what the two extracts together have shown about the issue. The comparison is a planned segment with its own claim, not leftover time. The five minutes of questions that follow should be prepared for with a question bank, since the teacher will probe the connection, the selection, and the comparison.

### Q: What happens when passage and issue do not connect?

The oral becomes two topics standing side by side: a competent analysis of a Gatsby passage and a global issue mentioned at the margins. The examiner hears description where argument was required, because the analysis would be identical if the issue were different, which means it was never about the issue at all. The questions then expose the gap, since any probe of how a detail serves the issue finds the speaker reaching for generalities. The damage is structural rather than cosmetic: no amount of fluent delivery repairs a disconnection created at the selection stage, because the sentences themselves lack the because that would tie them to the claim. The honest repair is to return to selection, run the illumination test on the candidate passages, and choose the one whose details genuinely argue the issue.

### Q: Why are selection and connection the key skills?

They are the two moves the task explicitly rewards and the two places where orals most often fail. Selection is choosing the extract whose details can carry an arguable claim about the global issue and defending its boundaries; it determines whether the oral has the raw material of illumination or is built on a floating passage. Connection is tying every quotation and detail to the issue through explicit claims; it determines whether the analysis argues or merely describes. Everything else a student might do, knowing the whole novel, speaking eloquently, covering both works at length, is secondary. The ten-minute constraint is what makes these two skills decisive: there is no time to say everything, so the speaker must choose what matters, and no sentence can be wasted, so each must be connected.

### Q: How long should the Gatsby extract be for the individual oral?

Aim for roughly forty lines, which is the length that balances depth against the ten-minute economy. Shorter extracts often lack enough detail to sustain four minutes of analysis, forcing the speaker into repetition or into drifting beyond the boundaries. Longer extracts cannot be covered closely in the time available, so the speaker skims and the analysis thins into summary. The forty-line guide is not a rule to be measured with a ruler but a discipline: the extract should be short enough that every line can be accounted for and long enough that the argument has evidence to work with. When choosing between a tight thirty lines and a sprawling sixty, take the thirty and use the saved time for the comparison, which is where orals most often run short.

### Q: How do you choose between two Gatsby passages that fit the same global issue?

Run the illumination test on both and keep the one whose because sentences come more easily. Take each candidate, write three claims about the issue supported by details from the passage, and compare the strain: the passage where the connections write themselves is the one where the issue genuinely concentrates, while the one that requires reaching is probably a keyword match rather than a fit. A second test is the quotation test: list the phrases each extract offers for close analysis, and prefer the one with more arguable language per line. A third consideration is the partnership: choose the passage that leaves the second work a clear job, extending, complicating, or reframing, rather than the one that says everything alone. The discarded candidate is not wasted; its failure teaches the standard.

### Q: Can you use the novel's final pages as your Gatsby extract?

You can, but the fame of the passage is a tax the speaker must pay in fresh observation. Every examiner has heard the green light meditated upon many times, so the expected reading is already in the listener's mind and the speaker must find something it does not use: the tense shift in the closing meditation, the sudden inclusion of the reader, the mechanics of how the image widens from one man's hope to a collective condition. If the speaker cannot find such a detail, the honest move is to choose a less famous passage, because an original analysis of the Dan Cody episode will outscore a competent repetition of the expected reading. The final pages do serve the nostalgia issue well, and a speaker with a genuinely new observation should use them without apology.

### Q: Should the Gatsby extract come from early or late in the novel?

Either position can work, and the choice should follow the issue rather than a rule. Early extracts, like the Buchanans' dinner or the valley of ashes, require efficient framing, since the speaker must establish the novel's world quickly, but they reward the speaker with passages where the novel's mechanisms are on open display. Late extracts, like the Plaza confrontation or the final pages, benefit from the weight of everything that came before, which the speaker can invoke in a sentence of context, but they risk the fame trap and the temptation to summarize the preceding plot. The test is the same wherever the extract sits: does this passage concentrate the issue, and can its boundaries be defended? Choose the lines that pass the test, not the chapter that feels safest.

### Q: How much of the ten minutes should the Gatsby extract occupy?

Roughly four minutes, with the understanding that the division serves the argument rather than a fixed rule. The common working shape gives the opening frame one minute, Gatsby four, the second work three, the explicit comparison a little under two, and the closing consolidation the remainder. The principle behind the numbers matters more than the numbers: the comparison is a planned segment with its own claim, not leftover time, so it must be protected in advance. Students who run long almost always overspend on the Gatsby analysis because it is the part they love, and the comparison gets compressed into a final sentence. The rehearsal discipline is to time the segments separately and to cut from the passage analysis rather than from the comparison, since the comparison is where the oral proves it is about the issue.

### Q: What should you say in the first minute of the oral?

State the global issue as a claim, name both works and their extracts, and land the central argument the ten minutes will prove. The opening is a contract with the listener: it tells the examiner what issue is being explored, through which passages, and toward what conclusion, so that every subsequent sentence can be heard as evidence for something already announced. What the first minute should not contain is plot summary, biographical background, or generalities about the novel's era; those waste the opening's goodwill and delay the claim on which everything depends. A strong opening might run: the issue is the authority of inherited wealth to define legitimacy; the extracts are the Plaza interrogation in Gatsby and its counterpart in the second work; the argument is that both show privilege operating as procedure rather than as mere possession. Three sentences, and the listener knows exactly what to listen for.

### Q: How do you answer an examiner question you did not anticipate?

Acknowledge the question directly, return to the extract, and build the answer from a detail rather than from generalities. The worst response is the long pause followed by a drift into everything the speaker knows about the topic; the drift signals that the connection was never built at the detail level. A better shape is: that is a fair challenge, and the detail that answers it is this one, followed by the quotation or paraphrase and the claim it supports. If the question genuinely exposes a weak point, concede the small point and redirect: the teacher may be right about that detail, and the main claim rests on these other details, which is why the extract's boundaries were drawn where they were. The concession displays ownership of the argument rather than defensiveness about a script. Students can rehearse this shape with the question bank, but the move itself is simple: detail first, claim second, concession when earned.

### Q: Should you memorize the oral or speak from notes?

Speak from notes, and keep the notes skeletal. A memorized oral sounds recited, and recitation collapses under the first unexpected question, because the speaker memorized sentences rather than owning an argument. The notes should carry the load-bearing elements: the global issue as phrased, the extract's boundaries, the one-sentence claim, the three or four anchor quotations with their frames, the comparative sentence stating the relationship between the works, and the transition sentences between segments. Everything else should come from understanding, which is what the drills build. The examiner is assessing whether the speaker can think with the texts, and thinking is audible: the slight pause before a claim, the return to a detail under questioning. A script hides exactly what the task wants to hear.

### Q: How many quotations can one extract support?

Fewer than students think: three or four well-framed phrases will carry an oral, while eight rushed ones will sink it. Each quotation needs a frame before it and a claim after it, and that unit takes time to deliver well; the arithmetic of ten minutes simply does not allow more. Every additional quotation is also a risk, because each unconnected one is a small float, a moment where the passage was displayed and the issue was not served. The preparation test is to list the planned quotations and write the claim each supports; any quotation whose claim duplicates another's should be cut, and any quotation without a claim should never have been listed. The rest of the extract's language should be handled through confident paraphrase, which keeps the oral moving between its anchor phrases without the stumbles that long memorized quotations invite.

### Q: How do you recover if you lose your place during the oral?

Return to the claim, not to the script. The speaker who loses the thread should pause, restate the extract's one-sentence claim aloud, and then pick up from the next detail that supports it, because the claim is the argument's spine and every paragraph hangs from it. What the speaker must not do is apologize at length, restart from the beginning, or fill the silence with plot summary while searching for the lost sentence; those moves surrender the oral's authority. The notes are the safety net here, which is why they should carry the load-bearing elements in large, legible form: a glance should recover the claim, the current quotation, and the comparative sentence. Rehearsal should include a deliberate disruption drill, where the student stops mid-sentence and practices the recovery, because the recovery is a skill and skills improve with practice.

### Q: How do you rescue a passage that feels disconnected from the issue?

First diagnose whether the problem is the passage or the phrasing of the issue, because the rescue differs. If the because sentences strain, try narrowing the issue before abandoning the passage: the Plaza scene floats beside a broad issue about class but illuminates a narrow one about the authority to define legitimacy. If narrowing does not help, run the quotation test, listing the phrases the extract offers for close analysis; a thin list means the passage is a plot turn or a keyword match, and the honest move is to select again rather than to force the fit. A third option is to shrink the extract: sometimes the disconnection lives in the outer lines, and cutting to the forty lines where the issue concentrates restores the illumination. What does not work is adding more issue-language to the commentary, because decoration cannot substitute for a genuine fit.

### Q: Should both extracts be the same length?

They should be roughly comparable, but exact equality matters less than the argument's needs. The reason for comparability is balance: if the Gatsby extract is sixty lines and the second work's is twenty, the oral will tilt toward Gatsby no matter what the speaker intends, and the comparison will feel like an afterthought. The practical rule is to keep both extracts in the same range, around forty lines each, and to give each enough analytical weight that the listener feels two witnesses rather than a main act and a footnote. Where the works differ in density, a slightly shorter extract from the denser text can balance a longer one from the sparser text, since what matters is the amount of arguable detail, not the line count. Decide the balance in preparation, not in performance.

### Q: How do you introduce the extract's place in the novel?

In one or two sentences of framing that locate the passage and establish only the context the argument needs. The listener requires orientation, not summary: where in the novel the extract falls, who is present, and what situation the lines respond to. For the Plaza extract, the frame might note that it comes at the novel's crisis, with the main figures trapped in a hotel suite on the summer's hottest day, and that the lines to be analyzed are the interrogation itself. The frame should also point forward, telling the listener what to listen for: these are the lines where privilege operates as procedure. Students who spend a minute on plot background are spending unrewarded time and training the listener to expect summary. The discipline is to trust that the argument carries its own context, since each detail arrives inside the claim it serves.

### Q: How do you rehearse the oral when you have no partner?

Record yourself and become your own skeptical listener. The phone's voice recorder is the partner the student lacks: deliver the ten minutes aloud, then listen back specifically for the float check, marking every sentence that would still make sense if the global issue were changed. The recording also exposes pacing problems, mumbled quotations, and transitions that exist in the notes but never made it into speech. For the question phase, write the ten-question bank and answer each aloud while recording, then listen for the drift into generalities that signals an unbuilt connection. A further discipline is the explanation test: deliver the oral's central claim to someone who has not read the novel, a friend or family member, and see whether the argument survives without the book's context. If it does, the connection is real; if it needs the novel to make sense, the analysis was describing rather than arguing.
