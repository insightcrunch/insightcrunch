## Romeo Must Die: The Loosest Romeo and Juliet That Still Counts

Romeo Must Die is the limiting case of Shakespeare adaptation, the film that keeps the least of Romeo and Juliet and still earns the comparison. Released by Warner Bros. in March 2000, directed by Andrzej Bartkowiak in his debut as a director, it stars Jet Li as Han Sing and Aaliyah, in her first film, as Trish O'Day, the son and daughter of two Oakland crime organizations whose rivalry drives the plot. Of the play, the film retains the feuding houses, the attraction across the divide, a pair of dead young men, and a title. It retains no line of the verse, no balcony, no secret marriage, no potion, no tomb, and no double suicide. Its Romeo does not die. Measured as fidelity, the film is a failure so complete that the measurement itself becomes suspect, and that is the film's value to the study of adaptation: it isolates the one element of the play that can survive the removal of everything else, and it proves that the element is the feud. The verdict of this article is that Romeo Must Die is a genuine but minimal adaptation, an analogy rather than a version, and that its failures are as instructive as its single success. The success is structural: two households, a forbidden attachment, a body count among the young. The failures are everything the play uses to make that structure tragic, and they cluster in one place, the romance the film advertises and then declines to stage.

![Jet Li and Aaliyah as the lovers of Romeo Must Die, the Oakland action remix of the play - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-45.webp)

The film's own credits announce the distance. The screenplay is by Eric Bernt and John Jarrell, from a story by Mitchell Kapner, and neither text makes any claim on Shakespeare's language. Producer Joel Silver, who built the film at Silver Pictures, arrived at the play from the opposite direction to a literary adaptation: by his own account he was frustrated by the staleness of American action cinema and turned to Hong Kong for a fresh grammar, casting Jet Li after Li's villainous turn in Lethal Weapon 4 (1998), which Bartkowiak had photographed as cinematographer. Shakespeare supplied the chassis, Hong Kong supplied the engine, and hip hop supplied the sound. A reputable account of the production therefore has to hold three facts together: the play is present as a premise, absent as a text, and marketed as a name. The title does the work that in earlier adaptations was done by quotation. Nothing in the film is called Romeo or Juliet, and the only Shakespearean words in it are the four syllables of its title, two of which are an instruction the film disobeys.

That disobedience is the subject. A series that has measured adaptations by the fraction of the text they keep, from Cukor's reverent two-fifths to Zeffirelli's youth-first half, here meets a film whose fraction is zero, and the fraction stops being the interesting number. What replaces it is a different question, the one the brief for this article poses and the one adaptation theory has argued for half a century: how loose can an adaptation be and still count? Romeo Must Die answers the question empirically. It counts, audiences and critics agreed in calling it a Romeo and Juliet film even while deploring it, and it counts on the strength of one structural rhyme alone. The rhyme is worth naming precisely, because the film's defenders and detractors alike have been content to gesture at looseness without measuring it. Measured element by element, the film keeps the play's outline and reverses nearly every term inside it, and the reversals, not the resemblance, are where its meaning lives.

## What the Film Keeps: Two Houses, One Attraction, No Play

The Chorus of the play compresses the whole design into its first lines: "Two households, both alike in dignity, / In fair Verona, where we lay our scene, / From ancient grudge break to new mutiny" (Prologue, lines 1-3, in Jill Levenson's Oxford edition). Every clause of that sentence has a counterpart in the film, and every counterpart is altered. The two households are the Sing organization, a Chinese triad family headed by Ch'u Sing, and the O'Day organization, an African American crime family headed by Isaak O'Day. Their dignity is the dignity of money rather than rank: both are in the same business, the acquisition of waterfront property in Oakland, and both are courting the same buyer. The ancient grudge, the play's unexplained inheritance of hatred, is replaced by a turf rivalry that the film eventually reveals to have been manufactured by the partners themselves. Verona becomes Oakland, a city the production never properly visited, since the film was shot almost entirely in Vancouver with a handful of establishing shots of the real city. The mutiny, the fresh outbreak of an old war, becomes a sequence of murders staged to look like a war. Nothing else in the Prologue survives. The "pair of star-crossed lovers" who "take their life" (Prologue, line 6) become a pair of investigators who take a case.

The lovers' positions inside the houses are also inverted. In the play, Romeo is the heir who drifts into the enemy's feast and Juliet is the daughter guarded at home. In the film, Han Sing is the elder son who has been absent for years, serving a Hong Kong prison sentence for a crime of his father's, and he arrives in Oakland as an exile returning, closer to a detective than to a bridegroom. Trish O'Day is the daughter who refuses the family business outright, runs a clothing store of her own, and is guarded against her will by her father's security detail. Where Juliet is obedient in public and disobedient in secret, Trish is disobedient in public from her first scene: her rebellion is not a marriage but a refusal of employment. The play's Juliet is destroyed by the conflict between the daughter she performs and the wife she becomes. The film's Trish has no second self to hide, and her story is about competence rather than concealment. She is the partner in the investigation, the better guide to Oakland, and, in the climax, the one who fires the shot that saves Han's life.

What the film keeps, then, is a geometry: two organizations of equal weight, a son and a daughter on opposite sides, an attraction that the geometry forbids, and a rising count of young corpses. The play's own account of why such geometry matters is given by the Friar, who agrees to marry the lovers because "this alliance may so happy prove, / To turn your households' rancour to pure love" (2.3). The film keeps the hope and drops the mechanism. No alliance is sealed, sacramentally or otherwise; Han and Trish end the film with an embrace, walking away from the police lights together. The rancour is not turned to love. It is exposed as a fraud, which is a different ending with a different politics, and the sections that follow take the film's choices one at a time.

### What exactly survives from the play in Romeo Must Die?

Four things survive: the two feuding households, the attraction between a son of one house and a daughter of the other, the deaths of young men from both families, and the expectation, created by the title, that the lovers' story will end in death. Everything else is absent. The film is the play's outline with its contents replaced.

The outline matters because it is the part of the play that the culture actually transmits. The feud between the Capulets and the Montagues is known to millions who could not quote a line of the text, which is why [the feud between the houses, its missing cause and its long escalation](/2022/04/01/capulet-montague-feud-explained/) has become the play's most portable property. Romeo Must Die is best understood as a controlled experiment in that portability: a major studio spent 25 million dollars to test whether the outline alone, dressed in Oakland real estate and Hong Kong choreography, would still register as Romeo and Juliet. The box office answered yes. The film opened at number two in North America behind Erin Brockovich, earned 18 million dollars in its first weekend across 2,641 theatres, and finished with 91 million dollars worldwide against its 25 million dollar budget. The experiment succeeded commercially. Whether it succeeded as adaptation is the harder question, and it cannot be answered until the mapping is set out in full.

## Silver's Wager: How the Film Was Built

The film exists because a producer changed his mind about where American action cinema should shop for its grammar. Joel Silver, whose Silver Pictures had defined the Hollywood action film of the preceding decade, had grown frustrated with the staleness of the domestic product and turned to Hong Kong, where directors and choreographers were staging fights with a speed, clarity and invention that American stunt work no longer attempted. The instrument of the conversion was Jet Li. Li had spent the 1990s as one of Hong Kong's defining stars, a wushu champion turned actor whose screen persona combined lethal speed with a boyish restraint, and his American introduction had come in Lethal Weapon 4 (1998), where he played the villain with such economy that the film's publicity leaned on him as heavily as on its established leads. Andrzej Bartkowiak, the cinematographer who photographed that film, saw the quality at close range, and when Silver built a vehicle around Li, Bartkowiak made the move from camera to director's chair. The chain of the production is therefore a chain of witnesses: Hong Kong convinced Silver, Li convinced Bartkowiak, and Bartkowiak's camera convinced the studio that a first-time director could stage what the film existed to sell.

The casting of Trish followed the inverse logic: the production needed a performer who could hold the screen opposite a star of few words without becoming either a hostage or a decoration. Bartkowiak recalled, in the retrospective account published in Vulture in 2021, that Warner Bros. had eyed Janet Jackson for the role before Aaliyah emerged; Aaliyah was the only actress invited to screen-test, and the test settled the question. Eric Bernt, who wrote the screenplay with John Jarrell from Mitchell Kapner's story, described the quality that won the part as an innocence combined with street, a badass who could also be vulnerable. The description matters because it is a casting note for Juliet rewritten for a different century: the play's heroine must likewise read as sheltered and formidable in the same breath, a child whose three days of decisions outrun every adult in her city. The film found its Juliet by the play's own criterion and then declined to give her the play's plot, which is the production's recurring pattern in miniature: the instincts are Shakespearean, the follow-through is not.

The economics were modest and the discipline visible. The film was made for a reported 25 million dollars, a mid-range budget even for its year, and photographed in Vancouver between May and July 1999, with Oakland present in establishing shots only. The restraint bought one extravagance: the X-ray device, tested in a single fight sequence and expanded when preview audiences responded, an unusual case of a signature effect being focus-grouped into existence before release. The marketing leaned on the title and on the soundtrack in equal measure, and the opening vindicated both: the film entered the North American chart at number two, behind Erin Brockovich, with an opening weekend of some 18 million dollars, and went on to earn roughly 91 million worldwide, a return of more than three and a half times its production cost. The wager, as a wager, succeeded completely. Silver had bet that a Hong Kong star, a debuting singer and a Shakespeare title with no Shakespeare in it could open an American action film, and the bet paid. What the receipts cannot settle is the question the title poses, and the rest of this article is the attempt to settle it from the film itself rather than from its campaign.

## The InsightCrunch Remix Note: Mapping the Play Onto the Film

The InsightCrunch remix note sets the play's elements beside the film's substitutes and grades each one, because loose adaptation invites vague talk and this film deserves exact accounting. Presented as a chart, the note is also the kind of mapping that transfers well to study materials and slide decks; teachers assembling a lesson on adaptation have long used [the ReportMedic PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) to walk a class through such a comparison, row by row, and the rows repay the walk, because the pattern of the grades is the argument. Of the sixteen elements tabulated, the film keeps four with their function intact, transforms seven beyond recognition, and drops five entirely. The kept elements are all structural. The dropped elements are all verbal or sacramental. The transformed elements are where the film's own interests, property, choreography and the policing of its romance, declare themselves.

Read as a whole, the note shows a film that understood the play's skeleton better than its reputation suggests. The substitutions are not careless. Whoever shaped the story knew that the feud needs two dead young men to become serious, one from each house, so Po Sing and Colin O'Day die as symmetrically as Mercutio and Tybalt do, and knew that the fathers must be forced into a reckoning, which the film arranges at gunpoint rather than over a tomb. What the filmmakers did not want was the play's engine of tragedy, the machinery of secrecy, haste and failed messages that turns a feud into a catastrophe for the innocent. The film's machinery is the opposite: investigation, disclosure and the patient following of a paper trail of deeds. Verona's tragedy happens because nobody learns the truth in time. The Oakland plot resolves because two people learn the truth exactly in time. The remix is therefore not the play with pieces missing. It is the play's outline run through a different genre's physics, in which information saves instead of arriving too late.

## The Feud Reversed: A Real Grudge Becomes a Manufactured War

The deepest divergence is the feud itself. In the play, the hatred is genuine, inherited and unexplained; the Prologue calls it an "ancient grudge" and no character in five acts supplies its origin. Its causelessness is its horror, since a hatred without a reason cannot be argued out of existence, and the Prince's threat hangs over both houses equally: "Your lives shall pay the forfeit of the peace" (1.1). In the film, the war between the Sings and the O'Days is a performance. Mac, Isaak O'Day's chief lieutenant, confesses in the climax that the gang war was a ruse concocted by himself and Ch'u Sing to cover the murder and extortion of waterfront business owners who refused to sign away their properties. The hatred that seems to structure Oakland is a stage set built by two men, one inside each house, to frighten small owners into selling. Where the play's feud is a truth nobody can remember starting, the film's feud is a lie everybody is paid to believe.

The buyer behind the lie is Vincent Roth, played by Edoardo Ballerini, a developer assembling deeds along the Oakland waterfront in order to sell the package to investors and build a stadium for a new football franchise. Roth is the film's most original figure, because he has no counterpart anywhere in the play: a third party who profits from the feud itself, neither Capulet nor Montague, a man for whom the ancient grudge is a business instrument. Verona has no Roth. Verona's feud consumes the city's wealth in corpses; Oakland's feud produces wealth, in discounted property, for whoever can keep it simmering. The film is often described as a crime thriller that borrowed the play's frame, but the description understates the coherence of the change. Once the feud becomes a property instrument, every other change follows: the fathers become businessmen, the war becomes public relations, the young men's deaths become liquidation costs, and the lovers become the two people positioned to audit the books. The remix is systematic, and its system is economic.

The fathers divide along the fault line the system creates. Isaak O'Day, played by Delroy Lindo with the film's most Shakespearean piece of casting, is a gang leader trying to convert himself into a legitimate developer: at the closing deal he refuses Roth's cash payment and demands instead an ownership share in the franchise, a father's attempt to buy his children a clean inheritance. Ch'u Sing, played by Henry O, takes the money. The play's fathers are mirrors of each other in rage and grief; the film's fathers are mirrors in ambition that crack in opposite directions, one toward legitimacy, one toward liquidity. It is Ch'u, the father who took the cash, who is revealed as the author of his own son's death, since Kai admits to Han that he killed Po personally because Po interfered with Ch'u's dealings. Isaak loses a son to his partner's treachery and his own lieutenant's ambition. Ch'u loses both sons by his own order and his own hand, one to Kai's violence and one, himself at the last, to his own. The play distributes its guilt across a whole city, and the Prince can close it with a general sentence: "All are punished" (5.3). The film concentrates its guilt in the fathers and the lieutenants, and lets the city off entirely.

## The Fathers' Bargain: Collusion Where Verona Had Enmity

The play's fathers are enemies who have forgotten why. The film's fathers are partners who remember exactly why, and the substitution reorganizes the moral weight of the story. Capulet and Montague appear in the play's first scene as old men calling for swords their servants are already swinging, and the comedy of their entrance, two patriarchs in nightgowns demanding weapons, is the play's verdict on the feud's management: the hatred is an inheritance administered by men too old to fight it themselves. Ch'u Sing and Isaak O'Day administer something else. They are co-investors in the waterfront acquisition, bound by a deal each understands precisely, and their organizations' war is waged beneath their partnership like a fire kept burning in a building both men own. The enmity the play treats as a mystery, "from ancient grudge", the film treats as a line item, and the fathers' scenes together are negotiations, not confrontations. When the two households finally stand in one room, in the closing movement, it is to settle accounts: Isaak at the deal table refusing Roth's cash and demanding an ownership share in the stadium franchise, converting a lifetime of crime into a legitimate asset his children can inherit; Ch'u, at his own compound, facing the son he sent to prison and the lieutenant who killed the son he kept.

Delroy Lindo's Isaak is the film's strongest claim to the play's territory. Lindo plays the patriarch as a man performing legitimacy before he possesses it, courteous, deliberate, a father who keeps his daughter at arm's length from the business and his ambitions for the family fixed on the clean side of the ledger. The performance invites the comparison the film never makes aloud: Isaak is the Capulet who has read the ending. Capulet's tragedy is that he tries to secure Juliet's future with the instruments of the feud world, a marriage arranged as strategy, and discovers the cost too late. Isaak tries to secure his children's future by exiting the feud world altogether, buying out of crime into ownership, and discovers that the exit is mined: his partner is a traitor, his lieutenant is a murderer, and his son is dead before the conversion completes. Henry O's Ch'u is the darker mirror, a patriarch whose courtesy conceals the colder arithmetic. Where Isaak's every scene reaches toward the future, Ch'u's reach backward, toward control: the son sacrificed to prison, the son managed into silence, the lieutenant empowered to kill. The film's last judgement on the fathers is exact. Isaak, wounded in the rescue of his own kidnapped person by his daughter and Han, is carried to an ambulance with his daughter beside him, alive inside a family that has lost a son and kept its soul. Ch'u dies by his own hand in an emptying compound, abandoned by the surviving son in the film's plainest sentence of judgement: Han tells him that he will not kill him, and leaves him to the police and the triads, a father sentenced to the public exposure his whole career was arranged to avoid.

The funeral confrontation between Han and Ch'u, placed early, is where the film states its generational thesis most clearly. Han blames his father for Po's death, not for ordering it, which he does not yet know, but for failing to protect him, and the charge lands because the audience has already seen the economy that produced it: a family in which the elder son's freedom was spent to buy the father's escape and the younger son's safety was spent to buy the father's deal. The play distributes this spending invisibly; nobody in Verona itemizes what the feud costs the young, until the Prince does it over the bodies. The film itemizes from the start, in contracts, deeds and sentences served, and its fathers are therefore guiltier than Verona's, not less. Montague and Capulet can plead the grudge. Ch'u and Isaak built theirs, priced it, and sold shares in it, and the film's sympathy follows the accounting: the father who tried to buy his way out is forgiven by the plot, and the father who took the cash is executed by it.

## Han Sing Is Romeo With the Poetry Replaced by Combat

Han Sing enters the film as Romeo never could: backwards, out of a prison, into a story that has already begun killing people. The play's Romeo is first seen sighing over Rosaline, a young man whose suffering is entirely rhetorical. Han is first seen escaping. He had been a Hong Kong police officer; he took the blame for his father's crime so that Ch'u and Po could flee to America, and he served the sentence in their place. When word of Po's murder reaches him, he breaks out and crosses the Pacific to investigate. The substitution is total. Where Romeo's defining activity is speech, sonnets, laments, vows, Han's is movement: he runs, climbs, fights and escapes, and his eloquence is choreographic. The film gives him almost no extended dialogue, and Jet Li's performance is built from watchfulness and sudden speed. The soliloquy has been replaced by the set piece, and everything the play lets its hero say about himself, the film makes its hero do in front of witnesses.

The prison also replaces Mantua. Romeo is banished to Mantua for a killing the audience watched and half forgives; Han is imprisoned before the film begins for a crime he did not commit, and his exile is the father's debt rather than the law's sentence. The difference matters to the film's moral bookkeeping. Romeo's exile is a consequence, the machinery of the feud closing on him. Han's imprisonment is a sacrifice, the family business consuming its most dutiful son in advance. When Han confronts Ch'u at Po's funeral and blames him for failing to protect the brother Han went to prison to save, the film states its version of the play's generational charge: the fathers spend the sons. Capulet spends Juliet by promising her to Paris; Montague spends Romeo by absence; Ch'u spends Han in prison and Po in Oakland, and the spending is itemized, contractual, a matter of deals rather than temper. Han's tragedy, so far as the film permits him one, is that his loyalty was the family's most exploited asset.

### Why does Han Sing begin the film in a prison?

Han accepted the blame for a crime of his father's, so that Ch'u Sing and the younger son Po could escape Chinese authority and settle in America, and he served the sentence in Hong Kong. News of Po's murder reaches him there; his escape and journey to Oakland open the plot.

As a fighter, Han is placed by the choreography in a lineage the play would recognize: he is the reluctant man of violence, as Romeo is. Romeo kills Tybalt in a spasm of grief and Paris almost apologetically; Han fights defensively, escapes from custody twice rather than killing his captors, and in the film's most discussed set piece refuses to strike a woman opponent at all, fighting the motorcycle fighter Meriana by guiding Trish's arms and legs like a puppeteer while Trish protests. The scene is comic, and it is also the film's clearest piece of characterization through action: Han's courtesy is a physical fact about him, demonstrated under pressure. The play's Romeo is courteous in language and catastrophic in action. The film's Han is silent in language and courteous in action, an exact inversion, and the inversion is the remake's most intelligent single idea. It collapses, however, at the climax, where Han kills Kai in a fist fight and the film requires the audience to accept the killing as justice. Verona's Romeo dies for his killings. Oakland's Han is rewarded for his, which is the difference between a tragedy and a thriller stated in one corpse.

## Trish O'Day Is Juliet Without a Balcony, a Paris or a Potion

Trish O'Day is the film's largest single departure from its source, and its most honourable one. Juliet is thirteen, betrothed by her father to a man she has not chosen, and possessed of exactly one space of her own, a bedroom with a window. Trish is an adult businesswoman. She runs a clothing store bought with her own refusal of the family trade, keeps her own apartment, and treats her father's security detail as an imposition to be shaken off, which is how she meets Han, in a chance street encounter while escaping her bodyguard Maurice. The balcony scene's whole apparatus, height, secrecy, the risk of discovery, the father's house as a fortress of sleepers, is replaced by a meet-cute engineered by her own disobedience in broad daylight. Nothing in her story is hidden. Her father does not plan her marriage; no Paris waits with a wedding date; no Nurse carries her messages; no potion will be required, because nobody intends to dispose of her. The film removes every instrument of Juliet's oppression and then discovers, as the play's adapters rarely admit, how much of Juliet's story those instruments were.

What remains of Juliet in Trish is position and function. She is the child of one house who gives the child of the other house access to the truth: Po had called her store the day before he died, and the list of threatened businesses that drives the investigation surfaces through her. In the play, Juliet is the plot's secret, the marriage nobody knows. In the film, Trish is the plot's witness, the person who knows where the evidence is. Aaliyah plays her with a self-possession that the reviews of the time singled out for praise even when the film around her was faulted, and the performance matters to the mapping because it refuses the two available clichés of the gangster's daughter, the moll and the victim. Trish is neither. She is the only principal in either family who earns money honestly, and the film quietly makes her store the moral centre of Oakland: the one business on the waterfront that is not for sale.

Her agency peaks where Juliet's peaks, in a death she causes. In the climax, Mac is about to shoot Han when Trish kills him first. The moment is the film's one true equivalent of the tomb scene's terrible arithmetic: a young woman of one house kills a man of her own house to save a man of the other. Juliet's violence is turned inward, against herself, because the play leaves her no outward target that could help. Trish's violence is turned outward and succeeds, because the thriller leaves her a target whose death solves the plot. The difference is the whole difference between the genres. It also exposes what the film has quietly rearranged: the Juliet figure performs the climactic rescue, the Romeo figure is rescued, and the gender of the saviour, unremarked by the film itself, is the remix's most modern feature. Aaliyah was twenty-one during production, a recording star with no prior acting credit, and the film builds Trish out of the qualities her screen test reportedly showed: Eric Bernt, the co-screenwriter, described an innocence combined with street, a performer who could be tough and vulnerable in the same register. The Juliet of the play is defined by the speed of her fall into love. The Trish of the film is defined by the steadiness of her aim, in commerce, in investigation and finally with a gun.

## The Missing Middle: Mercutio, Tybalt, the Nurse, the Friar and the Prince

The remix note's dropped column deserves its own anatomy, because the film does not merely omit the play's middle characters; it omits the functions that make the play a tragedy, and the omissions are connected. Mercutio is the largest absence. In the play he is the feud's first aristocratic casualty and its sharpest analyst, the man whose dying curse, "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1), names the structure that kills him. His death is the hinge of the tragedy: before it, the play is a comedy with deaths threatened; after it, a tragedy with laughter remembered. Romeo Must Die has no Mercutio. Its comic figure, Maurice, played by Anthony Anderson as the bodyguard Trish keeps escaping, supplies the Mercutio slot's screen time and none of its function. Maurice banters, blusters and survives; he leads Mac's henchmen in the late abduction and is defeated without consequence. The film thereby loses the play's turning mechanism. Nothing in Oakland corresponds to the moment where the story's wit dies cursing both houses and the hero's grief makes him a killer. Han's grief for Po exists from the first act and changes nothing in him; he begins as an avenger and ends as one, and the thriller's flatness of arc, often charged against the film, is precisely the absence of Mercutio's hinge.

Tybalt, by contrast, is not dropped but divided. The play's Tybalt is a single figure of proud, formal violence, the man who kills Mercutio and is killed by Romeo. The film splits his functions between two lieutenants. Kai, played by Russell Wong, is Ch'u's chief lieutenant and the feud's visible enforcer, the man who admonishes Po in the opening nightclub scene and who finally admits to having killed him. Mac, played by Isaiah Washington, is Isaak's chief lieutenant and the feud's invisible manager, the man who murdered Colin O'Day, his own employer's son, and who stage-managed the war with Ch'u. Tybalt's pride becomes Kai's professionalism; Tybalt's treachery to the peace becomes Mac's treason to both houses. The division is tidy, and it carries a cost the film never counts: the play's catastrophe requires the killer and the killed to be kin by marriage at the moment of the duel, so that Romeo's sword destroys his own new family. Han kills Kai as a stranger executing a sentence. No bond is broken, because the film has built no bonds to break, and the climax registers as procedure rather than horror.

The Nurse, the Friar and the Prince fall together, and their joint absence is the film's confession about its own plot. The Nurse exists because Juliet's love must be carried, hidden and nursed inside a hostile house. The Friar exists because the marriage must be performed by an authority willing to conspire against both houses for the sake of peace. The Prince exists because Verona has a public power that prices the feud in advance and collects payment at the end: "See what a scourge is laid upon your hate" (5.3). None of the three has work to do in Oakland. Han and Trish hide nothing, so no Nurse is needed to carry messages. They marry nobody, so no Friar is needed to conspire. And the film's Oakland has no Prince: its police are a sound effect, arriving with the ambulances after the private war has settled itself, and no figure in the film holds Escalus's office of public judgement. The result is a feud with no society around it, only two firms and a developer, and a story that can therefore end in a private settlement. Verona's ending is civic: the city gathers in the tomb and the fathers shake hands over the bodies while the Prince apportions blame. Oakland's ending is domestic: a daughter waits with her wounded father for an ambulance, and a son walks out of his father's compound into the street. The public dimension, the dimension in which the play's tragedy becomes a city's education, is the dimension the remix cannot use, because the thriller genre has no citizenry, only bystanders.

What the film supplies in place of the middle is the symmetry of the sons. Po Sing and Colin O'Day are the remix's true inheritance from the play, and they are more exact than the lovers are. Each is the younger, softer son of a crime patriarch; each is involved, before the film begins, in an attempt to cross the divide, since Colin was to meet Po on the night of Po's murder to discuss information that could end the war; each is murdered by his own side's machinery, Po by Kai on Ch'u's account, Colin by Mac within his father's own organization. The play's pattern, in which the feud kills the young of both houses impartially, survives in Oakland with its impartiality perfected into treachery: both boys are killed not by the enemy but by the feud's managers, who need the war to continue. In Verona the houses kill each other's children by hatred. In Oakland they kill their own children by policy. The film never states the parallel, but the parallel is the strongest evidence that its makers had absorbed the play's design rather than merely its title.

## The Citizenry Between the Houses: Silk's Club, Maurice and the Shopkeepers

Verona is crowded. Between the two households the play stations a whole society, servants who open the action with a brawl and a dirty joke, a watch that arrests nobody in time, citizens who cry out against the brawls in the street, a band of musicians hired for a wedding who find themselves playing at a funeral. The crowd is not decoration: the feud is a public nuisance in the play, priced by the Prince in public, and the lovers' privacy is precious because the city is so thoroughly shared. Oakland, as the film builds it, has the beginnings of the same crowd and then declines to use it. The opening sequence is the film's feast scene in negative. Po Sing, drunk and careless in a nightclub owned by Silk, played by DMX with the ease of a man playing a version of his own public persona, is admonished by Kai and removed; the club is the one room in the film where both worlds mix, music, money and menace in a single space, as Capulet's hall mixes masked Montagues into the Capulet feast. The play's feast produces the love plot. The film's club produces the corpse that produces the plot, and the difference is the genres again: in Verona the party is where the feud is suspended, in Oakland it is where the feud collects.

Silk himself is the film's citizen of stature, the one figure between the houses with an independent base: a club, a clientele, a neutrality purchased by usefulness to both sides. He corresponds to nobody in the play, though he occupies the social space of the play's Apothecary and its musicians at once, the tradesmen who serve all Verona and profit quietly from its arrangements. His fate is the film's measure of the war's escalation: when the manufactured conflict turns real, the neutral ground is attacked and its owner killed in the crossfire of the climax, the neutral consumed by the fiction he helped furnish a room for. Maurice, Trish's bodyguard, played by Anthony Anderson, supplies the retainer class its single developed face. A bodyguard who cannot keep his charge in sight, a kidnapper later pressed into Mac's service and beaten in the football game and the fights alike, Maurice carries the servant comedy of the play's first scene at feature length: Sampson and Gregory's swagger, incompetence and survival, without their one moment of consequence. He is beaten, humiliated and alive at the end, which is the film's consistent mercy toward its clowns, and the mercy, like the missing Mercutio, flattens the stakes: Verona's servants open a play in which servants' masters die, while Oakland's clown is guaranteed the immunity of comic relief.

The shopkeepers are the crowd's third element and the film's most pointed invention. The play's citizens appear in a single cry, "Down with the Capulets! Down with the Montagues!" (1.1), a populace that hates the feud and cannot end it. The film's small owners, the laundries and stores of the waterfront strip, are the feud's raw material: their signatures are the asset being assembled, their refusals are answered with arson and murder, and the paper trail of their dispossession is the evidence that finally dissolves the war. Verona's citizens can only shout. Oakland's can sign or refuse, and their refusals, recorded in deeds and threats, become the archive that convicts the fathers. The film never gathers them into a scene of judgement, as the play gathers Verona in the tomb, and the omission is structural rather than careless: the thriller has no civic stage, so the citizens' victory is administrative, a file of evidence rather than a public reckoning. What the film gains is a modern truth about how such wars actually end, in paperwork and prosecution. What it loses is the play's ending emotion, a whole city instructed by two bodies, and the loss is another entry in the remix note's ledger, on the side of everything public, spoken and shared.

## Why Romeo Does Not Die: The Ending the Title Promised and Withheld

The title is a spoiler for a film that does not exist. Romeo must die: the play's audience knows the ending from the Prologue, and the film's audience, promised the same ending by the poster, receives its opposite. Han survives. Trish survives. The closing movement belongs to the fathers and the lieutenants, and the death toll among principals runs Kai, Mac, Silk and, at the last, Ch'u Sing, who shoots himself in his compound as Han walks away, declaring that he will leave his father to the punishment of the police or the other triad families. The one suicide in the film is therefore assigned with a precision that cannot be accidental to the only character who ordered a child's death: the patriarch absorbs the ending that the title assigned to the son. Where the play kills the lovers and spares the fathers to grieve, the film kills the guilty father and spares the lovers to embrace. The title's grammar survives with its terms reassigned, Romeo's death transferred upward a generation.

### Who dies at the end of Romeo Must Die?

The lovers do not die: Han and Trish survive and leave together. The closing deaths belong to the feud's managers: Kai, killed by Han in the final fight; Mac, shot by Trish; and Ch'u Sing, who shoots himself as Han abandons him. The title's sentence falls on the guilty father, not the son.

The substitution transforms the story's moral physics. In the play, the deaths of the innocent purchase the peace: the fathers reconcile because the price of the feud has been presented to them in the form of their children's bodies, and the reconciliation is genuine because the price was real. In the film, the peace is purchased by information. Han and Trish expose the scam, Mac's confession is heard, Roth escapes by helicopter but loses the deeds, which scatter from his shot briefcase into the wind, the single most eloquent image in the film: the paper Verona, the accumulated instrument of the feud, dispersed as litter. Nobody reconciles. Isaak is carried to an ambulance; Ch'u is dead; the feud ends not because its parties repent but because its business model is destroyed. The film is a thriller, and a thriller's ending restores order by removing the disorderly. The play is a tragedy, and its ending restores order by indicting the orderly. Both endings can be described as the feud's conclusion, and the distance between them is the distance the word adaptation has to stretch to cover this film, which is why the film remains the best available test of how far the word stretches.

## Choreography as Verse: Corey Yuen, the X-Ray Shots and the Football Game

If the film has a text, it is the fights. The fight choreography is by Corey Yuen, one of the senior action directors of Hong Kong cinema, and the production's whole reason for existing was Joel Silver's conviction that Hong Kong's action grammar could refresh a stale Hollywood genre. The fights are therefore the place where the film's claims to artistry must be judged, as the verse is the place where the play's are judged, and the analogy is not decorative. The play's lovers are known by their language: they complete each other's sonnet at first meeting, and the audience measures their love by the quality of their improvisation. The film's hero is known by his movement, and the audience measures him the same way. A sonnet exchanged becomes a fight exchanged, call and response in kicks rather than lines, and the film's best sequences have the formal pleasure of the play's best exchanges: a challenge issued, topped, and topped again, with the last word going to the quicker wit of the body.

Two sequences define the system. The football game, in which Han takes on Maurice and his friends at their own sport and beats half a dozen men while scoring a touchdown, is the film's orchard scene in negative: instead of a lover scaling walls in whispers, a stranger walks into the enemy camp's playground in daylight and wins its ritual contest, courting the house by defeating its retainers at the house game. And the X-ray shots, the production's signature effect, interrupt two fights to show, in a flash of skeleton, a bone breaking or a spine buckling under a blow. The effect was tested in a single fight first, approved by preview audiences, and then used more widely. Its function is commentary: like a stage direction made visible, it tells the audience what a blow means inside the body, information the Hong Kong style usually leaves to sound and reaction. The X-ray is the film's footnote apparatus, its one gesture toward the explanatory density that the play achieves with soliloquy.

### What is the X-ray effect in the fight scenes?

The X-ray effect is a cutaway inside a fight that renders the fighters as skeletons at the moment of impact, so the audience sees a bone break or a joint fail. Tested in one sequence and extended after audiences approved it, the device works as commentary: the action film's equivalent of a soliloquy, stating what a blow costs.

The effect also drew the sharpest critical fire the film received, and the fire is instructive because it repeats, in action-cinema terms, the oldest quarrel in Shakespeare adaptation: whether technique serves the matter or replaces it. Roger Ebert, who gave the film one and a half stars in the Chicago Sun-Times, wrote that Li's scenes "are so clearly computer-aided that his moves are about as impressive as Bugs Bunny doing the same", and concluded that the effects "miss the point" of a martial arts film, whose pleasure depends on believing the body did the thing. Rene Rodriguez, in the Chicago Tribune, called the film "needlessly convoluted" and made the same objection from the other side: effects layered over Li's stunts make them less impressive, not more, because the artificiality insults the real skill on display. The objection has force precisely because Li's skill was real and documented across a Hong Kong career; the wire assistance and digital sweetening were a Hollywood insurance policy laid over a performer who did not need it. Yet the objection also proves the film's premise. A star whose body is his text has been treated exactly as the play's text is treated by a reverent adapter: preserved, framed, and quietly distrusted. Cukor's MGM surrounded the verse with professors; Silver's production surrounded Li with computers. The parallel is the strongest available answer to Ebert, though it does not overturn his verdict on the effect itself, which dates the film more than any other element in it.

The set pieces repay individual reading, because each one restates a scene of the play in the grammar of combat. The prison escape that opens Han's story is the balcony scene inverted by altitude and intention: Romeo climbs toward a window to declare himself; Han descends from confinement to conceal himself, and the athleticism that the play sublimates into verse, the lover light as a feather on a wall, the film literalizes as the body's first credential. The football game, which Ebert described with amused precision as the fight in which Li "defeats half a dozen men and scores a touchdown all at the same time", is the feud's ritual combat restaged as sport, the street brawl of the play's first scene with its casualties removed and its function exposed: young men of two camps demonstrating, before witnesses, which camp's champion is quicker, while the elders' war waits on the result. The fight against Meriana, the motorcycle fighter played by Françoise Yip, is the film's most inventive sequence and its most revealing. Han, bound by his own code against striking a woman, fights her by proxy, moving Trish's arms and legs through blocks and counters while Trish protests and then delights in the borrowed skill. The scene is a duet in the strict sense, two bodies producing one performance, and it is the closest the film comes to the play's central formal event, the shared sonnet at the Capulet feast, in which two voices produce one poem. What the lovers of the play do with fourteen lines, Han and Trish do with a choreographed defence: they improvise together, in public, faster than the threat, and the compatibility the scene demonstrates is the compatibility the film's romance is permitted to have, kinetic rather than erotic, a partnership proven in motion since it is forbidden in speech. The final duel with Kai closes the system. Kai is the one opponent of Han's weight class, the lieutenant who killed Po, and the fight is staged as an execution the audience has been promised since the opening titles: the film's Tybalt, met at last by its Romeo, in a city with no Prince to forbid the meeting and no marriage to make it fratricide.

Measured against Hong Kong's own standards, the choreography was received as diluted: the wire assistance, the digital X-rays and the Hollywood cutting rhythms softened the long-take clarity that had made Li's reputation, and the objection from Ebert and Rodriguez that the effects diminish the performer extends to the editing, which protects the star from the sustained visibility his home industry demanded. Measured against the American action cinema of its year, the fighting was the film's distinction, the element reviewers reached for when the plot failed them, and its grammar entered the mainstream: the pairing of martial movement with hip hop scoring, the set piece as character introduction, the choreography counted as the production's authored text. The play's adapters face the same fork. A production can preserve the verse and lose the audience that no longer hears it, or replace the verse with the era's fluent medium and lose the text; Romeo Must Die chose the second with the era's most fluent body, and its fights are the best evidence for the choice and the best evidence of its cost, sometimes within the same sequence.

## The Kiss That Was Filmed and Removed

Every account of this film eventually arrives at the same absence. Han and Trish investigate together, eat together, fight side by side, save each other's lives and embrace at the end, and they never kiss. The play's lovers share a sonnet and a kiss within minutes of meeting; the film's lovers share a case. The chastity is conspicuous because the film is named for the most famous romance in English, and the record of how it happened is unusually well documented, with two explanations on file that do not agree. The documentary The Slanted Screen (2006), which examines the representation of Asian men in Hollywood, reported that a kissing scene between Han and Trish was shot and removed after it tested badly with audiences, and Jose Antonio Vargas, reporting the documentary's argument in the Washington Post in 2007, quoted its charge that mainstream America was "uncomfortable with seeing an Asian man portrayed in a sexual light". Jet Li, on his own website, gave a different account: both versions of the ending had been filmed, with and without the kiss, and the kiss was dropped because it seemed strange and awkward for Han to kiss anyone in the moment after watching his father kill himself. The two accounts are not strictly incompatible, a test screening can supply the occasion and an actor's tact the rationale, but they point at different causes, one industrial and racial, one dramatic and personal, and the film's afterlife has been shaped by the first.

The scholar Sabrina Q. Yu, whose study Jet Li: Chinese Masculinity and Transnational Film Stardom (Edinburgh University Press, 2012) examines how Li's Hollywood vehicles rebuilt his screen masculinity for a new market, treats this film as a central exhibit in the rebuilding: the Hong Kong star who had played romantic heroes at home was relaunched in America as a fighter first, his desirability acknowledged by the casting and then carefully unshown by the plot. The pattern held beyond this film. Li's subsequent American leads kept the structure of a romance and withheld its consummation, and the withheld kiss in Romeo Must Die became the type case in a wider argument about which men Hollywood permits to be lovers. Beatrice Loayza, reassessing the film in Vulture in 2021, put the adaptation itself in the same frame: the film, she wrote, contains "the blueprint of a Shakespearean text chopped and screwed into a nearly unrecognizable object", and it is "not much of a romance, to be perfectly honest", though Li and Aaliyah remain "coy and cool" and sparks fly anyway. The reassessment is generous and exact at once. The sparks are real, because both performers are charismatic; the romance is not, because the film builds the machinery of attraction and refuses to run it.

Adjudicating between the two explanations matters less than seeing what both agree on: the film chose, at whatever prompting, to make its cross-divide love a partnership rather than a passion. The choice has a cost inside the adaptation's own logic. The play's feud is lethal because the love that crosses it is absolute; Romeo and Juliet's attachment, formed in an evening, is strong enough to die against the whole city. A partnership of investigators, however warm, cannot carry that weight, and the film knows it, which is why it transfers the motive force to a different attachment entirely: Han's loyalty to his dead brother. The engine of Romeo Must Die is not desire but fraternal grief, a brother avenging a brother, and the romance is cargo. Judged as a Romeo and Juliet, the film therefore fails at the centre even while succeeding at the frame, and the failure was a decision, documented in two versions of a single scene. The hug that ends the film, two survivors walking away together past the police cars, is charming and alive, and it belongs to a different story, one in which the lovers were always colleagues and the play was always a pretext. The film's honesty problem is not that it borrowed so little. It is that it borrowed the one thing it declined to use.

## The Soundtrack as the Film's Second Script

The film's verse, if it has any, is on its soundtrack. Romeo Must Die: The Album, released on Blackground and Virgin Records in association with Warner Bros. in March 2000, was executive produced by Aaliyah with Timbaland, Barry Hankerson and Jomo Hankerson, with a score for the film itself credited to Stanley Clarke and Timbaland. Aaliyah sings on four of its tracks, and the album's commercial performance outran the film's reputation in a way that has few parallels in the adaptation record. It debuted at number three on the Billboard 200 with some 203,000 copies sold in its first week, topped the rhythm and blues and hip hop album chart, and was certified platinum, selling a million and a half copies in the United States. Its lead single, Aaliyah's "Try Again", written by Stephen "Static Major" Garrett and Timbaland, reached number one on the Billboard Hot 100 on the strength of radio airplay alone, the first song in the chart's history to do so, and earned Aaliyah a Grammy nomination. The other singles tell the film's story in miniature: "Come Back in One Piece", a duet between Aaliyah and DMX, the film's Silk, and "We At It Again" by Timbaland and Magoo.

The soundtrack matters to the adaptation question because it performs the function the film's script declines to perform. Shakespeare's play is carried by patterned language, refrains, rhymes and songs built into its structure; the balcony scene is a duet, the aubade a debate in verse. Romeo Must Die replaces that verbal music with recorded music, and the replacement is carefully engineered. The film's Oakland is scored by the music its characters would own; Trish's world is rhythm and blues, the clubs are hip hop, and Aaliyah's voice, as actor and singer both, unifies the two halves of the enterprise. "Are You Feelin' Me?", one of her four contributions, was a track recorded for the film's world rather than its plot, and its afterlife as a fan favourite mirrors the film's own: the album remained in circulation and in esteem while the film settled into cult status. Critics who found the film thin as drama rarely found it thin as sound. Elvis Mitchell, in a New York Times review that called the film "dreary", still predicted its success from the combination of martial arts and hip hop, a judgement the chart record vindicated: the dreary film and the hit album are the same object weighed on two scales, and the scales disagree because the film's energies were genuinely divided. Half of Romeo Must Die is a thriller of average competence. The other half, the movement and the music, is a document of two industries, Hong Kong action and Black American recording, meeting at a moment when both were at commercial flood tide, and the second half is the half that history kept.

### Why did the soundtrack outsell the film's reputation?

The album was a primary work rather than a souvenir: executive produced by its female lead, built around a single that made chart history, and stocked with the era's defining producers and voices. The film carried divided reviews; the album carried none, and it could be taken home. Audiences kept the songs and let the plot go.

## How Loose Can an Adaptation Be? Analogy, Borrowing and the One-Premise Film

The brief for this article names the complication directly: how loose an adaptation can be and still count. Adaptation theory offers three classic answers, and the film tests all of them. Geoffrey Wagner, in The Novel and the Cinema (1975), divided adaptations into transposition, where a work is carried over with minimal change; commentary, where it is altered in emphasis; and analogy, where a departure is made for the sake of a parallel work that keeps only a resemblance. Dudley Andrew, in Concepts in Film Theory (1984), spoke of borrowing, in which a filmmaker takes a structure or idea from a source and makes no claim to reproduce it. Linda Hutcheon, in A Theory of Adaptation (2006), cut across both schemes by refusing fidelity as the measure altogether: an adaptation, on her account, is an acknowledged transposition, a repetition without replication, to be judged by what it does rather than by what it drops. Under Wagner's scheme the film is a pure analogy, perhaps the limiting case of the category. Under Andrew's it is borrowing raised to the scale of a franchise. Under Hutcheon's the question dissolves, and the film stands or falls as a thriller with a Shakespearean announcement attached.

Ebert's review is the fidelity position stated at its most respectable. He came, his review says, in a receptive spirit: Shakespeare had been "manhandled in countless modern-dress retreads", but after Abel Ferrara's China Girl (1987), which sets the story among the Italian and Chinese gangs of New York, and after Luhrmann's Romeo + Juliet (1996), a martial arts version in Oakland seemed a fair prospect. His complaint was not looseness as such. It was that the film "borrows one premise from Shakespeare (the children of enemy families fall in love), and buries the rest in a creaky plot and wheezy dialogue". That sentence is the strongest anti-remix argument available, because it grants the category and attacks the contents: an analogy still owes its audience a work that justifies the analogy, and on Ebert's evidence the plot and dialogue do not. The counterweight is the film's other contemporary record. Bob Graham in the San Francisco Chronicle called it a "cross-cultural kung fu extravaganza" and likened its pleasures to The Matrix; its aggregate scores settled at 32 percent on Rotten Tomatoes and 52 on Metacritic, the profile of a film whose reviewers split between those grading the action and those grading everything else.

### What is an analogy in adaptation theory?

An analogy, in Geoffrey Wagner's taxonomy, is an adaptation that departs so far from its source that only a parallel remains: a new work built to resemble the original rather than reproduce it. Romeo Must Die is a textbook case, keeping the feud and the cross-divide attraction while replacing plot, language and ending altogether.

The adjudication this article reaches is twofold. On the category question, Wagner and Hutcheon win: the film counts as an adaptation, because recognition is the test and the film was recognized, marketed, reviewed and received as a Romeo and Juliet film by audiences who had never been promised the text. Ebert himself reviewed it as one, which concedes the category while disputing the quality. On the execution question, Ebert wins the larger part: the creaky plot is a fair charge, since the investigation that replaces the love story is assembled from routine parts, and the dialogue carries nothing of the play's wit because the film removed the characters who speak it. Where Ebert overreaches is the implication that looseness itself is the offence. The offence is not that the film keeps one premise. It is that it handles the one premise timidly, building a cross-divide romance and then cutting its kiss, so that the single retained element is retained with an apology. Other remixes in the series keep the names and the world while changing the story's shape, as [Romeo x Juliet: The Anime Reimagining](/2028/10/06/romeo-x-juliet-anime/) keeps Neo Verona and the lovers' identities inside a fantasy plot Shakespeare never wrote, and their boldness exposes this film's caution. An analogy is a licence to change everything except the point. Romeo Must Die changed everything except the frame, and then hesitated at the point.

## The Portable Feud: From Verona's Houses to Oakland's Syndicates

The film's success, modest as it was, confirmed a fact about the play that the tighter adaptations only illustrate: the feud is a template that can be poured into any city with two tribes and a property line. The template's terms are few and robust. Two groups of equal standing, bound by loyalty and costume, whose young men fight in the street; an older generation that profits from the fight or cannot stop it; a pair of young people whose attachment exposes the fight's absurdity; a death or two among the young to price the lesson. West Side Story had already naturalized the template on the American stage, translating houses into gangs and the feud's honour code into turf, as [West Side Story on Broadway (1957)](/2028/08/25/west-side-story-broadway-1957/) set out when it replaced Verona's swords with the Jets' and the Sharks' street war. Romeo Must Die extends the proof in two directions at once. It shows the template surviving a change of medium, of genre and of continent of style, and it shows the template's floor, the minimum below which the story stops being this story. Below the feud and the attraction, there is nothing left to remove; the film stands exactly on the floor, which is its documentary value.

The comparison with the stage musical is sharper than chronology suggests. West Side Story keeps more than the frame: it keeps the tragedy's causality, the mistimed message, the survivor's indictment, and it kills its Romeo. Its Maria lives to accuse, as Trish lives to investigate, but Tony dies because the adaptation respects the play's deepest rule, that the feud's price is paid by the lovers' generation in the lovers' persons. Romeo Must Die declines the rule. Its young dead are brothers, not lovers, killed offstage in the plot's backstory and its middle, and the lovers walk out of the film into a future the play cannot imagine for its pair. The template survives the change, which proves the template is about the feud and not the lovers, a conclusion the play's own Prologue supports, since the Chorus introduces the households and their grudge before it introduces the lovers at all. The remix, in short, is loyal to the play's order of priorities and disloyal to everything else, and the loyalty is enough to make its title legible. That is a real discovery about the source, purchased by a film that scholars of the play rarely cite, and it aligns the loosest adaptation in this series with the thesis the series has built from the evidence of the tight ones: the story the culture keeps retelling is the feud, and the lovers are its most famous casualties rather than its subject.

The Oakland setting contributes one term the template had not previously been required to carry: race as the feud's visible uniform. In Verona the houses are indistinguishable but for their names, and the play insists on their sameness, "both alike in dignity". In the film, the houses are a Black American organization and a Chinese triad, and the divide between them is written on the body in a way Verona's never is. The film does surprisingly little with the difference it has staged: the war is about deeds, not blood, and both organizations are drawn as business cartels whose members happen to be raced, a colour-conscious casting of institutions rather than of persons. Critics of the film's politics have noted that this discretion is of a piece with the removed kiss: the film wants the frisson of a divided city without the consequences of division, difference as scenery. The charge is fair, and it is also the template's limit made visible. The play's feud can be poured into Oakland only if Oakland's actual divisions are drained out of it, and the draining is the film's most eloquent silence about its own moment.

The nearest relative in the film's own past is the one Ebert reached for: Abel Ferrara's China Girl (1987), which had set the story among the Italian and Chinese communities of New York a decade earlier, with a romance across the line at its centre and the gangs of two neighbourhoods around it. The parallel is instructive at passage length, since the fuller treatment belongs to that film's own study elsewhere in the series. China Girl kept the tragedy's outcome and the lovers' centrality; Romeo Must Die, arriving after Luhrmann's hit had re-familiarized the property, kept neither, and the sequence of the three films describes a market learning curve: 1987 proved the feud could carry an ethnic crime film, 1996 proved the text itself could still sell, and 2000 proved the title alone could sell, with the property reduced to the two terms the audience was guaranteed to recognize, the name Romeo and the promise of his death. Each step removed a component and found the remainder still legible, which is the empirical form of the portability argument: the feud template is not merely adaptable but subtractable, and Romeo Must Die is the measurement of how far the subtraction can run before the remainder stops being a story and becomes a logo. The film stands one step above the logo. Its Oakland war has named families, priced motives and symmetrically murdered sons, and those remainders, thin as they are, carry the archetype's charge.

## Aaliyah's Single Film and the Afterlife of the Remix

Aaliyah Dana Haughton was twenty-one when the film was released, already a star of rhythm and blues with two albums behind her, and Romeo Must Die was her acting debut and the only film of hers released in her lifetime. She filmed a second role, the vampire queen Akasha in Queen of the Damned, and released her third album in July 2001. On August 25, 2001, returning from the Bahamas where she had shot a music video, she was killed in the crash of an overloaded small aircraft shortly after takeoff, aged twenty-two; eight others died with her. The fact belongs in any account of the film, soberly and without ornament, because it fixed the film's place in memory: the loose remix became, by loss, the principal screen record of a performer whose career was beginning rather than ending. Her Trish is the only completed leading performance she left, and the film is watched, in large part, as her memorial, a fate no one connected with its making intended and one that retrospective writing, Loayza's included, handles as the centre of its legacy.

The film's other afterlives are industrial. It established Jet Li as an American leading man after a single villain role, and it proved the commercial formula of pairing Hong Kong action with Black American music and casting, a formula Silver Pictures repeated with Bartkowiak, DMX and Anthony Anderson in Cradle 2 the Grave (2003), with Li again at its centre. It demonstrated that a Shakespeare title could open an action film at number two, and its model of the story, two syndicates, one attraction, no text, became the floor plan for a generation of loose retellings in which the play survives as a marketing line and a geometry. The teen films that descend from the play, surveyed in [Modern Teen-Film Descendants of the Play](/2029/04/27/teen-film-descendants/), mostly keep more of the lovers and less of the feud; Romeo Must Die keeps the feud and dissolves the lovers, and the contrast completes the map of what can be kept. Between the descendants that keep the romance and this remix that keeps the war, the whole play is accounted for, in pieces, across a popular cinema that rarely keeps it whole.

## The Title as Contract: A Name, a Sentence and a Broken Promise

The title deserves its own audit, because it is the film's largest single borrowing and its most candid one. Romeo Must Die contains four words, of which the play supplies one name and one implication. Shakespeare's title names two people and promises nothing; the ending is disclosed by the Prologue instead, inside the work, where an audience that has bought a ticket hears in the first minutes that the lovers will die. The film's title performs the Prologue's office on the poster, outside the work, where a passing audience hears the same disclosure as a sales pitch, and then the film declines to honour it. The sequence is worth stating plainly: the title borrows the play's name for recognition, borrows the Prologue's spoiler for urgency, and spends both on a story that keeps neither the person nor the death. No other adaptation in this series goes so far on so little. West Side Story surrendered the names entirely and kept the tragedy; Romeo + Juliet kept the names, the words and the deaths and changed the century; Romeo x Juliet kept the names and the world and changed the physics. Romeo Must Die kept half of one name and the threat attached to it, and the threat is the point of pressure: a title that functions as a sentence of death becomes, in this film, a sentence commuted without announcement.

There is a defence, and it is stronger than the marketing cynicism it first resembles. A title is a contract of subject rather than of outcome, and this film is, in fact, about the death sentence that hangs over Romeo: its entire plot is the story of a man marked by his family's war, returning to a city where his brother has already been executed by it, and the film can be read as the working out of whether the sentence will be carried out on the second son. On that reading the title states the play's premise as a question rather than a result, and the film's refusal to kill Han is its answer, the thriller's optimism set against the tragedy's certainty. The defence explains the title. It does not explain the poster campaign's reliance on a romance the film withholds, nor the absence of any Romeo in the cast list, where the name survives only as a marketing morpheme, detached from the character it named for four centuries and attached to a surname-free promise of violence. The name Romeo had by 2000 become a common noun in English for a lover, a dictionary entry independent of the play, and the film trades on the noun. Its Romeo is the word, not the man, and the discovery that a common noun could carry a 25 million dollar production to 91 million dollars of receipts is the film's most quotable finding about the play's afterlife: Shakespeare's characters have become so thoroughly public property that a single one of their names, declined as a noun and conjugated with a threat, suffices to summon the whole story in an audience that is then content to watch a different one.

The commuted sentence also completes the film's pattern of generational transfer, and the pattern, once seen, organizes the whole mapping. In the play, the sentence falls downward: the fathers' feud kills the children, and the Prince's closing judgement, "All are punished", includes the fathers precisely because the punishment has already been executed on their heirs. In the film, the sentence is redirected upward at every joint. The exile Romeo suffers becomes the prison the father imposed. The deaths the lovers suffer become the deaths of the fathers' lieutenants and of the father who behaved worst. The suicide Juliet performs becomes the suicide Ch'u performs. Even the reconciliation is reassigned: Verona's fathers reconcile over the children's bodies, while Oakland's surviving father is reconciled to his daughter in an ambulance, the child ministering to the parent instead of the parent burying the child. The film is thus not a random abridgement but a systematic inversion along the axis of age, the play read backwards through its generations, the young absolved and the old billed. Whether the inversion was designed or merely precipitated by the thriller's need for a surviving hero, it gives the remix its claim to be an interpretation and not only an exploitation: it asserts, in plot, that the play's true criminals were always the fathers, and it executes them accordingly. The play itself half agrees, since its Prince says as much from the tomb steps. Romeo Must Die is the rare loose adaptation that can be shown, element by element, to have taken a position on its source, and the position is legible only because the remix note makes the transfers visible, which is why the loosest film in the series rewards the closest accounting.

## What the Critics Split Over: Two Films Inside One Release

The contemporary reviews divide so cleanly that they read as reports on two different films sharing a title. Elvis Mitchell, reviewing for The New York Times under the headline "Hip-Hop Joins Martial Arts but Lets Plot Muscle In", called the film "dreary" in its storytelling and still predicted its commercial success from the combination of its ingredients, a verdict that treats the film as an industrial event whose plot is the least of its functions. Bob Graham, in the San Francisco Chronicle, reviewed the other film: a "cross-cultural kung fu extravaganza" in the lineage of The Matrix, shining precisely where Mitchell saw machinery, in Li's stunts. Rene Rodriguez split the difference unhappily, calling the plot "needlessly convoluted" while faulting the effects for diminishing the stunts they decorated. Ebert's one and a half stars anchored the hostile pole, and the aggregates recorded the stalemate: 32 percent on Rotten Tomatoes across 94 reviews, and 52 out of 100 on Metacritic across 27 critics, the statistical portrait of a film that half its reviewers graded as an action showcase and half as a drama, with the grades cancelling.

The split has a structure that the adaptation question explains. Critics who weighed the film against the play found the deficit Ebert named: one premise, creaky plot, wheezy dialogue. Critics who weighed it against its genre found the surplus Graham named: a charismatic star correctly framed, fights of genuine invention, a soundtrack that functioned as a second feature. Both weighings are accurate, and their disagreement is the disagreement between the film's two contracts, the one its title signed with Shakespeare and the one its campaign signed with the action audience. The home video release extended the second contract's victory: the film reached DVD in August 2000 with Warner shipping more than half a million units in its first week, and its reputation settled into the durable category of the cult favourite, owned and rewatched by an audience that had never asked it to be faithful. The reassessment literature belongs to that audience's maturity. Loayza's 2021 account in Vulture is affectionate without amnesia, clear that the romance is withheld and the object nearly unrecognizable, and clear also that the object matters, as a blueprint of an era's industries and as the surviving screen record of its leading lady. Time has graded the film as Graham did, on its energies, while preserving Ebert's review as the standing statement of its debts, and the two verdicts together are the fairest summary the record offers: a hit that deserved its audience and a Romeo and Juliet that did not deserve its title, in the same 115 minutes.

## The Verdict on Romeo Must Die as a Romeo and Juliet

Romeo Must Die should be classed as an adaptation and judged as a near miss. It counts, because the recognition test is decisive and because its one retained element is the play's load-bearing structure, the feud between two houses with a forbidden attachment across it. It misses, because an adaptation that keeps the frame owes the frame its emotional logic, and this film replaces that logic twice over: the causeless hatred of Verona becomes a business fraud that investigation can dissolve, and the absolute love of the play becomes a partnership that a test screening could edit. The play's tragedy is that understanding arrives after the deaths. The film's comfort is that understanding arrives instead, and saves everyone worth saving. Both stories end their feuds. Only one of them shows what a feud costs, and it is not the one with Romeo in the title and no Romeo in the cast. The remix note's grades stand as the summary: the skeleton, kept; the poetry, dropped; the ending, reversed; the romance, advertised and withheld. As a document of what the culture actually retains of Romeo and Juliet when everything else is stripped away, the film is indispensable. As a Romeo and Juliet, it is the silhouette of one, and a silhouette is a true outline with nothing inside it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch remix note, and what does it record about the film's mapping of the play?

The InsightCrunch remix note is this article's comparison table, setting sixteen elements of Romeo and Juliet beside their counterparts in Romeo Must Die and grading each one as kept, transformed or dropped. It records that the film keeps the two feuding households, the divided city, the cross-divide attraction and the pattern of dead young men; transforms the fathers, the lovers, the villain's functions and the reconciliation; and drops Mercutio, the Nurse, the Friar, the Prince, Paris, the secret marriage and the verse altogether. Its pattern is the article's argument in miniature: everything structural survives, everything verbal or sacramental vanishes, and the ending is reversed, since both lovers live. The note exists because loose adaptation invites vague claims, and the film deserves exact accounting of what its title borrowed.

### Q: Who directed Romeo Must Die, and whose directorial debut was it?

Romeo Must Die was directed by Andrzej Bartkowiak, and it was his debut as a director. Bartkowiak came to the film as one of Hollywood's leading cinematographers, and the connection to his star was direct: he had photographed Lethal Weapon 4 (1998), the film in which Jet Li played the villain in his first American screen role. Producer Joel Silver, who developed the project at Silver Pictures after growing frustrated with the staleness of American action cinema, built the film as a meeting of Hong Kong action grammar and Hollywood production, and Bartkowiak, a cameraman steeped in both the studio system and the physical staging of fights, was the natural bridge. He went on to direct Exit Wounds and Cradle 2 the Grave, repeating the formula of martial arts paired with hip hop casting and music.

### Q: Who wrote the screenplay and the story of Romeo Must Die?

The screenplay of Romeo Must Die is credited to Eric Bernt and John Jarrell, and the story to Mitchell Kapner. The division of credits reflects the film's origins as a producer's project: the story supplies the premise, an ex-policeman drawn into a war between his father's triad organization and a rival Oakland family over waterfront property, while the screenplay builds the investigation plot that carries Han Sing and Trish O'Day from a chance street meeting to the exposure of the land scam. Neither text draws on Shakespeare's language in any form; the debt to Romeo and Juliet is structural and titular, a matter of feuding families and their children, rather than verbal. Bernt later spoke about the casting of Aaliyah, describing the combination of innocence and street that the role of Trish required and that her screen test displayed.

### Q: Who plays Han Sing and Trish O'Day in Romeo Must Die?

Jet Li plays Han Sing and Aaliyah plays Trish O'Day. Li was already a major star of Hong Kong cinema, with a career built on wushu championship skill and leading roles at home, and Romeo Must Die was his first leading role in an American film after his villainous appearance in Lethal Weapon 4. Aaliyah, a bestselling rhythm and blues singer, was making her acting debut; Warner Bros. had considered Janet Jackson for Trish, according to the director's later recollection, but Aaliyah was the only performer invited to screen-test, and the part was built around the qualities she showed. The supporting cast places Delroy Lindo and Henry O as the rival fathers, Isaak O'Day and Ch'u Sing, with Isaiah Washington as Mac, Russell Wong as Kai and DMX as the nightclub owner Silk.

### Q: Why is Han Sing in prison when the film begins?

Han Sing is in prison because he took the blame for a crime committed by his father. Han had been a Hong Kong police officer; when Ch'u Sing's activities drew the attention of the Chinese authorities, Han accepted responsibility so that his father and his younger brother Po could flee to America and establish themselves in Oakland, and he served the sentence in their place. The arrangement is the film's version of exile: where Romeo is banished to Mantua for a killing, Han is removed from the story in advance by an act of filial sacrifice, and his father's business consumes him as surely as the feud consumes the play's children. News of Po's murder reaches the prison, Han escapes, and the plot begins with the Romeo figure returning as an investigator of his own family's debts.

### Q: Who killed Po Sing in Romeo Must Die?

Po Sing was killed by Kai, his father's chief lieutenant, acting in the interests of Ch'u Sing's business dealings. The admission comes in the film's final movement at the Sing compound, where Kai tells Han that he personally killed Po because the younger son had begun interfering with Ch'u's arrangements with the developer Vincent Roth. Po had been preparing to share information with Colin O'Day that might have ended the war between the families, and his murder opens the film because it converts a business dispute into the appearance of a gang war. The killing also defines Ch'u's guilt: the patriarch did not merely fail to protect his son but presided over the machinery that destroyed him, which is why the film's ending assigns Ch'u the suicide that its title seemed to promise for Romeo, and why Han's vengeance settles on the lieutenant and the father rather than on the rival house.

### Q: Who killed Colin O'Day, and why?

Colin O'Day was killed by Mac, his own father's chief lieutenant, as part of the manufactured gang war. Mac confesses in the climax that the war between the O'Day and Sing organizations was a ruse he concocted with Ch'u Sing to cover a campaign of murder and extortion against waterfront business owners who refused to sell their properties. Colin had been due to meet Po Sing on the night of Po's murder to compare information that could have ended the conflict, and his knowledge made him as dangerous to the conspiracy as Po was. He and his girlfriend are murdered by an unseen assailant, and the killing is designed to be read as triad retaliation. The symmetry with Po's death is the film's most exact inheritance from the play: each house loses a son, and in Oakland both sons are killed not by the enemy but by the feud's own managers.

### Q: What do the two crime families want with the Oakland waterfront?

Both families are acquiring deeds to properties along the Oakland waterfront, by purchase where possible and by intimidation where necessary, in order to sell the assembled package to the developer Vincent Roth. Roth's plan, as the film presents it, is to use the waterfront site for a stadium tied to a new professional football franchise, and the value of the assembled land dwarfs the value of any single parcel, which is why holdout owners are threatened, burned out and murdered. The waterfront scheme replaces the play's ancient grudge as the feud's engine: the Sings and the O'Days are partners in the acquisition even while their organizations appear to be at war, and the war itself is maintained as an instrument to depress prices and frighten sellers. Isaak O'Day intends to convert his share into legitimacy, demanding an ownership stake in the franchise rather than cash; Ch'u Sing takes the money.

### Q: Who is Vincent Roth, and what does he plan to build?

Vincent Roth, played by Edoardo Ballerini, is the property developer buying up the Oakland waterfront through the two crime families, and he plans to build a stadium for a new football franchise on the assembled site. He is the film's most original figure because he has no counterpart in the play: neither a Capulet nor a Montague, but a third party for whom the feud itself is a business instrument, profitable exactly as long as it frightens owners into selling. Verona has no Roth, since its feud consumes wealth rather than manufacturing it. Roth's exit is the film's most eloquent image: escaping by helicopter during the climactic shootout, he loses his briefcase to Mac's gunfire and the deeds, the paper substance of the whole conspiracy, scatter into the wind. He survives unpunished, the one principal the ending leaves untouched, which is the film's quiet concession that the market outlives the feud.

### Q: Do Han and Trish kiss in Romeo Must Die?

Han and Trish do not kiss in the released film, and the absence became its most debated scene. A kiss was written and, on the documented record, filmed in two versions. The documentary The Slanted Screen reported that the kiss was removed after testing badly with audiences, and Jose Antonio Vargas, reporting that argument in the Washington Post, quoted the charge that mainstream audiences were uncomfortable with an Asian man portrayed in a sexual light. Jet Li gave a different explanation on his own website: both endings were shot, and the version without the kiss was chosen because a kiss would have seemed strange and awkward immediately after Han watched his father kill himself. The film ends instead with an embrace, the two walking away together past the arriving police. The two accounts point to different causes, one industrial, one dramatic, and the article treats the disagreement itself as evidence about the film's caution at the centre of its romance.

### Q: Does Romeo die in Romeo Must Die?

No figure corresponding to Romeo dies in Romeo Must Die. Han Sing survives the film, defeats Kai, confronts his father and leaves Oakland's war behind with Trish, and the title's sentence is transferred to others: to Po and Colin, the brothers killed before and during the plot, and at the last to Ch'u Sing, who shoots himself as Han abandons him. The reversal is systematic rather than careless. The play kills the lovers and spares the fathers to learn from the corpses; the film kills the guilty father and the feud's managers and spares the lovers to expose them. The title functions as an allusion the film is licensed to disobey, and its disobedience defines the difference between the genres: a tragedy discharges its debt in the lovers' deaths, while a thriller discharges it in the villains', and Romeo Must Die belongs wholly to the second economy while borrowing the first one's name.

### Q: Where was Romeo Must Die filmed?

Romeo Must Die was filmed almost entirely in Vancouver, British Columbia, although it is set in Oakland, California. Principal photography ran from May to July 1999, using locations that included Gastown, the Grandview-Woodland neighbourhood, Vanier Park and the city's Chinatown, with a working shipyard standing in for the Oakland waterfront and the classical Chinese garden of Dr. Sun Yat-Sen doubling for the Sing family's world. Oakland and San Francisco appear in a small number of establishing shots, the bridges and skylines that certify the setting before the story retreats to Canadian streets. The substitution was standard economics for a mid-budget studio film, and it drew at least one critic's complaint: Roger Ebert, who disliked the film on several grounds, noticed the geography. The doubling matters to the adaptation's texture, since Oakland's specific Black and Chinese American histories are invoked by the story but never photographed in their actual streets.

### Q: How much did Romeo Must Die cost, and how much did it earn?

Romeo Must Die cost about 25 million dollars to make and earned about 91 million dollars at the worldwide box office. Box Office Mojo's figures, the standard source for the film's commercial record, put the North American total at just under 56 million dollars and the worldwide total at just over 91 million. The film opened at number two in North America behind Erin Brockovich, taking some 18 million dollars in its first weekend across 2,641 theatres, a strong result for a mid-budget action film launching a Hong Kong star's American leading career. The home video performance extended the success: Warner shipped more than half a million DVD units on the film's first video release in August 2000. Against those numbers, the film's divided reviews read as a footnote; commercially, the experiment of opening an action picture under a Shakespeare title, with no Shakespeare in it, was fully vindicated.

### Q: What did Roger Ebert object to in Romeo Must Die?

Roger Ebert objected to the hollowness of the film's Shakespeare debt and to the digital assistance in its fights. Reviewing the film in the Chicago Sun-Times, he gave it one and a half stars, having arrived receptive: after China Girl and Romeo + Juliet, he wrote, a martial arts version in Oakland seemed a fair prospect, but the film "borrows one premise from Shakespeare (the children of enemy families fall in love), and buries the rest in a creaky plot and wheezy dialogue". His second charge was technical and has become the review's most quoted line: Li's scenes, he wrote, "are so clearly computer-aided that his moves are about as impressive as Bugs Bunny doing the same", and the effects missed the point of a form whose pleasure is believing the body performed the feat. This article accepts the dialogue charge and the substance of the effects charge, while disputing the implication that looseness itself was the offence: the offence was timidity about the one premise retained.

### Q: What chart record did Aaliyah's "Try Again" set?

"Try Again" was the first song in the history of the Billboard Hot 100 to reach number one on the strength of radio airplay alone. Written by Stephen "Static Major" Garrett and Timbaland and released as the lead single from the Romeo Must Die soundtrack, the song climbed to the top of the chart without a commercial single on sale, at a time when the chart's rules had long disadvantaged songs unavailable to buy; a vinyl pressing followed only after the record had been set. The achievement made Aaliyah the first artist to top the Hot 100 by airplay alone and earned her a Grammy nomination for the performance. The song's video, which intercut her performance with Jet Li's fight moves in a hall of mirrors, won two MTV Video Music Awards. The record stands as the soundtrack's most durable distinction and as the clearest case of the film's music outperforming its drama in the historical ledger.

### Q: How many songs did Aaliyah contribute to the Romeo Must Die soundtrack?

Aaliyah contributed four songs to Romeo Must Die: The Album and served as one of its executive producers, alongside Timbaland, Barry Hankerson and Jomo Hankerson. Her tracks were "Try Again", the lead single; "Come Back in One Piece", a duet with DMX, who plays Silk in the film; "Are You Feelin' Me?"; and "I Don't Wanna". The album, released by Blackground and Virgin Records in association with Warner Bros., debuted at number three on the Billboard 200 with some 203,000 copies sold in its first week, reached number one on the rhythm and blues and hip hop albums chart, and was certified platinum in the United States, where it sold a million and a half copies. The film's score was credited to Stanley Clarke and Timbaland. Aaliyah's dual position, female lead on screen and executive producer of the album, made the soundtrack a primary work of hers rather than a souvenir of the film.

### Q: Who choreographed the fights in Romeo Must Die?

The fights in Romeo Must Die were choreographed by Corey Yuen, one of the senior action directors of Hong Kong cinema and a formative figure in Jet Li's screen career. Yuen's team built the film's action grammar: wire-assisted exchanges staged for a cinematographer-director who understood both Hong Kong pacing and Hollywood coverage, a prison escape, a football game converted into a mass brawl, a fight in which Han, handcuffed and unwilling to strike a woman, defeats the motorcycle fighter Meriana by manipulating Trish's limbs like a puppeteer's, and the climactic duel with Kai. The production's signature device, brief X-ray cutaways showing bones break at the moment of impact, was tested in a single sequence, approved by preview audiences and extended across the film. The choreography drew the film's widest praise and its sharpest dissent, since critics including Roger Ebert and Rene Rodriguez argued that digital assistance diminished the very skill the choreography displayed.

### Q: What happened to Aaliyah after Romeo Must Die?

Aaliyah filmed one more leading role, the vampire queen Akasha in Queen of the Damned, and released her third studio album in July 2001 to strong reviews and a number two debut. On August 25, 2001, she travelled to the Bahamas to film the video for "Rock the Boat" and was killed, with eight others, when the small aircraft carrying her party crashed shortly after takeoff; she was twenty-two. Queen of the Damned was released after her death, leaving Romeo Must Die as the only film of hers released in her lifetime and Trish O'Day as her one completed contemporary leading performance. Her death fixed the film's place in public memory: a loose Shakespeare remix became the principal screen record of a major recording artist, and retrospective assessments of the film, including the reassessment published in Vulture in 2021, treat that inheritance as central to its legacy. The album she made for the film continued to sell; the week after her death her third album rose to number one.

### Q: Is Romeo Must Die a faithful adaptation of Romeo and Juliet?

Romeo Must Die is not a faithful adaptation, and it does not attempt to be one. It contains no Shakespeare text, none of the characters' names, no balcony, no secret marriage, no potion and no double suicide, and its lovers survive. Its fidelity is confined to a single structure: two feuding households of equal standing, an attraction between a son of one and a daughter of the other, and young men of both houses dying as the feud escalates. Adaptation theory has names for this degree of relation: Geoffrey Wagner called it analogy, a new work built to parallel a source rather than reproduce it, and Dudley Andrew called it borrowing. Judged as a version, it fails every test. Judged as an analogy, it is an instructive one, since its substitutions are systematic: the grudge becomes a land scam, the lovers become investigators, and the tragedy becomes a thriller whose ending replaces grief with evidence. The question is therefore not whether it is faithful, but whether the one element it keeps can bear the title; the argument here is that it can, barely, and that the film's timidity about its own romance, not its looseness, is the true shortfall.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch remix note to argue that the feud is the play's most portable element?

An essay should use the InsightCrunch remix note as a subtraction record: its three columns show what a major studio removed from Romeo and Juliet, in descending order of dispensability, until only the feud, the divided city and the cross-divide attraction remained, at which point the result still registered with audiences and critics as a Romeo and Juliet film. The essay can then argue that portability is a property of structures rather than language, since the verse, the names and the ending all proved removable while the two-household geometry did not, and can test the claim against the note's reversals: the grudge that becomes a fraud, the suicide that migrates to a father, the reconciliation that becomes an exposure. The note's value is that it replaces impressions of looseness with a countable inventory, sixteen elements graded one by one, from which the essay's thesis about the feud's portability follows as a conclusion rather than an assertion.
