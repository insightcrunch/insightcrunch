Frantz Fanon was a psychiatrist from Martinique, a French colony in the Caribbean, who resigned a senior hospital post in Algeria to join the Algerian independence movement, and who wrote, inside a working life of barely a decade, the books on which the theory of decolonisation largely rests. His subject throughout was the psychology and politics of colonial rule: what domination does to the minds of the dominated and of the dominators, and what sort of political order could bring domination to an end. His central modification of the Marxism he inherited can be given in a word of his own, stretching. Marxist analysis, he held, should always be slightly stretched when it is applied to the colonial problem, because in the colony the categories of race and class refuse to sit in separate compartments of the theory. They are fused together, and an analysis that insists on keeping them apart will misdescribe the society standing in front of it.

Three corrections organise everything this profile has to say, because the literature on Fanon is unusually crowded with confident error. The celebrated chapter on violence is habitually read through a preface written by another man, a preface considerably more inflammatory than the chapter it introduces, and a substantial part of what the world attributes to Fanon on violence originates there. The most cited chapter of his first book travels in English under a title that mistranslates the French, converting a phenomenological account of lived experience into a flat assertion about fact. And the chapter of his final book that events have vindicated most completely, the anatomy of what national consciousness becomes after independence, is the chapter his readers discuss least. A reader who holds those three corrections in view can approach the famous material with the proportions restored.

The profile proceeds from the life to the arguments. It begins with the four postings that made the theorist, Martinique, wartime and student France, the psychiatric hospital at Blida, and exile in Tunis, because Fanon's concepts were formed in institutions and carry the marks of them. It then takes up the psychology of colonial racism set out in his first book, the stretching of Marxist analysis that is this article's organising idea, and the disputed question of who makes a colonial revolution. The final book is then assessed chapter by chapter, which is the only way its very unequal reception makes sense, before the violence chapter and its preface are disentangled and the prediction about post-independence states is examined on its own. Readers who want the wider framework of imperialism theory into which Fanon was intervening will find it in the series [marxism and imperialism guide](/2017/02/01/marxism-and-imperialism-guide/), which this profile assumes rather than repeats.

A note on texts, since this profile has made an argument of mediation: quotations follow the rule of the series, short, single, and checked against the standard translations, and where the two English versions of a book divide, the profile has said so rather than silently choosing. The French titles are given where a rendering is in dispute. Dates of birth, death, posts, resignations, and publications have been verified against the biographical record before being asserted, and the authorships that matter to the argument, above all the preface's, are stated from the title pages and the correspondence, not from the reputation.

## A life in four postings

Fanon was born on 20 July 1925 in Fort-de-France, the capital of Martinique, into a middle class family of mixed African and European descent, in a colony whose inhabitants held French citizenship and whose economy rested on sugar plantations worked by the descendants of enslaved Africans. His father worked in the customs service, his mother ran a shop, and the household was respectable, francophone, and oriented toward the metropole in the manner of the island's educated class. The young Fanon attended the Lycée Schoelcher, the school that had educated Aimé Césaire a generation earlier, and grew up inside the particular Martinican arrangement in which France was simultaneously the mother country, the source of all advancement, and a place almost nobody in the family had seen. That doubleness, formal equality layered over a racial hierarchy everyone could feel, is the biographical root of everything he later wrote about assimilation.

### How did the war change the young Fanon?

It stripped away the assimilationist bargain before he was twenty. Volunteering for the Free French forces as a teenager, he crossed the Atlantic in wartime, trained in North Africa, fought in France, and was wounded and decorated; inside that army he watched white officers reserve advancement and courtesy for white soldiers

while treating the Black and North African troops who filled the ranks as auxiliaries in their own liberation.

The experience mattered because it was administered by the very institution that embodied French universalism. The Martinican volunteers had been told, and had told themselves, that they were going to defend a civilisation that counted them as its own. What they met instead were segregated arrangements, contempt from French civilians who mistook them for American or African troops of a lesser sort, and, in the campaign's final phase, the deliberate whitening of the forces that entered liberated territory, so that the photographs of liberation would carry the right faces. Fanon drew the lesson he never abandoned: the racial order was not a misunderstanding inside the universalist promise but a structure that the promise routinely concealed. He returned to Martinique, helped in Césaire's election campaign, and then left for France to study, first dentistry briefly, then medicine at Lyon, where he added philosophy and psychology to a medical training and attended lectures by Maurice Merleau-Ponty, whose phenomenology gave him instruments he used for the rest of his life.

Lyon also gave him his specialism. Fanon trained in psychiatry under the influence of the institutional psychotherapy pioneered at Saint-Alban by François Tosquelles, the Catalan psychiatrist who had fled Franco's Spain and rebuilt a wartime asylum into a therapeutic community in which patients and staff shared the work of the institution. The Saint-Alban lesson, that a hospital's social organisation is itself a treatment or a poison, stayed with Fanon permanently. Before taking a post, he wrote and defended the work that became his first book. Offered initially as his doctoral thesis, it was refused in that form, a committee evidently unwilling to accept a dissertation written in a voice that moved between clinical observation, philosophy, poetry, and polemic; he produced a conventional neuropsychiatric thesis instead and published the rejected manuscript with Éditions du Seuil in 1952 as Peau noire, masques blancs, Black Skin, White Masks. He was twenty-six. After a year practising at Pontorson in Normandy, he competed successfully for a senior appointment abroad and chose Algeria, arriving in November 1953 to take charge of a service at the Blida-Joinville psychiatric hospital, the largest in the colony.

The year at Pontorson, in a psychiatric post near Mont Saint-Michel, gave him the metropolitan baseline against which the colonial institution would be measured: the French asylum's own rigidities, its custodial habits and its medical hierarchies, were considerable, and Fanon arrived in Algeria no romantic about the profession he served. He had also, in 1952, married Josie Dublé, a young Frenchwoman from Lyon whom he had met as a student; the marriage, which lasted his life and produced their son Olivier, placed a European wife inside the biography of the theorist of the Manichean world, a fact his polemical summarists find inconvenient and his serious readers find instructive, since the corpus's distinction between the settler as a function and the European as a person was not an abstraction for its author but a domestic fact, tested daily. His family by birth remained in Martinique, where a daughter from an earlier relationship, Mireille, was growing up; the man who wrote that the colonised intellectual must choose his camp was himself distributed across the colonial world's compartments, and the distribution is part of the evidence for the sincerity, and the cost, of the choice he describes.

### What did Fanon find at Blida?

A colonial institution in brick and routine. The hospital held some two thousand patients, overwhelmingly Algerian Muslims treated by European staff through interpreters, in wards organised by the assumptions of a colonial psychiatry that had spent decades explaining North African minds as primitive, impulsive, and constitutionally prone to violence;

Fanon set about dismantling the regime he inherited, unlocking wards and rebuilding the service around work, meetings, and social life.

The Algiers school of psychiatry, associated above all with Antoine Porot, had built an entire doctrine on the supposed mental particularities of the North African, attributing to Algerian patients a childlike cortex, a dominance of primitive instincts, and a congenital criminality that conveniently explained both their illnesses and their rebellion. Fanon had reviewed this literature as a student and despised it, not only as bad science but as the psychiatric wing of the colonial order: a theory that relocated the causes of Algerian misery from the colony's arrangements to the Algerian skull. The doctrine he was dismantling deserves its own specification, because its defeat inside Algerian psychiatry was one of the century's quieter intellectual victories and Fanon was its instrument. The Algiers school, founded in the colonial faculty and codified in Antoine Porot's writings from the war of 1914 onward, taught that the North African Muslim possessed a distinct mental constitution: a cortex of limited development, a predominance of the primitive and the instinctual, an impulsiveness that explained in one motion his criminality, his credulity, and his illnesses. The doctrine's convenience to the administration was total, since a population whose rebellions were symptoms required policing as therapy, and its scientific standing rested on the circular method Fanon had already identified in Mannoni, the observation of colonised men inside the colony's institutions read back as the nature of the colonised man. His counterattack proceeded on the school's own terrain, in the journals and congresses of colonial psychiatry, with case series and ward experiments as the ammunition, and its conclusion was the sociogenic principle in uniform: put the Algerian in a different social world and the supposed constitution changes with the world, which is the experiment the Blida reforms and the revolution itself then performed at scale. The school's doctrines did not survive the war it had helped to legitimate, and its disappearance from respectable psychiatry is part of Fanon's unlisted bibliography, the work he did inside his profession's institutions before he resigned from the empire's.

At Blida he attempted the Saint-Alban method wholesale, introducing staff and patient meetings, a hospital newspaper, film screenings, sports, and workshops, and he published clinical papers with his colleague Jacques Azoulay that record, with unusual honesty, where the method failed. It failed, they concluded, with the Muslim men of his service, because the social therapy had been designed around European forms of sociability, cafés, festivals, and associations that had no equivalent weight in the patients' own world. The admission is characteristic. Fanon's psychiatry was never a technique applied to passive material; it kept running into the fact that the patient's world, its languages, kinship structures, and humiliations, was the real clinical territory.

In November 1954, a year after his arrival, the Algerian insurrection began, and the hospital's position became impossible in a new way. Blida stood in a region of intense guerrilla activity. Wounded fighters were brought to Fanon for treatment and concealment; French soldiers and police, including men involved in interrogation and torture, were also among his patients, and he treated the torturers' breakdowns alongside the tortured. Through the doctor and resistant Pierre Chaulet, he made contact with the Front de Libération Nationale in 1955 and began the double life of a senior colonial official who was also, in secret, a servant of the rebellion: treating maquisards, training nurses for the underground health service, and passing what he learned to the movement. His clinical notes from these years, some of which close his final book, are the record of a psychiatrist discovering that the war outside the ward explained the symptoms inside it more completely than any diagnosis he had been taught.

### Why did Fanon resign his Algerian post?

Because he concluded that a psychiatrist in a colony at war was being asked to repair patients for return to the system that had broken them, and that continuing required a dishonesty he spelled out in his letter to the Resident Minister, Robert Lacoste, in November 1956:

a doctor cannot treat the Algerian as a stranger in his own country and call the treatment neutral.

The letter, later collected in Toward the African Revolution, remains one of the great documents of professional refusal. Its argument is clinical before it is political. Psychiatry, Fanon wrote, aims to enable a person to cease being a stranger to his environment; the Arab in Algeria is permanently alienated in his own country, living in a state of absolute depersonalisation, inside a social structure that is hostile to any attempt at individuation. A society that drives its members to desperate solutions is a non-viable society, he told the minister, and should be replaced; there comes a time, in the letter's most quoted sentence, when silence becomes dishonesty. The administration's reply was expulsion. In January 1957 Fanon was deported from Algeria, travelled briefly and discreetly through France, and made his way to Tunis, where the FLN's external leadership received a recruit of unusual value: a French trained psychiatrist, a published author, and a man who had seen the colonial machine from inside its own administration.

Tunis gave him the fourth posting, and it was several posts at once. He worked on the editorial collective of El Moudjahid, the FLN's newspaper, writing unsigned leaders and analyses for the remainder of his life. He practised psychiatry again, at the Manouba hospital and then in a day clinic he helped to found at the Charles-Nicolle hospital, where he pioneered psychiatric day hospitalisation in the region so that patients could be treated without being severed from their families. He travelled as the movement's envoy across Africa, attending the conferences of the liberation era in Accra, Conakry, Addis Ababa, and Cairo, and in 1960 the Provisional Government of the Algerian Republic appointed him its ambassador to Ghana, a posting that placed him at the centre of the period's pan-African diplomacy. It was also in these years that he wrote the two books of the Algerian period: A Dying Colonialism in 1959, a set of essays on the revolution's transformation of Algerian society, its women, its family structures, its relationship to radio and to medicine; and the essays and speeches later gathered as Toward the African Revolution. He undertook, in 1960, an exhausting reconnaissance across the Sahara toward Mali, exploring a southern supply route for the army of liberation, and returned ill. The diagnosis was leukaemia.

What followed has the compression of a parable, though it was simply his method applied to his own ending. He travelled to the Soviet Union for treatment and won a remission; he went to the United States in October 1961 for further care, admitted to a hospital in Bethesda, Maryland, under the name he had used on an earlier mission, Ibrahim Fanon; and before and between these journeys, in Tunis and in Rome, where he met Jean-Paul Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir over several days of intense conversation, he dictated to his wife Josie and to secretaries the book that became Les Damnés de la terre, The Wretched of the Earth, published by François Maspero in Paris in the autumn of 1961. He died in Bethesda on 6 December 1961, aged thirty-six, a few weeks after the book appeared and a few months before the independence it had argued for was won. His body was flown back and buried with honours in Algerian soil, at Aïn Kerma near the eastern border, in a martyrs' cemetery of the army he had served. The brevity of the career matters for reading him: nearly everything he wrote was written in motion, in exile, in illness, or in the margins of clinical and diplomatic work, and the texts carry both the urgency and the unfinished quality of that haste.

The Lyon years deserve one further notice, because they show the writer forming alongside the doctor. Fanon wrote plays as a student, dramas in verse on philosophical themes that remained unpublished in his lifetime, and the theatricality never left his prose: the great chapters are staged, with scenes, voices, and a narrator who steps forward to address the audience, and readers who find the political writings overwrought are sometimes registering, without knowing it, a dramatist's training operating inside a theorist's argument. Lyon also placed him inside the intellectual weather of postwar France at its most concentrated: the cafés and journals where existentialism and Marxism were fighting for the succession, the early rumblings of the colonial wars in Indochina and Madagascar that France's students debated while its governments waged them, and the presence in the city of a large population of North African workers whose treatment by French society was a standing refutation of the republic's account of itself. He observed that treatment, recorded it, and filed it; when the first book describes the North African syndrome, the cluster of pains without lesions that French doctors dismissed as simulation in their Maghrebi patients, and reinterprets the pains as the body's testimony to an impossible social position, the case material is Lyonnaise as much as Algerian. The colonial world, he was learning before he ever reached Blida, was not overseas; it had an annex in every French industrial city, and the psychiatry that would decode it had to be built where the two worlds met.

## Fanon the psychiatrist: sociogeny and the clinic

Fanon's politics cannot be detached from his profession, because the profession supplied the method. Where a conventional Marxist begins from production and asks how consciousness follows, the clinician Fanon began from damaged minds and asked what had damaged them, and the two enquiries converged on the same object from opposite directions. His first book already contains the methodological coinage that announces the convergence. Alongside phylogeny and ontogeny, the development of the species and of the individual, he placed sociogeny: the proposition that a large class of disorders in the colonised patient originates neither in biology nor in private biography but in the social world, in an economic and racial order that reaches into the patient's self-relation and installs itself there. The disorders he treated were, in his later phrase for the Algerian situation, the direct product of the colonial situation, and a therapy that ignored the situation would be a therapy that mopped the floor while the tap ran.

### What is sociogeny?

Fanon's term for the social causation of psychological damage: beside the development of the species and the biography of the individual, the clinician must reckon with the society that surrounds the patient, because under colonialism the racial and economic order enters the mind itself and produces the inferiority, self-division, and neurosis the consulting room then records.

The coinage did quiet but decisive work. Classical psychoanalysis, as Fanon received it, treated the family drama as the theatre of neurosis and the wider society as scenery. Sociogeny reversed the lighting. If an Algerian patient's delusions were populated by French soldiers, if a Martinican schoolgirl's dreams were structured by the wish to be white, the content was not private symbolism awaiting decoding; it was the colony, reproduced in the one place the coloniser could not patrol. This is why Fanon's clinical writing is never merely clinical. The case study, in his hands, becomes evidence about a social order, gathered with a clinician's discipline and a clinician's respect for the particular patient, and his political writing conversely retains a diagnostician's interest in symptoms, in what a society's professed beliefs fail to explain about its behaviour.

At Blida the same conviction produced his practical reforms, and their instructive failure. The therapeutic community he imported assumed that patients would recover agency by participating in the institution's social life; with the European and Jewish women of one service the method worked tolerably, while with Algerian Muslim men it collapsed, because the rituals of sociability on offer, the café evening, the newspaper committee, the fête, belonged to the coloniser's culture and asked patients to rehearse their own assimilation as the price of recovery. Fanon and Azoulay published the failure and its analysis, concluding that a social therapy has to begin from the patient's own cultural world or it becomes one more colonial demand dressed in therapeutic language. Few psychiatrists of any period have printed so candid an account of their own method's cultural limits, and the paper anticipates by decades the arguments of transcultural psychiatry.

### How did Fanon reform the ward at Blida?

On the Saint-Alban model: locked doors opened, straitjackets and isolation reduced, patients given meetings, a newspaper, films, work, and football, and staff required to treat the hospital's own social order as part of the prescription; where the imported forms of sociability failed his Algerian patients, he said so in print and traced the failure to culture.

The Tunis years completed the clinical education. At the Charles-Nicolle day hospital he treated patients who returned home each evening, an arrangement that kept families inside the therapy and suited a society in which the asylum carried deep stigma; the experiment drew visiting psychiatrists from Europe who came to see a model they associated with far richer health systems. Simultaneously he was receiving the war's casualties in both directions, Algerian victims of torture and French soldiers who had tortured, and his case notes grew into the final chapter of The Wretched of the Earth. Those notes repay attention for their method: each case is presented with the patient's history, the presenting symptoms, and Fanon's refusal to separate the disorder from the war that organised it, so that a police inspector who can no longer sleep and beats his wife is diagnosed inside the same system as the fighter who hears the voices of the family he failed to save. The clinic had become, by the end, the place where the colonial order confessed what it was doing to everyone it touched.

The day hospital experiment had an afterlife of its own that completes the clinical record. Visiting psychiatrists from Europe reported on the Tunis service as a model of what a poor health system could achieve by redesigning the institution rather than enlarging it, and the model's logic, treat the patient inside his family's life rather than in exile from it, anticipated by years the community psychiatry that richer systems later adopted as reform. Fanon's part in it is the practical corollary of everything the texts argue: the institution is the treatment, the social world is the clinic's true territory, and a psychiatrist who wants different patients must build a different world in the one ward, the one service, the one city within his reach, while working, as Fanon did, for the larger world whose building was not within his reach alone.

A persistent misreading separates the psychiatrist from the revolutionary, as though Fanon practised a humane profession by day and preached violence by night. The texts refuse the separation at every point. The man who wrote that the colonised patient's disorders were the colony's product also wrote that decolonisation was the precondition of a psychiatry worthy of the name in Algeria, and he meant the two statements as one: no rearrangement of the ward could cure what the arrangement of the country kept causing. His final book's architecture embodies the claim, opening with the politics of violence and closing with clinical case files, as if to insist that the argument and the evidence are the same material viewed at different distances.

His clinical publications of the Algerian years show the method being built in public, paper by paper. With Charles Geronimi he examined the Thematic Apperception Test, the projective instrument that asks patients to narrate stories from standard pictures, and demonstrated that the pictures' world of European interiors, gestures, and family arrangements was illegible to the Algerian Muslim women it was used on, so that the test measured not the patient's imagination but her distance from the examiner's culture, and returned the distance as pathology. The paper is a small classic of its kind, an early and rigorous statement of the cultural loading of psychological instruments, and its argument is the sociogenic principle at bench level: change the society inside the instrument and the diagnosis changes with it. Papers on the day hospital method, on the social therapy experiment's failure and redesign, and on the psychiatric consequences of the war accumulated alongside, most of them destined for the posthumous collections where specialists now read them. The cumulative picture is of a clinician who was rebuilding his discipline's tools one by one for a world they had not been designed for, at the same time as he was rebuilding, in the political texts, the categories of the tradition that claimed to explain that world; the two rebuildings are one project, and the project's name, in both registers, is the refusal to mistake the instrument's limits for the patient's nature.

## Black Skin, White Masks: the psychology of colonial racism

The book of 1952 is a young man's book, written between his twenty-fourth and twenty-seventh years, and it remains the most original thing he wrote, the work in which the psychiatrist's ear and the philosopher's training fuse into a method nobody had used before. Its stated object is the psychology of the Black man in a white world, and more precisely of the Antillean, the French speaking Caribbean islander educated to believe himself French and discovering, at every border of that belief, that he is regarded as something else. Fanon called the book a clinical study, and its chapters move through language, sexuality, a colonial psychologist's theory, lived experience, psychopathology, and recognition, drawing on case material, literature, cinema, and his own remembered humiliations, in a prose that alternates analysis with passages of controlled fury. Nothing in the Marxist literature of the period looks like it, because nothing in that literature had attempted a phenomenology of racism from inside the racialised body.

The book opens, before its arguments begin, by naming the territory they will occupy: a zone of nonbeing, as the introduction calls it, an arid and sterile region where the colonised are held below the threshold of recognised humanity, from which an authentic upheaval can be born. The phrase is the first book's whole programme in an image. The Black man of the colonial world does not merely occupy a low position inside the human order, as the poor man does inside the class order; he is placed outside the order's census altogether, in a zone where his suffering does not register as a claim and his death does not register as a loss, and the psychology the book goes on to anatomise is the psychology of a being struggling to exist in the eyes of a world that has defined existence in terms that exclude him. This is why the book's demands escalate so quickly beyond reform: a man in a low position can ask for a higher one, but a man in the zone of nonbeing must first compel the world's recognition that there is a man there at all, and every subsequent Fanonian theme, the violence that forces recognition, the culture that manufactures a people, the land that guarantees dignity, is a strategy for exiting the zone. The Marxism he inherited had a category for the proletarian, the man whose labour is stolen; it had no category for the man whose being is refused, and the stretching can be redescribed, from this angle, as the construction of that missing category out of clinical and phenomenological materials.

Language comes first, and the analysis sets the pattern. To speak a language, Fanon argues, is to assume a world and a culture; the Antillean who perfects his French, who polices his Creole out of his own mouth and corrects it in his children's, is not merely acquiring a skill but performing a claim to whiteness, since in the colony's scale of value the mastery of the coloniser's tongue is the nearest available approach to the coloniser's status. The claim is always refused at the moment it is made most fluently, because the white listener hears the performance as performance. Fanon is pitiless about the trap's construction: the colonised are ordered to become French and then mocked for the effort, credited with an imitation that confirms the original's superiority. His own education had run exactly this course, and the chapter's authority is the authority of a man describing the inside of a machine he had personally inhabited.

The language analysis has a second movement that the summaries drop and the seminars should not. The Antillean who arrives in France speaking the flawless French he was promised would make him French discovers that his white interlocutors answer him in a broken pidgin reserved for Black speakers, a simplified baby talk of dropped articles and infinitives whose message is that his fluency will not be received, that the colour of the speaker outranks the grammar of the speech. Fanon lingers on the device because it concentrates the whole colonial theory of language into one social reflex: speech is not a neutral instrument whose mastery equalises, it is a border post at which the speaker's papers are inspected and stamped by race before content is heard. The colonised intellectual's famous linguistic insecurity, his overcorrection, his dread of the misplaced liaison, is the border post internalised, the epidermal schema applied to the tongue. A Marxism of the metropole, where class accents mark a speaker within a shared presumption of belonging, has no exact equivalent for this, since the Antillean's problem is not that he speaks from the wrong class position within the nation but that his speech is heard as evidence about his species; the stretching is required in sociolinguistics as surely as in economics, and Fanon performs it there first because language was where the refusal reached him earliest.

From language the book turns to the intimate life, examining relationships between Black men, white women, women of colour, and white men in chapters that remain among its most contested, since Fanon reads desire itself as colonised, shaped by the wish for recognition and whitening, in terms that later critics have found both penetrating about racism and limited about gender. The chapters matter here for their method rather than their conclusions: again and again Fanon refuses to treat a private anguish as private, tracing it back to the structure that assigned the partners their values before they met. A woman of colour who longs for a white partner is not, in his reading, exhibiting a personal pathology; she is responding rationally to a world that has made whiteness the currency of worth, and the pathology lies in the currency.

### What did Fanon reject in Mannoni's account of the colonised mind?

Octave Mannoni had explained colonisation by a dependency complex supposedly native to the colonised, who in his account desired subordination and met the European as the father they were waiting for; Fanon answered that the psychology Mannoni described was produced by the colonial situation itself, and that a psychology of the colonised written without the colony's economics was an evasion.

The demolition of Mannoni's Prospero and Caliban, published in 1950 after its author's years in Madagascar, occupies the book's fourth chapter and is the point at which Fanon's Marxism visibly enters his psychology. Mannoni, a colonial administrator turned ethnopsychoanalyst, had interpreted the Malagasy through a psychology of dependence and the European through a complementary Prospero complex of superiority, treating the colonial encounter as the meeting of two preformed temperaments. Fanon's reply is the sociogenic principle in action. Whatever dependency exists among the colonised was installed by conquest, land seizure, and the destruction of indigenous institutions, not discovered in the native soul; a rising in Madagascar was answered with a repression that killed on a scale the book describes qualitatively, since the figures remain contested, and to explain the survivors' psychology without mentioning the repression is to write the history of a wound while omitting the weapon. The argument generalises far beyond its target: any psychology of an oppressed group that begins after the oppression, and naturalises its effects, is ideology in clinical dress. It is the same charge he laid against the Algiers school's doctrine of North African primitivism, and it is the charge his own clinical writing is organised to escape.

Then comes the fifth chapter, the one on which the book's fame rests, and the one English readers have been given under the wrong name. The French title, L'expérience vécue du Noir, names the lived experience of the Black man: an account, from within, of what it is to discover oneself as an object in the eyes of others. Fanon narrates the discovery through scenes, a train compartment, a cold day, a child on a platform whose frightened exclamation, containing a racial epithet the original prints and this profile will not reproduce, fixes him in public as a thing to be pointed at. What follows is the book's theoretical core. The body schema, the tacit sense of one's own physical presence that phenomenology had described, collapses under the white gaze and is replaced by what he calls a historico-racial schema, woven by the other out of a thousand details, anecdotes, and stories, and beneath that an epidermal racial schema, in which the skin itself becomes the prison of meaning. He writes of being sealed into a crushing objecthood, of discovering himself responsible, in the stranger's eye, for his race and his ancestors and every crime the folklore of racism had catalogued.

### What does epidermalisation mean in Fanon's psychology?

His word for the process by which inferiority, first imposed economically and historically, is taken into the body and fixed at the level of the skin, so that the colonised person experiences his own blackness as the visible proof of the verdict

the colonial world has passed on him; inferiority is no longer argued with, because it appears to be worn.

The chapter's famous opening sentence sets its register, and it can stand here as Fanon's single quotation from this book: "I came into the world imbued with the will to find a meaning in things". The will is defeated, provisionally, by the discovery that meaning has been assigned in advance by others. Out of that defeat Fanon builds the book's most durable analytic gains. He revises the Hegelian dialectic of recognition for the colonial case: the master in the colony, unlike Hegel's, does not seek recognition from the slave but laughs at his consciousness, since the colonial economy wants work, not acknowledgment, and so the route to selfhood that runs through the master's recognition is blocked by design. He tests the psychoanalytic instruments of his training, Adler and Freud and Jung, against the Antillean material and finds them provincial, calibrated to European family structures. He confronts Negritude, the movement of Césaire and Senghor that answered racism by exalting Black civilisation, and his treatment is grateful and anguished at once: grateful for the affirmation, anguished because Sartre, in the essay Black Orpheus, had welcomed Negritude as a necessary but transitional moment, an anti-racist racism destined to be surpassed, and Fanon felt the ground of his recovered identity dissolved by the compliment. A white philosopher's dialectic had once again scheduled the Black man's experience as a phase.

### How did a mistranslated chapter title change the chapter's meaning?

The French announces a lived experience, something undergone by a subject and available to phenomenological description; Charles Lam Markmann's English rendering of 1967 announced instead the fact of blackness, something objective and given, and generations of English readers were handed an assertion

about what blackness is where Fanon had written an account of what racism does to a man's experience of himself.

The correction arrived with Richard Philcox's translation of 2008, which restored the chapter's title as The Lived Experience of the Black Man, and the episode deserves its place in this profile for more than antiquarian reasons. It is the first instance of a pattern that governs Fanon's whole reception, and that this series has met before in other vocabularies: what a thinker is taken to have said is decided partly by renderings and framings he did not choose. A reader who meets the fifth chapter as a statement of fact will read it as doctrine, and will accordingly find its author dogmatic; a reader who meets it as lived experience will read it as testimony disciplined by analysis, which is what the text is. The mistranslation also flattened the chapter's contribution to philosophy, since the claim to be doing phenomenology, describing the structures of an experience from inside it, is carried by the title itself. Fanon's English audience was thus deprived, for four decades, of the signpost that tells them how to take the most personal and the most theoretical pages he ever wrote.

The first book closes with a chapter on recognition and a short conclusion that together disclose the moral centre of everything Fanon wrote afterward. The recognition chapter works through Hegel's parable of master and slave under colonial conditions and finds the parable broken at its hinge: recognition, to do its work, must be risked for and reciprocated, but the colonial master seeks labour and deference, not the acknowledgment of the colonised, and the colonised who turns toward the master for confirmation of his humanity is asking a structure designed to refuse him. Liberation therefore cannot be received as a grant; a freedom given by decree, Fanon argues in a passage aimed at emancipation proclamations as much as at Hegel, leaves the recipient's inner posture untouched, still oriented to the giver, still waiting. The self must be seized in action or it remains a tenancy. Readers who know the later book will recognise the seed of its argument about struggle here, stated in the vocabulary of recognition rather than of arms: what the colonised lack is not permission but the experience of their own agency, and no benefactor can donate an experience.

The conclusion then turns the whole analysis toward its author and its reader in a handful of pages that have been quarried ever since. Fanon refuses, in turn, the two consolations on offer: the European humanism whose universal subject had somehow never included him, and the retrospective glory of a recovered past, which he declines to inhabit as a costume. What he asks for instead, in the book's closing prayer addressed to his own body, is to be made always a man who questions, a formulation this profile paraphrases rather than quotes because its force survives summary: the colonised intellectual's final discipline is not a doctrine but a posture, an enforced openness toward a future that neither the coloniser's categories nor the nationalist's nostalgia is permitted to close in advance. It is an extraordinary ending for a book that began in a clinic, and it explains the hold the first book exercises over readers who care nothing for the politics of the second: the diagnosis is of a condition, the prescription is a way of standing in the world while the condition is fought.

## The Manichean world

Running beneath both famous books is a single image of the colonial order that Fanon returned to so often it amounts to his social ontology: the colonial world is a world cut in two. He calls it Manichean, borrowing the name of the ancient dualist religion whose universe was divided between light and darkness, and the borrowing is exact, because the division he describes is moral and metaphysical in form before it is economic in content. The settler and the native are not rich man and poor man within one moral universe; they are, in the colony's operative ideology, two species, one of which has been pronounced good, rational, and human by definition, and the other pronounced, by the same definition, the negation of those things. The settler, in Fanon's pitiless summary of the arrangement, makes and constantly recalls the native's status as native; the native is not a citizen who happens to be poor but a category of being whose poverty is the outward sign of an inward sentence.

The division is built into space with a literalness that his description made famous. The settler's town is a town of stone and iron, of light and asphalt, its streets cleaned and its refuse collected, a satiated town that belongs, in the ideology's own terms, to the white man and to God. The native town, the medina, the shantytown, is its arranged opposite: a place without spaciousness, men crouching one against another, a town on its knees, hungry not for bread alone but for everything the other town displays and withholds. Between the two towns there is no common measure and no neutral ground; the frontier between them is marked, as he writes, by the barracks and the police station, and the agents who cross it, the policeman and the soldier, are the colony's true go-betweens, the counsellors and spokesmen of the settler's world in the native's streets. Their language is not the language of administration, of files and procedures, but what he calls the language of pure force, and it is spoken daily, in raids, in fines, in the casual destruction of a market stall or a family's stores, so that the abstract proposition that colonialism rests on violence is, for the town's inhabitants, a timetable.

Language does the same work at the level of description. The settler's vocabulary for the native, as Fanon catalogues it, is drawn from zoology and from the imagery of swarming, breeding, and infestation: the colonised are spoken of as a teeming mass, a fauna, a biological fact to be managed, in terms this profile characterises rather than reproduces, because Fanon's point is precisely that such speech is not description but manufacture. To call a man an animal often enough, in law, in the press, in the schoolroom, is to prepare the moral conditions under which his treatment as one requires no justification, and Fanon's analysis of the vocabulary is among the earliest accounts of dehumanisation as an administrative technique rather than a private prejudice. The colonised, for their part, internalise the division and dream in its terms: the dreams Fanon recorded in his patients, of running, of leaping, of muscular release, are the dreams of a body the Manichean world has sentenced to stillness, and his politics can be read as the attempt to convert that nocturnal motion into a daytime one.

The Manichean image does heavy lifting inside the larger argument of this profile, and its connection to the stretching should be made explicit. A world divided into two species cannot be analysed as a world divided into two classes without remainder, because the division is not, in the first instance, about who owns what; it is about who counts as human, enforced by who owns everything. That is why race cannot be filed as the superstructure's decoration in the colony: the racial sentence is the constitution under which property, wages, law, and schooling are then distributed. It is also why Fanon's later insistence that decolonisation is the substitution of one species of men for another is not a flourish but a definition drawn from the image: what has to be abolished is not a government but a zoology.

## Stretching Marxist analysis for the colony

The formula appears in the opening chapter of Les Damnés de la terre, in a passage where Fanon pauses the polemic to address his method directly. Marxist analysis, he writes, "should always be slightly stretched" when the colonial problem is the object, and the modesty of the adverb has misled inattentive readers into expecting a modest claim. What follows the formula is not modest at all. It is a redescription of the colonial social order in which the standard architecture of Marxist explanation, economic base below, ideological superstructure above, determination running upward, is declared unfit for the terrain, and it deserves to be unpacked piece by piece, because nearly every dispute about whether Fanon was a Marxist is a dispute about this passage and its consequences.

### What does stretching Marxist analysis mean?

Fanon's phrase for modifying Marxist categories for the colony. In the metropole economic position explains social position; in the colony race and class are fused, so that one is rich because one is white and white because one is rich, and determination runs in both directions.

Begin with the metropolitan model as Fanon understood it, the model of the industrial capitalist societies Marx had analysed. There, a person's place in the relations of production, owner of capital or seller of labour power, settler of nothing but his own hands, determines his income, his security, his neighbourhood, his politics in the rough, and race enters the analysis as one of the ideologies through which that order legitimises and divides itself. The economist's map and the sociologist's map roughly coincide: the factory owner's world and the factory hand's world are different worlds, and the boundary between them is drawn by property. Fanon did not dispute the map for the terrain it was drawn from. He disputed its export. The colony, he argued, is not arranged like that, and the traveller who arrives with the metropolitan map will misread every street.

In the colony the governing division is drawn first in racial terms, and the economic facts hang from it. The settler's town and the native town, in his famous description, are not two quarters of one city but two zones opposed in kind: the settler's sector built in stone and steel, lit and paved and fed, the native sector a place of ill fame, hungry for bread and meat and coal and light, crouching and on its knees. Between these zones stands not a labour market but a frontier, marked by barracks and police stations, and the intermediary who moves across it is not the employer but the soldier and the gendarme. Fanon's economic point is embedded in the geography. The settler does not encounter the native primarily as a worker whose labour he buys; he encounters him as a member of the race that has been allotted poverty, in a distribution enforced by the state before any contract is signed. Hence the compressed formula in which the whole analysis sits: a man is rich because he is white, and white because he is rich, the Fanonian sentence that the snippet answer above carries in its own wording. Wealth and whiteness certify each other in a circle, and the circle is the colonial economy's constitution.

### Why do race and class fuse in the colonial situation?

Because conquest assigned economic position by race in advance: land, law, wages, and education were distributed along the racial line by state violence, so the man who is poor is poor as a native and the man who is rich is rich as a settler,

and neither fact can be stated in purely economic or purely racial terms without losing the mechanism that produced both.

The theoretical consequence is the one the formula announces. In the metropolitan model, what Marxists call the superstructure, law, ideology, racial contempt, is explained by the economic structure beneath it; determination runs one way, however many refinements are added. In the colony, Fanon writes, the economic substructure is also a superstructure: the racial hierarchy is not the decorative ideology of an economic arrangement but is itself load bearing, an organising principle of production and distribution in its own right. The cause is the consequence. This is why the stretching is necessary rather than decorative. A Marxism that treats colonial racism as a mystification laid over class relations will predict that class organisation dissolves racial division as workers discover their common interest; the colonial record, in which the European working class of the settler colonies repeatedly defended its racial privileges against both the colonised and the metropolitan left, refutes the prediction in case after case. The full treatment of that record, and of the racial capitalism literature it generated, belongs to the series article on [black marxism and racial capitalism](/2017/03/15/black-marxism-racial-capitalism/); Fanon's priority in stating the fusion thesis, and his psychiatric evidence for it, are what concern this profile.

The second departure follows from the first. If race and class are fused, then the inventory of classes itself changes shape. The industrial proletariat of the colony, the class the metropolitan model promotes to universal subject, is in Fanon's account a small and comparatively protected stratum: the workers of the ports, the railways, the mines and the administration, unionised, better paid than the mass of the colonised, and integrated into the colonial economy at its most modern points. Around them stretches the real colonial majority: the peasantry of the interior, dispossessed of land by degrees and governed through chiefs the administration has made its own; and in the towns, the swollen population of the dispossessed, the lumpenproletariat of the shantytowns, men and women expelled from the countryside who have found no stable place in the urban economy and who live by expedients at the edge of legality. A theory that waits for the colonial proletariat to lead will wait, Fanon thought, for the most conservative element of the colonised population to discover a revolutionary vocation its material position does not supply.

His third departure concerns the party and the nation. In the colony, the nationalist parties are urban creations, led by intellectuals and traders whose horizon is a negotiated transfer of power, and their politics is correspondingly a politics of petition, election, and legal pressure addressed to the colonial power. Fanon had watched this politics in Tunis and in the conferences of the period, and his suspicion of it hardens across the final book into the prediction examined later in this profile. For the stretching argument, the relevant point is analytical: because the colonial state is a racial state, the struggle against it cannot be decomposed into an economic struggle and a political struggle conducted by different organisations at different speeds. Liberation is one movement or it is a rearrangement of the furniture, and the national framework, dismissed by doctrinaire Marxists as a bourgeois mystification, becomes for Fanon the necessary form in which the fused struggle appears. He is explicit that this is a modification of Marxism and not its abandonment: the analysis of exploitation, the attention to who labours and who appropriates, the insistence that consciousness follows material life, all survive the journey. What does not survive is the metropolitan table of categories, applied neat.

### What does the stretching leave unchanged in Marxism?

The core of the method survives the journey intact: the insistence that material life explains consciousness rather than the reverse, the analysis of who works and who takes the product, and the refusal to treat ideas as self-moving;

what Fanon discards is the metropolitan inventory of classes and the one-way direction of determination, not the engine of the theory itself.

It is worth pausing on the word he chose, because the whole question of Fanon's Marxism is encoded in it. He did not say replaced, corrected, or refuted. A stretched garment is the same garment under tension, extended to cover a body it was not tailored for, and at risk, if the tension is too great, of tearing. Fanon's prose performs the tension deliberately. He retains the vocabulary of class, exploitation, and alienation while bending each term around the colonial evidence, and he keeps the Marxist confidence that the order he describes will generate its own gravediggers, while relocating those gravediggers from the factory floor to the countryside and the shantytown. Critics who call him a heretic and defenders who call him orthodox are both responding to something real in the text; the formula licenses both readings because it asserts continuity and rupture in a single breath. The sections that follow take the two most consequential products of the stretching in turn: the reassignment of the revolutionary agent, and the analysis of the state that independence would inherit.

The settler working class is the stretching's hardest exhibit, and it is worth dwelling on because it converts the fusion thesis from a slogan into a mechanism. In the metropole, the worker's racism, where it exists, can be analysed as a division sown among men whose interests are objectively common, and the analysis carries a prediction: organisation and struggle will wear the division down. In the colony, the European worker's position is not a prejudice laid over an interest; it is an interest. His wage is set by a racial scale, his job is reserved by a racial boundary, his housing, his school, his hospital, and his pension are dividends of the colonial arrangement, and the colonised worker whose cheapness guarantees the arrangement is, from inside that arrangement, correctly perceived as a threat to every one of those dividends. To ask this worker to dissolve the racial line in the name of class is to ask him to volunteer for the abolition of his own standard of life, and Fanon's cold observation is that men rarely do, that the settler unions' defence of French Algeria was not a failure of education but a success of arithmetic. The orthodox tradition had a word for this phenomenon, the labour aristocracy, and had filed it as a metropolitan complication affecting a thin stratum; the colony generalises it, in Fanon's account, to an entire racial caste, which is why the stretching reaches the agent question with its verdict already formed. Determination runs both ways because interest itself has been racialised: the economy explains the racism and the racism organises the economy, in a single circuit that no analysis entering at only one point can describe.

## The revolutionary agent: peasantry and the urban dispossessed

From the fusion of race and class, Fanon drew the conclusion for which orthodox Marxism has never forgiven him. The revolutionary class in the colony, he argued, is the peasantry, and beside it the urban dispossessed: the landless and the hungry, those with nothing to lose by the system's fall, are in his account the revolution's true material. The peasantry alone is revolutionary, he wrote, outside the towns, because it has nothing to lose and everything to gain; the dispossessed of the shantytowns, the men without work or papers who crowd the colonial city, constitute the horde from which a liberation movement recruits its shock troops, and their spontaneity, their readiness to rise without instruction, is the raw energy of the colonial revolution. The industrial working class, by contrast, appears in his pages as the fraction of the colonised population most nearly bought by the colonial order, its unions defending sectional gains inside that order, its parties committed to legalism. The inversion of the Marxist hierarchy of agents is complete and deliberate.

The argument had an Algerian ground that should not be forgotten when it is judged as theory. The insurrection of 1954 had begun in the countryside, in the Aurès mountains, and the maquis that sustained it drew its fighters overwhelmingly from the rural poor, while the cities were policed into a managed quiet and the established nationalist organisations hesitated, negotiated, and were outflanked by the new front. Fanon had treated the maquisards, hidden them, trained their nurses; his sociology of the revolution was written from inside a revolution that seemed, at the moment of writing, to be confirming it. The colonial working class of Algiers and Oran, moreover, included the European workers of the settler population, whose unions had mobilised in defence of French Algeria with a fervour no Marxist theory of proletarian internationalism could comfortably digest, and the Algerian workers organised in French unions had experienced those unions' metropolitan leaderships as obstacles at every crisis. Fanon's portrait of the colonial proletariat is a polemic, but it is a polemic with exhibits.

The boldest single move inside the agent thesis is the rehabilitation of the lumpenproletariat, and it deserves its own paragraph because it shows the stretching at maximum tension. In Marx's own writings the lumpenproletariat, the stratum of the ruined, the criminal, and the casually employed that hangs beneath the working class, is the refuse of the revolutionary account: politically inert at best, purchasable at worst, the reserve of reaction, famously deployed as the praetorian guard of Bonapartism. Fanon takes the category into the colony and reverses its sign. The shantytown population of the colonial city, uprooted peasants, porters, shoeblacks, servants dismissed and never rehired, men whose relation to the colonial economy is exclusion itself, are in his account among the most radically combustible of the colonised, precisely because the system has given them no stake to defend and no legal existence to protect; they are, he writes, one of the most spontaneous and radically revolutionary forces of the colonised people. The reversal is not arbitrary. Marx's lumpen were the debris of a society whose productive classes were the story; Fanon's are the product of a society whose productive process is exclusion, and a category that tracks debris in the first setting tracks the central human product in the second. He is not naive about the stratum's volatility: the same pages warn that the colonial power recruits its informers, provocateurs, and hired gangs from exactly this population, and that the movement which fails to win the dispossessed will meet them in the enemy's pay. The category is thus double edged in his own text, an energy that will serve whichever side organises it first, and the warning half of the analysis has worn as well as the hopeful half: the urban dispossessed of the postcolonial city have been the infantry of movements of every description, including several that Fanon would have recognised as the brokerage's militias.

### Why did Fanon distrust the colonial working class as a revolutionary agent?

Because in the colony that class was small, comparatively well paid, unionised inside the colonial economy's modern enclaves, and often, in its European settler form, an active defender of the racial order; its material interest pointed toward a better bargain within colonialism,

whereas the landless peasantry and the urban dispossessed had been given nothing by the system that they could be bribed to keep.

The countryside had its own intermediary stratum, and Fanon's account of it completes the agent thesis's sociology. Between the colonial administration and the fellahin stood the caids and appointed chiefs, the rural notables through whom taxes, conscription, and surveillance reached the village, men whose authority was the administration's manufacture and whose persons concentrated the peasantry's hatred of the system in a local and reachable form. The first violence of the rural rising was habitually directed at this stratum and its symbols, the chief's house, the tax records, the forest guard, before it ever met a European soldier, a sequence the metropolitan press reported as tribal savagery and Fanon read as political literacy: the peasant struck the system at the joint nearest his own life, the point where the abstraction called colonialism kept its local address. The observation matters to the theory because it shows the peasantry's consciousness arriving already structured by the colonial economy's own organisation, the intermediary system having taught the village, by daily tuition, exactly where power lived and what it was for. Spontaneity, in his account, is never innocence; it is the explosion of a knowledge the system itself had been obliged to distribute.

The distrust extended to the parties that claimed to speak for the workers and the nation alike. Fanon's second chapter, on spontaneity, is in large part an anatomy of the mutual suspicion between the nationalist parties and the rural masses: the parties, urban and legalistic, regard the peasantry as backward and dangerous; the peasantry regards the parties as talkers who arrive from the town to collect taxes of loyalty and deliver speeches. His own sympathies are declared in the chapter's balance: spontaneity is the revolution's strength, its uprisings the moments when the colonised discover their power, and also its weakness, since a rising without programme, coordination, or political education will be crushed or will exhaust itself in local scores. The remedy he proposes is not the party's tutelage imposed from above but political education carried into the struggle itself, commissars and cadres who learn from the masses while they teach. Whether that remedy is a synthesis or a wish is one of the standing questions of Fanon scholarship.

The strongest objection to the peasantry thesis must now be stated at full strength, as the brief for this profile requires, and attributed. It should first be sharpened by the Algerian record itself, which contains the objection's best internal exhibit: the revolution's most famous urban episode, the network that fought the Battle of Algiers, was a work of meticulous urban organisation, cells, couriers, safe houses, and discipline, and the general strike that accompanied it was a working city's action, coordinated to the hour. The maquis of the interior, for all their heroism, survived where supply, intelligence, and political organisation reached them from the towns and the borders, and the war was ended in the end by negotiation between governments, conducted by the movement's most organised and least spontaneous elements. Fanon's own movement, in its own practice, thus kept refuting the pure form of his thesis even as its rural rank and file kept confirming the thesis's sociology, and the tension is present in his text for any reader who watches his examples as carefully as his conclusions. In New Theories of Revolution, published in 1972, the British communist writer Jack Woddis subjected Fanon's class analysis to a systematic Marxist critique, in chapters whose headings announce the charges: the peasants as a revolutionary class, and Fanon's rejection of the working class. Woddis argued that the peasantry, dispersed, property dreaming, and divided by locality and kin, cannot generate a socialist politics from its own condition; that the anti-colonial revolutions which succeeded did so under the leadership of disciplined parties staffed by urban intellectuals and sustained, where they prevailed, by organised military force, in several cases with decisive urban working class participation; and that Fanon's inversion therefore mistook the social composition of the insurgent rank and file for the agency that made insurgency into revolution. The objection generalises beyond one book. The historical record of the period's liberation struggles shows again and again the pattern Woddis describes: rural grievance in abundance, rural rising in plenty, and victory only where an organisation existed that could coordinate, supply, negotiate, and govern. Spontaneity lit the fires; parties and armies decided what the fires built.

### What did Woddis object to in Fanon's account of the peasantry?

Writing as a Marxist in New Theories of Revolution, Jack Woddis argued that Fanon had mistaken the most oppressed class for the most revolutionary one: the peasantry in his critique is too scattered and too attached to land and locality to lead,

and the revolutions Fanon generalised from were in fact made by organised parties, urban intellectuals, and disciplined armed forces.

The answer available to Fanon's text is partial, and this profile is required to say plainly which part of the objection stands. What the thesis correctly identifies is the colonial working class's position. In the settler colonies the organised working class was indeed small, comparatively privileged, and, in its European sections, a pillar of the colonial order rather than its gravedigger; any strategy that assigned it the leading role Marxist theory reserved for it would have been fantasy, and the parties that tried to build on it in Algeria were marginal to the revolution that occurred. Fanon's sociology of the colonial city, the shantytowns filling with the expelled while the unionised enclaves defended their differentials, remains a penetrating description, and the practice of the period's movements is examined comparatively in the series article on [anti colonial marxism case studies](/2017/05/01/anti-colonial-marxism-case-studies/). What has not been borne out is the positive claim. The peasantry and the dispossessed did not, anywhere, make a revolution by their own spontaneous motion and hold its fruits; where liberation was won, it was won by the organised forms Fanon treated with suspicion, and the states those forms built then illustrated the pathologies his own later chapters predicted. The most defensible reading, and the one this profile adopts, is that the stretching thesis survives as diagnosis and fails as assignment: Fanon located the colonial order's enemies correctly, and misjudged the instrument by which they could win.

## The five chapters assessed

Les Damnés de la terre appeared from François Maspero's Paris house in the autumn of 1961, weeks before its author's death, and its title, taken from the opening of the Internationale in its French wording, announces a book addressed to the wretched of the whole earth rather than to Algeria alone. It is not a treatise composed at leisure. Fanon dictated much of it in a race against his illness, assembled some of it from speeches and articles prepared for the movement's conferences, and arranged it as a descent: from the violence that founds the colonial order, through the forces that rise against it and the state they risk building, to the culture a nation makes in struggle, and finally to the psychiatric ward where the war's damage is itemised in case files. To assess the book as a whole, as its reputation invites, is to miss that its five chapters have had five different fates. One made him infamous, one made him a prophet, one made him a classic of cultural theory, one was ignored for a generation, and one, the first, is still fighting its way out from behind a preface. The five chapters assessed, in the table that follows, is this profile's findable summary of the whole argument; the prose after it takes each chapter in turn.

| Spontaneity: Its Strength and Weakness | The rural masses rise without waiting for parties, and their uprisings are the revolution's energy, but spontaneity without programme or political education is crushed or dissipates. | The least quoted of the political chapters, overshadowed by the chapters around it. | Jack Woddis argued that spontaneity cannot substitute for class organisation and party leadership. | Mixed: uprisings repeatedly began outside the parties, as he said, and outcomes were decided by organisation, as he also conceded within the chapter. |

The architecture deserves a remark before the chapters are separated, because Fanon arranged them as an argument about sequence. Violence comes first not because it is the most important thing in the book but because it is the first thing in the colony: the order the other chapters discuss was installed by conquest and is patrolled into permanence. Spontaneity follows, because the first response of the colonised arrives before any organisation does. National consciousness follows, because the question of what the struggle builds cannot be postponed until after victory without being answered by default. Culture follows, because a people fighting for a nation must decide what the nation is. And the case files close the volume because they are its evidence locker: everything asserted in the earlier chapters about the colonial world's effects on human beings is there exhibited, patient by patient, in a clinician's hand. Read in that order, the book is less a manifesto than a diagnosis with a prognosis attached, and its uneven reputation says more about its readers' appetites than about its construction.

The chapter's Algerian exhibits should be named, since Fanon wrote with them open on his desk. The established nationalist movement he inherited had spent two decades in legal and semi legal politics: parties of petition and programme, leaders exiled, imprisoned, and released in cycles, an electorate managed by fraud so routine that the phrase administrative election entered the language. When the insurrection came in November 1954 it came from a younger generation acting outside those parties, and the older formations spent the war being absorbed, outflanked, or destroyed, while the insurrection's centre of gravity settled in the countryside the parties had neglected. The spontaneity chapter is that experience theorised in real time: the parties' suspicion of the rural rising was the suspicion of organisations watching their own supersession, and the rising's suspicion of the parties was the countryside's judgment on twenty years of petitions. Fanon's even handedness in the chapter, his insistence that the rising needs the education only organisation can carry and that the organisation needs the rising's energy or it is a ministry in waiting, is the record of a man trying to hold a movement together across the fault line he was describing, and it is best read as an internal memorandum of the Algerian revolution that happened to become a classic of revolutionary theory.

### Which chapter of the final book is read least?

By the evidence of citation and commentary, the second, on spontaneity: the violence chapter is quoted, the national consciousness chapter is taught, the culture chapter is anthologised, and the clinical chapter has its specialists, while the patient analysis of how uprisings begin and why they fail

sits in the middle of the book comparatively unvisited by its admirers and its prosecutors alike.

The neglect of the spontaneity chapter is a genuine loss, because it contains Fanon's most careful political writing, the record of a man trying to hold two observations at once. The uprisings he had witnessed or studied, in the countryside of Algeria and across the colonised world, did not wait for permission: peasants rose over land, over taxes, over an official's insult, and the nationalist parties, whose leaders he respected and whose caution he understood, repeatedly found themselves following events they claimed to lead. Yet he had also seen risings burn out, massacre their neighbours, or be annihilated for want of coordination, and the chapter's second half is a sober inventory of spontaneity's ceiling: without political education, without a programme that tells the fighter what the fighting is for, the energy of the rising is spent and its survivors are herded back into the old order or into a new one built by whoever kept their organisation intact. The parties, in his prescription, must go to school with the people rather than merely conscript them, and the people must acquire from the struggle the habits of a citizenry. That this is easier to prescribe than to perform, he knew; the chapter's tension is between the revolution he saw beginning and the revolution he could already see being lost, and it points directly at the chapter that follows it.

The culture chapter's fate was the reverse: canonised early and often, sometimes by readers who skipped the politics around it. Its anatomy of the colonised intellectual remains the sharpest short account of a familiar itinerary. First comes assimilation, the phase in which the educated colonised writer proves his mastery of the coloniser's forms and is rewarded with the suspicion of both worlds. Then, stung by rejection, comes the plunge into the recovered past, the glorification of precolonial civilisation, folklore, and custom, a phase Fanon treats with respect and impatience in equal measure, since a culture dusted off for display is a museum, and a people cannot live in a museum. Last comes the fighting phase, in which the writer turns to the people in struggle and produces a literature of combat that does not describe a nation but helps to summon one. His claim that national culture is forged in the fight rather than excavated from the tomb was aimed at two targets simultaneously: the colonial anthropology that froze colonised peoples in an ethnographic present, and the nationalist nostalgia that would freeze them again in the name of authenticity. Critics have objected that the scheme prices every poem by its usefulness to the struggle and leaves no room for art that simply consoles, adorns, or plays; the objection is fair as far as it goes, and the chapter's defenders answer that Fanon was describing a wartime literature from inside a war, not legislating for peacetime aesthetics. The scheme's stages have been applied far beyond Algeria, sometimes mechanically, which Fanon, whose whole method was suspicion of imported schemas, would presumably have resisted in the particular case even as he enjoyed the compliment.

The culture chapter's doctrine had a practical career that belongs in its assessment. The liberation movements of the following decades built cultural fronts on its plan, theatre groups, literacy campaigns, and radio stations instructed to treat culture as a weapon system and the people's own forms as its ammunition, and the doctrine's strengths and limits were both exhibited at scale: strengths, in the speed with which movements could manufacture a national symbolism that the population recognised as its own; limits, in the flattening that overtook the art when the mobilisation ended and the ministry of culture inherited the front, prescribing in peace the combat literature that war had produced in freedom. Fanon's schema predicts the flattening, since a phase is defined by the struggle that generates it and cannot be continued by decree after the struggle's end, and the cultural bureaucracies of the successor states stand in his pages' logic as the brokerage's artistic department, intermediaries between the people and their own expression. The chapter's final position is thus more austere than its reputation: culture is the people's self description in motion, and any institution, colonial or national, that undertakes to describe the people on their behalf has begun the expropriation again in the aesthetic register.

### What does the last chapter of the final book contain?

Case files: a series of clinical presentations from Fanon's Algerian practice, covering fighters who broke down after killing, survivors of massacres and of torture, a police torturer whose violence followed him home, and patients whose symptoms had no story except the war itself,

each written up with a clinician's restraint and arranged so that the colonial order, not the patient's constitution, stands as the pathogen.

Those files have had the strangest afterlife of anything he wrote. The political readers of the nineteen sixties mostly skipped them, embarrassed, perhaps, to find their manifesto ending in a clinic; the psychiatric and trauma literature that grew up decades later found in them a founding document, the earliest systematic presentation of war's psychiatric casualties by a doctor who treated the tortured and the torturers in adjoining rooms and refused to call one group monsters and the other merely mad. The methodological criticism recorded in the table is real: a case series from one hospital, without comparison groups, cannot prove a general theory of colonial psychopathology, and Fanon the scientist would have known it. But the chapter's purpose is not really proof. It is a demand that the reader who has followed the argument through four chapters of theory now look at the material the theory is about: this man, this woman, this symptom, this war. A demand of that kind does not expire when its statistics are queried, and the chapter's reunion of the book with its author's day work is the strongest internal evidence against the lazy portrait of Fanon as an apostle of violence intoxicated by his theme. Apostles do not close their books with case notes about the damage.

The book's composition explains features of it that pure interpretation struggles with, and the facts belong in this assessment. Fanon assembled The Wretched of the Earth in a matter of months in 1961, dictating to Josie Fanon and to secretaries in Tunis and during his last journeys, while undergoing treatment for the leukaemia that was killing him and while continuing, until weeks before the end, the diplomatic work of the provisional government. Portions had prior lives: the argument on national culture reworked a paper he had delivered to the congress of Black writers and artists in Paris in 1956 and developed at the Rome congress of 1959, where his address on the mutual foundations of national culture and liberation struggles had announced the combat phase doctrine to the assembled intellectuals of the diaspora; other passages drew on his El Moudjahid journalism and his conference interventions across Africa. The book is therefore best pictured not as a testament composed in retreat but as the theoretical deposit of five years of movement work, clinical practice, and diplomacy, set down at dictation speed by a man who knew he would not revise it. The repetitions, the shifts of register between the sociological and the prophetic, the compression that lets a decade's argument occupy a paragraph, are the marks of that production, and readers who forgive the book its haste, as readers forgive the haste of Marx's own pamphlets, will find the forgiveness repaid. What is astonishing is not that the book is uneven but that its most considered chapter, the third, was produced under the same conditions as its most incandescent, the first: the prophecy and the polemic share a deathbed, and the deathbed explains neither, since the analysis of the national bourgeoisie had been maturing in its author since the conference corridors of the late nineteen fifties.

The book's brief conclusion deserves separate notice in any assessment, since it is the only part of the volume addressed to no enemy. Having anatomised the colony, the struggle, the successor state, and the ward, Fanon turns to the survivors of his own argument and asks what they are for. The answer refuses every available inheritance: not Europe's states and institutions, whose humanism he charges with having been built on the colonial foundation; not the recovered past, whose glories he honours and declines to reinhabit; but a new beginning worked out by the colonised and their allies together, new concepts and a new man, a humanity to be invented rather than inherited. The conclusion's brevity, a few pages against the chapters' mass, is itself a position: the future cannot be dictated at the length the diagnosis required, because a future dictated in detail would be one more colonial office memorandum. Readers who find the ending thin have usually wanted a programme; the text offers a direction and a refusal, and leaves the programme to the political education it has spent five chapters describing as the only legitimate author of programmes.

## The violence chapter on its own terms

Before the preface can be separated from the chapter, the chapter must be stated as it stands, because the separation is pointless if what remains is caricatured in the opposite direction. Fanon's first chapter argues three distinguishable things, and much confusion in the literature comes from treating them as one. The first claim is descriptive: colonial rule is violence in its natural state, a system installed by conquest and maintained by the permanent display and periodic use of force, so that the colonised live inside violence as a fish lives in water, whether or not a shot is fired in a given week. The second claim is strategic: decolonisation, defined as the substitution of one species of men for another in the government of the territory, is always a violent phenomenon, because no ruling caste in history has surrendered its position to argument, and the colonised who understand this cease to waste their politics on petition. The third claim is psychological and constitutive: for the colonised person, formed by the system into self-contempt and muscular tension and dreams of running and leaping, participation in the struggle, including its armed moments, dissolves the inferiority complex and restores him to himself as an agent; violence, in the chapter's notorious phrase as Farrington rendered it, is a cleansing force, freeing the native from despair and inaction and making him fearless.

One clarification inside the chapter is habitually lost and should be recovered before the claims are graded, because it governs the sense of the book's most violent sounding formula. The substitution of one species of men for another, which the opening pages announce as decolonisation's definition, is a substitution of orders, not of populations: what is to be abolished is the species making machine, the arrangement under which one kind of human is manufactured as settler and another as native. The individual settler is not the formula's target. Fanon is explicit in the movement's practice and consistent in the text that Europeans who abandon the settler's function and join the liberation become, by that act, citizens of the nation being born, and the FLN's ranks and services included such men and women, doctors, teachers, and couriers whose presence in the struggle the colonial press treated as treason's scandal and Fanon treated as the formula's proof. The Manichean world divides functions and structures; it is the tragedy the book anatomises, not the programme it recommends, that the division so often ran through persons, and a reading that converts the anatomy into a programme of racial replacement has mistaken the diagnosis for the prescription in the corpus's most consequential place. The point matters beyond exegesis, because the successor states' treatment of their European and minority populations was decided, in part, by which reading of this chapter the victors carried into office.

Only the third claim is properly Fanon's innovation, and it is a psychiatrist's claim before it is a strategist's. It grows directly out of the clinical material: the colonised patients whose aggression, denied any legitimate object, turned inward as depression or sideways against their neighbours in the knife fights and feuds the colonial press catalogued as native criminality. Fanon's analysis of that lateral violence is among the chapter's most penetrating passages. The colonised, he argues, discharge against each other the violence the system stores in them, in tribal quarrels, in vendettas, in the ecstatic dances and possession rituals through which a people's musculature finds the release its politics is forbidden. The struggle against the coloniser re-aims the stored force at its source, and in that re-aiming the colonised recover a selfhood the system had organised out of them. A reader may reject the psychology, but should reject the thing itself: a clinical hypothesis about aggression and selfhood under domination, continuous with the case files that close the book, and argued with a practitioner's confidence that purely textual critics rarely bring to it.

The chapter also contains, and its prosecutorial readers usually omit, an extended warning about the forms violence takes when it is not politically digested. Fanon describes the colonised intellectual's temptation to answer colonial violence with a mirror mythology of racial revenge, and he rejects it: the struggle's target is a system, and the settler who lays down his function is not the struggle's prey. He describes the lumpenproletariat's availability to the highest bidder, the colonial power's practiced use of provocateurs and purchased gangs, and the danger that armed spontaneity curdles into banditry or is rented by the enemy. He is severe about the intoxication of the first days of rising. None of this makes the chapter a pacifist text, and this profile will not pretend otherwise: Fanon held that the colonised would have to fight, that the fighting would remake them, and that squeamishness about the fact was a luxury of those whose countries were not occupied. But a text that analyses the pathologies of violence at this length is not celebrating violence; it is doing with violence what the author did with every other phenomenon of the colonial world, which is to follow it into the minds it deforms and ask what would end the deformation.

The strategic claim rests on an institutional argument that its critics rarely engage, and fairness requires it to be stated. Fanon's colonised subject faces a political order with no legal aperture: elections rigged or racially rationed, parties banned or driven underground, the press muzzled, assembly criminalised, the courts an arm of the administration, the very franchise in Algeria extended and withdrawn as an instrument of management across a century. A struggle conducted by petition inside such an order is not a peaceful alternative to violence; it is an application for permission addressed to the power that profits from refusing it, and the colonial record, in which every legal nationalist formation in Algeria was in turn co-opted, outlawed, or answered with massacre when it mobilised beyond its permit, is the argument's evidence. The claim, properly stated, is conditional: where a colonial order closes every legal road and enforces the closure by force, the colonised who mean to end it will be driven to force, and a theory that deplores the destination without examining the closures has mistaken a description for an incitement. Whether the closure was as total in every colony as in settler Algeria is an empirical question the stretching itself teaches us to ask case by case; that it was total in the case Fanon knew is not seriously disputed by the war's historians on any side.

Set beside this, the criticisms of Fanon's own argument, stated at full strength and kept separate from the preface affair, are formidable, and the brief for this profile requires them in their strongest form. Hannah Arendt's On Violence, published as an essay in 1969 and as a book in 1970, lodged the most influential philosophical objection. Violence, in Arendt's analysis, is instrumental through and through: it is a means, mute in itself, capable of destroying power but constitutionally incapable of creating it, since power in her sense is the human capacity to act in concert, which arises from people binding themselves together and evaporates under coercion. A movement that expects violence to constitute a new political subject therefore confuses categories; what violence can produce is obedience, ruin, or a vacuum, and the new man Fanon promised from the struggle would have to be made, if at all, by the acting together that violence at best clears a space for and at worst renders impossible. She added a historical observation with teeth: the glorification of violence she was answering had flourished among students and intellectuals in the metropoles, people intoxicated by a rhetoric whose costs would be paid elsewhere, and she doubted the sincerity of a cult of violence preached from safety.

Renate Zahar's study Frantz Fanon: Colonialism and Alienation, which appeared in English in 1974, pressed a different objection from inside the Marxist camp. Zahar, broadly sympathetic to Fanon's project, argued that the cathartic account of violence assigns to the armed act a pedagogical and therapeutic power it cannot bear: destroying the coloniser's world does not by itself produce socialist consciousness, political organisation, or the habits of a free citizenry, and a movement that treats the act of violence as self sufficient education will discover after victory that it has manufactured soldiers rather than citizens, a discovery several liberation movements were making as she wrote. Between Arendt's conceptual objection and Zahar's practical one, the constitutive claim of the first chapter is squeezed hard: violence may clear, avenge, and exhilarate, but the subject it constitutes is indeterminate, as likely to be the party enforcer or the warlord as the new man, and the chapter's confidence that the re-aiming of aggression is intrinsically emancipatory is the least defended of its three claims. The descriptive claim, by contrast, has passed into the common sense of the field: few historians now describe colonial rule as a basically administrative arrangement occasionally interrupted by force. The strategic claim stands between the two, true of most decolonisations that occurred against settler resistance, and refuted as a universal law by the negotiated transitions that also occurred. That is the chapter on its own terms: one claim absorbed, one claim largely borne out in its home terrain, one claim still carrying the argument. It is neither the sermon of the preface nor the scandal of the summaries.

A factual note belongs inside this assessment, because the chapter's subject was a real war and the profile's neutrality discipline requires the war to be reported whole. The Algerian war of 1954 to 1962 was conducted with extreme violence by the colonial power and with considerable violence by the movement Fanon served, and neither fact is an argument about the other. French forces institutionalised torture in interrogation, practised collective punishment against civilian populations suspected of sheltering the maquis, and killed Algerian civilians in reprisals and in the suppression of demonstrations, in Algeria and in the metropole itself; the French state spent decades declining to call the conflict a war at all. The FLN, for its part, used assassination and bombing against civilians as instruments of pressure, conducted a murderous parallel war against the rival nationalist movement of Messali Hadj, executed Algerians it judged collaborators, sometimes on evidence its own cadres later doubted, and purged its own ranks violently at several points, including the killing of its most gifted military organiser, Abane Ramdane, by his own comrades. Fanon knew the second ledger as well as the first; he worked beside the men who kept it. His chapter's argument is not that one side's violence was innocent, and it should not be recruited to that use: it is that the colonial order's founding and daily violence is the prior fact that organises all the rest, the medium in which every actor's choices were made. Readers may accept or reject the priority claim; the facts it orders are as stated, and estimates of the war's dead remain contested among historians, which is why this profile gives none.

## The preface displacement

The preface displacement is this profile's namable claim, and it is time to state it as a claim: a substantial part of what is attributed to Fanon on violence originates in a preface he did not write, and separating the two texts is a precondition of assessing his actual argument, which is psychological and strategic rather than celebratory. The preface was written by Jean-Paul Sartre, at the request, relayed through the publisher François Maspero, of Fanon himself, who had spent long days with Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir in Rome in the summer of 1961 and wanted the most famous intellectual in France to launch his book. Sartre obliged with a text of extraordinary rhetorical violence, addressed over the heads of the colonised to the European reader, whom it arraigns, taunts, and dares. In the preface, and only in the preface, the killing of a European is celebrated as a double achievement, the destruction of an oppressor and the birth of a man in a single act; in the preface, violence is figured as man re-creating himself, an almost sacramental formula; in the preface, the colonised's rage is praised in the accents of a Parisian philosopher working out, in public, his own reckoning with French guilt. Sartre's text is a great performance and a terrible introduction, because it tells the reader, before Fanon has said a word, that the book to come is an intoxication, and generations read the chapter that followed as the intoxication's source.

### Did Fanon write the famous preface to his own book?

No. The preface to the first edition of The Wretched of the Earth was written by Jean-Paul Sartre, France's most prominent philosopher, at the dying Fanon's own request; it is markedly more inflammatory than the text it introduces, and the displacement of its formulations onto Fanon is the single largest distortion in the book's reception.

The mechanics of the displacement repay attention, since they operated at several levels at once. At the level of the physical book, the preface stood first, carried the more famous name, and supplied the phrases that journalists and reviewers quoted, invariably, as the book's message; a preface is read as a key, and this key fitted a lock the chapter had not made. At the level of translation and circulation, the English edition of 1963 carried Sartre's text with it, so the Anglophone world received the compound as a unit, and the student movements of the decade, hungry for a doctrine of redemptive violence, found it ready made in the preface's cadences and cited the compound under Fanon's name alone. At the level of scholarship, the compound was then attacked as a unit, and here the documented misattribution at the centre of this profile occurred: the well known philosophical attack of 1970, Arendt's On Violence, directs its fire at the glorification of violence as the making of a new man, a doctrine whose fullest statement is Sartre's, and treats Fanon's chapter as the doctrine's source text. Arendt was too careful a reader not to glimpse the seam; she remarks that Fanon himself is more doubtful about violence than his admirers, and that only the first chapter seems to have been read by them. Yet the architecture of her polemic still assigns to Fanon the preface's exaltation, the very formulations, man re-created through violence, the redemptive killing, that Fanon never wrote, and her immense authority fixed that assignment for two generations of political theorists. A documented misattribution is not a hanging offence, and Arendt's conceptual objection, examined in the previous section, retains its force against the third of Fanon's claims; but the objection was aimed, in part, at a target wearing the wrong name, and Fanon studies has had to spend decades repossessing the chapter.

The Rome meeting itself belongs in the record, because it shows the displacement being prepared in friendship rather than in malice. In the summer of 1961 Fanon, already ill and travelling for treatment and for the movement, spent several days with Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir in Rome, in conversations that ran, by Beauvoir's later account, for hours at a stretch and ranged across the whole of his work and situation; Sartre, who had broken publicly with the French war and had seen his own apartment attacked for it, was the European intellectual Fanon most trusted, and the request for a preface was the request of a dying author for the strongest available escort for his book into a hostile literary capital. The escort arrived with its own army. None of this makes the misattribution innocent in its effects, since Fanon could not foresee, or could not afford to weigh, how completely the escort's voice would drown the author's in the reception; but it forbids the melodrama, sometimes indulged by his defenders, of a text stolen from a helpless man. He chose his sponsor with open eyes, in a war, for reasons of war, and the price was entered in a currency he was spending freely that summer: everything he had, including control of his own meaning, was being converted into the book's chances.

Two cautions complete the picture, without which the preface displacement becomes an alibi rather than an analysis. The first is that Fanon commissioned the preface. He sought Sartre out, argued with him for days in Rome, admired the older man's anti-colonial commitments, and cannot have been surprised by the register Sartre adopted, since Sartre's interventions on Algeria had been notorious for years and since the two men's conversation in Rome ranged across the whole question of violence and the new man. The displacement was not a theft committed against an unwilling author; it was a collaboration whose terms escaped the collaborator's control, as collaborations with more famous men tend to do. The second caution is the one the brief for this profile insists on: the misattribution must not be used to imply that Fanon's own argument is unobjectionable. Strip the preface away and the first chapter still asserts that violence remakes the colonised subject, still treats the armed struggle as the school's first classroom, still declines to mourn the settler's world. Arendt's power objection, Woddis's organisational objection, and Zahar's pedagogical objection all survive the separation, because they answer claims that are genuinely in the chapter. What the separation changes is the temperature and the target: debate can finally proceed about a clinical and strategic argument, instead of about a Parisian peroration fathered on a Martinican psychiatrist.

There is a further strand to the reception that belongs here, since it concerns who owns Fanon's meaning. His relationship with Sartre had a longer history than the Rome meeting, reaching back to the wound of Black Orpheus, in which Sartre had folded Negritude into a dialectic as its passing moment and left the young Fanon, by his own account in the fifth chapter of Black Skin, White Masks, feeling that a white friend had explained his experience out from under him. The author of that wound then supplied the preface that overwrote his final book. Fanon scholarship has not known whether to read the sequence as reconciliation, irony, or the colonial intellectual's structural dependence on metropolitan sponsorship: to be heard in Paris, the Martinican still needed the Parisian's signature, and the signature came with the Parisian's voice attached, louder than the text it introduced. The series article on [sartre and marxism](/2016/01/15/sartre-and-marxism/) takes up Sartre's own Marxism in full; for this profile the relationship matters as the machinery of the displacement, the channel through which another man's formulations entered Fanon's book and stayed there, wearing his name, for sixty years of quotation.

## The Algerian writings: a revolution observed from inside

Between the first book and the last stand the writings of the Algerian years, and they change the picture of Fanon that the two famous books alone would leave. A Dying Colonialism, published in 1959 under a French title that names the fifth year of the Algerian revolution, is a set of essays written from inside a society at war, and its method is the reverse of the manifesto's: where The Wretched of the Earth generalises, these essays document, following particular institutions through the revolution's transformation of them. The veil is his most famous exhibit. The garment that French colonialism had made the symbol of Algerian backwardness, and had staged public unveilings to abolish, became under the pressure of the struggle an instrument its wearers controlled: donned to pass as traditional and therefore unsuspected, discarded to pass as Europeanised and therefore invisible, its meaning reversed by the women who used it until, Fanon argues, the veil was no longer worn the way it had been worn before the revolution, whatever cloth was on the body. The essay is a small masterpiece of his method: an object of colonial fixation is shown to be a variable in a struggle, its significance assigned not by tradition or by the coloniser's fantasy but by the practice of the colonised themselves.

The companion essays work the same transformation across Algerian life. The radio, before the revolution a colonial voice in a box that Algerian households shunned or endured, became with the clandestine broadcasts of the Voice of Fighting Algeria an instrument the whole society reorganised itself to hear: sets were bought, batteries hoarded, listening became a collective act, and a population the coloniser had addressed as subjects began to experience itself, through a technology it now owned, as a nation receiving its own news. The family, whose patriarchal structure Fanon describes without sentimentality, is shown cracking under the war's pressures, as daughters joined the struggle and fathers discovered that authority had migrated to the movement. Algerian attitudes to European medicine, long and understandably suspicious of the colonial doctor, are traced through their wartime reversal, as the movement's own health service taught the population to claim the medicine it had feared. Running through the essays is a single thesis that softens nothing in the later book but deepens it: the revolution is remaking the colonised at the level of daily life and intimate custom before it wins a single province, and the new man of the final book's rhetoric is, in these pages, not a prophecy but a field report.

The essays and speeches collected after his death as Toward the African Revolution fill out the political Fanon of the conference years: the resignation letter discussed earlier; analyses of the French left's failures over Algeria; interventions on African unity that argue for a continental solidarity built from below rather than proclaimed by governments; and the diary of his Saharan mission of 1960, written in a notebook as the expedition crossed toward Mali, which records the logistics of opening a southern front with a strategist's eye for wells, distances, and loyalties. These texts matter to the Marxism question because they show the stretch being performed in practice: class analysis applied to colonial armies and peasantries, appeals to African unity that route through the continent's labouring poor rather than its presidents, and a persistent refusal, years before the final book's famous chapter, to trust the new states' elites with the revolutions their peoples were making. The diplomat who wrote them was already the author of the pitfalls chapter in embryo; the independence settlements being negotiated around him were teaching him, in real time, what a national bourgeoisie does with a flag.

## The trials of national consciousness: the vindicated chapter

The third chapter of his final book carries, in Constance Farrington's translation, the title The Pitfalls of National Consciousness, and in Richard Philcox's the more faithful Trials and Tribulations of National Consciousness; either way it is the chapter the first decade of the book's life largely skipped, and the chapter the subsequent half century has been annotating with events. Its subject is the class that independence installs. Fanon names it the national bourgeoisie, and his portrait is drawn with a clinician's patience, trait by trait, in the course of an argument that deserves to be followed rather than summarised, because its power lies in the mechanism it specifies: not that the new rulers are wicked, but that the class they belong to is constituted, by its history and its balance sheet, for a single function, and performs that function whatever its intentions.

The mechanism begins with what the class is not. The national bourgeoisie of the underdeveloped country, Fanon argues, is not a bourgeoisie in the classical sense at all: it has accumulated no capital stock, built no industry, commanded no technology, and it therefore cannot perform the historical task that once justified the European bourgeoisie, the organisation of production. What it possesses is position. Formed under colonialism as intermediaries, traders, brokers, officials, and professionals servicing the colonial economy, it steps at independence into the vacated offices of that economy and discovers that its vocation is the one it was trained for: mediation. It becomes, in his phrase, the transmission line between the nation and foreign capital, taking its commission on the passage of wealth out of the country, converting the state into a brokerage, and measuring development by the growth of its own opportunities to intermediate. The casinos and beaches and tourist industries he prophesies for such a class are not moral garnish; they are the precise economic form of a bourgeoisie that cannot produce and must therefore sell access, scenery, and stability.

### What exactly did Fanon predict about the national bourgeoisie?

That it would fail to develop the country's productive forces and would govern as an intermediary for foreign capital; that the ruling party would decay from an instrument of the people into a screen for private advancement and then into an apparatus of control;

that the leader would harden into a proprietor; and that national unity, having no economic content, would be replaced by ethnic, regional, and religious appeals, with the single party as the modern dress of a bourgeois dictatorship.

From the economic mechanism the political consequences unfold in the chapter's sequence. The party, which during the struggle was the people's instrument for coordinating sacrifice, is converted after victory into a means of private advancement, a ladder and a licence; its militants become informers and placemen, its congresses become acclamations, and its function inverts from mobilising the population to immobilising it, since an awakened citizenry is now the chief threat to the brokerage's quiet. The leader, who incarnates the nation's unity because no institution does, gathers the state into his person and treats criticism as treason, while the army and police, the budget's favoured children, absorb the resources that production was denied. Regionalism and tribalism, which the struggle had subordinated to the national project, return as the brokerage's instruments: contracts, posts, and licences are distributed along ethnic lines because a citizenry divided into clienteles cannot combine against the broker, and Fanon's bleakest sentence on the subject observes that the national bourgeoisie, having no nation building work to do, discovers chauvinism as its governing programme, turning the state toward the racism of the tribe and the region. The chapter closes with the demand that was its point all along: that the nation's wealth be returned to the nation in work, education, and production, that the party be rebuilt as the people's direct expression rather than its warden, and that political education, the making of citizens who understand what is being done in their name, is the only known antidote to the brokerage state.

Two elements of the mechanism deserve separate emphasis, because they are the parts of the chapter most often confirmed by readers who begin as its sceptics. The first is the treatment of the leader. Fanon had watched the liberation era's presidents consolidate, and his anatomy is structural rather than personal: where the nation has no institutional life independent of the party, and the party none independent of its chief, the leader becomes the state's living constitution, and the cult that gathers around him is not flattery but a functional substitute for the missing institutions, the form in which unity is performed in the absence of anything that could produce it. The leader's tours, his speeches distributed as scripture, his photograph in the office that was once the district commissioner's, are in this reading the brokerage state's liturgy, and the tragedy Fanon records is that the leader often begins as the struggle's genuine hero, so that the cult's raw material is real devotion curdling in an institutional vacuum. The second element is the treatment of the capital city and the interior. The brokerage concentrates its commissions, its licences, its roads and its clinics in the capital and the ports, the zones of its own circulation, while the countryside that made the revolution discovers that independence has changed the address of its neglect; the regional resentments that the brokerage then plays upon are thus of its own manufacture, harvested from the inequalities its economic function required it to deepen. A mechanism that explains both the cult and the map has earned its readers, and the chapter's quiet insistence that the remedy is not better men but a different political economy of citizenship, wealth returned as work and schooling to the people who produced it, the party rebuilt as a school rather than a screen, is the part of the argument its celebrants quote least, because it indicts the audience that quotes the rest.

That the prediction has been borne out repeatedly does not need this profile to labour the catalogue, and the contested figures protocol that governs it in any case forbids decorating the argument with borrowed statistics. The pattern Fanon specified, an intermediary elite, a party converted to a screen, a leader proprietor, an economy of access and tourism and extraction, a politics decaying into ethnic clienteles, has been recognised by readers on several continents as the biography of states whose names this profile, under its neutrality discipline, will not enumerate. The recognition matters more than the catalogue: a chapter dismissed in its decade as an embittered aside became, within twenty years, the part of the book that officials, oppositionists, and historians of the postcolonial state all reached for, because it had specified a mechanism rather than a mood, and mechanisms can be checked. Where the mechanism's premises fail, the prediction should fail too, and the chapter's critics have identified the cases: new states in which a disciplined leadership did build industry, education, and an administrative nation, at whatever other cost, stand as evidence that the brokerage outcome was a tendency under colonial deformations rather than a law of independence. Fanon, who insisted that his categories were stretched from evidence and not deduced from heaven, is not dishonoured by the exceptions; the chapter's claim is about the class colonialism actually produced, and where a different class, or a party that subordinated its bourgeoisie to a production programme, took power, the premises were different and so was the result.

The chapter also completes the answer this profile owes to the strongest objection recorded earlier. Woddis and the record showed that revolutions are made by organised parties rather than by spontaneous masses; Fanon's third chapter shows that he understood, from the inside of a revolution's diplomacy, exactly what those parties might become, and that his suspicion of them was not romantic anarchism but a forecast with a mechanism attached. The two insights do not refute each other. Organisation wins liberation, and organisation, unexamined, inherits the colony's economic constitution and administers it under a flag; the first proposition is Woddis's, the second is Fanon's, and the history of the period's liberated states is largely the history of movements that proved the first and then illustrated the second. A Marxism stretched for the colony has to hold both, and the holding of both, in a single book, by a man writing against the clock of his own illness, is the best evidence that the stretching was a theoretical achievement and not a slogan.

## Fanon and Marxism: the balance sheet

Was Fanon a Marxist? The question has been put to his texts for half a century, and the texts answer it the way the stretching formula predicts: yes, with the categories under tension, and the tension is the position. Consider first the evidence of allegiance. Fanon described his own procedure as the stretching of Marxist analysis, not its replacement; a writer does not stretch a garment he has discarded. His explanations run, at every decisive point, through material life: racism is explained by the colonial economy that installs and rewards it, the national bourgeoisie by its balance sheet, the colonised intellectual's itinerary by his class position between the people's world and the coloniser's culture. His library and his lectures show a man working inside the Marxist tradition's questions even in exile, lecturing FLN cadres on Sartre's Critique of Dialectical Reason in the year of its appearance, measuring his own departures against the doctrine he had inherited. And his political commitments were those of the revolutionary Marxist milieu of his time: anti-imperialist, internationalist in aspiration, contemptuous of the social democratic management of empire, and aligned with a liberation movement whose programme he pressed, from within, toward social transformation rather than a mere change of flags.

Consider then the evidence of distance, which is equally documentary. He never joined a communist party, and the communist parties of the metropoles, whose Algerian record included the French party's parliamentary vote of March 1956 granting the government the special powers under which the war's repression was massively extended, a vote Fanon's Algerian comrades neither forgot nor forgave, appear in his pages as part of the problem the colony poses to imported doctrine. He reassigned the revolutionary subject from the class the tradition anointed to classes it had filed as residual. He made race a load bearing structure where the tradition filed it as ideology. He grounded his account of the colonised mind in a clinical practice whose evidence no economic text could supply, and he reached, in the first book, for phenomenological and psychoanalytic instruments that orthodox Marxism of the period treated as bourgeois deviation. A Marxism that must be stretched at the agent, at the structure, and at the method is not the Marxism of the manuals, and Fanon's defenders and prosecutors within the tradition have both built cases on the fact.

### Did Fanon consider himself a Marxist?

He described his work as the stretching of Marxist analysis and lectured the movement's cadres on the Marxist philosophy of his day, which is the self-description of a man inside the tradition and arguing with it; he held no party card, submitted to no orthodoxy's discipline, and treated the doctrine's categories as instruments

to be adapted to evidence rather than as a creed to be applied, which is why both claims on him, orthodox and anti-Marxist, find their passages.

The most productive way to close the question is to ask what the stretching amounts to as a theoretical event, since that is what survives the labelling dispute. Three things, on this profile's assessment. First, a relocation of evidence: Fanon brought the clinic and the phenomenology of racial embodiment into a tradition that had theorised exploitation largely from the factory and the text, and the relocation permanently enlarged what counts as material for a materialist analysis; after Fanon, a Marxism that cannot discuss what domination does to bodies and selfhood is a Marxism that has declined evidence. Second, a correction of sequence: in the colony, the political and racial forms are not the late expression of an economic base but are present at the founding, in the conquest itself, and any analysis that waits for the economy to explain them will explain them too late. Third, a warning addressed to the tradition's own organisations, delivered from inside a revolution: that the party and the national state, the instruments orthodoxy trusted, are themselves subject to the colonial economy's gravity, and will reproduce its intermediary class unless a political education stronger than the gravity is built against it. Each of the three is a contribution a Marxist can use without accepting the whole corpus, which is precisely the relationship Fanon recommended toward Marx.

His relation to Marx's own texts should be specified, since the balance sheet's verdict depends on it. Fanon was not an exegete: the corpus contains no commentary on Capital, no disputation over the falling rate of profit or the transformation problem, and his citations of Marx are sparse beside his citations of the clinic and the war. The Marxism in him is structural and temperamental rather than textual, an engine adopted and a loyalty felt, the conviction that material arrangements explain the life of the mind, that exploitation is the colonial fact under its racial form, and that the order he fought was historical and therefore endable. Purists of the tradition have always found this insufficient, and by the standard of the seminar they are right; by the standard of the tradition's own founder, who revised his categories across four decades of evidence and expected his followers to do the same, the man who stretched the analysis for the colony was performing the most orthodox act available to him. The purists' objection is recorded in the balance sheet's debits and answered there; it is noted here only to fix the verdict's terms, which are fidelity to the method against fidelity to the inventory.

Against the balance sheet's credit side stand the debits this profile has recorded and will not now minimise. The agent thesis failed its historical examination. The constitutive claim about violence survives only in a weakened, heavily qualified form after Arendt and Zahar have done their work. The analysis of the postcolonial state, for all its vindication, is an anatomy of one class in one kind of colony, settler colonialism of the Algerian type, and its export to every postcolonial situation repeats the error Fanon charged against the exporters of the metropolitan model. The writings on gender and the family, adventurous in A Dying Colonialism, are marked by the assumptions of their moment and have drawn justified criticism from the feminist scholarship this series treats in its own cluster. And the corpus is unfinished in the plainest sense: three books and a sheaf of essays across nine years, much of it dictated in haste, none of it revised in tranquillity. A thinker is not required to be complete to be major. He is required to have changed what can be thought about his object, and on the colony, on race inside class, and on the state after the flag, Fanon changed it in ways his critics continue to use even while they argue.

## The readers who remade him

Fanon's afterlife has been as contested as his politics, and it falls into discernible readings, each of which keeps part of the corpus and mislays the rest. The first reading was the one the preface prepared: the revolutionary of the nineteen sixties, prophet of the wretched and of the cleansing storm, whose book passed through the liberation movements and the metropolitan student left as a warrant and a thrill. That reading built his fame and buried his caution; it is the reading Arendt answered, and it survives wherever a single chapter, or a single preface, is made to stand for the man. The second reading arrived with the academic institutionalisation of postcolonial studies, which recovered the Fanon of Black Skin, White Masks as a theorist of identity, ambivalence, and the gaze, a Fanon for the seminar on representation, and correspondingly mislaid the strategist of Blida and Tunis, the party question, the balance sheet of the national bourgeoisie. Homi Bhabha's influential forewords and essays were central to this recovery, and the Fanon they installed, psychoanalytic and textual, is a real Fanon, one book of him, promoted to the whole.

The third reading is the reclamation, mounted from within Marxist and materialist scholarship against both predecessors. Ato Sekyi-Otu's Fanon's Dialectic of Experience, published in 1996, reread the corpus as a single dramatic argument about the stages of anti-colonial consciousness and restored the political Fanon without the preface's inflation, insisting that the texts be followed in their own sequence rather than strip mined for slogans. Nigel Gibson's studies of Fanon's engagement with Marxism and of his South African readers pressed the same restoration from the side of political theory, and Neil Lazarus argued, against the postcolonial establishment, that the field had domesticated its founding figure by dissolving a revolutionary who wrote about classes, states, and armies into a theorist of textual ambivalence. Meanwhile the clinical reading matured on its own track: psychiatrists and trauma scholars returned to the case files and the Blida reforms and found a practitioner whose work anticipated the social psychiatry of war by decades, while David Macey's biography supplied the documented life, the archive against which all three readings could be checked. The dispute among these Fanons is not a scandal to be regretted; it is the normal fate of a corpus that contains a phenomenologist, a clinician, a strategist, and a prophet in one short bibliography, and this profile's method, chapter by chapter and claim by claim, is offered as the way to hold the readings answerable to the texts. The dispute over his reception in the field that claims him most loudly is treated in the series article on [marxism vs postcolonial theory](/2017/05/15/marxism-vs-postcolonial-theory/), where the two traditions' claims are set side by side.

Beyond the academy, the corpus kept a second life in the movements of the colonised and racialised world, and the record of that life belongs to an honest reception history. In South Africa, Steve Biko and the Black Consciousness movement drew on Fanon's psychology of internalised inferiority as a founding text for their insistence that the oppressed must first exorcise the slave mentality the system had installed, a reading that took the first book's clinic into the township meeting and found it exact. In the United States, the Black liberation movement of the same years read the final book as a manual of their own situation, its two towns mapped onto the American city, its intermediary class recognised in the politics of the ghetto's brokers, and the reading, however loose its scholarship, testified to the fusion thesis's portability: where race assigns economic place by visible rule, Fanon's categories arrive before his books do. In Iran, the sociologist Ali Shariati translated Fanon and built the translated Fanon into the ideological preparation of a revolution whose theocratic outcome its source would not have recognised, the century's most consequential demonstration that a corpus, once exported, belongs to its importers. In Latin America, the guerrilla generation read him beside their own theorists, and Ernesto Guevara's writings share with Fanon's the conviction that the revolutionary's subjective transformation is made in the struggle itself, a convergence the two men's different continents make more striking than any influence would. These appropriations were not always faithful, and Fanon, who warned against the sloganising of his own schema, would have audited them as this profile has audited the preface; but a corpus that is only ever read faithfully is a corpus that has stopped working, and the movements' Fanon, like the academy's, is evidence about the texts as well as about the readers.

The archive has also grown under the argument. The publication in French in 2015, and in English in 2018 as Alienation and Freedom, of a large body of Fanon's unpublished and uncollected writing, the psychiatric papers, the student plays, the correspondence, the political journalism that the posthumous collections had missed, gave the dispute a new evidentiary base, and its effect has been to strengthen the integrated reading this profile has followed: the clinician and the revolutionary appear in the recovered papers as one continuous worker, the plays illuminating the prose's theatricality, the case papers the politics' method, so that the three rival Fanons of the reception history look increasingly like artefacts of a partial archive. Scholarship's Fanon is converging, slowly, on the man the documents describe, which is the best outcome a reception history can have and the one his own method, evidence first and category after, would have prescribed.

One measure of the corpus's durability is the company its errors keep. The mistranslated chapter title misled two generations without preventing the chapter from founding a field; the preface's usurpation distorted the first chapter's meaning without preventing the third chapter from becoming the standard anatomy of the postcolonial state; the agent thesis failed without taking down the fusion thesis, which the racial capitalism literature has since rebuilt on larger evidence. A minor thinker's mistakes are fatal because there is nothing else; Fanon's have been absorbed as the growing pains of arguments that outgrew them. That is why he is on every reading list, and why the reading lists keep changing their reasons.

## Decolonisation and the new man

Underneath the disputes this profile has been conducting stands Fanon's definition of the thing itself, and it is more radical than the transfer of power it is usually taken to name. Decolonisation, in the final book's opening movement, is the process by which the colonised cease to be the species the colony made them: the native, in his formulation, is a creation of the settler, manufactured as a native by the same acts that manufacture the settler as a settler, and decolonisation is therefore not complete when the settler's flag comes down. It is complete only when the manufactured categories themselves are abolished, when the last are made first not by inversion of the queue but by the dissolution of the order that arranged human beings in a queue of species at all. He reaches, characteristically, for a scriptural cadence to describe the reversal, the last becoming first, and then immediately complicates the cadence: a mere reversal, the native enthroned where the settler sat, the same zoology with the labels swapped, is the outcome his third chapter exists to warn against. The substitution he demands is of one species of men by another in the strong sense, a new species, which is why the book's vocabulary keeps returning to creation: new men, a new humanity, a new skin for old ceremonies refused.

The new man is the most mocked phrase in the corpus and the least examined. In the preface it belongs to Sartre, sacramental and bloody; in Fanon's own text it is a clinical and pedagogical idea before it is a rhetorical one, the name for the colonised person after the epidermal schema has been dismantled, the patient of the colonial order discharged from its diagnosis. Its content, where Fanon specifies it, is unspectacular and demanding: a citizen who understands the national wealth as his own work's product and claims it; a party member who treats the organisation as an instrument rather than a ladder; a culture made by a people in motion rather than curated for them; a mind that has stopped asking the former master's mirror whether it exists. The famous close of the book, addressed to his comrades, begs them not to imitate Europe, not to pay tribute to it by adopting its states, its institutions, and its sicknesses, and asks instead for new concepts and a new man set afoot from the colonised world's own materials; the sentence is quoted in fragments everywhere, and this profile keeps to its rule of one quotation per source and so paraphrases it, because the paraphrase preserves what the slogan loses, namely that the new man is a research programme and not a cheer. Fanon's humanism, for which the label existentialist is often used, consists exactly in this refusal: humanity is not a property the European owns and the native petitions for admission to, but a task the colonised must define in the act of ending the system that defined them out of it.

The definition carries a distinction the corpus insists on and its summarists routinely blur, between national consciousness and nationalism. National consciousness, in Fanon's usage, is the people's growing awareness of themselves as the authors of a common life, the sentiment the struggle manufactures as it educates, and it is precious precisely because it is a beginning: the form in which a population first experiences itself as a subject. Nationalism is what the intermediary class makes of that beginning when it arrests it, the consciousness frozen into a property, a flag worshipped in place of the life it was meant to announce, a chauvinism that turns inward against the region, the tribe, and the neighbour once the foreign enemy has gone. The pitfalls chapter is the anatomy of the arrest: at each stage, an institution of the living consciousness, party, leader, army, culture, is shown hardening into its nationalist mummy, and the hardening is always performed by the same class for the same reason, that a frozen nation can be brokered and a moving one cannot. The distinction disciplines the whole corpus's politics: Fanon is not a nationalist thinker with Marxist decorations, as the lazy filing has it, nor a Marxist embarrassed by the nation, but a theorist for whom the nation is a phase of consciousness that must be surpassed into social and human content or it will be confiscated, and the surpassing is not a betrayal of the liberation struggle but its completion, the difference between a people who have seized a state and a people who have become, in his demanding phrase, a new humanity's first draft.

Set against the definition, the historical decolonisations mostly failed it, and Fanon, who died before the Algerian settlement, had already written the failure's anatomy in advance: flags changed, species remained, the intermediary class administered the old economy in national dress. The definition's severity is therefore diagnostic rather than utopian in intent. It supplies the standard by which the pitfalls chapter measures the new states, and it explains the otherwise puzzling harshness of a liberation movement's own theorist toward liberation's ordinary fruits. If decolonisation is the abolition of the native as a category, then an independence that preserves the category under new management is not a partial success to be celebrated with reservations; it is a different event wearing the expected event's clothes, and the theorist's duty is to say so early, while the saying can still instruct. That duty, discharged in advance of the facts, is the core of Fanon's claim on the attention of readers who have never seen a colony: he wrote the theory of a disappointment before the disappointment, from inside the hope.

## The method: case, category, stretch

It is worth isolating the method itself, because Fanon's way of knowing is as exportable as his conclusions and considerably less dated. The method has three movements, visible in every text from the first book to the case files. The first movement is clinical attention to the case: a patient, a scene, a garment, a radio set, examined until it yields the structure it belongs to. Fanon almost never begins from the abstraction; he begins from the train compartment, the ward round, the woman deciding whether to veil, and lets the category arrive when the evidence calls for it. The second movement is the location of the case inside the colonial economy's arrangements: the symptom is traced to the wage, the land seizure, the schoolroom, the racial statute, until the private trouble stands disclosed as a public fact with a case history. The third movement is the stretch: the inherited category, class, neurosis, nation, culture, is tested against the located case and modified where the case resists it, openly, with the modification announced as the finding. A category that survives the case unchanged has been confirmed; a category that must stretch has been improved; a category that tears has been refuted, and Fanon's texts are the record of all three outcomes, which is why they read as enquiries rather than as applications of a finished doctrine.

The method explains both his originality within Marxism and his quarrels with its custodians. Orthodox critics of the Woddis type proceeded in the opposite direction, from the category to the case, and found Fanon undisciplined exactly where he was being most rigorous: the peasantry's promotion, the proletariat's demotion, the fusion of race with class, are all findings of the third movement, announced with their evidence, where a deduction from the metropolitan inventory would have returned the orthodox answers and missed the colony entirely. It also explains his quarrel with the psychoanalysis of his training, which performed the first movement superbly and refused the second, decoding the patient's dream as a family romance while the patient's country burned outside the window. And it explains the peculiar authority of the writing, which persuades readers who reject its conclusions: the case is always there, on the page, checkable against the reader's own knowledge of wards, streets, and humiliations, so that disagreement must begin from evidence rather than from allegiance. A method that can be turned on its author's own claims is the best inheritance a thinker can leave, and this profile has used it throughout, testing the agent thesis against the record, the violence thesis against its critics, and the prediction against the states that followed, in the belief that the procedure honours Fanon better than reverence would.

## The problems Fanon left open

A profile that ends in a balance sheet owes its reader the unresolved items in a separate ledger, because Fanon's open problems are not blemishes on the work but the places where the work is still happening in other people's hands. The first is the problem of organisation, which his corpus states from both sides and settles from neither. The spontaneity chapter knows that risings without programme are crushed; the national consciousness chapter knows that programmes administered by parties become the brokerage's charter; between the two knowledges there is a space where a theory of revolutionary organisation adequate to the colony ought to stand, and what stands there in Fanon is an exhortation, political education, repeated with mounting urgency and never institutionalised into an answer: education by whom, guarded against capture by what, corrected when the educators become the new intermediaries by which mechanism? The movements that inherited his texts answered the question with the party state he feared or with the fragmentation he feared more, and the problem remains exactly where he left it, which is not a refutation of his diagnosis but a measure of its depth: he had identified the disease of postcolonial organisation correctly enough that no remedy in his kit, or in most kits since, has cured it.

The second open problem is the content of the new man. Fanon can describe the unmaking of the colonised subject in clinical detail, because the clinic supplied the evidence; the remaking he describes by negation and by exhortation, a citizen vigilant, a party servant rather than master, a culture in motion, but the institutional and economic substance that would fill the portrait, the property forms, the educational order, the relation of plan to market and of centre to region, is absent from the corpus, partly because the corpus is short and wartime, and partly because its author's method distrusted blueprints drawn away from cases. The revolutions that claimed him filled the blank with the materials to hand, centralised states and single parties among them, and then illustrated his warnings with a fidelity that suggests the blank was not accidental modesty but a genuine theoretical lacuna: a theory of liberation that cannot specify, even provisionally, the institutions of the liberated order leaves the specification to the men with the offices, and Fanon of all writers knew who the men with the offices would be.

The third problem is the standing of women in the corpus, and it should be recorded without either anachronistic prosecution or protective silence. The essays of A Dying Colonialism gave Algerian women an agency and a centrality that the male literature of the nationalist period rarely matched, following the veil's transformation and the woman fighter's emergence with close and respectful attention. The first book's chapters on intimate life, by contrast, read desire through a framework that later feminist critics, including readers sympathetic to his project, have found constrained by the assumptions of its moment, quick to diagnose the woman of colour's longing for whiteness as pathology while treating the man's symmetrical longing with a different sympathy. The corpus thus contains both the resources for a Fanonian feminism, the sociogenic method, the attention to the intimate as political evidence, and passages that such a feminism must argue with rather than inherit. The Marxist feminist literature treated in this series' preceding cluster performs exactly that double operation on its own founders, and Fanon's case is best filed beside theirs: a method that survives its author's blind spots, provided the blind spots are named.

The fourth problem is the peasantry's fate inside his own theory as the colonial world urbanised. Fanon wrote at the high tide of the rural colony, when the landless cultivator was the colonised majority and the shantytown was the countryside's overflow; the decades after his death moved the colonised and postcolonial majority into cities, into informal labour and service work and the vast peri-urban settlements whose inhabitants are neither peasants nor proletarians in his senses. Whether the stretching can be performed again, on his own categories, for that world, is the live question his method poses to his inheritors, and it is a question he equipped them to ask: the categories were always instruments, the evidence was always to govern, and a Fanonian analysis of the informal city that began by demoting his rural agent, on evidence, in his manner, would be more faithful to the stretching than any defence of the letter. His best readers have understood this, which is why the corpus continues to generate work rather than merely commentary.

A fifth open problem concerns the career of the constitutive claim about violence among the movements that cited him. Whatever its standing as psychology, the claim that the armed act remakes the colonised subject was detached from its clinical moorings by admirers and prosecutors alike and circulated as a licence, and the movements of the following decades that acted on the licence produced, alongside genuine liberations, their full share of the enforcers and warlords this profile's assessment predicted from the claim's own indeterminacy. Sorting Fanon's responsibility for the licence is a genuine historical problem: the chapter contains the warnings, the preface removed their effect, the author died before the career began, and the texts cannot cross examine their readers. The problem is filed here rather than solved because it belongs to the ethics of reception as much as to Fanon studies, and because any honest profile must record that the most influential sentence shaped idea in the corpus is also the one its author surrounded with the most qualifications and the world received with the fewest.

The sixth open problem is the tension between his national frame and his continental one. The theory of liberation is built for the nation: national consciousness, national culture, the national bourgeoisie, because the colony he knew was administered as a territory and its liberation would be proclaimed as a state. Yet his diplomacy and his essays reach continually beyond the national frame toward an African unity he suspected the new states' elites of betraying in advance, and his Saharan mission was an attempt to give the continental idea a supply line. The corpus never reconciles the two scales: it does not say whether the nation is the revolution's home or its first obstacle, whether the pitfalls chapter's state is to be perfected or transcended, and the tension is arguably the most prophetic thing in it, since the postcolonial order has lived exactly there, between national sovereignties too weak to develop and continental projects too proclaimed to function. Fanon left the problem open because the war left it open; the honesty of the corpus is that it declines to solve on paper what its author could not solve in the conference hall.

## The settler colony as the theory's home terrain

The stretching was calibrated on a particular kind of colony, and the calibration explains both the theory's power and the limits recorded in this profile's balance sheet. Algeria was a settler colony of the most developed type: a large European population, rooted for generations, holding the best land and the commanding heights of the economy, protected by a metropolitan state that had declared the territory not a colony at all but France itself, an integral portion of the republic across the sea. In such a colony the fusion of race and class approaches completeness, because the settler population's prosperity is visibly and legally a racial entitlement: the land was taken by racial confiscation, the franchise was rationed by race, the wage scales, the schools, the hospitals, the very pavement of the two towns, all were distributed along the line the Manichean image describes. Fanon's categories fit this terrain the way a glove fits the hand that filled it, and the fit is the source of the final book's authority: its every generalisation can be walked back to an Algerian institution.

The export problem begins where the settler thins out. In colonies of exploitation rather than settlement, where a small administrative caste ruled through indigenous intermediaries and the racial line cut across a class structure with its own indigenous dynamics, the fusion is partial, and class analysis of the more orthodox kind recovers some of its metropolitan traction; Fanon knew the difference, travelled the conference circuit of a continent that contained every variety, and wrote about the Portuguese colonies and the settler redoubts of the south with the same categories he had built for Algeria, because his political horizon was the continent's liberation as a whole. The scholarly question his corpus leaves is whether the stretching is a general theory of colonialism or a theory of the settler colony with imperial ambitions, and the honest answer, on the evidence assembled in this profile, is the latter with a generalisable method: the fusion thesis travels as a question, how far are race and class fused here, and by what mechanisms, rather than as a conclusion to be assumed. That is not a small bequest. It converts the oldest quarrel in the Marxist theory of empire, whether race is real or epiphenomenal, into an empirical enquiry with a procedure attached, and the racial capitalism literature that later rebuilt the fusion thesis on the evidence of other centuries and other oceans is, whether it cites him or not, working his seam.

His conference writings show him applying the calibration consciously across the continent's variety. The settler belt of the south, the Portuguese territories, the Rhodesias, South Africa, Kenya's highlands, presented the Algerian pattern in its pure form, land seized, a racial caste entrenched, a frontier patrolled, and his analyses of those struggles proceed by the fusion thesis without adjustment. The territories of West Africa moving toward negotiated independence presented the pattern diluted, and it is there that the pitfalls analysis does the work the fusion thesis cannot: where the settler is absent, the intermediary class is present, and the danger is not the frontier but the brokerage. The two instruments together, fusion for the settler colony, brokerage for the administered one, amount to a typology of colonial situations that the corpus never draws as a diagram but everywhere assumes, and drawing it, as this profile has done, is the condition of exporting Fanon without repeating the export error he diagnosed in the metropolitan Marxists. A category calibrated in Algiers and applied in Accra without the brokerage correction will misread Accra as surely as the proletarian category misread Algiers.

His Algeria also explains the theory's silences. A writer formed by a settler colony at war thinks in binaries because the war thinks in binaries; the subtleties of collaboration, gradation, and the middle strata that a longer peace would have forced into view are present in his pages, the intermediary is his most original character, but they are drawn in the stark light of a struggle that sorted men by camp. The party cadres, officials, and intellectuals who populate the pitfalls chapter are seen from the maquis and the embassy, not from the ministry and the market stall, and the portrait's prophetic quality owes something to that vantage: he saw the new elite as the struggle's camp follower before it became the state's proprietor, which is when its nature is easiest to see and hardest to say. Readers applying the corpus to quieter places and longer timescales should perform the adjustment his method prescribes, stretching the stretcher, and the adjustment is not a concession wrung from his defenders but the operating instruction printed on the instrument itself.

## The book in other languages: two translations, two Fanons

The final book's Anglophone life ran through two translations whose differences belong in any serious profile, because they amount to a second, quieter instance of the translation dependency pattern that the mistranslated chapter title of the first book established. Constance Farrington's version, published by Grove Press in 1963, was the text of the heroic reception: vigorous, journalistic, and loose, it rendered the third chapter's title as The Pitfalls of National Consciousness, a phrase that entered the language, and it carried Sartre's preface in full, so that the compound this profile has been separating was the only Fanon the English speaking world could buy for four decades. Richard Philcox's translation, published in 2004, retranslated the book entire: the first chapter's title became On Violence where Farrington had Concerning Violence, the third chapter recovered its French sense as The Trials and Tribulations of National Consciousness, and hundreds of sentences recovered a precision, and sometimes a harshness, that the earlier rendering had smoothed. Scholars now cite Philcox and teach Farrington's chapter titles, an unstable arrangement that mirrors the corpus's whole reception history, and the student meeting the book should be warned, as this profile warned over the earlier mistranslation, that the Fanon in hand is partly a translator's Fanon, and that checking a contested passage against the French, or against the rival rendering, is not pedantry but method.

The first book's two translations tell the same story in miniature, Charles Lam Markmann's of 1967 giving the fifth chapter its fact of blackness and Philcox's of 2008 restoring the lived experience, so that Fanon's English bibliography is doubled throughout: two Black Skins, two Wretcheds, and a scholarly literature that cites across the divide without always noticing it has crossed one. The French texts, meanwhile, had their own material history, published by houses of the anti-colonial left, Maspero above all, in a period when such publishing was a form of action and its products were liable to seizure by the police of the republic whose empire they indicted; A Dying Colonialism appeared in 1959 in the middle of the war it described, and the collected essays of Toward the African Revolution were assembled after his death by his widow and his publisher from the journalism of the struggle. A corpus that arrives through this much mediation, translation, seizure, posthumous editing, and prefatory sponsorship, asks to be read with the mediation in view, and the asking is itself one of Fanon's lessons, since nobody understood better than the author of the epidermal schema that a text, like a body, is received inside a world that has already decided what it is.

The French publication itself was an episode of the war it described. François Maspero's house operated as the anti-colonial movement's Parisian arsenal, issuing the literature of the Algerian cause under police attention that included raids, seizures of stock, and prosecution of its publisher, and the appearance of Les Damnés de la terre in the autumn of 1961, carrying Sartre's signature on its front like a flag, was received by the Parisian press as an event of the conflict rather than of the book trade. Reviews divided as the country divided; the book's enemies reviewed the preface, its friends reviewed the revolution, and almost nobody, in the first season, reviewed the five chapters in their order and their proportions, which is why this profile has treated the restoration of those proportions as its task. The season's noise has long since faded into the archive; the chapters remain, awaiting the reader the noise delayed, in Paris and in every capital that received the volume as a dispatch from its own unfinished war.

## Where to start with Fanon

The reading order matters more with Fanon than with most thinkers, because the famous door into the corpus is the wrong one. The new reader who begins, as the reputation instructs, with the first chapter of The Wretched of the Earth will meet the argument at its most compressed and most mediated, will mistake the temperature of the preface for the temperature of the text, and will have no equipment for noticing what the chapter is doing. Begin instead with the fifth chapter of Black Skin, White Masks, the lived experience chapter, in the Philcox translation: twenty pages that contain the method entire, the scene, the schema, the revision of Hegel, the wound of Sartre's compliment, and the voice, unmistakable and unrepeatable, that the rest of the corpus spends itself extending. From there the rest of the first book opens naturally, and the reader who has finished it possesses the psychology that the political writings presuppose.

Then take the final book whole, in the order its author arranged and with its preface deliberately postponed: read Sartre's text after the five chapters, as an appendix by a famous contemporary, and feel the displacement happen in your own reading, the preface's phrases trying to recolour chapters you have already met unaccompanied. Within the book, give the third chapter the slowness its neglect has earned it, and close with the case files, which retroactively change the status of everything before them from rhetoric to evidence. A Dying Colonialism follows as the corpus's most approachable volume, the essays on the veil and the radio being the finest short introductions to the method in practice, and Toward the African Revolution completes the shelf with the resignation letter, which no reader should leave unmet, the conferences' analyses, and the Saharan diary. The reader who arrives by this road will find, at its end, that the questions the summaries argue about, whether he celebrated violence, whether he was a Marxist, whether he matters, have quietly reformed themselves into the questions the texts actually ask, which are harder and better.

## Fanon and Negritude: the teacher, the movement, the wound

No account of Fanon's formation is complete without the movement that gave his generation its first affirmative word for what they were. Negritude, the literary and political affirmation of Black civilisation forged in Paris in the nineteen thirties by Aimé Césaire, Léopold Sédar Senghor, and Léon Damas, reached the schoolboy Fanon through the most direct channel imaginable: Césaire taught at the Lycée Schoelcher during Fanon's years there, returned to Martinique as its mayor and deputy with the young volunteer's help in the campaign of 1945, and stood in the island's imagination as the proof that a Martinican could master the French language so completely as to bend it against France. Césaire's Discourse on Colonialism, published in 1950 while Fanon was a student at Lyon, supplied the era's most quotable indictment of the colonising republic, arguing that colonisation decivilised the coloniser and that Europe's tolerance of its own colonial barbarism had prepared its domestic catastrophes. The young psychiatrist absorbed the indictment whole; what he could not absorb, as Black Skin, White Masks records in its most anguished pages, was the movement's temptation to answer the racial sentence with a racial essence, to meet the settler's zoology with an inverted zoology of Black soul, rhythm, and emotion.

His position, worked out against both Senghor's celebrations and Sartre's dialectical compliment, is a precise one and worth the precision. Negritude was necessary: a people told for centuries that they have no civilisation cannot be argued out of the lie by statistics, and the recovery of African and diasporic achievement, the insistence that blackness is a value and not a defect, performed the psychic repair his clinic would have prescribed. But the affirmation becomes a trap the moment it is mistaken for a destination, because an essence, however proudly worn, is still an essence, and the Manichean world is delighted to house its prisoners in a gilded compartment as long as the compartment walls stand. Sartre's Black Orpheus then performed the dialectical version of the same manoeuvre from the other side, welcoming Negritude as the negative moment of a progression whose synthesis would dissolve it, and Fanon's recorded response, that he had been robbed of his last chance, that he needed not to know that his recovered self was a phase in someone else's argument, is the sound of a man discovering that even his allies' philosophy had scheduled his experience as a transition. His own road out of the triangle was the one the rest of the corpus travels: neither essence nor phase, but a universalism to be built by the colonised themselves in struggle, in which the particular cultures of the oppressed are neither museum pieces nor solvents but the materials of a humanity that does not yet exist. The 1956 address to the Paris congress of Black writers and artists, where he argued that racism is not a constant of the human spirit but the element of a system, and that national culture is the whole body of efforts by which a people describes itself in the act of liberation, is the hinge text: Negritude's affirmation retained, its metaphysics discharged, its energies reassigned to the nation in arms.

## Inside the movement: the FLN's envoy and internal critic

Fanon's relation to the Front de Libération Nationale is usually told as a romance of commitment, and the commitment was real: he treated its wounded under the noses of the police who employed him, resigned his post in a letter that was itself a propaganda instrument, and served the movement without interruption from his expulsion to his deathbed. But the relation was also, from the inside, an argument conducted in the movement's own media about what the movement was for, and the argument's terms are the ones this profile has been tracking: the primacy of the peasantry, the danger of the intermediary class, the necessity of African unity built beneath the level of governments, and the insistence that political education, not command, was the bond between the struggle and the people in whose name it was fought. His unsigned editorials in El Moudjahid pressed these themes on the movement's cadres week by week, in the plain style of a man writing for readers who would carry the paper into the maquis, and his diplomacy carried them into the conference halls: at Accra he argued for the coordination of the continent's liberation armies under joint African direction, an all-African military effort that would make the Algerian struggle the school and the arsenal of a continental revolution rather than a national negotiation with France.

The diplomatic record shows the stretch being applied to strategy. Where the provisional government's interest lay in recognition, borders, and the arithmetic of votes at international assemblies, Fanon's interventions kept returning to the social content of the coming independences: which classes would inherit them, what the inherited states would do with the inheritance, whether unity proclaimed by presidents could substitute for the unity of peoples whose economies the colonial map had deliberately fragmented. He was, by the testimony of the movement's own historians, an uncomfortable envoy, valued for his brilliance and his devotion and regarded with reserve by men who found his analyses of their own class futures less than tactful; the pitfalls chapter, after all, was written by the provisional government's ambassador about the class from which provisional governments are recruited. That he wrote it anyway, and that the movement published and honoured him, says something worth recording about the FLN's internal culture at its best, and something about Fanon's understanding of loyalty, which was to the revolution's stated aims rather than to its personnel. He died a servant of the movement and the author of the most searching criticism its victory would face, and the two facts belong in one sentence because he intended them in one life.

## The colonised intellectual: Fanon as his own exhibit

The three phases of the colonised intellectual, set out in the culture chapter, describe a itinerary that Fanon had himself travelled station by station, and recognising the autobiography inside the schema changes how the schema should be read: not as a law of other people's development but as a confession generalised. The assimilation phase is Lyon: the student mastering French medicine and French philosophy, writing French verse drama, proving in the coloniser's own examinations that the colonised mind could beat the metropole at its curriculum, and discovering in the army and the lecture hall that the proof purchased nothing, that the perfectly assimilated Antillean remained, in the decisive glance, a Negro who had learned his lessons well. The rediscovery phase is Negritude: the plunge into the affirmed past, Césaire's poetry as oxygen, the recovery of a civilisation to set against the zoology. The combat phase is Blida, Tunis, and the deathbed dictation: the intellectual's skills, clinical, literary, diplomatic, placed without remainder at the service of a people's struggle, and his writing turned from the mirror to the barricade. When the culture chapter insists that the third phase's literature is distinguished by its addressee, written not for the coloniser's judgment nor for the archive of the race but for the people in movement, it is describing the difference between Black Skin, White Masks, a book still pleading its case before a European tribunal of Hegel, Freud, and Sartre, and The Wretched of the Earth, a book that addresses the colonised as its first readers and permits the European to listen if he can bear it.

The reflexive reading also disciplines the schema's use on others. Fanon knew the itinerary's stations from inside, knew that each phase feels like arrival to the man inside it, and knew the assimilationist's contempt for the rediscoverer and the militant's contempt for both as the amnesia of travellers who have forgotten their own road; his schema, used as he used it, is an instrument of patience, a reminder that the intellectual's position is a stage of a collective journey and not a rank in a hierarchy of enlightenment. The movements that later wielded the three phases as a sorting machine for writers, dismissing the unfashionable as stuck in phase one or two, were doing to his schema what the preface did to his chapter: converting an analysis into a slogan. The schema's real claim is quieter and harder: that the colonised intellectual cannot skip his formation, that the struggle will educate him as he educates it, and that the final phase is not a purity but a function, the moment when the skills the colony paid to train are spent, at last, against the colony.

## A Caribbean parallel: Fanon among the Marxists of his generation

Fanon was not the only Caribbean Marxist of his century obliged to stretch the doctrine for the world that made him, and the parallel this series draws in its own profile of [clr james and marxism](/2017/04/01/clr-james-and-marxism/) sharpens what is distinctive in each. C.L.R. James, a generation older, Trinidadian, had written The Black Jacobins a quarter century before The Wretched of the Earth, demonstrating on the Haitian revolution that the enslaved of a sugar colony could enact, ahead of the metropolitan working class and largely without it, the most advanced politics of their age; James likewise made race and slavery load bearing in an account that remained Marxist in its engine, and likewise quarrelled with the communist orthodoxy of his day over the autonomy of the Black struggle. The convergences are not influence in the simple sense, though Fanon knew the older man's work; they are the signatures of a shared problem. Two Martinican and Trinidadian intelligences, formed in the plantation's shadow and the metropole's libraries, arrived independently at the conclusion that the doctrine's metropolitan inventory would not describe the Americas or Africa until it had been stretched around slavery, race, and the colonial state. Where they differ is instructive: James remained a historian and organiser whose stretch ran toward the self activity of the working class in all its forms, Fanon a clinician whose stretch ran toward the psyche and the peasantry, and the difference of emphasis maps the difference between a revolution remembered and a revolution in progress as their respective evidence.

Set against the metropolitan Marxism of their shared moment, both men appear as early practitioners of a lesson the tradition learned slowly and at cost: that the world's majority did not live where the doctrine's map was drawn, and that a materialism which cannot revise its categories under the pressure of colonial evidence has ceased to be materialist in anything but name. Fanon's version of the lesson is the more violent in form and the more clinical in substance, and it carried, as the Jamesian version did not, the authority of a man who had resigned an office, joined an army, and dictated his conclusions against a prognosis. Posterity has filed them in different departments, James with the historians, Fanon with the prophets, but the series is right to shelve them together: the stretching of Marxism for the colonial world was the joint work of the century's Caribbean Marxists, and Fanon's formula is its most quoted statement because it is its most honest one.

## Political education: the struggle as a school

The remedy Fanon prescribes, in text after text, for the weaknesses his analysis diagnoses is political education, and the prescription is more specific than the phrase's later career as a slogan suggests. He does not mean indoctrination, a doctrine delivered to the masses by their betters; he means the practice, which he had watched work in the maquis and attempted in the ward, of explaining to people the reasons for what is being asked of them until the asking is no longer necessary. The party militant in his prescription does not command the village; he lives in it, learns its grievances in their own order, and connects them, in discussion, to the structure that produces them, so that the political conclusion arrives as the villagers' own discovery rather than as an imported instruction. The struggle itself is the curriculum: each action undertaken for reasons the fighter understands teaches more than a year of speeches, and each action undertaken blindly teaches only obedience, which is the brokerage's favourite lesson. Hence his otherwise puzzling tenderness toward the uprising's errors: a rising that fails after discussion has educated its survivors, while a victory delivered by a vanguard's manoeuvre has educated nobody but the vanguard, and the difference compounds across a revolution's life until it decides what the victory is worth.

The radio essay of A Dying Colonialism is the pedagogy's finest case study. A population that had experienced broadcasting as the coloniser's voice learned, within months of the clandestine station's appearance, to organise its evenings around the news of its own struggle, to interpret the jamming and the communiqués, to argue about the movement's claims in cafés and courtyards: an entire society conducting its own political education through a medium it had seized, with the movement's broadcasts serving less as instruction than as the agenda for a national seminar. Fanon the psychiatrist recognised the process because it was his ward method translated to a people's scale: agency recovered through participation in an institution that is finally one's own, the treatment consisting not in messages received but in a social order rearranged so that receiving becomes doing. When the final book demands that the party be the direct expression of the masses, that the nation's capital of experience be decentralised into every village, that leaders explain until the people no longer need the explanation repeated, it is prescribing the ward and the radio station as the constitution of the future state, and the prescription's utopian reputation says more about the states that ignored it than about its author's realism, since he had twice watched it work at the scales available to him.

The title's own category deserves its sentence in this account. The damned of the earth, in the French phrase Fanon took from the Internationale's first line, names not the poor in general but those the world's order has sentenced: the colonised, the enslaved's descendants, the inmates of the zone of nonbeing, gathered into a single subject by the fact of their common exclusion rather than by any common position in production. The category is the stretching performed at the level of the revolutionary subject's name: where the anthem's original singers meant the proletariat of all countries, Fanon's book reassigns the inheritance to the colonised of all colonies, and the socialism the anthem promises is correspondingly redefined, from the factories' expropriation outward to the abolition of the species order itself. Critics who accuse the book of vagueness about its subject have usually stopped at the title; the title is the thesis, that the wretched are a class made by a system of racialised expropriation whose unity is political before it is economic, and the chapters that follow are the title's evidence.

## The reckoning with Europe

Fanon's indictment of Europe is the element of the corpus most often reduced to a mood, and it is in fact an argument with an economic spine. Its first proposition is accounting: the wealth of Europe, its accumulated capital, its industries and its comforts, was built substantially on the colonised world's substance, on enslaved and coerced labour, on land and minerals taken without payment, on trade arranged by cannon; Europe, in the final book's deliberately outrageous compression, is literally the creation of the Third World, a sentence whose rhetoric should not be allowed to hide its status as a claim about capital formation that economic historians have since documented in detail. Its second proposition is moral and follows from the first: a civilisation that preached humanism while practising the zoology cannot be repaired by its own sermons, and the colonised owe its categories no deference, since the categories arrived in the colonies as the administration's letterhead. Its third proposition is the one his European readers found hardest, because it withdrew the consolation of allyship on the cheap: the European working classes and their parties, in his account, had been made junior partners in the colonial enterprise by its dividends, and their anti-colonialism, where it existed, would be tested not by manifestos but by their willingness to see the enterprise ended at cost to themselves. The French left's Algerian record, which he had observed from Tunis in weekly detail, is the proposition's standing evidence in his pages.

Yet the reckoning is not a rejection of Europe's people or of the universalism Europe preached and practised against itself. The conclusion of the final book addresses Europeans as potential comrades in a humanity that does not yet exist, and the demand it makes of them is the same it makes of the colonised: to stop imitating a civilisation that had produced, alongside its cathedrals and its sciences, the colonial world as its foundation and its disgrace, and to join in working out the new concepts under which a genuinely common humanity could be built. The distinction matters for the Marxism question, because it marks the distance between Fanon's anti-colonialism and any doctrine of civilisational essence: Europe is indicted for its acts and its accounts, not for its blood, and the settler who abandons the settler's function is, in the corpus's own logic, no longer the enemy the zoology made. A thinker capable of that distinction at the height of a war in which his own patients were being tortured by the civilisation he indicted had earned the right to have his violence chapter read with the care this profile has tried to give it.

## The diagnosis of the coloniser

Fanon's psychology has two patients, and the second is read less often than the first. The settler, in his analysis, is not a healthy norm against which the colonised's damage is measured; he is a production of the same system, deformed in the opposite direction, and the corpus's scattered remarks on the coloniser's mind assemble, on inspection, into a diagnosis as systematic as the one the epidermal schema gives the colonised. The settler's founding symptom is a bad conscience that dare not speak: he knows, at a level his ideology works full time to suppress, that his comfort is the native's dispossession wearing the mask of law, and the knowledge returns as fear, the famous fear of the colonised that organises the settler town's architecture, its curfews, its armed sociability, its demand for ever more complete repression as the only available sedative. His second symptom is the splitting that the clinical chapters record in the torturers who came to Fanon's consulting room: men who performed the administration's violence by day and disintegrated by night, beating their wives with the same hands, unable to sleep in the silence their work had purchased, presenting at the clinic of a doctor they did not know was the enemy's servant with the system's confession on their lips. Fanon's case notes treat them neither as monsters nor as victims but as the colonial order's damage in its privileged form, and the evenness of the clinical gaze across the ward's divide is the strongest evidence for the claim this profile made at the outset: that the psychiatry and the politics are one enquiry, since only a single system could produce pathologies so exactly complementary.

The diagnosis extends to the metropole that employed the settler. A republic that tortures in its colonies and calls the torture pacification will, in Fanon's analysis, import the methods and the men home, and the corruption of the colonising democracy by its empire is a process he traces in the French case with a historian's specificity: the police who learned their trade in Algiers practising it in Paris, the parliament voting special powers and then deploring their use, the press managing the nation's knowledge of its own acts. He had read the same argument in Césaire's Discourse, where colonisation works to decivilise the coloniser, and his contribution was the clinical evidence and the institutional mechanism: not a metaphor of moral rot but a circuit, with postings, promotions, and pensions, through which the colony's practices became the metropole's habits. The readers who file Fanon as a theorist of the colonised alone have missed half the corpus's claim, which is that the Manichean world deforms everyone it contains, and that its abolition is therefore not the native's revenge upon the settler but the rescue of both from the species the system made of them. The new man of the conclusion has a European shadow, the man Europe might become once it stops being a settler civilisation, and Fanon's address to his European comrades in the final pages is unintelligible without it.

## Land, labour, and the colonial surplus: Fanon's economics

Fanon was not an economist, and his corpus contains no treatise on value; but it rests on an economic argument that should be extracted and stated, because the stretching is unintelligible without it. The argument begins from the land. For the colonised, he writes in the first chapter, the most essential and concrete value is the land, the soil that must guarantee bread and, above it, dignity; the colonial economy begins, accordingly, not with the factory but with the expropriation, the seizure of the territory's best acres for the settler and the compression of the colonised onto reserves of rock and sand from which a living must be begged. Every later feature of the system descends from that founding distribution: the peasant's poverty is not backwardness but arithmetic, too many mouths on too little and too poor land; the migration to the towns is the arithmetic's overflow; the wage the migrant accepts is set by the desperation the land settlement manufactured. An economics that starts from the labour contract, as the metropolitan model does, starts the story at its middle, and Fanon's insistence on beginning from the conquest's land settlement is his version of what Marx had analysed for Europe as the primitive accumulation, translated to a terrain where the accumulation never ended and never changed its racial uniform.

The land settlement also explains the peasantry's programme, which Fanon reports from the maquis rather than inventing for it: the fellah's demand was never abstract, it was the return of the stolen acres, the end of the chief's exactions and the tax farmer's visits, bread and the dignity of feeding his own household from his own soil, and the movement that carried that programme in its knapsack had an army wherever the programme was believed. A revolutionary theory for the colony that cannot state the land demand in its first sentence has, in his judgment, already failed the population it claims to describe, and the parties that arrived in the villages with programmes drafted in the capital's vocabulary learned the judgment at the price of the villages' allegiance. The land is thus both the economics' foundation and the agent thesis's root system: the class Fanon promoted is the class the land settlement made, and its revolutionary energy is the energy of a demand that can be stated in one word. The colonial economy, in his account, is organised as a siphon: raw materials and agricultural produce flow out through the settler's ports, manufactured goods flow back at monopoly prices, and the colony's own development is not neglected by accident but prevented by design, since a colony that processed its own wealth would cease to perform its function in the metropolitan economy. The national bourgeoisie chapter is this economics applied to the successor state: an elite trained inside the siphon can administer it, skim it, and decorate its intake, but cannot redirect it toward production without ceasing to be the class it is. Underdevelopment, in the vocabulary the next decade would standardise partly under his influence, is not a stage on the way to development but a product, manufactured by the same process that manufactures the metropole's wealth, the two being one event viewed from its two ends. His economics is therefore a theory of a single world economy with a racial constitution, which is why the fusion thesis could be stated as economics at all: the circuit that makes the settler rich and the native poor is one circuit, and its current is the colony's substance flowing outward.

The limits of the economics should be recorded beside its strengths. Fanon has little to say about the mechanisms of price, credit, and industrial organisation that a development strategy would need, nothing systematic about the terms on which a liberated economy should trade, and a confidence, inherited from the period's planning orthodoxies across the spectrum, that the state which had won the war could organise production by the same mobilisations that had won it. His economics is a theory of how the colony is robbed, not a manual for how the republic should be run, and the gap mattered: the states that claimed his authority inherited his diagnosis of the siphon without a Fanonian account of what to build in its place, and filled the gap, as the open problems section recorded, with the brokerage or the barracks. The diagnosis has outlasted the century's development fashions because it identified the mechanism rather than the mood; the manual's absence is the corpus's most consequential unfinished business, and the economists of the dependency generation who supplied the missing analytics, whatever their other quarrels with him, were completing his circuit diagram.

## The sequence reversed: nation first, and what follows from it

One consequence of the stretching reorganises the whole revolutionary timetable, and it has been less noticed than the agent thesis it governs. In the orthodox sequence Fanon inherited, the colonial revolution was assigned a stage: first the national and democratic struggle, conducted in alliance with the national bourgeoisie under the hegemony, ultimately, of the working class and its party; then, the conditions ripening, the socialist struggle against that same bourgeoisie. The schema presupposed that the political and the social could be separated in time, the flag first and the factories after. Fanon's Algeria refuted the separation in both directions. Because the state was racial, the national struggle was already the social struggle: to fight the settler's republic was to fight the wage scale, the land settlement, and the school system in the same act, and the peasant who rose for the nation rose simultaneously against the chief, the landlord, and the tax that the nation's enemies collected. And because the national bourgeoisie was an intermediary by constitution, the stage assigned to its leadership was a stage in which the revolution's social content would be negotiated away by its own managers, so that postponing the social question until after independence meant delivering it, bound, to the brokerage. The sequence in Fanon therefore collapses into simultaneity: the national liberation that does not carry its social transformation inside itself, in its methods, its education, and its treatment of the people's claims from the first day, will arrive at independence with the transformation already lost.

The collapse explains several features of the corpus that puzzle readers trained on the stage theory. It explains his indifference to the precise label of the coming regime and his ferocious attention to its methods: if the social content is decided inside the national struggle's conduct, then the struggle's internal arrangements, who decides, who is educated, who eats, are not preliminaries to the revolution but the revolution itself in its formative period. It explains the pitfalls chapter's timing, published before the independence it describes, as a wartime intervention rather than a posthumous wisdom: the stage theory's second act had to be fought during the first, or not at all. And it explains his distance from both camps of the period's Marxism, the orthodox who counselled patience through the stages and the romantics who counselled spontaneity against organisation, since his position denied the premise both shared, that form and content could be scheduled separately. Later liberation movements that attempted the simultaneity, and theorists of permanent and combined development who formalised versions of it, were working a seam he had opened under fire; the seam's presence in his work is the surest sign that the stretching was a theoretical event and not a change of personnel in the revolutionary drama.

## The prose itself: clinical, prophetic, and the risks of the register

Fanon's style is an argument, and a profile that ignored it would miss one of the mechanisms of both his influence and his misreading. He writes in two registers and modulates between them without warning. The clinical register is precise, taxonomic, and dry: symptoms are listed, mechanisms specified, cases distinguished, and the prose carries the authority of a man who has signed reports on which liberty and treatment depended. The prophetic register is incantatory and architectural: sentences lengthen into catalogues, the two towns rise in their stone and their hunger, the scriptural cadence sounds, and the reader is no longer being informed but arraigned. The alternation is the method made audible, since the corpus's claim is precisely that the case and the prophecy are the same material at different distances, and the style enforces the claim by refusing to let the reader settle into either distance for long. It also explains the hold of the writing on readers who dissent from every conclusion in it: the clinical passages earn a credit that the prophetic passages then spend, and the spending feels, from inside the reading, like evidence arriving rather than like rhetoric succeeding.

The register carries a risk, and the risk has a name in this profile's argument: the prophetic cadence is the part of Fanon that travels without the clinical ballast, the part that anthologies quote and movements chant, and it is the part most easily conscripted by the preface's reading of him, since Sartre's text is written entirely in a borrowed version of that register, prophecy without a clinic anywhere in sight. Fanon's own discipline, the case file at the end of the book, the failed experiment reported in the journal, the mechanism specified before the slogan is permitted, is the corpus's internal defence against its own music, and readers who honour the defence read him whole: the prophecy audited by the ward round, the ward round dignified by the prophecy. Teachers who set the first chapter without the fifth are not abridging him; they are disarming the audit and releasing the music, and the Fanon who then circulates, dangerous and intoxicating and simpler than any man who ever kept a clinic, is a collaboration between his laziest readers and his most famous sponsor, in which the author, who built the audit into the architecture, has the smallest share.

## Medicine as the revolution's evidence

The essay on medicine and colonialism in A Dying Colonialism deserves extraction, because it is the point where Fanon's two professions most completely explain each other and where his account of the colonised's suspicion of the coloniser's gifts reaches its subtlest form. The Algerian patient's refusal of European medicine, he argues, was never the superstition the colonial doctor diagnosed. It was a rational reading of an institution: the doctor arrived with the administration, his dispensary stood in the shadow of the gendarmerie, his campaigns coincided with censuses and conscriptions, and his science, whatever its private benevolence, functioned inside the colony as one of the conquest's instruments, so that refusing the tablet and the injection was a way of refusing the hand that offered them. The revolution reversed the valence by building a medicine of its own: the maquis's nurses and stretcher bearers, the clandestine clinics, the doctors of the movement who treated without the administration's forms, taught the population in practice that technique is neutral only when its ownership is, and the same villagers who had hidden their sick from the colonial dispensary carried their wounded for days to reach the movement's. Fanon's conclusion is characteristic and generalisable: a people's relation to any expert system, medical, educational, administrative, is determined by the political order the system serves, and the revolution that wants the people's trust must therefore build its own institutions and not merely capture the enemy's, a conclusion the pitfalls chapter would shortly apply to the state itself.

His own practice inside the movement enacted the doctrine. The health service he helped to organise and train for was simultaneously a clinical provision, a school of cadres, and the revolution's most persuasive argument in the countryside, since a population that has been treated by the future republic has already seceded in the part of life that matters most. That the theorist of violence spent the war years substantially as a medical organiser is not an irony to be savoured but a proportion to be learned: the corpus's centre of gravity is constructive, and the armed chapter that made its fame is one instrument among several in a project whose other instruments were the ward, the radio, the school, and the clinic. Readers who reverse the proportion, in either direction, misread the life as well as the books.

The training work deserves its own sentence in that proportion. The movement's health service had to be built from almost nothing, since the colonial order had trained Algerian doctors in numbers deliberately insufficient for the population and had concentrated what it trained in the settler's towns; Fanon's contribution, in Blida's last clandestine phase and then from Tunis, was pedagogical as much as clinical, the formation of nurses, orderlies, and medical auxiliaries from the ranks of the movement itself, young women among them in numbers that quietly revised the society's arrangements as the veil essay describes. A nurse trained by the revolution is a cadre the revolution owns outright, skilled, trusted, and unintimidated by the expert's mystique, and the health service's schools were, in the strict sense of his own theory, the new society rehearsing itself inside the war: competence without intermediaries, knowledge carried by the people it serves, an institution whose authority rested on its work rather than on the administration's stamp. When the pitfalls chapter later demands a party that teaches rather than commands, it is generalising from services like this one, where Fanon had watched the alternative constitution function under fire.

## Two towns, one economy: a close reading

The description of the two towns, in the first chapter of the final book, is the most anthologised passage Fanon wrote, and it is usually read as scene setting, the rhetoric before the argument. Read closely, it is the argument, the fusion thesis rendered as urban geography, and its details are economic propositions in disguise. The settler's town is introduced through its metabolism: it is a town of light and asphalt whose belly is permanently full of good things, a town that eats. The native town is introduced through the same organ: it is a hungry town, starved of bread, of meat, of shoes, of coal, of light. The pairing is exact and deliberate, because the two hungers are one fact: the settler town's satiety is not a parallel condition to the native town's hunger but its cause and its content, the same calories, the same coal, the same wages fund, appearing in one ledger as fullness and in the other as want. A reader who wants the colonial economy's whole theory in a paragraph can take it here, in the indicative mood and without a single abstraction: wealth and poverty are not distributed between the towns; they are manufactured as a pair, by one process, along a line that is patrolled.

The passage's other famous detail, the frontier of barracks and police stations, does the same work for the state. In a metropolitan city, the boundary between rich and poor districts is a gradient, enforced by price; in the colony it is a border, enforced by arms, because the distribution it protects could not survive a free movement of the hungry toward the full. The policeman and the soldier are therefore not the colonial order's emergency reserve but its daily constitution, the form in which the economic arrangement appears in the street, and Fanon's later description of them as the government's spokesmen to the native is precise institutional analysis wearing the dress of invective. Even the passage's zoological counterpoint, the native town described as kneeling and crouching, is a proposition: a population held in a posture, by identifiable holders, for identifiable gains, whose rising to its feet is therefore not a metaphor the book indulges but the literal event the book exists to theorise. Close reading of this kind is the antidote the corpus prescribes for its own reputation: attend to the sentences, and the prophet dissolves into the analyst he always was, whose most quoted purple passage turns out to be a balance sheet.

## The stretch in one page

The argument of this profile can be gathered, for the reader who has come with it this far, into the sequence the series exists to record. Fanon inherited a Marxism whose map of society had been drawn in the industrial metropole: an economic base determining a political and ideological superstructure, a working class appointed by its position to lead the transformation, race filed among the ideologies by which the order disguised itself. Confronted with the colony, where conquest had assigned every economic position by race in advance and enforced the assignment with a standing army, he announced that the analysis should always be slightly stretched, and the stretching, examined across the corpus, amounts to four operations. Determination is made reciprocal, wealth certifying whiteness as whiteness certifies wealth. Race is promoted from ideology to structure, a load bearing element of the economy itself. The revolutionary agent is reassigned, from the small and comparatively protected colonial working class to the landless peasantry and the urban dispossessed, with consequences this profile has judged severely. And the nation, which the doctrine suspected as a mystification, is recognised as the necessary form of a struggle whose enemy is a racial state, with the warning, developed into the corpus's most vindicated chapter, that the nation so won will be stolen by its intermediary class unless the struggle's own methods have educated a citizenry capable of keeping it. Around these operations stand the corrections with which the profile began: a violence chapter to be read apart from the preface that usurped it, its criticisms answered at full strength and its strongest claims surviving in qualified form; a mistranslated chapter title whose correction restores a phenomenology where English readers were given a dogma; and a clinical corpus, closing the final book and filling the recovered papers, that forbids the separation of the psychiatrist from the revolutionary on which the lazy Fanon depends. The profile's verdict is the one the evidence sustains: a Marxist, because the engine of the analysis, material life explaining consciousness, exploitation anatomised from the case outward, never stops running; a heretic, because the categories arrive at the colony and are rebuilt there; and, in the chapter nobody read, the most accurate prophet the decolonising century produced.

## The conference years: Fanon and the Afro-Asian moment

Between the resignation and the deathbed, Fanon lived the most public years of his life in the conference halls of the decolonising world, and the setting shaped the theory's final form. The Afro-Asian moment, as historians name the decade of Bandung and Accra, was an attempt to constitute the colonised world as a political subject in its own right, neither Washington's nor Moscow's, and Fanon was among its most tireless intellectual workers: present at the Paris congress of Black writers and artists in 1956 where his paper on racism and culture argued that a people's culture cannot flower under occupation; at the All-African Peoples' Conference at Accra in 1958, where the Algerian delegation he served carried the war into the assembly of a continent; at Rome in 1959 for the second congress of Black writers, where the national culture argument reached its mature form. The conferences taught him the era's two lessons at once. The solidarity was real, and it was the first world scale audience the colonised had ever addressed as authors rather than as evidence. And the solidarity's custodians were already becoming the class his third chapter would anatomise, ministers and party secretaries whose fluency in the new diplomacy was itself a brokerage skill, so that the pitfalls chapter can be read as the conference years' confidential report, filed by their most decorated delegate. His insistence, in speech after speech, that African unity be built from the peoples upward, through their movements, their armies, and their economies, rather than proclaimed from the summit downward, is the same argument in its diplomatic dress, and its defeat in the decade that followed, when the summits inherited the continent, is the largest single confirmation his prediction has received.

The ambassador's posting to Accra in 1960 concentrated these years into a single appointment. Ghana under Kwame Nkrumah was the conference world's capital and its laboratory, the first sub-Saharan colony to win independence and the loudest advocate of the continental project, and the provisional government sent Fanon there as its representative with instructions that amounted to a contradiction he was left to embody: win recognition and support for a government in exile from the very fraternity of new states whose class character his analysis distrusted. He worked the posting as he had worked the ward, building the institution he needed inside the one he had been given, arguing in Accra's assemblies and in the continent's press for the armed coordination of the liberation movements, for the southern front his Saharan mission then surveyed, and for a unity of peoples that would discipline the unity of presidents. The diplomats who dealt with him remembered a representative who negotiated like a theorist, conceding nothing on the nature of the coming order while he bargained for its birth certificates, and the record of the posting, read beside the pitfalls chapter written in the same months, is the record of a man practising loyalty and diagnosis in the same hour, each at full strength, neither anaesthetising the other.

## For students and exam candidates

Fanon is examined as a thinker of decolonisation, of race, and of revolution, and the marking traps are the three this profile was built to disarm. Do not attribute the preface to Fanon: examiners treat the conflation as the signature of the unread candidate, and the correction, Sartre wrote it, it is more inflammatory than the chapter, a famous 1970 attack leans on it, is the cheapest credit available on the topic. Do not summarise the violence chapter as a celebration: state the three claims separately, descriptive, strategic, and constitutive, and evaluate them separately, with Arendt named for the power objection and Woddis for the agent objection, since unnamed evaluation earns nothing. Do learn the third chapter as the answer to any question about Fanon's prediction or his view of postcolonial states, and learn the stretching formula as the answer to any question about his Marxism, with race and class fused as its content and the colonial working class's position as its consequence. ## For teachers and seminar leaders

The most teachable text in the corpus is the one the syllabuses reach last: the chapter on national consciousness, which gives a seminar a prediction with a mechanism, a class with a balance sheet, and a half century of evidence to test both against, and which reliably reverses the room's expectation of what Fanon is for. Pair it with the spontaneity chapter and the seminar has the whole argument in two sittings, energy and its ceiling, organisation and its corruption, with the Woddis critique supplying the prosecution and Fanon's own third chapter, awkwardly for every party, supplying half the prosecution's evidence. The preface affair makes the finest single exercise in source criticism the topic affords: set the preface and the first chapter side by side, have the room separate the claims by author before any evaluation is permitted, and the lesson about attribution, mediation, and the manufacture of reputations teaches itself. Handle the period's racial vocabulary as this article does, characterised and cited, never reproduced for effect; the texts analyse such language and a classroom that repeats it has mistaken the analysis for the material.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The live research fronts are three. The archive front has been transformed by the recovered writings published as Alienation and Freedom, which supply the clinical and journalistic bulk against which the three canonical books can now be read as the visible fraction of a continuous practice; work that cites the famous texts without the archive will date quickly. The reception front remains the dispute between the psychoanalytic Fanon of postcolonial studies and the political Fanon of the materialist reclamation, Sekyi-Otu and Gibson against the textual reading, with the translation history, two English versions of each major book and a chapter title mistranslated for four decades, as the dispute's material substrate; the series treatment of marxism vs postcolonial theory, linked from this article's reception section, states the rival claims in full. The third front is the export question this profile has pressed throughout: how far the categories, calibrated on settler Algeria, travel to other colonial and postcolonial formations, which is an empirical programme rather than a loyalty test, and the one most likely to produce findings Fanon himself would have had to stretch for. Journalists reaching for Fanon on any contemporary disorder should be refused the loan of his authority until they can say which Fanon they mean, the psychiatrist of the case files, the strategist of the maquis, or the preface's ventriloquised prophet, since the three give three different quotations and only two of them are his.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was Frantz Fanon?

Frantz Fanon (1925 to 1961) was a psychiatrist and political theorist from Martinique, then a French Caribbean colony, who became the most influential analyst of colonial domination of his century. Trained in medicine at Lyon after volunteering for the Free French forces as a teenager, he took charge of a psychiatric service at Blida in Algeria in 1953, resigned in 1956 in a celebrated letter refusing to treat patients for return to a system that manufactured their disorders, and served the Algerian independence movement as writer, doctor, and diplomat until leukaemia killed him at thirty-six. His three books, Black Skin, White Masks, A Dying Colonialism, and the posthumously assembled essays frame a final volume, The Wretched of the Earth, dictated in the last months of his life.

### Q: Was Fanon a Marxist?

He was, by the standard his own formula sets: a thinker who adopted the materialist engine of Marxism, its insistence that arrangements of labour and property explain the life of the mind, while openly modifying the doctrine's inherited categories for colonial conditions. He held no party card and submitted to no orthodoxy, reassigned the revolutionary role from the industrial working class to the peasantry and the urban dispossessed, and treated race as a structure of the colonial economy rather than as its decorative ideology. Orthodox critics such as Jack Woddis judged the modifications heresy; defenders reply that revising categories under the pressure of evidence is the tradition's own founding gesture. The label is therefore best given with its qualification attached: a Marxist of the stretching, loyal to the method and disloyal, on evidence, to the metropolitan inventory.

### Q: What does stretching Marxist analysis mean?

It is Fanon's instruction, issued in the opening chapter of his final book, that the concepts of Marxism must be adjusted, slightly in wording and substantially in effect, before they are applied to a colony. The adjustment has a definite content. Where the metropolitan scheme lets position in production settle a person's social place, with ideas and prejudices following after, the colony arranges matters so that racial membership fixes economic fate beforehand, land, wages, schooling, and law allotted along the colour line by armed authority. An analysis that arrives expecting the European sequence will therefore misread the society at every point, mistaking the constitution for a symptom. Stretching names the correction: keep the materialist engine, rebuild the categories around the evidence, and announce the rebuilding as the finding.

### Q: What is Black Skin, White Masks about?

Published in 1952, when its author was twenty-six, the book is a clinical and philosophical study of what colonial racism does to the inner life of the colonised, above all the French speaking Antillean educated to consider himself French. It moves through language, intimate life, a colonial psychologist's theory of dependency, and a celebrated chapter of lived experience, arguing throughout that the inferiority the Black patient presents is not a trait but an installation, put in place by an economic and racial order and then experienced, in Fanon's coinage, at the level of the skin itself. Its method, which he called sociogeny, reads private symptoms as public evidence. The book closes by refusing both consolations on offer, the coloniser's humanism and a museum of recovered glory, and asking instead for a man who questions.

### Q: What does Fanon argue about violence?

Three separable things, which the summaries habitually fuse. First, a description: colonial government rests on force from its founding conquest to its daily policing, so the colonised inhabit violence as a permanent environment rather than an occasional event. Second, a strategic judgment: a caste so installed does not retire on petition, so liberation struggles against settler orders become armed whether or not their theorists wish it. Third, a clinical hypothesis from his psychiatric practice: participation in the struggle, including its armed episodes, can dissolve the inferiority and self directed aggression that the system stores in its subjects, remaking the patient as an agent. The first claim is now close to scholarly common ground; the second held for the settler colonies he knew; the third remains the contested heart of the chapter, pressed hard by Arendt and by Marxist critics alike.

### Q: Is the violence chapter misread?

Yes, in a specific and documentable way. The first edition carried a preface by Jean-Paul Sartre far more incandescent than the chapter it introduced, celebrating in its own voice what Fanon's text analyses, and the compound was then read, quoted, and attacked under Fanon's name alone for decades. The celebrated philosophical assault of 1970 builds its portrait of a violence intoxicated Fanon substantially on formulations that are the preface's. Separating the texts does not acquit the chapter, whose own claims about struggle remaking the subject are strong and were answered at full strength within this article; it restores the proportions, so that criticism can aim at a clinical and strategic argument instead of at a Parisian peroration fathered on a Martinican doctor who died weeks after his book appeared.

### Q: Who does Fanon think will make the revolution?

The peasantry, and beside it the dispossessed crowds of the colonial city. His reasoning is positional. The organised working class of the colony is, in his account, small, comparatively paid and protected, and in its European settler form an active garrison of the racial order, so its interest points toward a better bargain inside the system. The landless cultivator and the shantytown migrant have been given nothing the system can threaten to withdraw, and the countryside's uprisings, which began the Algerian war while the parties hesitated, are his standing exhibit. He rehabilitates even the lumpenproletariat, the stratum Marx had filed as reaction's reserve, as combustible material, while warning that the colonial power recruits its informers from the same streets. The assignment has not been vindicated as the diagnosis has: organised parties and armies, not spontaneity, are what history shows winning.

### Q: What did Fanon predict about independence?

That the class independence installed would betray its commission in specified ways. The national bourgeoisie of the new state, formed under colonialism as brokers and intermediaries without capital or industry of their own, would not build production; it would administer access, skim the passage of foreign capital, and convert the state into its commission house. The ruling party would decay from the people's instrument into a screen for advancement and then into an apparatus of surveillance; the leader would gather the nation into his person; the interior that made the revolution would be starved for the capital's benefit; and national unity, denied economic content, would be replaced by ethnic and regional clientelism, chauvinism as a governing programme. He published the prediction before the independences it described, which is why the chapter that contains it, the least read in his lifetime, became the most cited in the decades after.

### Q: Is The Fact of Blackness a mistranslation?

Yes. The fifth chapter of Black Skin, White Masks bears in French a title meaning the lived experience of the Black man, a phenomenological announcement: an account of racism as undergone by a subject, structured by scenes, a gaze, a body schema collapsing under a stranger's frightened cry. Charles Lam Markmann's English version of 1967 rendered the title as The Fact of Blackness, which converts testimony into doctrine, an experience into an essence, and generations of Anglophone readers duly received a dogmatic assertion where Fanon had written a disciplined report from inside an encounter. Richard Philcox's translation of 2008 restored the title's sense. The episode is the small, checkable instance of this article's large claim, that Fanon's meaning has repeatedly been decided by mediations he did not choose, renderings and prefaces through which the world received a different author.

### Q: Where should you start with Fanon?

With the fifth chapter of Black Skin, White Masks, in the Philcox translation, where the method is entire in twenty pages, then the whole of that first book; then The Wretched of the Earth complete and in its author's order, postponing Sartre's preface until the five chapters have been met unaccompanied, so the displacement can be felt rather than merely believed; then A Dying Colonialism, whose essays on the veil, the radio, and colonial medicine are the most approachable things he wrote; and finally the essays collected as Toward the African Revolution, for the resignation letter and the conference analyses. Beginning with the famous first chapter, as the reputation instructs, starts the reader at the most mediated and compressed point of the corpus, without the psychology that makes the politics intelligible.

### Q: How does the preface displacement change the book's reception?

It changed the book's temperature, its audience, and its charges. Sartre's preface, written at the dying author's request by the most famous intellectual in France, told European readers before page one that the volume was an intoxication and supplied the phrases, redemptive killing, man remade through violence, that reviewers quoted as the book's message. The student movements of the following years received the compound as a doctrine of cleansing violence; the philosophical attack of 1970 answered the compound under Fanon's name; and the scholarly recovery of the actual chapter, clinical, strategic, and hedged with warnings about violence's own pathologies, has had to be conducted against sixty years of quotation ever since. The displacement is this article's namable claim because it is the precondition of fair assessment: separate the sponsor's peroration from the author's argument, and a different, harder, more interesting book appears.

### Q: What should a student separate between Fanon's psychiatry and his politics?

Nothing, and the attempt to separate them is the error this article most wants to retire. The politics is the clinic's conclusion drawn at scale: a doctor who finds his patients' disorders manufactured by the colonial situation, who watches a therapeutic method fail because it imports the coloniser's sociability, who treats the torturer and the tortured in adjoining rooms, does not change professions when he concludes that the situation itself is the pathogen and must be ended. The final book performs the unity in its architecture, opening in political theory and closing in case files. What a student should separate is something else: Fanon's texts from Sartre's preface, his claims from his summarists' slogans, and each of his arguments, descriptive, strategic, clinical, from the others, grading them one by one instead of inheriting a verdict on the bundle.

### Q: Why did the colony force the categories to stretch?

Because the colony's facts refused the metropolitan scheme at every joint. Land had been assigned by conquest along racial lines, so property, the category on which the scheme builds, arrived pre sorted by colour. Wages, schools, hospitals, and courts were distributed by the same line, so that a person's economic position could not be stated without stating his race, nor his race without his position. The state was not a committee above the classes but a garrison between two legally distinct populations. And the class the scheme appointed to lead, the industrial proletariat, was in the settler colonies a small enclave whose European members drew dividends from the arrangement they were summoned to abolish. A theory faithful to its own materialist rule, that categories must answer to the evidence, had either to stretch or to describe a country that did not exist.

### Q: How does the five chapters assessed table order a reading?

The table in this article arranges the final book as its author arranged it, and the order is the reading. Begin with the violence chapter read as three claims rather than as a slogan, and read it without the preface. Proceed to spontaneity, where the revolution's energy and its ceiling are weighed together, then to the trials of national consciousness, the prediction to be tested against every postcolonial state the reader can examine, then to national culture and its three phases of the colonised intellectual, and close with the case files, where the book's assertions return as patients and the argument's evidence is exhibited in a clinician's hand. Each row of the table supplies the chapter's argument, the reception that distorted or neglected it, the strongest criticism it must answer, and the verdict of the decades, so a reader can hold the whole assessment while working through any single part.

### Q: What separates stretching an analysis from abandoning it?

Three tests, all of which Fanon's procedure passes and a mere relabelling would fail. The engine is retained: material arrangements are still summoned to explain consciousness, exploitation is still the order's central fact, and no appeal to spirit, blood, or destiny is allowed to do explanatory work. The modification is announced and evidenced: each rebuilt category, the fused race and class, the promoted peasantry, the nation as necessary form, is presented with the colonial facts that forced the rebuilding, open to inspection and to the counterevidence this article has weighed, including the agent thesis's failure at history's examination. And the revision is reversible in principle: where a different colony's evidence restores the orthodox category's traction, the stretched analysis itself instructs the reader to relax the stretch. Abandonment keeps the vocabulary and changes the subject; stretching keeps the subject and earns the vocabulary again, colony by colony.
