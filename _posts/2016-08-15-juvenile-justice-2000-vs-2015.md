## Juvenile Justice Act 2000 vs 2015: the decision this comparison has to make

The Juvenile Justice Act 2000 vs 2015 comparison is usually conducted as an argument about a single provision, the power to try a child of sixteen or above as an adult, and it is usually won by whichever side describes that provision loudest. That is a poor way to judge two statutes. Each of these Acts is a complete machine with two working halves: a track for the child accused of an offence, and a track for the child who needs the state to act as a parent. A comparison that reads only the first half of each machine will reach a verdict about a fraction of the law and present it as a verdict about the whole. The question this article answers is the one a reader actually has to decide: taking both tracks together, did the statute of 2015 improve on the statute of 2000, and if the answer depends on where one looks, which part of the machine should decide it?

![The Juvenile Justice Act 2000 and the Juvenile Justice Act 2015 compared across ten dimensions - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-38.webp)

The framing of the two titles as opponents, one Act against the other, also misleads in a quieter way, and the comparison is fairer once the misdirection is named. These statutes are not rival systems between which a citizen chooses, as two tax codes or two tenancy regimes might be. They are successive occupants of a single office: each repealed its predecessor, each inherited the same two-track design, and each was described by its sponsors as the completion of the work the earlier one began. The 2015 Act is unintelligible except as a revision of the 2000 Act, section by section, and the 2000 Act is unintelligible except as a revision of 1986. A versus framing invites a scoreline. What the material supports is closer to an audit of a revision: which clauses of the inherited text the revisers rewrote, which they retained without a second thought, and whether the rewrites, taken together, made the instrument better at the job its own preamble sets for it.

The stakes of getting the comparison right are not academic. A lawyer holding a file in which the offence predates the newer Act needs to know which statute governs, and the answer changes the forum, the procedure and the maximum outcome. An aspirant facing the standard examination question on the two Acts needs the differences organised by dimension rather than by slogan. A researcher needs to know which of the changes were real changes in the legal position and which were continuations wearing a new section number. And a reader who followed the public argument of 2015, an argument conducted almost entirely about one clause, deserves to be told what else the replacement statute did while the argument was happening elsewhere. The method here is the one the series applies to every comparison: state both positions exactly, dimension by dimension, mark the direction of each change, and let the verdict be derived from the count rather than asserted before it.

One discipline governs everything that follows. The statute being compared on the 2015 side is the statute as it stands after its amendment of 2021, not the text as enacted, because a comparison drawn against a superseded text would misdescribe the law a reader has to use. Where the 2021 amendment changed a dimension, the change is flagged at that dimension rather than folded silently into the 2015 column.

## Two statutes, one design

The older statute is the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2000, Act 56 of 2000. It received the assent of the President on 30 December 2000 and was brought into force on 1 April 2001. It repealed the Juvenile Justice Act, 1986, Act 53 of 1986, and its preamble states its reasons on its face: the Government of India had ratified the Convention on the Rights of the Child on 11 December 1992, and the law relating to juveniles was being re-enacted bearing in mind the standards the Convention prescribes, together with the United Nations Standard Minimum Rules for the Administration of Juvenile Justice of 1985, known as the Beijing Rules, and the United Nations Rules for the Protection of Juveniles Deprived of their Liberty of 1990. The 2000 Act was, in other words, a compliance statute: its purpose was to bring a patchwork of state children Acts and a limited central Act of 1986 up to an international standard India had accepted eight years earlier.

The newer statute is the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, which is Act 2 of 2016 in the numbering of the Gazette, having received assent on 31 December 2015 and commenced on 15 January 2016. Its enactment, its passage through Parliament and the controversy that surrounded one of its provisions are the subject of this series' account of [the Juvenile Justice Act 2015 explained](/2016/05/15/juvenile-justice-act-2015-explained/), and this comparison does not retell that story. What matters here is the statute's shape. The 2015 Act repealed the 2000 Act and re-enacted the same two-track design at greater length and in greater detail: a child in conflict with law goes before a Juvenile Justice Board, a child in need of care and protection goes before a Child Welfare Committee, and the two tracks never share a forum. Both statutes are built on that single architectural decision, and both take their short title from it, care and protection first in the sentence and first in the scheme.

The frames around the two statutes differ in one respect worth noticing before the dimensions begin. The 2000 Act carries its philosophy in its preamble and in the structure of its chapters; it nowhere lists governing principles. The 2015 Act opens with a chapter of sixteen general principles in Section 3, among them the principle of the best interest of the child, the principle of diversion away from judicial proceedings, and the principle of a fresh start, under which all past records of a child under the system should be erased except in special circumstances. A principles chapter does not decide cases by itself, but it gives every Board, Committee and court under the Act a stated canon of construction, and the difference between a statute that announces its principles and one that leaves them to be inferred is a real difference in how the law argues with itself.

The sixteen principles reward a closer look, because several of them do work in the comparison that follows. The principle of presumption of innocence holds that a child shall be presumed innocent of any mala fide or criminal intent up to the age of eighteen, a presumption the transfer provision qualifies in practice without repealing in terms. The principle of non-stigmatising semantics directs that adversarial or accusatory words are not to be used in processes pertaining to a child, which is why the 2015 Act's vocabulary speaks of a child in conflict with law and of findings rather than convictions. The principle of institutionalisation as a measure of last resort subordinates every placement order in the Act to a prior question about whether the child can be kept out of an institution altogether. The principle of non-waiver of rights forecloses the argument that a child, or anyone acting for the child, surrendered a protection by failing to invoke it. None of these principles has an equivalent stated text in the 2000 Act, whose nearest approach to them is distributed among its operative provisions: the bail rule, the identity prohibition, the removal of disqualification. The 2015 Act's scheme is therefore not merely longer. It is self-describing in a way the older Act never attempted, and when the two texts conflict in spirit, the newer one has given its interpreters a printed tiebreaker and the older one has not. That is a strengthening of a diffuse kind, and it is recorded here rather than in the table, because the table scores dimensions on which a child's position measurably changes, and a canon of construction changes positions only at one remove. The wider frame in which both statutes sit, the constitutional provisions for children and the neighbouring protective statutes, is mapped in [the guide to child protection laws in India](/2016/04/01/child-protection-laws-india-guide/), which owns that frame; this article stays inside the two juvenile justice statutes and their direct lineage.

That lineage runs through one earlier replacement. The Act of 1986, which the 2000 Act repealed, was the first central statute on the subject, and it defined its central term by sex: a juvenile was a boy who had not attained the age of sixteen years or a girl who had not attained the age of eighteen years. The same child, accused of the same act, entered or escaped the protective system depending on a classification the Convention does not recognise. The 2000 Act's Section 2(k) replaced that definition with a single line: juvenile or child means a person who has not completed the eighteenth year of age. Everything in the comparison that follows happens downstream of that sentence, because both statutes inherit it.

## The age line: the change that never happened

### Did the age of a child change between the 2000 Act and the 2015 Act?

No. Both statutes draw the line at the same place: a child is a person who has not completed eighteen years of age. The 2000 Act set that line for boys and girls alike, replacing the split ages of the 1986 Act, and the 2015 Act kept it, so the belief that it lowered the age of juvenility is wrong.

The age dimension is the most misreported in the entire comparison, and it is worth being exact about what each text does. Under the 2000 Act, Section 2(k) defines the juvenile or child by the single age of eighteen, and Section 2(l) defines a juvenile in conflict with law as a juvenile alleged to have committed an offence. Under the 2015 Act, Section 2(12) defines a child as a person who has not completed eighteen years of age, and Section 2(13) defines a child in conflict with law as a child alleged or found to have committed an offence who has not completed eighteen years of age on the date of commission of such offence. The second definition does quiet work: by pinning the status to the date of commission inside the definition itself, the 2015 Act writes into the text the rule the courts had already built on the 2000 Act, that juvenility is a question about the day of the act and not about the day of arrest, production or trial.

What the 2015 Act changed was never the line. It changed what can happen to a child who stands on the older side of it but on the younger side of eighteen, by creating a class of children, those of sixteen and above accused of heinous offences, who may be moved out of the protective track after an assessment. The debate about whether that movement should exist, who argued for it and who against, and what the courts had held about the age line before Parliament acted, belongs to the article that owns the controversy, and the two statutes' shared age line is all the comparison needs from it. For the table at the end of this article, the age row is therefore marked unchanged, and it is the only row on which the loudest public claim about the 2015 Act, that it reduced the age of childhood, is simply false as a statement about the text.

There is a further continuity inside the age dimension that practitioners rely on and summaries omit. Both statutes route a doubtful age to a determination exercise rather than to the criminal court's ordinary assumptions. The 2000 Act dealt with presumption and determination of age in Section 49 and with the claim of juvenility, after 2006, in Section 7A. The 2015 Act deals with the claim in the procedure around the Board and with determination of age in Section 94, under which the Board or Committee seeks evidence in a stated order of preference, beginning with school and municipal records and turning to medical opinion only in their absence. The machinery is more elaborately specified in the newer Act; the destination, age on the date of the offence, is identical.

The order of preference in Section 94 deserves its sentence, because it reverses the instinct of the older practice. Under the 2000 Act, age determination habitually collapsed into a medical contest, with ossification tests and their margins of error deciding a question that school registers often answered on paper. Section 94(2) of the 2015 Act subordinates the medical route to the documentary one: the Board or Committee seeks first the date of birth certificate from the school, or the matriculation or equivalent certificate, then the birth certificate issued by a municipal authority or a panchayat, and only in the absence of both a medical opinion from a duly constituted medical board. Where the evidence leaves the matter at the boundary, the settled approach under both regimes gives the benefit of the margin to the individual, taking the lower end of the age range the evidence supports. A hierarchy of proof is a small thing beside the transfer provision, but in the run of cases it decides more childhoods than the assessment does, since age is disputed in a far larger class of cases than heinousness is. On this sub-dimension the 2015 Act is the more careful text, and the care is of the same character as the registration and criteria changes: unglamorous, procedural, and aimed at the point where the system actually touches the child.

## Adult trial: the one door the 2015 Act opened

Under the 2000 Act, the door to a criminal court did not exist for a child, and the statute is emphatic about the closure. Section 16 provides that no juvenile in conflict with law shall be sentenced to death or to imprisonment, or committed to prison in default of payment of a fine or of furnishing security. Section 18 provides that no juvenile shall be charged with or tried for an offence together with a person who is not a juvenile, and that where such a joint charge arises the Board shall direct separate trials. The gravest allegation in the penal code, laid against a child of seventeen, therefore went to the same Board, under the same inquiry procedure, with the same menu of outcomes, as a petty theft laid against a child of thirteen. The system's answer to gravity was not a different forum but a different disposition inside its own walls.

### What could the Board do under the 2000 Act when the offence was grave and the child was over sixteen?

It kept the case. The proviso to Section 16 of the 2000 Act answered gravity inside the system: where a juvenile over sixteen had committed an offence so serious, or whose conduct was such, that a special home was inadequate, the Board could order the child kept in a place of safety and report it to the State Government.

That proviso is the honest ancestor of the transfer debate, because it shows the 2000 Act's own draftsmen confronting the same anxiety the 2015 Act answered differently: the fear that the system's maximum response is not enough for its gravest cases. The 2000 answer was custodial and internal. The 2015 answer is jurisdictional. Under the 2015 Act, a child of sixteen or above alleged to have committed a heinous offence is subject to a preliminary assessment by the Board under Section 15, an assessment of the child's mental and physical capacity to commit the offence, the child's ability to understand the consequences of the offence, and the circumstances in which the offence was allegedly committed. The Board is to complete that assessment within three months of the child's first production, and it may take the assistance of experienced psychologists or psycho-social workers. If the Board is satisfied that the matter should be disposed of by itself, it proceeds as a Board. If it is satisfied that the child should be tried as an adult, it transfers the case under Section 18(3) to the Children's Court, a designated court of session, which then decides the question afresh under Section 19: it may try the child as an adult, or it may decide that the child should not be so tried and inquire into the matter itself as a Board.

Two limits on the far side of transfer belong in any accurate statement of the 2015 position, and both are continuities with the older Act rather than novelties. Section 21 of the 2015 Act forbids an order of death, and forbids life imprisonment without the possibility of release, against a child in conflict with law, including a child tried as an adult. And a transferred child who is convicted spends the custodial part of the sentence in a place of safety until the age of twenty one, at which point Section 20 requires the Children's Court to review the child's reformative progress and decide between release on conditions and transfer to a jail for the remainder of the term. The 2015 Act therefore opens a door the 2000 Act welded shut, and then builds a corridor behind the door that no adult prisoner walks through. Whether the door should exist at all is the controversy; the full argument on both sides, the parliamentary committee's contrary advice and the government's reasons for proceeding, is set out in [the juvenile age debate in Parliament](/2016/06/01/juvenile-age-debate-india/) and is not reopened here.

The courts have since narrowed the doorway, and the narrowing is part of the 2015 side of the comparison because it defines the power as it actually operates. In Shilpa Mittal v. State of NCT of Delhi, decided on 9 January 2020, the Supreme Court held that an offence carrying no minimum punishment of seven years cannot be treated as heinous, and that the unclassified fourth category of offences, those with a maximum above seven years but no minimum or a minimum below seven years, is to be treated as serious rather than heinous until Parliament decides otherwise. The transfer gate therefore does not open for a large family of grave offences at all. In Barun Chandra Thakur v. Master Bholu, decided in 2022, the Court treated the preliminary assessment as a delicate and demanding exercise directed at the capacity of the individual child, in which expert assistance is the expected route rather than an ornament. Both decisions are analysed in the pillar article on the 2015 Act; for the comparison, what matters is that the weakening this row records is a weakening as narrowed by the courts, not the wider power the first summaries of the Act described.

For the table, the adult trial row is marked weakened, and it is the row the whole public argument was about. A system that cannot move any child to a criminal court protects every child in it at the level the system itself sets. A system that can move some children, on an assessment of capacity, protects those children less, by design, whatever the corridor behind the door. No honest comparison can mark that row anything else.

## Classification: the sorting machine the older Act never had

The 2000 Act contains no classification of offences at all. An offence is an offence: the definition in Section 2(n) reaches any offence punishable under any law in force, and the Board's procedure makes no structural distinction between the trivial and the grave, beyond the four-month discipline the Act places on completing an inquiry and the disposition the Board chooses at the end. The statute trusts the Board with the whole range and sorts nothing in advance.

The 2015 Act builds a sorting machine into its definitions chapter. Section 2(45) defines petty offences as those for which the maximum punishment under the penal law is imprisonment for up to three years. Section 2(54) defines serious offences as those for which the punishment is imprisonment for a term between three and seven years. Section 2(33) defines heinous offences as those for which the minimum punishment is imprisonment for seven years or more. The classification is not decorative. It routes procedure: Section 14(5) has the Board dispose of petty offences through summary proceedings and conduct the inquiry into serious offences by following the procedure for trial in summons cases, and the heinous class alone can trigger the preliminary assessment and the possibility of transfer. It also routes time, since the Act attaches different expectations of expedition to the classes, and it routes the fresh start, since the records rule in Section 24 treats a heinous conviction after adult trial differently from every other finding, as the next-but-one section of this article explains.

The classification has one further consequence that practitioners feel and summaries miss: it makes the drafting of the charge outcome-determinative at the threshold. Because the tier is fixed by the punishment clause of the offence charged and not by the facts alleged, the same street fight can enter the system as a serious case or as a heinous one depending on the penal provision the police record, and the preliminary assessment machinery either engages or does not on the strength of that record. The pillar article on the 2015 Act works the point through a single stabbing charged two ways, and it is the clearest demonstration that the classification is not a passive label but an allocation of cases among procedures. The 2000 Act had no such threshold sensitivity, because it had no thresholds: the Board received the grave and the trivial through the same door and sorted them itself, by disposition. Whether a statute should sort in advance by punishment clause, exposing the sort to the charging decision, or sort afterwards by a bench's judgment of the whole case, is a genuine design question on which the two Acts take opposite positions, and it is a better question than most of the ones the public argument asked.

As a dimension of comparison, classification is best marked neutral, and the marking deserves a sentence of defence because both camps in the larger argument try to claim it. A classification is a device, not an outcome. It neither protects nor exposes a child until it is connected to a consequence, and its consequences run in both directions: the same three tiers that expose the top tier to transfer also formalise the lightness of the bottom tier, giving the child accused of a petty offence a procedural promise of summary treatment that the 2000 Act's undifferentiated inquiry did not state. The verdict on classification therefore belongs to the rows it feeds, adult trial above and the fresh start below, and the row itself is recorded as machinery: new in 2015, absent in 2000, direction neutral on its face.

## Ceilings inside the system: three years in both

Strip away the transfer question, and the maximum coercive outcome for a child kept inside the juvenile system is identical under the two statutes, a continuity that surprises readers who absorbed the 2015 Act as a general hardening. Under the 2000 Act, Section 15 sets out the orders the Board may pass after an inquiry, running from advice and admonition through probation of good conduct under the care of a parent, guardian or fit institution, to placement in a special home, and the special home order is framed around a period of three years, reducible for recorded reasons where the Board thinks a shorter stay expedient. Under the 2015 Act, Section 18 sets out a materially similar menu for a child found in conflict with law, ending in placement in a special home or a place of safety or with a fit facility or fit person for a period not exceeding three years. The ceiling of the internal system did not move.

The bail position is likewise a continuity, and an important one, because it is the provision of each Act that a police station meets first. Section 12 of the 2000 Act made bail the rule for a juvenile, releasable with or without surety notwithstanding anything in the Code of Criminal Procedure, subject to recorded exceptions where release would bring the child into association with known criminals or expose the child to moral, physical or psychological danger or defeat the ends of justice. Section 12 of the 2015 Act carries the same rule and the same three exceptions forward for a person who is apparently a child. A comparison that lists only what changed would miss the fact that the two provisions a child encounters at the system's front door, apprehension by a special juvenile police unit and production before the Board within twenty four hours, and bail as the default, were settled in 2000 and deliberately retained.

The identity protection is a third continuity of the same kind. Section 21 of the 2000 Act prohibited the publication of a juvenile's name, address, school or other particulars calculated to lead to identification, in any report of a proceeding under the Act. Section 74 of the 2015 Act states the prohibition more elaborately, extends it across media and to children in need of care and protection and child victims and witnesses, and attaches a penalty of imprisonment up to six months or a fine up to two lakh rupees or both. The direction of travel inside a continuity can still matter: the 2015 text widens the protected class and prices the breach. But the row in the table is the ceiling, and the ceiling is unchanged, and any verdict on the 2015 Act that proceeds as if the whole internal system had been made harsher is working from the transfer row alone.

## At the front door: apprehension, production and the discipline of time

Between the ceiling and the fresh start sits the part of each statute a child experiences first, and here the comparison is mostly a study in refinement rather than replacement. Apprehension under the 2000 Act, in the form the 2006 amendment gave Section 10, placed the apprehended juvenile immediately under the charge of the special juvenile police unit or the designated police officer, who was to produce the child before the Board without loss of time and within twenty four hours of apprehension, excluding journey time, and the section forbade in terms what practice most needed forbidden: in no case was a juvenile in conflict with law to be placed in a police lockup or lodged in a jail. Section 10 of the 2015 Act carries the same architecture forward, the special juvenile police unit, the designated officer, the twenty four hours, the prohibition on lockup and jail, and Section 107 builds the supporting establishment around it: a Child Welfare Police Officer, not below the rank of Assistant Sub-Inspector, designated in every police station, and a special juvenile police unit in every district, staffed also by two social workers. The older Act's unit existed on paper in every district the state notified; the newer Act names the officer in every police station, which is the difference between a system designed at district level and one designed at the point of first contact.

Information follows the same pattern of continuity with added specification. Section 13 of the 2000 Act required the parent or guardian, or failing them the probation officer, to be informed of the apprehension. Section 13 of the 2015 Act keeps the duty and ties it to the machinery of the inquiry, the social investigation report that the Board must obtain and consider before it disposes of the child, a document the 2000 Act also required, in Section 15(2), through a probation officer or a recognised voluntary organisation. The report is the child's biography in official form, family, schooling, neighbourhood, the circumstances of the offence, and the continuity of the requirement across both statutes is a reminder that the reformative theory of the system, decide about this child on knowledge of this child, was fully formed in 2000.

Time is where the newer Act tightens hardest. The 2000 Act's Section 14 required the Board's inquiry to be completed within four months of its commencement, unless the Board extended the period having regard to the circumstances of the case and, in special cases, after recording reasons in writing; the care track's inquiry before the Committee carried the same four-month discipline. Section 14 of the 2015 Act fixes the four months from the date of first production rather than from the commencement of the inquiry, caps the Board's own extension at two further months on recorded reasons, requires the preliminary assessment in a heinous case to be disposed of within three months of first production, and adds a consequence the older Act did not state: where an inquiry into a petty offence remains inconclusive even after the extended period, the proceedings stand terminated. For serious and heinous offences needing longer still, the extension must come from the Chief Judicial Magistrate or Chief Metropolitan Magistrate. Section 16 then institutes a review of the pendency of inquiries, so that delay in the Board is itself supervised by a named judicial officer rather than left to accumulate. The direction of this cluster is plain, and it runs in favour of the child on both tracks: the system that holds a child longest while deciding is the system least entitled to call itself reformative, and the 2015 Act prices delay in a currency the 2000 Act did not use, the termination of the proceeding itself. The cluster is recorded in prose rather than as a table row because it refines the operation of both tracks at once; but a reader tallying the direction of the whole comparison should know that on the provisions governing how a case begins and how long it may last, the newer Act is the stricter and the more child-sided text.

## The fresh start: where the newer Act quietly took something back

Both statutes promise the child a future unencumbered by the case, and they promise it in nearly the same words, which is exactly why the difference between the promises is easy to miss. Section 19 of the 2000 Act provides that a juvenile who has committed an offence and has been dealt with under the Act shall not suffer disqualification, if any, attaching to a conviction of an offence under any other law, and directs the Board to order that the relevant records be removed after the expiry of the period of appeal or a prescribed reasonable period. The promise is unqualified: it covers every child the Act dealt with, however grave the offence, because every child the Act dealt with had been dealt with by the same Board under the same ceiling.

Section 24 of the 2015 Act opens with the same promise and then withdraws it from one class. The general rule stands: a child who has committed an offence and has been dealt with under the Act shall not suffer disqualification attaching to a conviction. A proviso then provides that where a child of sixteen or above has been found in conflict with law by the Children's Court after trial as an adult, the removal of disqualification shall not apply, and a second proviso requires that the records of conviction in a heinous case so tried shall be retained by the Children's Court rather than destroyed. The principle of a fresh start, which Section 3 of the same Act announces for all past records of a child under the system except in special circumstances, here acquires its special circumstance, and the circumstance is precisely the class the transfer provision creates.

For the comparison, this row is the second weakening, and it is in some ways the more instructive of the two, because nobody debated it. The transfer row was argued in Parliament, in the press and in the courts; the records row changed in silence, although its consequence may outlast the sentence in an individual life: a young adult who was tried as an adult for a heinous offence carries a retained record and an unremoved disqualification into employment, licensing and every other process that asks the question a conviction answers. A child advocate assembling or defending such a file works across social investigation reports, school records, assessment notes and court orders in several formats at once, and keeping that bundle complete, ordered and private is the unglamorous half of the fresh start in practice; a single tool that opens every document in the file, spreadsheets included, without conversion, such as [an office file viewer for Excel, Word and presentation files](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html), is built for exactly that clerical reality. The statutory point stands apart from the clerical one: on this dimension the 2015 Act protects a defined class of children less than the 2000 Act protected everyone, and the table marks it weakened.

### Does the 2015 Act erase the record of every child it deals with?

No. Section 24 protects the generality of children with removal of disqualification and destruction of records, but its provisos exclude the child of sixteen or above convicted by the Children's Court after trial as an adult for a heinous offence: that child's disqualification is not removed and the conviction record is retained by the Court.

The fresh start row also illustrates a general feature of this comparison that the verdict will use. The two weakenings in the 2015 Act are not scattered through the statute. Both are consequences of a single design decision, the decision to create the adult trial track, and both fall on the same small and identifiable class of children: those of sixteen and above, accused of offences in the heinous class as the courts have narrowed it, whom a Board assesses and a Children's Court agrees to try as adults. Every other child in the system is untouched by either weakening. That concentration matters to how the weakenings should be weighed, and it matters equally that concentration is not the same as triviality for the children inside the class.

## Appeals and revision: supervising the deciders

A last piece of shared machinery deserves a paragraph of its own, because the comparison of the two statutes is incomplete without the route by which their deciders are corrected. Both Acts build the same two-tier supervision. Under the 2000 Act, Section 52 gave an aggrieved person an appeal from an order of the competent authority, lying to the Court of Session within thirty days, and barred a second appeal from the Court of Session's appellate order, while Section 53 gave the High Court a revisional power to call for the record of any proceeding before a competent authority or the Court of Session and satisfy itself as to the legality and propriety of the order passed. The 2015 Act reproduces the design in Sections 101 and 102 with the forums renamed for its own architecture: appeals from Board and Committee orders lie as the section directs, principally to the Children's Court, and the High Court's revision under Section 102 reaches any order of the Children's Court, Board or Committee. The 2021 amendment inserted its own appellate consequence into this scheme when it moved adoption orders to District Magistrates, sending the appeal from such an order to the Divisional Commissioner rather than into the judicial channel, the one point in either statute where a determination about a child's permanent status is reviewed inside the executive. The appellate dimension is therefore a continuity with a single, pointed exception, and it is recorded in prose for the same reason as the other continuities of design: the verdict's counting is confined to the ten dimensions the comparison set out to score, and a reader who suspects the count has been arranged to flatter the newer Act is entitled to see the unscored continuities stated as plainly as the scored changes. On appeals, the two statutes are the same statute, twice enacted, with the 2021 adoption appeal the only structural novelty, and that novelty belongs to the contested side of the adoption row rather than to the appellate design itself.

## The care track: a wider door in 2015

The second half of each statute concerns the child who has committed no offence: the child found without a home or means of subsistence, the child being abused, neglected or exploited, the child whose parent or guardian cannot or will not provide care. Both Acts define this child, route the child to a Child Welfare Committee rather than a Board, and empower the Committee to order placement, foster care, sponsorship, restoration to family and, at the end of the road, adoption.

The 2000 Act's definition, in Section 2(d), ran through nine numbered heads, one of which, covering the child found begging or working or living on the street, was itself inserted by the amendment of 2006: the child without home or settled abode or ostensible means of subsistence; the child living with a person who has threatened or committed abuse; the mentally or physically challenged or ill child without support; the child whose parent or guardian is unfit or incapacitated; the child without parents, or abandoned, surrendered, missing or run away; the child being or likely to be abused, tortured or exploited for sexual abuse or illegal acts; the child vulnerable to induction into drug abuse or trafficking; the child likely to be abused for unconscionable gains; and the child who is a victim of armed conflict, civil commotion or natural calamity.

The 2015 Act's definition, in Section 2(14), runs to twelve heads. It carries the substance of the earlier list, rearranges parts of it, and adds heads with distinct work to do. A child who has been or is being or is likely to be abused is joined expressly by the language of injury and exploitation and by violation of any other law meant for the protection of children. The child in a household where another child has been killed or abused is carried forward. And a wholly new head reaches the child at imminent risk of marriage before attaining the age of marriage, where the parents, family members, guardians or others are likely to be responsible for solemnising it. That head connects the care track to child marriage in a way the 2000 text never did: under the older Act, the child threatened with marriage had to be fitted into the general heads of abuse or unfit guardianship before the Committee's jurisdiction attached in terms; under the 2015 Act the risk of the marriage is itself the ground.

The direction of this row is not seriously contestable. A wider definition of the child the state may protect, with the additions aimed at documented gaps, is a strengthening of protection, and the table marks the care and protection categories strengthened. The strengthening is jurisdictional rather than rhetorical: each new head is a new set of facts on which a Committee can take cognizance, direct a social investigation and pass a placement order, and on which any person producing a child can invoke the machinery. It is also worth recording what did not change on this track's front end. Both Acts treat restoration to the family as the first object of the care process, and both build the same ladder of alternatives when restoration fails: fit person or fit institution, foster care, sponsorship, adoption and institutional care as the last resort. The 2015 Act lengthens and specifies the ladder; it does not replace it.

The Committee's working powers show the same pattern of specification laid over continuity. Section 31 of the 2000 Act vested the Committee with the final authority to dispose of cases for the care, protection, treatment, development and rehabilitation of children, and armed it with the powers of a magistrate under the Code of Criminal Procedure for the conduct of its proceedings. Section 30 of the 2015 Act keeps the authority and writes the job description out at length: taking cognizance of children produced before it, directing social investigations, declaring fit persons, directing foster care placements, selecting the registered institution for each child requiring institutional support with regard to age, gender, disability and need, conducting at least two inspection visits every month to the residential facilities for children in need of care and protection and recommending action on what the visits find, declaring children legally free for adoption after due inquiry, and taking suo motu cognizance of children in need who have not been produced before it, on a decision of at least three members. A committee told by its statute to visit the institutions in its charge twice a month is a different instrument from a committee handed a general final authority, even where the personnel are identical, because the enumerated duty can be demanded, audited and defaulted in a way a general power cannot. This is the care track's version of the criteria change in the next section: the 2015 Act repeatedly converts the 2000 Act's trusts into instructions.

## Who decides: the membership rules for Boards and Committees

A statute for children is administered by two small bodies in every district, and the quality of the whole system is set by who is allowed to sit on them. On this dimension the two Acts differ in a way the public argument never mentioned, and the difference runs in one direction.

The 2000 Act constituted the Child Welfare Committee in Section 29: a Chairperson and four other members appointed by the State Government, of whom at least one was to be a woman and another an expert on matters concerning children. On the question of who those members should be, the statute then stopped. Section 29(3) provided that the qualifications of the Chairperson and the members, and the tenure for which they may be appointed, shall be such as may be prescribed, which is to say the Act handed the entire question of eligibility to subordinate rules made by the same governments doing the appointing. The termination grounds were stated, misuse of power, conviction for an offence involving moral turpitude, habitual absence, but the entry door was as wide as a rule-maker chose to leave it. The Board's composition on the conflict track was similarly framework-like: a magistrate and two social workers, one of whom was to be a woman, with the social workers' qualifications again largely a matter of the rules.

The 2015 Act writes the entry conditions into the statute. Section 27(4) provides that no person shall be appointed a member of the Committee unless that person has been actively involved in health, education or welfare activities pertaining to children for at least seven years, or is a practising professional with a degree in child psychology or psychiatry or law or social work or sociology or human development. The appointment is capped at three years, induction training and sensitisation of members is required within two months of notification, the District Magistrate is charged with a quarterly review of the Committee's functioning, and the District Child Protection Unit must supply the Committee's secretariat. On the conflict track, Section 4 applies the same legislative instinct to the Board: the presiding magistrate must have at least three years of experience, and the two social worker members must have been actively involved in health, education or welfare activities relating to children for at least seven years or hold a professional degree in the listed disciplines. Set that against the older Board, which Section 4(2) of the 2000 Act constituted as a Metropolitan Magistrate or a Judicial Magistrate of the first class sitting with two social workers, of whom at least one was to be a woman, with no statutory minimum of magisterial experience and no statutory test of the social workers' background at all. The bench's shape is the same in both statutes, three people, one in robes and two from the social work world; what changed is that the newer Act tells the appointing government, in the statute itself, what kinds of people the two lay seats are for.

The 2021 amendment then tightened the same screws a second time, and the tightening is part of this dimension's present position. Section 27(4) as substituted requires both a degree in one of the listed disciplines and seven years of active involvement in children's health, education or welfare work, or practising professional status with such a degree, and a new sub-section 27(4A) bars selection outright for a person with any past record of violation of human rights or child rights, a person convicted of an offence involving moral turpitude whose conviction stands, and a person removed or dismissed from government service. The movement across the three texts is continuous and one-directional: from eligibility left to rules in 2000, to eligibility stated in the Act in 2015, to eligibility stated more demandingly, with named disqualifications, in 2021.

The reason this row belongs among the strengthenings, and not in a footnote, is that the membership criteria answer documented failures rather than hypothetical ones. Committees staffed as political accommodation, members without any background in children's work, and benches that could not command the confidence of the professionals appearing before them were recurring findings in the monitoring of the 2000 Act's system, and they were failures no exhortation could cure, because the statute itself had declined to set a floor. A criteria row does not guarantee good members; it gives an appointing government a standard it can be held to, and a court or a monitoring commission a text to point to when the standard is ignored. The table marks deciding-body criteria strengthened.

## The adults' chapter: offences against children

Each statute reserves part of its length for the adults who harm children, and the contrast in scale between the two treatments is itself a finding of the comparison. The 2000 Act addressed adult misconduct in a run of four provisions standing among its procedural sections. Section 23 punished cruelty to a juvenile or child by a person having actual charge or control over the child, assault, abuse, exposure or wilful neglect in a manner likely to cause unnecessary mental or physical suffering, with imprisonment extending to six months, or fine, or both. Section 24 punished employing a juvenile or child for begging, Section 25 punished giving a child intoxicating liquor or a narcotic drug or psychotropic substance in a public place, and Section 26 punished the exploitation of a juvenile or child employee by an employer who withheld or misapplied the child's earnings. The four provisions were sensible and small: they policed the household, the street corner, the liquor vendor and the employer, at penalties measured in months, and they left the general penal law to carry everything else.

The 2015 Act gathers the subject into a chapter of its own, Chapter IX, running from Section 74 to Section 85, and enlarges every aspect of it. The identity prohibition of Section 74, already noted in this article as a continuity with the older Act's Section 21, is placed at the chapter's head and carries its own penalty. Cruelty to a child under Section 75 is graded, with the base offence and heavier treatment where the offender is employed by or manages an organisation entrusted with the child's care. The begging provisions reach not only employing or procuring a child for begging but the maiming of a child for that purpose; the intoxicant provisions reach using a child for vending, peddling, carrying, supplying or smuggling liquor or narcotic or psychotropic substances; and the chapter adds offences the 2000 Act did not contain at all, among them the sale and procurement of children for any purpose, corporal punishment administered in a child care institution, and the use of a child by militant groups or by adult gangs. Penalties are restated in the register of the newer drafting, imprisonment measured in years and fines measured in lakhs, where the older Act spoke in months.

This chapter is not scored as a row of the table, for the reason the time-discipline cluster was not: the brief for this comparison fixes the ten dimensions the verdict counts, and the adults' chapter operates across both tracks at once, deterring the harms that push children into the care track and policing the institutions the conflict track also uses. Its direction, however, is unambiguous and consistent with the pattern the table does record. On the provisions that protect children from the adults around them, the 2015 Act is broader in coverage, heavier in penalty and more particular in its grading than the 2000 Act, and the widening attracted no controversy at all, because no constituency organises in defence of cruelty to children. The contested ground in 2015 was never the statute's protectiveness toward children as victims. It was exclusively its protectiveness toward children as accused, and a comparison that keeps those two questions separate, as this one has tried to do, explains both the statute and the argument about it better than one that merges them.

## Registration: from certifying some homes to registering every institution

If one change in the 2015 Act touches more children's daily lives than any other, it is the change nobody argued about: the treatment of the institutions in which children in the system actually live. The comparison here is between two different legal techniques, and the difference in technique is the substance.

The 2000 Act proceeded institution type by institution type. A children's home was an institution established by a State Government or by a voluntary organisation and certified by that Government under Section 34. Observation homes under Section 8 and special homes under Section 9 were established and maintained by the State Government, directly or through voluntary organisations, and shelter homes under Section 37 were recognised in a similar pattern. Each named category had its own gateway, and the gateways were administrative: a home existed inside the system because the state had established it or certified it as the kind of home it claimed to be. What the technique never produced was a single register of every institution housing children, a duty on every such institution to be on it whether or not it sought a rupee of public money, or a criminal consequence for housing children outside the system altogether. An unregistered home was not, as such, an offence under the 2000 Act; it was a home the Act's machinery simply did not reach, and the children inside it were correspondingly invisible to the Committees, to the inspection provisions and to the standards the Act set for the homes it did certify. Inspection committees existed under the older scheme, and Section 35 provided for inspection of the homes the Act covered; the gap was jurisdictional, the class of institutions standing outside the categories.

The 2015 Act replaces the technique with a universal duty. Section 41(1) provides that all institutions, whether run by a State Government or by voluntary or non-governmental organisations, which are meant, either wholly or partially, for housing children in need of care and protection or children in conflict with law, shall be registered under the Act in the manner prescribed, and it states expressly that the duty applies notwithstanding anything contained in any other law and whether or not the institution receives any grant from the Central or the State Government. Registration runs for five years and is renewable; a registered institution is duty bound to admit children as directed by the Committee, subject to capacity, whether or not it receives grants; and the State Government may cancel or withhold registration from an institution failing to provide the rehabilitation and reintegration services Section 53 specifies, managing the institution itself in the interval. Section 42 then gives the duty its teeth: a person in charge of an institution housing such children who fails to comply with Section 41 is punishable with imprisonment extending to one year, or a fine of not less than one lakh rupees, or both, and every thirty days of delay in applying for registration counts as a separate offence.

The inspection architecture follows the register. Inspection committees appointed under Section 54 may inspect any institution housing children, and Section 41(9) makes the power explicit even against an institution that has not registered, in order to determine whether it is housing children in need of care or protection. The monitoring of that inspection system, and the commissions charged with pressing it, are the subject of the institution article on [the NCPCR and child rights commissions](/2016/08/01/ncpcr-child-rights-commission-india/), which owns that machinery. For the comparison, the legally significant movement is from a map with blank spaces to a map that purports to be complete: under the 2015 Act there is, in law, no such thing as a children's institution outside the register, and operating one is a crime rather than an omission.

### Can an unregistered children's home still be inspected under the 2015 Act?

Yes. The inspection committee appointed under Section 54 may inspect any institution housing children, and Section 41(9) states expressly that the power extends to an institution that has not registered under the Act, so the state can determine whether the place is housing children in need of care and protection.

The Supreme Court's implementation proceedings under the 2015 Act, in Sampurna Behrua v. Union of India, treated the registration of child care institutions as an obligation still being worked toward under judicial supervision, with the Court returning to the state of the system across successive hearings. That record belongs to the implementation assessment of the 2015 Act rather than to this comparison, but one consequence of it is comparison business: the rows of the table record what the texts do, and the registration row records the largest gap between technique and outcome in the whole comparison, a point the verdict section takes up rather than buries. On the text, the direction is unmistakable, and the table marks institutional registration strengthened. It is the clearest of the five strengthenings, and it is the one a reader searching the public debate of 2015 in vain would never learn about.

## Adoption: the dimension that moved twice

Adoption sits inside both statutes because, for the orphaned, abandoned and surrendered child, adoption is the system's preferred ending: a permanent family in place of an institution. But adoption entered the juvenile justice framework late and by instalments, and the comparison has to track three texts rather than two.

The 2000 Act's rehabilitation chapter carried adoption in Section 41, and the section's operative content arrived by amendment. In the form left by the Amendment Act of 2006, Section 41 provided that adoption shall be resorted to for the rehabilitation of children who are orphan, abandoned or surrendered, through the mechanism prescribed; that in keeping with the guidelines for adoption issued by the State Government or the Central Adoption Resource Agency and notified by the Central Government, children may be given in adoption by a court after the court satisfies itself that the required investigations have been carried out; and that the State Government shall recognise one or more institutions or voluntary organisations in each district as specialised adoption agencies for the placement of such children. The 2006 amendment also built the front end of the process into the section: no child was to be offered for adoption until two members of the Committee declared the child legally free for placement, in the case of an abandoned child, or until the two-month period for reconsideration by the parent had run, in the case of a surrendered child, or without the child's own consent where the child could understand and express it. The architecture was real but derivative: the procedure lived in guidelines issued under the Act rather than in the Act, the central agency figured as a guideline-maker named the Central Adoption Resource Agency, and the section itself was four operative ideas deep.

The 2015 Act devotes a chapter to the subject. Chapter VIII, running from Section 56 to Section 73, sets out adoption as a regulated statutory process: the definition of adoption in Section 2(2), under which the adopted child is permanently separated from the biological parents and becomes the lawful child of the adoptive parents with all the rights of a biological child; the eligibility of prospective adoptive parents; the procedure for adoption of orphaned, abandoned and surrendered children, of children by relatives, and of children by step-parents; inter-country adoption; the effect of adoption; and offences and penalties for adoption given or received in contravention. The Central Adoption Resource Authority is constituted as a statutory body by Section 68, with a steering committee under Section 69, charged with promoting in-country adoption, regulating inter-country adoption and framing the Adoption Regulations that govern the process in detail. Under the Act as enacted, the adoption order itself was made by a court under Section 61, on the application of the specialised adoption agency.

What codification changed for the child at the centre of the process is worth isolating, because it is easy to mistake a longer chapter for a better one. Under the guidelines regime of the 2000 Act, the proposition that an adopted child stands in the adoptive family exactly as a born child does rested on the general law and on the guidelines' assumptions; the section itself was about placement machinery. The 2015 Act states the status in its definitions: adoption means the process through which the adopted child is permanently separated from the biological parents and becomes the lawful child of the adoptive parents, with all the rights, privileges and responsibilities that are attached to a biological child. A definition that carries the child's legal identity is a different kind of provision from a direction that agencies be recognised, and for the child whose inheritance, name and filiation are later questioned, it is the definition and not the machinery that will be read. The chapter likewise gives inter-country adoption a stated procedure and gives the Authority the regulation-making power by which eligibility, timelines and the declaration that a child is legally free are governed uniformly, in place of guidelines whose force varied with the issuing authority. None of this appears in a debate about the age of criminal responsibility, which is the point the brief for this article insists on: the adoption dimension is one of the places where the 2015 Act did its most consequential work in complete quiet.

The dimension then moved a second time. The Amendment Act of 2021, Act 23 of 2021, transferred the power to issue adoption orders from the courts to District Magistrates, with an appeal lying to the Divisional Commissioner, a change defended on the ground of delay in court-made orders and criticised on the ground that a judicial act had been handed to the executive. The working of the adoption process, the regulations and the portal through which applications move are the territory of [adoption law under the JJ Act and CARA](/2016/07/15/adoption-law-india-cara-explained/), which owns them; the comparison records only the structural movement. Across the three texts, adoption travelled from a guidelines-driven rehabilitation option resting on a four-idea section, to a codified chapter with a statutory regulator, to the same chapter with its order-making relocated to the district executive. A codified framework with a statutory authority is a stronger legal position for the child at the centre of it than a guidelines regime, because rights, timelines and offences can be pointed to in the text; the 2021 relocation of the order is a change of a different character, trading a judicial forum for an administrative one, and the table marks the adoption row strengthened on the strength of the codification while the verdict section notes the relocation as the row's unresolved argument.

## Aftercare: the exit door

Every child the system holds eventually leaves it, and both statutes had to answer the question of what, if anything, the state owes a young person whose eighteenth birthday arrives inside an institution and who has no family to be restored to. The answers differ in form more than in generosity.

The 2000 Act answered organisationally. Section 40 listed sending the child to an after-care organisation among the routes of rehabilitation and social reintegration, and Section 44 provided for after-care organisations established for the purpose of taking care of juveniles and children after they leave children's homes and special homes, and of enabling them to lead an honest, industrious and useful life, with a probation officer or other appointed officer reporting on each young person before discharge as to the necessity and nature of the aftercare required and the period for which it should run. Support, under this design, meant a place: another institution, softer than the one left behind, in which the transition to an independent life was supervised.

The 2015 Act answers in the language of entitlement. Section 2(5) defines aftercare as making provision of support, financial or otherwise, to persons who have completed eighteen years of age but have not completed twenty one, and who have left any institutional care to join the mainstream of society. Section 46 gives the definition operative form: a child leaving a child care institution on completion of eighteen years of age may be provided with financial support in order to facilitate the child's reintegration into the mainstream of society, in the manner prescribed. The shift is from placement to provision: the young person is not sent onward to another organisation but is supported, in money or otherwise, in the life outside. Neither text is generous, and the support under both remained dependent on budgets, rules and district machinery that the statutes could command but not conjure. As a statement of what the law promises a care leaver, though, a defined entitlement to support across the years from eighteen to twenty one is a clearer and more usable right than a direction that organisations be established, and the table marks aftercare strengthened, the most modest of the five.

Two neighbouring provisions complete the alternatives ladder and show the same drafting movement at smaller scale. Foster care stood in Section 42 of the 2000 Act in a deliberately provisional form: foster care might be used for the temporary placement of infants who were ultimately to be given in adoption, and for children who could not be placed for adoption for other reasons, in the manner prescribed. It was a bridge concept, a holding arrangement on the way to the Act's preferred permanent outcome. Section 44 of the 2015 Act treats foster care as a placement in its own right, under which a child in need of care and protection may be placed in the domestic environment of a family, or of a person outside the child's biological family who is willing to undertake the child's care and upbringing, for a short or extended period as the Committee directs, with the foster family's circumstances, the child's individual care plan and monthly inspection by the Committee built into the scheme, and with group foster care added as a variant. Sponsorship travelled the same road: Section 43 of the 2000 Act introduced it as supplementary support to families to improve the quality of a child's life, and Section 45 of the 2015 Act carries it forward in materially the same terms, financial or other support to individuals, families and children's homes alike. Neither provision is a table row, and the foster care development is recorded here for the same reason the principles chapter was: the direction of the newer Act's care drafting is consistent enough that a reader tallying only the scored dimensions will understate it. Where the 2000 Act sketched a welfare instrument and left its content to rules and practice, the 2015 Act tends to write the instrument's operating conditions into the statute, and foster care, a placement that succeeds or fails entirely on the quality of its supervision, is the kind of provision where that tendency matters most.

## Which law governs an old case: the transition rule

The dimension with the largest daily caseload is one the table does not score, because it is not a difference in protection but a rule for choosing between the two statutes, and it resolves a large share of the practical queries the comparison generates. The rule has three layers, and all three run in the same direction: the law of the offence date governs.

The first layer is constitutional habit, built on the earlier transition. When the 2000 Act replaced the Act of 1986, the Constitution Bench in Pratap Singh v. State of Jharkhand, reported at (2005) 3 SCC 551, settled that the benefit of the new Act's definition turned on the person's age, and that the relevant inquiry is anchored to the date of the offence: a person who was below eighteen on the date of the offence is a juvenile for the purposes of the Act, and the proceedings take the juvenile route, while Section 20 of the 2000 Act provided that proceedings pending on the Act's commencement continue in the court where they stood as if the Act had not been passed, with the court recording its finding and forwarding the juvenile to the Board for orders rather than passing sentence itself.

The second layer is the 2015 Act's own savings. Section 111 repeals the 2000 Act and preserves what was done under it, deeming things done under the repealed Act to have been done under the corresponding provisions of the new one, and Section 25 provides that all proceedings in respect of a child alleged or found to be in conflict with law pending before any Board or court on the date of commencement of the 2015 Act shall be continued in that Board or court as if the Act had not been enacted. The pending case is therefore finished under the discipline in which it began.

The third layer is the one that decides the question readers actually ask. Because Section 2(13) of the 2015 Act defines the child in conflict with law by age on the date of commission, and because the transfer machinery is part of the 2015 Act's scheme for offences under it, an offence committed before 15 January 2016, the date the 2015 Act came into force, is governed by the 2000 Act, including its absolute bar on adult trial, whenever the case is heard. A young person who was seventeen on the date of a grave offence committed in 2014 cannot be put through a preliminary assessment or transferred to a Children's Court in a proceeding begun in 2017; the Board deals with the case under the old Act's dispositions, and the three-year ceiling of the old system applies. The date of arrest, of charge sheet, of first production and of trial are all legally beside the point, and an argument built on any of them is an argument about the wrong date.

Within that rule sits the claim of juvenility, the procedural device by which the question of age is put to a court at all. The 2000 Act as enacted channelled the claim through Section 7 and the age determination provision, and the amendment of 2006 inserted Section 7A, which provides in terms that a claim of juvenility may be raised before any court and shall be recognised at any stage, even after final disposal of the case, and shall be determined under the Act even if the person has ceased to be a juvenile before or after its commencement; if the court finds the person was a juvenile on the date of the offence, it forwards the person to the Board and any sentence passed is deemed to have no effect. The 2015 Act maintains the same position through its own procedure, with age determined under Section 94 on the documentary order of preference the section prescribes. The rule's operation is easiest to see with the boundary walked in both directions. Take a grave offence committed on 10 January 2016 by a young person of seventeen, with arrest and charge following in 2018. Every date after the offence is later than the 2015 Act's commencement, and none of them matters: the offence predates the Act, the 2000 Act governs, the Board holds the inquiry, and no preliminary assessment can be set in motion, because the assessment is machinery of a statute that did not exist when the act was done. Reverse the offence date to 20 January 2016, five days after commencement, and the same young person stands inside the 2015 Act from the start: production before the Board, the classification of the offence by its punishment clause, and, if the offence is heinous as the courts have confined that word, the assessment and the possibility of transfer. Ten days separate the two files, and the files belong to different legal worlds, which is precisely what a commencement date is for. The 2015 Act also writes the mid-case position into its early sections: Section 5 deals with the placement of a person who ceases to be a child during the process of inquiry, and Section 6 with the placement of a person who committed an offence when below eighteen, providing in each case that the person continues to be treated under the Act notwithstanding the birthday that intervenes, so that the system's grip on a case, once lawfully fastened by the offence date, is not loosened by the calendar.

On this dimension, therefore, the newer Act changed nothing of substance, and the continuity is itself a holding of the comparison: the most consequential practical rule in the whole field, that old offences are judged by the old law and that the age question never goes stale, was settled law before 2015 and remains settled law after it. A reader with a live matter should treat everything in this section as the general position only, because the outcome in an individual case turns on the documentary record of age and on forum and limitation questions that general writing cannot answer, and advice on the specific case is not a courtesy but a necessity where a sentence is already running.

The distribution is as informative as the totals. Both weakenings sit on the conflict track, and both are products of the single decision to admit adult trial: the transfer itself, and the records carve-out that follows a transferred child out of the system. The two unchanged rows are the foundations, the age line and the internal ceiling, and the neutral row is the sorting machine that makes the transfer administrable. A reader who wants to know what kind of statute the 2015 Act is could do worse than start from that shape: a care statute that was substantially rebuilt, carrying a conflict-track exception that cuts the other way.

## The verdict, with the deciding factor named

The verdict follows from the count, and it is a distributive verdict rather than an aggregate one. The 2015 Act improved the legal position of most of the children its system touches and worsened the position of a small, precisely identified class. For the child in need of care and protection, who is the large majority of the system's population, the newer Act is better law on every dimension that reaches that child: the door is wider, the committee at the door is better qualified, the institution behind the door must be on a register and can be inspected whether it is or not, the exit into adoption runs on a codified chapter under a statutory authority, and the exit into adulthood carries a defined promise of support. For the child in conflict with law who is not transferred, the position is materially the 2000 position: the same age line, the same bail rule, the same three-year ceiling, the same fresh start. For the child of sixteen or above who is assessed, transferred and convicted as an adult of a heinous offence, the position is worse in kind and not merely in degree: exposure to the ordinary penal scale up to life imprisonment with the possibility of release, a retained record, and a disqualification the statute declines to remove.

The deciding factor, the one on which any two honest readers of the same texts can reach opposite overall judgments, is which track the reader treats as the statute's centre. Child rights organisations, whose case against the transfer provision was made at length during the passage of the Act and is recorded in the controversy article, describe the statute by its exception, and on the conflict track their description is accurate: the 2015 Act is the statute that ended the absolute rule that no child faces a criminal court, and no improvement elsewhere in the Act restores that rule for the children the exception reaches. Those who administer the system, the state child protection machinery, the district units and the functionaries who staff Boards and Committees, describe the statute by its rebuilt centre, and on the care track their description is also accurate: the 2015 Act is the statute that made every children's institution registrable and punishable if unregistered, that wrote qualifications for the deciding bodies into the law, and that gave adoption and aftercare a statutory form the guidelines era never had. Each camp is describing a different part of the same machine, and the description each gives is true of the part it describes. A comparison that resolves the dispute by declaring one part the whole is not analysis; it is advocacy with the tables turned.

This article's verdict, stated as the series requires a comparison to state it, is that the 2015 Act was an improvement taken as a whole statute, because the dimensions it strengthened govern the daily operation of the system for nearly every child in it, while the dimensions it weakened govern a single class that the courts have since narrowed twice, first by confining the heinous category in Shilpa Mittal and then by disciplining the assessment in Barun Chandra Thakur. The verdict carries two riders without which it would mislead. The first is that an aggregate improvement is not an offset: for the individual child tried as an adult, the registration of institutions and the qualifications of committees are no consolation at all, and a distributive judgment that improves the position of many by worsening the position of a few is a choice, to be defended as a choice and not disguised as an arithmetic result. The second rider concerns the amendment of 2021, which is part of the statute being judged.

### What did the 2021 amendment change in the 2015 Act that matters to this comparison?

Three things. Adoption orders moved from courts to District Magistrates, with an appeal to the Divisional Commissioner. Committee membership was tightened, with a degree and seven years of child welfare work required and named disqualifications added. Offences under the Act punishable with three to seven years of imprisonment were made non-cognisable by the reworking of Section 86.

The 2021 amendment is a reminder that the comparison's right-hand column is a moving target, and it cuts in more than one direction: it strengthened the deciding-bodies row further, it relocated the adoption order into the executive in a way that remains contested, and it reduced the procedural seriousness with which a band of offences against children under the Act itself is treated, by making them non-cognisable, a change in tension with the protective direction of the rest of the statute. A verdict on the 2015 Act that ignores its amendment is a verdict on a statute that no longer exists in that form, which is why this comparison has carried the amendment inside the relevant rows rather than in a postscript.

One consequence of the verdict deserves its own paragraph, because it is the form in which the comparison meets the recurring public question whether the 2015 Act should be amended again. There is a standing item of unfinished legislative business that no verdict is needed to identify: the fourth category of offences. When the Supreme Court in Shilpa Mittal treated offences lacking a minimum punishment of seven years as serious rather than heinous, it did so expressly until Parliament takes the call on the matter, which is a judicial invitation to legislate that remains open. An amendment that took up the invitation would have to decide, in words, whether offences such as culpable homicide not amounting to murder belong inside or outside the transfer gate, and either answer would be a decision about the size of the weakened class this article has described. Beyond that item, the evidence agenda set out in the next section defines what any further amendment would need to know before it could claim to be an improvement on evidence rather than on sentiment: the transfer data, the register's coverage, the disposal record. The comparison's verdict is about the two statutes as they stand. It is not a brief for leaving the newer one alone, and the honest reading of the table is that its weakened rows are amendable in a way its strengthened rows show no need of, which is a conclusion the counter-reading of the statute, that amendment talk is always regression talk, does not survive.

## The implementation qualification

Every row of the table records the law in the text, and one qualification hangs over the strengthened rows in particular: a duty is only as strong as the machinery that enforces it, and the machinery of Indian child protection is district machinery, unevenly built. Mandatory registration is the comparison's clearest strengthening and also its clearest exposure to this qualification. The offence in Section 42 exists; the inspection committees under Section 54 exist; and the record of the Act's working, visible in the Supreme Court's continuing supervision of the system's implementation in the Sampurna Behrua proceedings and in the monitoring reports of the statutory commissions, includes institutions housing children that remained outside the register and Committees and Boards working without their full complement or their full complement of qualified members. A register with institutions standing outside it is a better law than no register and a worse outcome than the column in the table suggests. The same qualification applies, with less drama, to the membership criteria: a qualification floor protects only where appointing governments observe it and monitoring bodies call out its breach.

There is a structural reason the qualification bites where it does, and naming it sharpens the verdict rather than weakening it. The 2000 Act's machinery was district machinery without a district institution: it spoke of Boards, Committees and homes, and left the connective tissue, the unit that would staff the system, chase the register, service the committees and report upward, to executive design outside the statute. The 2015 Act writes the connective tissue into the text, constituting the State Child Protection Society and the District Child Protection Units under Section 106, charging the Unit with the district's implementation work from maintaining the units and institutions to completing the registration process, and giving the Commissions for Protection of Child Rights a stated monitoring role in Section 109. On paper, therefore, the strengthening rows of the table are not freestanding promises; they are attached to an office whose job is to keep them. In the districts where that office exists as one officer holding additional charge among several portfolios, which the monitoring record shows to be a common condition rather than an aberration, the attachment fails at exactly the point the statute innovated. The comparison of the two Acts is, in this respect, also a comparison of two theories of how a welfare statute gets itself obeyed: the 2000 Act trusted the named authorities, and the 2015 Act built a named bureaucracy, and the second theory is better law even where the bureaucracy is understaffed, because an understaffed office can be funded and a missing one cannot.

The honest limit on the verdict follows from that qualification, and it should be stated without varnish. This comparison decides which statute is better law, on the texts, dimension by dimension. It does not and cannot decide which statute produced better outcomes for children, because the outcome evidence, disposal patterns under each regime, the number of transfers actually ordered and their results, the state of the register district by district, is published in forms that are partial, dated and difficult to compare across the change of regime, and the series' discipline is to describe such evidence by its pattern and direction rather than to manufacture precision from it. What the evidence would need to show, to convert this textual verdict into an outcome verdict, is identifiable: consistent transfer and assessment data from the Boards, registration and inspection coverage from the districts, and disposal and restoration figures for the care track under both statutes measured the same way. Until that evidence exists in comparable form, the verdict offered here is the strongest one the record supports, and it is labelled for what it is.

One feature of the newer Act may, in time, supply part of that evidence from inside the system, and it is worth marking as the comparison closes. The pendency review written into Section 16 of the 2015 Act, under which the Board's unfinished inquiries are periodically reviewed by the Chief Judicial Magistrate or the Chief Metropolitan Magistrate, is not only a discipline on delay; it is a data-generating device, the first point in either statute at which the working of the conflict track is required to be counted by an officer outside it. The 2000 Act generated no equivalent return: its four-month discipline had no reporting shadow, and the consequence is that the older regime's performance is recoverable only from committee reports and litigation records, assembled after the fact and never in the same form twice. A future comparison of outcomes, if the returns the newer Act contemplates are kept and published, will be the first one able to test the textual verdict this article has given against a counted record, and the asymmetry is itself a small parable of the larger comparison: the 2015 Act repeatedly builds the instruments by which it can be judged, as its predecessor did not, even where the instruments' early readings are unflattering.

## What the verdict means for each reader

For the advocate and the child welfare professional, the comparison's practical yield is the transition rule and the narrowed transfer gate: offences dated before 15 January 2016 are 2000 Act cases whatever the calendar says at trial, the claim of juvenility can be raised at any stage including after final disposal, and on the 2015 side the heinous category after Shilpa Mittal excludes every offence without a minimum punishment of seven years. For the aspirant, the yield is the table: the standard examination demand on this subject is precisely a comparison of the two Acts, and the ten dimensions with their direction markers are the answer's skeleton, with the age line's non-change and the registration change being the two points examiners use to separate candidates who have read the statutes from candidates who have read the headlines. For the institution and the non-governmental organisation, the yield is Section 41 and Section 42 read together: registration is not a funding condition but a precondition of lawful operation, delay compounds the offence month by month, and the inspection power reaches the unregistered. For the researcher, the yield is the distributive shape of the verdict and the evidence agenda the preceding section names.

There are cases where the general verdict does not hold, and a comparison article owes its readers their shape. Where the question is the position of the individual transferred child, the verdict reverses: the 2000 Act was simply and entirely better for that child, and nothing in this article's aggregate judgment should be quoted against the proposition. Where the question is a district in which the care machinery of neither Act was ever built, the comparison of texts approaches irrelevance, because both statutes fail at the same point, the point where paper institutions were supposed to become staffed ones. And where the question is adoption practice on the ground, the operative law is the amended text with its District Magistrate orders, and the cleaner codification of 2015 has to be read with the 2021 relocation rather than against the 2000 guidelines regime alone. Subject to those cases, the verdict stands: on the count of its dimensions, the 2015 Act improved on the Act it replaced, the improvement is concentrated where the public debate never looked, and the price of the improvement was charged to the smallest and most exposed class of children in the system, which is the fact about the statute that any honest summary has to carry in the same sentence as the verdict.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the main difference between the 2000 and 2015 juvenile justice laws?

The main difference is that the 2015 Act created an exception to the rule that every child is dealt with inside the juvenile system. Under the 2000 Act, no child could be tried as an adult, sentenced to imprisonment, or tried jointly with an adult, whatever the offence. Under the 2015 Act, a child of sixteen or above accused of a heinous offence can be assessed by the Juvenile Justice Board and transferred to a Children's Court for trial as an adult. Almost everything else in the newer Act is a strengthening of machinery the older Act had already built: wider care categories, qualified committees, universal registration of institutions, a codified adoption chapter and a defined aftercare entitlement.

### Q: Did the 2000 Act allow trying a child as an adult?

No. The 2000 Act contained no transfer mechanism and no route by which a child could be placed before a criminal court. Section 6 vested the powers over a juvenile in conflict with law exclusively in the Juvenile Justice Board, Section 16 barred any sentence of death or imprisonment against a juvenile, and Section 18 barred a joint trial of a juvenile with an adult. The gravest cases were answered inside the system: where a juvenile of sixteen or above had committed an offence so serious in nature, or whose conduct was such, that a special home was inadequate, the proviso to Section 16 let the Board order the child to be kept in a place of safety and report the matter to the State Government.

### Q: How did adoption provisions change between the two juvenile justice laws?

Under the 2000 Act, adoption rested on Section 41, whose operative content was written in by the amendment of 2006: adoption of orphaned, abandoned and surrendered children by order of a court, under guidelines notified by the Central Government, through specialised adoption agencies recognised by the State Government, with the Child Welfare Committee declaring a child legally free for adoption first. The 2015 Act converted that guidelines-driven arrangement into a codified chapter, Sections 56 to 73, with eligibility, procedure, inter-country adoption and offences set out in the statute, and with the Central Adoption Resource Authority constituted as a statutory body under Section 68. The amendment of 2021 then moved the adoption order itself from the courts to District Magistrates.

### Q: What changed for children in need of care and protection?

The class of children the state may protect widened. The 2000 Act defined a child in need of care and protection through nine heads in Section 2(d), from the child without a home or means of subsistence to the child who is a victim of armed conflict or natural calamity. The 2015 Act expanded the definition to twelve heads in Section 2(14). It restated the abuse, neglect and exploitation heads in fuller language, and it added a ground the older Act did not contain: the child at imminent risk of marriage before attaining the age of marriage, where family members or guardians are likely to be responsible for the marriage. Each head is a jurisdictional trigger for the Child Welfare Committee to take cognizance, order a social investigation and direct placement, foster care, sponsorship or restoration.

### Q: When can juvenility be claimed?

At any stage, including after the case has been finally disposed of. The amendment of 2006 inserted Section 7A into the 2000 Act, which provides that a claim of juvenility may be raised before any court and shall be recognised at any stage, even after final disposal, and that it is to be determined under the Act even where the person has ceased to be a juvenile by the time the claim is raised. The question the court answers is whether the person was below eighteen on the date of the offence. If the finding is in the person's favour, the court forwards the person to the Juvenile Justice Board for appropriate orders, and any sentence already passed is deemed to have no effect. The 2015 Act maintains the same position, with age determined under Section 94.

### Q: Which juvenile justice law applies to an offence committed before 2016?

The Juvenile Justice Act 2000 applies. The 2015 Act came into force on 15 January 2016, and juvenility is determined by the age of the person on the date of the offence, a date the 2015 Act itself writes into its definition of a child in conflict with law in Section 2(13). An offence committed before the commencement date is therefore governed by the older statute whenever the case is heard, which carries with it the older statute's absolute bar on trial as an adult and its three-year ceiling on placement in a special home. Cases already pending when the 2015 Act commenced are separately protected by Section 25 of the newer Act, under which pending proceedings continue in the Board or court where they stand as if the 2015 Act had not been enacted.

### Q: Did the 2015 juvenile justice law improve institutional oversight?

On the text, yes, and this is among its clearest improvements. The 2000 Act dealt with institutions category by category: children's homes were certified under Section 34, observation homes and special homes were established by the state, and an institution standing outside those categories owed the Act no duty and committed no offence by existing. The 2015 Act imposed a universal duty of registration in Section 41 on every institution housing children in need of care and protection or children in conflict with law, whether or not it receives any government grant, and made non-registration an offence under Section 42, punishable with imprisonment of up to one year or a fine of not less than one lakh rupees or both. Inspection committees under Section 54 may inspect even an unregistered institution.

### Q: Was replacing the 2000 juvenile justice Act justified?

On the comparison of the texts, the replacement was justified as a whole, though not on the ground that dominated its defence. Five of the ten dimensions examined in this comparison strengthened protection: wider care categories, statutory criteria for the members of Boards and Committees, universal registration of institutions backed by an offence, a codified adoption framework under a statutory authority, and a defined aftercare entitlement. Two dimensions were carried forward unchanged, the age line of eighteen and the three-year ceiling inside the system. Two weakened protection, both as consequences of the adult trial provision: the transfer of some children of sixteen and above to a Children's Court, and the retention of their records with no removal of disqualification. The justification is therefore distributive, resting on gains for the many children on the care track rather than on the contested provision.

### Q: Which Act first made eighteen the age of a child for both boys and girls?

The Juvenile Justice Act 2000. Its predecessor, the Juvenile Justice Act 1986, defined a juvenile in Section 2(h) as a boy who had not attained the age of sixteen years or a girl who had not attained the age of eighteen years, so a boy of sixteen or seventeen stood outside the protective system altogether. Section 2(k) of the 2000 Act replaced the split definition with a single rule: a juvenile or child means a person who has not completed the eighteenth year of age. The change brought the domestic definition into line with the Convention on the Rights of the Child, which India had ratified in 1992, and the 2015 Act retained the uniform age without alteration.

### Q: Did the 2000 Act classify offences as petty, serious and heinous?

No. The classification is entirely a creation of the 2015 Act. The 2000 Act defined an offence simply as an offence punishable under any law in force, and routed every allegation against a child through the same Board inquiry without distinguishing the trivial from the grave in its procedure. The 2015 Act defined three tiers in Section 2: petty offences, punishable with imprisonment for up to three years; serious offences, punishable with imprisonment for a term between three and seven years; and heinous offences, for which the minimum punishment is imprisonment for seven years or more. The tiers route the procedure the Board follows and decide which cases can reach a preliminary assessment. In Shilpa Mittal, the Supreme Court held that offences with no minimum of seven years fall outside the heinous class and are to be treated as serious.

### Q: When did each of the two juvenile justice Acts come into force?

The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2000, Act 56 of 2000, received the assent of the President on 30 December 2000 and was brought into force on 1 April 2001, repealing the Juvenile Justice Act 1986. It governed the field for nearly fifteen years, amended in the meantime in 2006 and again, in a smaller way, in 2011. The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, Act 2 of 2016, received assent on 31 December 2015 and came into force on 15 January 2016, repealing the 2000 Act. Its own amendment followed in 2021, as Act 23 of 2021, and was brought into force on 1 September 2022.

### Q: How did the rules for who can sit on a Child Welfare Committee change between the two Acts?

The 2000 Act named the Committee's shape and left its membership open. Section 29 provided for a Chairperson and four other members, of whom at least one was to be a woman and another an expert on matters concerning children, and Section 29(3) left the qualifications and tenure of members to be prescribed by rules. The 2015 Act moved the eligibility conditions into the statute itself: Section 27(4) requires a member to have been actively involved in health, education or welfare activities pertaining to children for at least seven years, or to be a practising professional with a degree in child psychology or psychiatry or law or social work or sociology or human development. The 2021 amendment tightened the test further and added named disqualifications, including a past record of violation of child rights.

### Q: What is the longest period a child can be kept in a special home under the two Acts?

Three years under both. The 2000 Act's Section 15 empowered the Board, after an inquiry, to direct that a juvenile be sent to a special home for a period of three years, with a power to reduce the period of stay for reasons recorded where the Board considered a shorter stay expedient. The 2015 Act's Section 18 likewise empowers the Board to place a child found in conflict with law in a special home, a place of safety or the care of a fit facility or fit person for a period not exceeding three years. The ceiling is one of the comparison's unchanged dimensions. It applies to children dealt with inside the juvenile system; it does not govern a child transferred to a Children's Court and tried as an adult under the 2015 Act.

### Q: How does aftercare under the 2015 Act differ from the 2000 Act?

The form of the support changed. The 2000 Act worked through institutions: Section 44 provided for after-care organisations, established to take care of children after they leave children's homes and special homes and to enable them to lead an honest, industrious and useful life, with an officer reporting before discharge on the aftercare each young person needed and for how long. The 2015 Act defines aftercare in Section 2(5) as support, financial or otherwise, to persons who have completed eighteen years but have not completed twenty one and have left institutional care to join the mainstream of society, and Section 46 provides that a child leaving a child care institution at eighteen may be provided with financial support to facilitate reintegration. The newer design supports the young person directly rather than placing the young person in a further organisation.

### Q: What is the difference between certification of homes under the 2000 Act and registration under the 2015 Act?

Certification under the 2000 Act attached to kinds of institutions. A children's home, the Act's main care institution, was one established by a State Government or a voluntary organisation and certified by that Government under Section 34; observation homes, special homes and shelter homes had their own gateways. An institution that was never certified simply stood outside the Act's categories, and its existence was not an offence. Registration under the 2015 Act attaches to the fact of housing children. Section 41 requires every institution meant wholly or partially for children in need of care and protection or children in conflict with law to be registered, whether or not it receives grants, and Section 42 makes failure to register an offence punishable with imprisonment of up to one year or a fine of not less than one lakh rupees or both.

### Q: What did the 2006 amendment change in the 2000 Act?

The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Amendment Act, 2006, Act 33 of 2006, made the most consequential repairs to the older statute. It inserted Section 7A, providing that a claim of juvenility may be raised before any court and recognised at any stage, even after final disposal of the case. It required a Juvenile Justice Board and a Child Welfare Committee for every district, where the original text had permitted boards for a district or a group of districts. It recast the adoption section, Section 41, building in the Committee's declaration that an abandoned child is legally free for adoption, the two-month reconsideration period for surrendered children, and adoption by order of a court. And it added the child found begging, or a street child or a working child, as an express head of the definition of a child in need of care and protection.

### Q: Which juvenile justice law applies if a child turns eighteen while the case is still pending?

The statute under which the case began continues to govern it, because the legally significant date is the date of the offence and not any later birthday. Juvenility is defined by age on the date of commission, so a person who was seventeen on that date remains a child in conflict with law for the whole proceeding, however long the inquiry runs and whatever age the person reaches while it runs. The statute book makes the continuity express at each change of regime: Section 20 of the 2000 Act continued proceedings pending at its commencement in the courts where they stood, and Section 25 of the 2015 Act continues proceedings pending at its commencement in the Board or court as if the newer Act had not been enacted. Turning eighteen mid-case changes neither the forum nor the governing Act.

### Q: Why did India replace the Juvenile Justice Act 1986 with the 2000 Act?

Because the older Act could not carry the obligations India had accepted under the Convention on the Rights of the Child. The preamble of the 2000 Act records the reasoning: the Government of India ratified the Convention on 11 December 1992, and it was expedient to re-enact the law relating to juveniles bearing in mind the standards of the Convention and of the Beijing Rules of 1985 and the United Nations Rules for the Protection of Juveniles Deprived of their Liberty of 1990. The 1986 Act's own definition showed the distance: it treated a boy as a juvenile only up to sixteen and a girl up to eighteen. The 2000 Act unified the age at eighteen, renamed the field around care and protection rather than delinquency, and built the two-track system of Boards and Committees that the 2015 Act inherited.

### Q: Why do child rights organisations and child protection administrators judge the 2015 Act so differently?

Because each is describing a different track of the same statute, and the statute genuinely moved in opposite directions on its two tracks. Child rights organisations judge the Act by its conflict track, where it ended the absolute rule that no child faces a criminal court: on that track the Act is a reduction in protection, and their description is accurate. Administrators, the state child protection societies, district units and the staff of Boards and Committees, judge it by its care track and its machinery, where it imposed universal registration of institutions, statutory qualifications for the deciding bodies, a codified adoption chapter and a defined aftercare entitlement: on that track the Act is a substantial strengthening, and their description is accurate too. The disagreement is over which track should count as the statute's centre, which is a question about weight, not about fact.

### Q: What evidence would show whether the 2015 Act improved outcomes over the 2000 Act?

Outcome evidence of a kind that is not published in comparable form across the two regimes. Four bodies of data would decide it. The number of preliminary assessments conducted each year, the proportion ending in transfer, and the sentences and reformative outcomes that followed for transferred children, to test whether the adult trial track is narrow in practice as the courts have made it in law. Registration and inspection coverage of child care institutions district by district, to test whether the Section 41 duty closed the gap the 2000 Act left. Disposal times and restoration rates for children in need of care and protection under each statute, measured the same way. And appointment records showing whether the statutory criteria for Board and Committee members are observed in practice. Without those series, measured consistently, verdicts about outcomes rest on anecdote, and the defensible verdict remains the textual one this comparison gives.
