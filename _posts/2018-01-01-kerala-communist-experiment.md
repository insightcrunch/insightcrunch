Kerala is the name that development literature gives to a puzzle, and the puzzle is easy to state in qualitative terms. A state at the southwestern tip of India, formed in 1956 from territories that had belonged to princely kingdoms and to a directly administered British district, achieved levels of literacy, life expectancy, infant survival, and schooling that the research literature consistently describes as far above the Indian average and comparable to those of far wealthier societies, while its per capita income long remained modest by national standards. The achievement was real, it was documented across decades by demographers and economists, and it was accomplished in a state governed, in alternation, by communist-led coalitions and by their rivals from the late 1950s onward. Because communist parties were so visibly present in the government of the state during the decades when the indicators consolidated, the outcomes were widely credited to communist government, and the phrase Kerala model entered international development writing as shorthand for what redistribution and public action could achieve at low income.

![A Kerala backwater scene with schoolchildren in uniform, a small health clinic, and a panchayat notice board, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

That crediting is exactly what this article disputes, and it disputes the opposite crediting too. The outcomes long preceded communist government. Substantial public provision of education and health existed in the princely territories from the nineteenth century. Social reform movements organised among subordinated caste communities had built schools, temples, associations, and a reading public before any communist party had a presence worth the name. When communist-led governments did arrive, they contributed policies of their own that other Indian states did not match: a land reform that abolished tenancy in law and largely in fact, an extensive public distribution system, a mass literacy campaign, and a decentralisation programme that moved a large share of plan resources to elected local bodies. Then, from the 1970s, a fourth cause arrived from outside the state altogether, as migration to the Gulf carried remittance income into hundreds of thousands of households and transformed consumption, housing, and private spending on schooling and care. Any account that assigns the phenomenon to one of these four causes is wrong, and the wrongness is not a matter of emphasis. It is a matter of sequence, since major outcomes preceded communist government, and of composition, since the economic base sustaining household consumption in later decades was substantially external. The analytical instrument this article builds and defends is the four-cause allocation: a grading of each contribution against the outcomes it plausibly explains, the evidence that supports the grading, and the outcomes it cannot explain. Kerala serves the wider series on Marxism in India as the standing test of causal discipline, because nowhere else does a celebrated outcome attract so many single-cause stories, and nowhere else is the sequence so unkind to all of them.

## What the Kerala model names

The Kerala model is a label for a pattern, not a policy document, and the pattern has three elements that must be held together if the label is to mean anything. The first element is the level of the social indicators themselves. Successive rounds of demographic and survey research, synthesised in the development literature associated with scholars such as Jean Drèze and Amartya Sen, describe a population in which schooling became near universal, in which survival through infancy and childhood improved dramatically relative to the rest of India, in which life expectancy rose to levels associated with much richer economies, and in which fertility fell early and far. The second element is the income at which this was achieved. For most of the period in which the indicators were consolidated, Kerala was not a rich state; its per capita income sat at or below the national average, and its industrial sector was small. The combination, high social development at low income, is the phenomenon. Either element alone would be unremarkable. Rich societies are expected to be literate and long lived, and poor societies are expected not to be, and a state that was simply poor and unhealthy or simply rich and healthy would have generated no literature. The third element is the political setting: the outcomes were consolidated in a polity marked by intense party competition, strong unions and peasant organisations, high newspaper readership, and repeated alternation in office between a communist-led front and a Congress-led front, so that no single party administered the state continuously and every government inherited its predecessor's institutions under hostile scrutiny.

Defined this way, the Kerala model is a claim about public action rather than about prosperity. The state's economy, for most of the model's classic period, was not dynamic; agriculture was dominated by cash crops and food crops grown on small holdings, industry was thin, and the tertiary sector grew on the back of government employment, trade, and later remittances. Writers who describe Kerala as an economic success story misdescribe it, and the misdescription matters because it reverses the model's actual lesson. The lesson the celebratory literature drew was that a poor society could buy health and education before it bought income, by directing public resources and political pressure toward basic services. The lesson the critical literature drew was that a society could also strand itself in that position, educated and healthy and short of productive employment, if the social achievement was never matched by an economic transformation. Both lessons refer to the same pattern. The pattern is the model, and the argument is over its causes and its costs.

It is worth pausing on the evidentiary status of the pattern itself, because the four-cause allocation depends on it. The indicators are findings of the relevant research literatures: census enumerations, sample registration data, national family health surveys, and the long programme of studies produced at the Centre for Development Studies in Thiruvananthapuram and by university demographers. This article reports those findings qualitatively, as the literatures state them, and asserts no figures of its own. Where the literatures disagree, about the quality of schooling behind the access statistics, for instance, or about the interpretation of mortality trends, the disagreement is reported as a disagreement. The pattern, however, is not in serious dispute. No credible strand of the research literature holds that Kerala's social indicators were ordinary by Indian standards, or that its income was high when they were achieved. What is disputed, vigorously and for decades, is attribution. That dispute is the article's subject.

## Four causes, one outcome

The allocation defended here distinguishes four causes because the historical record presents four distinct mechanisms, operating in different periods, through different institutions, leaving different evidence behind. Collapsing them into a single story about enlightened government, or about communism, or about culture, destroys information that the record supplies in abundance. The first cause is princely-state provision. The kingdoms of Travancore and Cochin, which between them covered most of what became the modern state, spent on schools and medical services from the nineteenth century at levels that historians of education describe as exceptional among Indian princely states, and a Travancore proclamation of the 1930s opened temples to all castes in a single legislative stroke that reformers elsewhere in India were still demanding. The second cause is social reform from below. From the late nineteenth century, movements organised among Ezhava, Nair, Christian, Muslim, and other communities built the associational machinery of a mobilised society: caste associations that founded schools and colleges, religious reformers who preached dignity and education against the caste order, and a print culture that made public argument a habit. The third cause is communist-era policy in the broad sense, meaning the policies of the communist-led governments and the movements behind them: land reform that ended tenancy, food distribution that reached most households, support to public schooling and health services, a literacy campaign that mobilised volunteers at scale, and the People's Plan Campaign that decentralised planning to village assemblies. The fourth cause is remittance income from Gulf migration, which from the 1970s raised household consumption, financed private education and health spending, rebuilt the housing stock, and plugged the state's households into an external labour market, all without any government in Thiruvananthapuram directing it.

Each cause explains part of the pattern and fails to explain the rest, and the failures are as informative as the successes. Princely provision explains why the region entered the twentieth century with a head start in schooling and clinics, and it cannot explain the land relations of the countryside or the political mobilisation of the tenantry. Social reform explains the demand side, a population organised to claim services and to value schooling for daughters as well as sons, and it cannot by itself explain the abolition of tenancy or the fiscal commitments of a state government. Communist-era policy explains the redistribution of land and the deepening of public provision after 1957, and it cannot explain why the ground was so receptive, or why the first communist ministry inherited districts where literacy work had proceeded for a century. Remittances explain the consumption levels and the private building boom of the later decades, and they cannot explain the social indicators, which were already distinctive before large-scale migration began, nor the public institutions through which much health and education continued to be delivered. An allocation that keeps these boundaries visible is the article's answer to the question the case always provokes, and the sections that follow take each cause in turn before the allocation is assembled and graded.

## The princely inheritance: Travancore and Cochin

The territory of the modern state was assembled from three pieces with distinct administrative histories: the princely states of Travancore and Cochin in the south and centre, and the Malabar district in the north, which had been governed directly as part of the Madras Presidency. The distinction matters to the allocation because the social indicators were historically strongest in the princely territories, and the princely advantage predates every political movement discussed later in this article. Travancore's rulers committed state funds to education early. A royal rescript of 1817, issued in the name of Rani Gowri Parvathy Bayi, declared that the state should defray the cost of educating its people, a formulation historians of Indian education quote precisely because a princely government accepting financial responsibility for mass schooling in that period was so unusual. Through the nineteenth century the Travancore administration opened and subsidised schools, supported teacher training, and extended a network of grant-in-aid institutions, while its medical department built hospitals and dispensaries and ran vaccination programmes. Cochin followed a comparable path on a smaller scale. Missionary societies, Protestant and Catholic, added their own schools and printing presses, often in cooperation with the state and sometimes in competition with it, and Syrian Christian communities, long established on the coast, invested heavily in education as a communal strategy.

The result, documented in the historical literature on Travancore society associated with the historian Robin Jeffrey and others, was a region in which schooling was not a foreign implant of the twentieth century but a familiar institution with generations of accumulated presence. Families in large parts of the princely territories had encountered the school, the dispensary, and the printed word long before independence. Demand for education was not created by a government campaign in the 1950s; it was inherited by every government of the 1950s, communist and non-communist alike, as a settled feature of the society they sought to rule. This is the sense in which the first cause is genuinely a cause and not merely a backdrop. A different princely history, one of fiscal neglect and courtly indifference such as characterised many Indian states, would have bequeathed a different starting point, and the subsequent movements and governments would have faced a harder task. The allocation credits princely provision with the head start in supply: the schools and clinics that existed, the administrative habit of funding them, and the legitimacy that public provision enjoyed as a proper function of government in the region's political culture.

The princely record also contains a social intervention of a different order. In 1936 the Maharaja of Travancore, Chithira Thirunal Balarama Varma, issued the Temple Entry Proclamation, opening temples under state control to all Hindu worshippers regardless of caste. The proclamation followed years of agitation, including the Vaikom Satyagraha of the mid-1920s, in which reformers had demanded access to roads around a temple for communities excluded by caste rule, and it was pressed on the state by a reform movement that had made exclusion a public scandal. Its significance for the allocation is double. As an act of a princely government, it belongs to the first cause. As a product of organised pressure from the reform movements, it belongs equally to the second, and the overlap is instructive: the causes in this article are distinct mechanisms, not sealed boxes, and several of the decisive episodes sit at their junctions. A state that could be pressured into legislating against caste exclusion in worship was already a state in which the reform movements had changed what government was expected to do.

### What did the princely states actually provide?

Schools, hospitals, dispensaries, vaccination, and grant aid to private institutions, funded from state revenues from the nineteenth century onward, together with an administrative expectation that public provision was a duty of government. The provision was uneven across regions and communities, strongest in Travancore, weaker in Malabar, but substantial enough to give the region an educational and medical head start.

### Why did temple entry matter beyond religion?

Because it demonstrated that caste exclusion could be abolished by public authority under organised pressure, and it did so a decade before independence. The proclamation weakened the ritual sanction behind the wider caste order, encouraged the reform associations that had campaigned for it, and taught a generation that collective agitation could move the state.

Against these credits stand the limits of the princely contribution, which the allocation records with equal care. The princely states were not democracies, and their provision coexisted with a rigid caste hierarchy, with tenancy relations in the countryside that left cultivators insecure and indebted, and with political repression of the movements that pressed for responsible government. Malabar, under direct British administration, lagged the princely territories in provision while experiencing some of the region's most exploitative tenancy conditions, and it later became a stronghold of the communist-led peasant movement for reasons the princely story cannot supply. Nor did princely provision extend itself automatically into equality of access: communities at the bottom of the caste order benefited last and least from schools their taxes helped to fund, which is why the demand for provision had to be organised, fought for, and monitored by the movements of the second cause. The head start was real. It was also incomplete, unevenly shared, and politically inert until other forces took it up. Grading the first cause means holding both halves of that sentence at once, against celebrants of princely benevolence and against accounts that treat everything before 1957 as darkness.

## Reform from below: the movements that mobilised a society

The second cause is the dense world of social reform movements that reorganised Kerala society between the late nineteenth century and the middle of the twentieth. Its central figures and organisations are well documented. Sree Narayana Guru, a religious teacher from the Ezhava community, preached a doctrine of one caste, one religion, one God for humanity, consecrated temples open in defiance of caste restriction, and urged his followers toward education, organisation, cleanliness, and enterprise. The Sree Narayana Dharma Paripalana Yogam, founded in 1903 as the movement's organisational arm, built temples, schools, and colleges and turned a subordinated community into a collective political presence. Ayyankali, organising among Pulaya agricultural labourers, led struggles for the right to use public roads, to wear upper garments, and to send children to school, including a celebrated strike of agricultural workers whose demand was precisely admission of their children to classrooms. Among Nairs, the Nair Service Society, founded by Mannathu Padmanabhan in 1914, campaigned against the internal subdivisions and decaying joint-family property arrangements of that community and built its own network of educational institutions. Christian churches expanded schooling through their parishes and missions, and Muslim reform currents in Malabar promoted education within their community as well. Over the same decades, print expanded explosively: newspapers, journals, reading rooms, and libraries multiplied, and the region developed the newspaper-reading, argument-following public that later political movements could address.

The mechanism of this cause is demand and organisation. Princely supply met, in these movements, a population learning to claim provision as a right and to build institutions of its own where the state fell short. Girls' schooling is the clearest case. The reform movements, and the matrilineal property arrangements that in several communities gave women a recognised position in the household, together normalised female education generations before it became a policy slogan elsewhere. Historians of the region stress that the social indicators of the later period are unintelligible without this demand side: schools fill where families send daughters as well as sons, clinics are used where patients regard modern medicine as theirs to claim, and both behaviours were cultivated by movements that framed education and health as instruments of communal dignity. The movements also trained the organisers. The caste associations and reform campaigns were schools of public life, producing the meeting habits, the petitioning skills, the fund-raising networks, and the leadership cadres from which the political parties, including the communists, later recruited. One influential reading of the case, associated with the political sociologist Manali Desai and supported by the historical work on Travancore's transformation, holds that communism succeeded in Kerala because these movements had already done the work of mobilisation: the party arrived in a society whose subordinated communities were organised, literate in significant numbers, and practised in collective claim-making, and it inherited that machinery as much as it built its own.

### Who was Sree Narayana Guru and what did he organise?

An Ezhava religious reformer whose teaching rejected caste hierarchy in the name of a single humanity, and whose followers built his Yogam into an association running temples, schools, and colleges. His movement converted religious dissent into educational investment and collective organisation, making dignity and literacy the programme of a community caste rule had assigned to subordination.

### How did caste associations become schools and hospitals?

By collecting subscriptions and endowments from their communities and spending them on institutions the state had not provided in sufficient quantity or on equal terms. The associations treated a school or hospital as communal property and proof, competed in founding them, and created both the physical network and the expectation that such institutions belonged in every locality.

The allocation therefore credits the second cause with the mobilisation without which neither supply nor policy would have bitten as they did. Its limits are the mirror of the first cause's limits. Reform movements could dignify and organise, but they could not abolish tenancy, fix rents, or compel a state to redistribute land; several of them, as they prospered, hardened into caste lobbies defending communal shares rather than attacking hierarchy as such; and their geographical and communal coverage was uneven, leaving some regions and communities, particularly in Malabar and among the most subordinated groups, to be organised later and by other means, frequently by the communists themselves. The movements also operated inside the caste order they resisted, negotiating its boundaries more often than they denied its existence, and the caste question they left unresolved returns in this series as the subject of its own article on caste and class in Indian Marxism. What the movements indisputably achieved was the transformation of a subject population into a claiming public. Every later cause presupposes that transformation. The princely state supplied services to subjects; the reform movements produced citizens who would audit the supply, and the communist movement, when it came, addressed itself to citizens already in motion.

## The communist arrival and the first ministry

Communist organisation in the region grew out of the nationalist and labour movements of the 1930s and 1940s, through the socialist wing of the Congress in Malabar, through trade unions in the coir and cashew and plantation belts, through peasant associations in the tenancy-ridden north, and through cultural fronts that carried radical ideas into the reading rooms the reform era had built. The movement's social geography followed grievance: it grew strongest where tenancy was harshest, where plantation and factory labour was concentrated, and where the reform movements had already taught organisation without delivering land or wages. By the first elections of the republic it was a major force, and in 1957, in the newly formed state of Kerala, a communist ministry under E. M. S. Namboodiripad took office through the ballot box, an event of international note because a communist party had won a free election in a large polity and formed a government bound by a constitution it had not written. The wider history of the movement, its splits and its national trajectory, belongs to the article on the communist movement in India; here the ministry matters for what it attempted and for what its fate revealed about the limits of state-level power.

The ministry's two signature measures were an education bill and an agrarian relations bill. The education bill sought to regulate the large private aided sector, standardising teachers' service conditions and curbing the power of managements, many of them church and caste bodies, over appointments. The agrarian bill proposed ceilings on holdings, security and eventual ownership for tenants, and protection for hutment dwellers. Both measures struck directly at organised interests that had flourished in the interstices of the old order: private school managements, churches that ran them, and landlords whose rents the tenancy system had guaranteed. The opposition that formed, drawing those interests together in an agitation remembered as the liberation struggle, pressed the union government until, in 1959, the ministry was dismissed under the constitution's emergency provisions for state government. The episode is usually told as drama. For the allocation it is evidence. It shows a communist government attempting, within two years of taking office, precisely the redistributive and regulatory programme that distinguishes the third cause, and it shows the attempt being defeated not at the ballot box but by a coalition of the interests the programme threatened, acting through constitutional machinery. Later communist-led governments governed with that lesson absorbed: redistribution in Kerala would proceed by legislation when majorities allowed, by mass organisation continuously, and in permanent negotiation with entrenched communal and propertied bodies that no single ministry could dissolve.

### What did the first communist ministry attempt?

Regulation of the private aided school sector, standardisation of teachers' conditions, and an agrarian law proposing ceilings, tenancy security, and protection for hutment dwellers. The programme announced the durable agenda of communist-led government in the state: redistribution of land, control of the terms on which private institutions delivered public services, and expansion of the state's obligations toward labouring households.

### Why was the first ministry dismissed?

Because its education and agrarian bills mobilised the school managements, churches, caste bodies, and landlords they threatened into a sustained agitation, and the union government used its constitutional power to remove the ministry in 1959. The dismissal set the pattern for decades: communist-led governments in Kerala would alternate with their opponents and legislate under constraint.

Between the ministry's fall and the land reforms of the following decade, the state's politics settled into the rhythm that still defines it in the historical literature: two fronts, one led by the communists and one by the Congress, alternating in office, each inheriting the other's statutes and expanding the common stock of provision. That alternation is itself part of the third cause and must not be edited out of it. Public distribution, school expansion, and health services in Kerala were not the work of one party's decades in power, because no party had decades in power. They were the work of a competitive system in which organised voters punished neglect and in which the communist movement, in and out of office, functioned as the standing pressure that kept distributive questions at the centre of politics. Attribution that ignores the alternation will overstate what government by one party achieved and understate what the movement achieved by existing as a permanent alternative. The allocation's third cause is therefore named carefully: communist-era redistribution and mobilisation, meaning the policies communist-led governments enacted plus the competitive pressure the movement exerted on every government, within a polity the earlier causes had already mobilised.

## Land reform: the abolition of a tenancy order

The centrepiece of the third cause is the land reform. Kerala's countryside, particularly in Malabar, had been governed by layered tenancy relations in which cultivating tenants held under intermediaries who held under superior landlords, with rents, renewal fees, and customary exactions draining the cultivator and with hutment dwellers occupying homestead plots at the pleasure of the landowner. Reform legislation was passed in the early 1960s and recast by the communist-led government of the late 1960s in an amendment that took effect at the start of 1970, and from that point the reform was implemented with a thoroughness that comparative studies of Indian land reform consistently treat as exceptional, alongside the West Bengal case treated elsewhere in this series. Tenancy was abolished: the state interposed itself between landlord and tenant, ownership of the cultivated land passed to the tenant against a purchase price administered through land tribunals, intermediaries lost their rent-receiving position, and ceilings on holdings, though evaded in places through transfers and exemptions for plantations, rendered a surplus for assignment. Hutment dwellers, the kudikidappu households who had lived on plots within others' compounds, received ownership of their homestead plots, a provision whose social significance exceeded its acreage because it converted the most dependent rural families into proprietors of the ground beneath their homes.

The analytical literature on Indian land reform, including the work of the political scientist Ronald Herring on Kerala, treats implementation as the variable that separates this case from the many states where reform statutes decorated the books while tenancy persisted underground. Implementation in Kerala rested on the same foundations as the rest of the third cause: an organised tenantry that knew its rights and could staff the struggle over individual claims, unions and peasant associations that monitored officials, and governments for which the reform was a defining commitment. The allocation credits the land reform with a specific and bounded set of outcomes. It ended a structure of rural subordination that had underwritten caste hierarchy in the countryside, since the landlord class and the subordinated tenantry had mapped closely onto caste rank. It gave former tenants an asset and a measure of security from which to bargain over wages and to invest in children's schooling. It weakened the material base of the old landed elite and thereby changed state politics. It did not, by itself, raise agricultural productivity in any dramatic fashion, it did not create non-agricultural employment, and its ceilings were substantially blunted by exemptions and evasion, so that the redistribution of owned area, while real, was less than the reform's rhetoric suggested. A grading that omits those qualifications has stopped grading and started celebrating.

### What did the land reform abolish in practice?

The tenancy relation itself. Intermediaries lost the right to collect rent, cultivating tenants became owners of the land they worked through a state-administered transfer, and hutment dwellers gained ownership of their homestead plots. The renewal fees, customary exactions, and the landlord's social authority over the tenant household were what the reform dismantled.

### Who were the kudikidappu dwellers?

Households that lived in huts on small plots inside landowners' compounds, holding their homes at the owner's pleasure and owing labour and deference in return. The reform's grant of ownership over these homestead plots reached the most dependent families in the countryside, for whom a few cents of land meant the end of eviction as an instrument of discipline.

Alongside land, the third cause includes the quieter machinery of public provision that communist-led governments expanded and their successors maintained. The public distribution system grew into a network of fair price shops covering nearly the whole population, supplying staple grains and other essentials at controlled prices in a state that produced only a fraction of its own food. Public health services were extended through a hierarchy of hospitals and primary centres staffed in depth unusual by Indian standards, and public schooling expanded with the aided sector regulated rather than displaced. Wage struggles by agricultural labour unions, supported by legislation setting minimum wages and by welfare funds for categories of workers such as headload workers, toddy tappers, and cashew and coir workers, raised the floor under the rural poor. None of these measures is unique in conception; versions existed across India. What the literature identifies as distinctive is coverage and use: distribution that reached most households rather than a rationed urban minority, clinics that patients actually attended, schools that children of labouring families actually entered. Coverage of that order is a political product. It reflects the organised demand of the second cause pressing on governments that the third cause's movement alternately formed and besieged.

### How did the public distribution system reach households?

Through a dense network of fair price shops entitled to supply most of the state's population with staple foods at controlled prices, sustained by procurement from outside the state and defended politically by every governing front. Its near universal coverage in a food-deficit state made it a standing transfer to poor households and a discipline on open-market prices.

### What did alternating coalitions change and what did they leave alone?

They changed emphases, personnel, and the pace of particular programmes, and they alternately expanded and trimmed subsidies under fiscal pressure. They left alone the settled stock: the land settlement, the distribution network, the school and hospital systems, and the expectation among voters that governments would maintain them. Alternation disciplined governments without dismantling the institutions, which is why single-party attribution fails.

## The literacy campaign: mobilisation as method

By the late 1980s the state's remaining illiteracy was concentrated among older adults, coastal fishing communities, plantation workers, and tribal populations in the hill districts, groups the school system had historically missed. The response was not another administrative programme but a campaign, and the campaign form is itself evidence about the third cause. The Ernakulam district total literacy campaign of 1989, followed by a statewide campaign in 1990 and 1991, was built through the Kerala Sastra Sahitya Parishad, a popular science movement with decades of experience in taking science and print into villages, working with local bodies, unions, student and youth organisations, and thousands of volunteer instructors who taught in learners' homes and workplaces. Jathas of cultural performers moved through villages to build the campaign's public atmosphere; learners were enrolled through surveys conducted street by street; teaching materials were written for adults whose working lives were in fishing, coir, cashew, and the fields. When the state was declared fully literate in 1991, the declaration rested on campaign assessments rather than on the slower machinery of census enumeration, and the research literature treats the claim with the caution due to any campaign's self-assessment. The durable finding, on which the literature is firmer, is that the campaign reached populations the schools had not, and that it did so by mobilisation rather than by departmental routine.

In the allocation's terms, the campaign is a nearly pure specimen of the third cause operating through the second. The state government of the day, communist-led, sponsored and legitimised it; the volunteer machinery came from movements and associations built over generations; the learners came forward because the reform era had made literacy a public value and illiteracy a remediable shame rather than a fate. A government attempting the same mobilisation in a society without that associational density would have had to hire its volunteers, and hired voluntarism is a contradiction the campaign never had to test. This interdependence is why the allocation resists assigning the campaign to a single row. It sits in the third row because communist-led government chose the campaign form and staked its prestige on the result. Its conditions of possibility sit in the second row, and a reader who understands why will understand the allocation's method better than any single grading can teach it. The campaign also marks the model's characteristic strength and its characteristic limit in one episode. Mobilisation could teach adults to read. It could not, by the same act, create the factories and offices in which their newly literate children would later search for work, and the distance between those two achievements is the distance the crisis literature would soon measure.

### What made the literacy campaign a campaign rather than a programme?

Volunteers rather than salaried teachers carried the instruction, cultural processions built public expectation, learners were found by street surveys rather than awaited at centres, and a popular science movement supplied the organisation. The campaign treated illiteracy as a collective condition to be ended by a society mobilised for a season, instead of a service backlog for a department to process.

## Decentralisation: the People's Plan Campaign

The last major policy of the third cause arrived in the mid-1990s. National constitutional amendments of the early 1990s had mandated elected local governments across India, and most states complied formally while keeping funds and functions at the state capital. The communist-led government that took office in Kerala in 1996 chose the radical reading of the mandate. Through the People's Plan Campaign, launched that year, a substantial share of the state's plan funds, more than a third in the campaign's own accounting, was devolved to village panchayats, municipalities, and block and district bodies, which were required to prepare their own development plans through gram sabhas, the open assemblies of village voters. The campaign was organised in phases: assemblies identified needs, sectoral working groups converted them into projects, trained volunteers and officials, many mobilised through the same popular science and movement networks as the literacy campaign, supported the drafting, and beneficiary committees supervised execution. The political scientist Patrick Heller's studies of the experiment treat it as one of the most ambitious attempts anywhere to institutionalise participatory planning, and the collaboration between the planner T. M. Thomas Isaac and the scholar Richard Franke documented its mechanics for an international audience.

The allocation's interest in the campaign is diagnostic. Decentralisation of this depth was attempted in Kerala, and functioned as well as it did, for reasons that reach back through every earlier row of the table. Local assemblies could deliberate about schools and clinics because the population was literate and habituated to public argument, the inheritance of the first two causes. The campaign could staff itself with volunteers because the movement networks of the third cause supplied them. The resources it distributed flowed into households whose consumption was increasingly supported by the fourth cause, remittances, so that local plans could assume a private building and spending boom they did not have to finance. Even so, the campaign's own evaluators and its sympathetic scholars record its limits plainly: participation thinned after the first surge, plan quality varied sharply with local capacity, productive investment lagged behind infrastructure and welfare projects, and the devolved funds depended on a state exchequer whose stresses the crisis literature was already describing. The People's Plan Campaign is therefore entered in the allocation as a genuine achievement of participatory governance and as a demonstration that participation redistributes voice more readily than it generates production. Both halves belong to the grading.

### How did the People's Plan Campaign move money?

By devolving a large share of state plan funds to elected local bodies and requiring those bodies to build their plans upward from village assemblies, through working groups and drafted project lists, rather than receiving schemes designed in the state capital. Funds followed locally prepared plans, and beneficiary committees, not contractors alone, supervised much of the execution.

The campaign also closes the third cause's account in a way that clarifies the whole allocation. Taken together, land reform, distribution, the literacy campaign, and decentralisation describe a consistent repertoire: redistribute assets, guarantee consumption floors, mobilise the population to complete what bureaucracies start, and push decisions downward toward the organised citizenry. It is the repertoire of a movement formed by the second cause and constrained by the first cause's inheritance, operating in a federation that limited its fiscal and industrial room. The repertoire raised the social indicators from the high base it inherited and equalised them across class and caste lines to a degree the inheritance alone had never achieved. What the repertoire did not include, because the state's powers and the movement's theory alike left it underdeveloped, was a strategy for the productive economy: for industry, for agricultural modernisation beyond redistribution, for employment of the educated. That absence is not an oversight to be noted in passing. It is the hinge on which the article turns from the causes of the model's success to the fourth cause, which filled the economic gap from outside, and then to the crisis literature, which asked what the model was worth once its bill arrived.

## The fourth cause: Gulf migration and remittance income

From the 1970s, the expansion of oil economies in the Persian Gulf created an enormous demand for labour at every skill level, and Kerala, with its literate workforce, its established traditions of out-migration to other Indian cities and to Ceylon and Malaya in earlier generations, and its shortage of domestic employment, supplied that demand at scale. Construction workers, technicians, nurses, drivers, shop assistants, and professionals left in large numbers for the Gulf states, on contracts that separated them from their families for years at a time, and sent earnings home. The flows were documented over decades by a series of migration surveys conducted by researchers at the Centre for Development Studies, associated particularly with K. C. Zachariah, S. Irudaya Rajan, and their collaborators, which tracked the scale of emigration, the destinations, the characteristics of migrants, and the uses of remittances. Those surveys, and the wider literature built on them, describe remittances as a major factor in the state's household economy: financing consumption at levels domestic production could not have supported, underwriting a transformation of housing from thatch and tile to concrete, paying school fees and hospital bills in a growing private sector, settling debts, funding marriages and further migration, and sustaining the retail and construction trades that became the state's most visible employment growth.

The allocation treats remittances with deliberate precision, because they are the cause most often abused in both directions. In hostile accounts, remittances swallow the model: the indicators are dismissed as bought with Gulf money, as if the clinics and schools were private purchases and the whole achievement an accounting illusion. In celebratory accounts within the Marxist tradition, remittances are minimised, mentioned as a supplement, because they complicate the story of what domestic politics achieved. The sequence disciplines both abuses. Large-scale Gulf migration postdates the consolidation of the state's distinctive position in literacy and health; the princely and reform inheritances and the first decades of public provision precede it, and the indicators that made Kerala famous in the development literature of the 1970s were recorded before remittance income reached its later scale. Migrants could leave, moreover, because they were literate and often trained, products of the school system the earlier causes built; the nurse in a Gulf hospital is the Kerala model's export, not its refutation. What remittances explain is the later economic surface of the model: consumption levels far above what the domestic economy generated, the housing stock, the private education and health markets that grew up beside the public ones, and the state's ability to sustain its social settlement without the tax base a richer economy would have supplied. They also explain part of its vulnerability, since an economy whose households depend on an external labour market inherits that market's shocks, from oil price collapses to the periodic nationalisation drives of Gulf employment policy.

### When did Gulf migration change the household economy?

From the 1970s, as oil revenues expanded Gulf demand for foreign labour and Kerala's educated, underemployed workers answered it in large numbers. Within a generation the remittance flow had become a structural feature of household finance across the state, visible in housing, consumption, private schooling and care, and documented as such by the migration survey literature.

### What do the migration surveys actually document?

A research series conducted over decades from the Centre for Development Studies, tracking who leaves, from which districts and communities, to which destinations, on what terms, and what the money sent home is used for. The surveys established remittances as a measurable pillar of the household economy and charted migration's shift toward new destinations and skill levels across successive waves.

The social costs and transformations of migration belong in the grading too. The literature records households headed for long stretches by women managing alone, elderly parents cared for at a distance, a generation of children raised with absent fathers, and return migrants whose savings and expectations did not always survive re-entry into a slow economy. It records gains in the status and bargaining power of women in migrant households alongside new burdens on the same women. It records a labour market at home reshaped by scarcity, as the departure of workers raised local wages and drew in replacement labour from poorer Indian states, an inflow that reproduced, inside Kerala, the migrant condition Keralites knew abroad. None of this cancels the fourth cause's contribution to living standards. It establishes that the contribution arrived as a social transformation with its own casualties, not as a neutral transfer, and that a model partly financed by exporting its workforce has answered the employment question by relocating it. The crisis literature begins exactly there.

## The allocation assembled

The four causes can now be set side by side. The table below is the article's findable artifact, the four causes allocation, and the prose around it in this section and the next two performs the grading the table summarises. Read it as an argument in compressed form: each row claims a mechanism, assigns the outcomes that mechanism plausibly produced, names the kind of evidence behind the assignment, and states, in the final column, what the cause cannot explain, because a cause that is never told what it cannot explain will expand until it explains everything and therefore nothing.

Three features of the table deserve emphasis before the objections are heard. First, the rows are ordered by time, and the order is the argument. Causes that begin earlier constrain and enable the ones that follow: the reform movements presuppose enough princely and mission schooling to produce their first organisers, the communists presuppose the movements' public, and the migrants presuppose the communists' and reformers' schools. Single-cause accounts fail not because their favoured cause did nothing but because they crop the sequence at the point where their cause enters, treating inherited conditions as background. Second, the rows operate through different kinds of mechanism: state supply, social mobilisation, political redistribution, and external income. A grading that mixed them into one index of credit would conceal that the model's components are not substitutes for one another. Remittance income cannot be converted into a literacy campaign by spending it; a literacy campaign cannot be converted into land. Third, every row's final column is load-bearing. The allocation is falsifiable in a modest, usable sense: evidence that an outcome assigned to one row in fact preceded that row's operation, or persisted where the row's mechanism was absent, would force a regrading, and the next sections apply exactly that test to the strongest rival allocation on offer.

### Did communism cause Kerala's social outcomes?

partly. Substantial education and health provision predates communist government, and social reform movements had already mobilised the population. Communist-led governments contributed land reform, public distribution, a literacy campaign, and decentralised planning. Migrant remittances from the 1970s are a further major factor.

### Why does sequence matter more than size in causal argument?

Because a later cause, however large, cannot explain an outcome that already existed before it operated, while an earlier cause shapes what later causes can do. Remittances are enormous and explain consumption; they cannot explain indicators recorded before migration reached scale. Communist redistribution transformed land relations; it cannot explain the literacy of the society that demanded it.

### What would count as evidence against the four-cause allocation?

A demonstration that one row's mechanism was absent where its assigned outcomes appeared, or present in full where they did not. If districts without princely provision, reform organisation, or communist strength had matched the rest on the indicators at the same dates, the first three rows would collapse, and the argument would need rebuilding around whatever those districts shared.

## The strongest objection: the inheritance did it

The allocation must now face the objection that would, if sustained, demote its third row from cause to decoration. Stated at full strength, and attributed to the scholarship that advances it, the objection runs as follows. The outcomes that make Kerala distinctive were substantially in place before communist government existed. Princely provision had built the schools and hospitals; the reform movements had built the demanding public; female literacy and the social attitudes behind the health transition were products of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, of matrilineal custom in several communities, of missionary and communal schooling, and of the princely administrations. The historical scholarship on Travancore, including Robin Jeffrey's accounts of the decline of the old caste order and the spread of education, locates the transformation's engine in that earlier period. The political sociology associated with Manali Desai adds the organisational version: communist success in Kerala was possible because social mobilisation preceded the party, so the party is better read as the inheritance's beneficiary than as the outcomes' author. Then, the objection continues, look at what sustains the model's living standards in its later decades: not domestic production, which stagnated, but remittances earned in the Gulf, an income stream no Kerala government created and none controls. On this account the communist contribution reduces to an addition, real but secondary, laid over an inheritance produced by princes and reformers and financed in its maturity by migrant labour. The celebration of Kerala as a communist achievement mistakes the custodian for the creator.

The objection is partly right, and the allocation concedes its chronology without reservation. The sequence does show major outcomes, above all in education and in the social demand for health services, preceding communist government by generations. Any version of the third row that claims origination for those outcomes is false, and the allocation has been written throughout to make no such claim: the third row is graded on redistribution, equalisation, and completion, not on invention. Where the objection overreaches is in its treatment of addition as a minor category. Consider what the addition consisted of. The inheritance, for all its strength, coexisted in 1956 with a tenancy system that kept cultivators insecure and subordinated, with landlord authority woven into caste hierarchy, with access to the princely inheritance distributed steeply by caste and class, and with pockets of the population, tenants, hutment dwellers, plantation labour, fishing communities, largely untouched by its benefits. The communist-era contribution attacked precisely the points where the inheritance stopped. Land reform dissolved the rural power that the reform movements had protested but never broken, and it did so on a scale comparative studies judge exceptional within India. Distribution and service expansion pushed the inherited institutions toward universal use, and the literacy campaign closed the residual gap among the adults the schools had missed. Equalisation of an inherited stock is not a footnote to the stock's creation. A society can possess schools for a century while the children of tenants attend them at the pleasure of landlords, and the political act that removes the pleasure and installs a right changes what the schools are.

The objection's remittance arm requires the same treatment: concede the fact, contest the inference. Remittances plainly sustain later consumption, and the allocation's fourth row says so. But the inference that the model's social achievement is therefore a purchase confuses the financing of households with the production of the indicators. The indicators were produced by institutions, schools, clinics, distribution shops, staffed and used over decades, and the earliest of them were distinctive before the Gulf flow began. Moreover, the remittance stream itself ran through channels the earlier causes dug: workers migrated with literacy and certificates the public system issued, nurses trained in the state's institutions staffed Gulf hospitals, and the money returned to households whose spending on schooling and health expressed the demand the reform era had normalised. The fourth cause is real and external, and it is also downstream of the first three. An allocation is a map of such dependencies, and the objection, properly answered, improves the map rather than overturning it: it forces the third row to surrender origination, which it never owned, and leaves it holding redistribution and completion, which the record will not let any other row claim.

### What survives of the objection once its chronology is granted?

As decisive in redistribution and completion, derivative in origination. Communist-led governments and the movement behind them abolished tenancy, universalised the use of inherited services, finished mass literacy by campaign, and decentralised planning. They did not create the educated, organised society they governed, and the allocation refuses them that credit in terms.

One further discipline belongs to this section. The objection has been stated from named kinds of scholarship, the social history of Travancore, the political sociology of the state's social democracy, and the migration economics of the survey literature, because an objection without owners can be answered too easily. Its owners would not all accept the use made of their work here, and some historians of the princely period are equally severe on the princely record's caste blindness, while the migration economists are the first to insist that remittances presuppose the school system. That untidiness is faithful to the literature. What the objection establishes, at minimum and beyond serious dispute, is that the single-cause celebration of the Kerala model as a communist creation cannot survive the chronology. What it does not establish is that the communist decades were a custodianship. Between creation and custodianship lies the actual record, and the allocation is an attempt to draw its map.

## The crisis literature at full strength

From the 1990s a body of work associated with development economists of the Thiruvananthapuram Centre for Development Studies and with critics across the state's universities argued that the model was in difficulty, and the instruction governing this article, that the literature be presented at full strength, reflects its standing as the principal qualification on everything claimed so far. Its most cited early statement, the economist K. K. George's study of the limits of the Kerala model, set the terms: the social achievements had been won at the cost of economic stagnation, and the settlement they rested on was fiscally and productively unsustainable. Developed across the following decades by many hands, the literature's case has five strands, and each deserves statement in its strongest form.

The first strand is fiscal. The state's governments, bound by the settlement's commitments to salaries, pensions, subsidies, and an extensive public payroll, ran persistent revenue deficits and accumulated debt that consumed a growing share of income in interest. Fiscal stress of that order narrows every government's room: capital expenditure is squeezed, maintenance of the very schools and hospitals the model boasts of is deferred, and each front in turn discovers that the inheritance costs more to run than the economy yields to run it. The second strand is industrial. Manufacturing in the state remained thin and its traditional industries, coir, cashew, handloom, fisheries processing, faced decline, mechanisation elsewhere, or resource exhaustion, while new industry was deterred, in the critics' account, by militant labour practices, high wages relative to productivity, land scarcity, and an investor image the state's politics had earned fairly or not. The third strand is employment. Education expanded faster than the economy's demand for it, producing the phenomenon the literature calls educated unemployment: queues of degree holders for government posts, long waits between qualification and work, and particular distress among educated young women whose families had invested most hopefully in their schooling. K. P. Kannan's research on the state's labour market documented the structure of this unemployment and the informalisation beneath the headline settlement. The fourth strand is the quality question. Behind impressive access statistics, critics pressed evidence and argument about learning outcomes in schools, about absenteeism and dual practice in public health facilities, and about a steady middle-class exit into private schooling and private hospitals, which they read as the consumers' verdict on the public stock. The fifth strand is dependence. The consumption that fiscal policy could not finance was financed by remittances, making the model's living standards hostage to Gulf labour markets and oil prices, so that the state appeared, in the critics' sharpest formulation, to have educated its people for export and to live on the proceeds.

To these five the literature adds a sixth, political strand: a critique of politicisation. Appointments, transfers, union recognition, and the management of public institutions were alleged to run through party networks of both fronts, corroding professional autonomy in universities, hospitals, and the civil service, and converting the organised strength that built the model into a machinery of spoils. Defenders of the settlement answer each strand, and their answers have force: fiscal stress afflicted Indian states generally under the same federal arrangements; industrial decline had national and global causes in the traditional sectors; educated unemployment is the shadow of educational success and was partly relieved by the very migration the critics count as dependence; private exit reflects rising incomes as much as public failure; and politicisation, they note, is the tax a mobilised democracy pays, preferable to the quiet capture of institutions by caste and money elsewhere. The allocation does not adjudicate this exchange, because the exchange concerns the model's future rather than its causes. It records only what the crisis literature establishes for the allocation's purposes: that the third cause's repertoire, redistribution and mobilisation, did not generate a productive economy adequate to the society it educated, and that this gap, visible from the 1990s in the work of the state's own development economists, is a fact about the model and not a hostile invention. A grading of causes that omitted it would leave the fourth row of the table unintelligible, since it was the gap that migration filled.

### Why do educated young people feature so heavily in the crisis account?

Because the model's success in schooling outran the economy's creation of jobs suited to the schooled, leaving degree holders queueing for scarce government posts and waiting years for work matching their qualifications. The critics treat that queue as the settlement's internal contradiction: education supplied the aspiration and the credential, while production supplied neither post nor industry.

### Which part of the model depends least on Kerala's past?

The remittance economy. Gulf demand for labour was an external event that would have raised incomes in any literate, migration-ready society, and its scale owed more to oil prices and Gulf construction than to anything in the state's history. Even here the past mattered, since literacy made the workers employable, but this is the cause least authored at home.

## Three regions, three starting points

The state's internal geography supplies the closest thing the case offers to a controlled comparison, and the allocation leans on it heavily. Travancore in the south entered the union with the deepest princely provision, a dense network of mission and community schools, and social reform movements of exceptional reach. Cochin, smaller and similarly governed, stood not far behind. Malabar in the north entered with the weakest provision of the three, having been administered as a district of Madras, and with tenancy relations of notorious severity, in which layers of intermediaries stood between the soil and the cultivator and eviction hung over the tenant household as a routine instrument. If princely provision and reform organisation were the whole story, the modern state's indicators should display a permanent southern advantage and a northern lag. If communist organisation were the whole story, the pattern should track the movement's strength instead. What the record shows is more interesting than either expectation. The princely territories did hold their early advantage in literacy for generations, exactly as the first two rows predict. Malabar, meanwhile, became the heartland of the peasant movement and of communist electoral strength, and its districts closed much of the historical gap during the decades of land reform and service expansion, exactly as the third row predicts for an organised tenantry suddenly possessed of rights and organisation. The regional pattern thus corroborates the allocation's structure rather than any single row: inheritance set the gradient, and redistribution flattened it.

The same geography disciplines the remittance row. Gulf migration drew from all three regions but with different social profiles, and the survey literature traces how remittance spending transformed housing and consumption in the northern districts with particular speed, since those households had the most ground to make up. A reader testing the allocation against the map will find each cause strongest where its mechanism was strongest, which is the pattern genuine causes produce and rhetorical ones do not. The point is worth stating as method, because it generalises across this series. When a movement or a government claims an outcome, the claim can be audited against variation inside the case: across regions, across communities, across decades. Kerala rewards that audit unusually well because its three regions entered the modern state with different combinations of the four causes, and their subsequent histories read almost as if designed to separate the causes' effects. Almost, but not quite, and the residue of confounding, the fact that Malabar combined weak provision with harsh tenancy and therefore with both communist strength and reform energy of its own, is recorded here so that the regional argument is not asked to carry more than comparison can bear.

## Women, matriliny, and the demand side of the model

No account of the demand side is complete without the position of women, and here the region's social history departs from the Indian mainstream in ways the literature treats as foundational. Several of the state's major communities, most prominently the Nairs, organised kinship matrilineally: descent and property ran through the female line, women held recognised rights in the ancestral household, and residence patterns gave wives a security unusual in patrilineal India. Matriliny in its classical form decayed across the twentieth century under legal reform, partition of joint estates, and the pull of the conjugal household, a transformation Robin Jeffrey chronicled in his study of the decline of Nair dominance. Its legacy for the model was not the property system itself, which largely vanished, but the social expectation it had normalised: that women were persons of standing within the household, that their movements and schooling were ordinary subjects of family strategy, and that a daughter's education was an investment rather than an anomaly. Layered over that legacy came the reform movements' explicit campaigns for girls' schooling, the mission schools that admitted girls early, and the princely administrations that funded female education and trained women teachers and nurses in numbers the literature describes as remarkable for the period.

The consequences run through every indicator the model is famous for. Literate mothers are the single most reliable predictor the demographic literature knows for child survival, school continuation, and early fertility decline, and Kerala's fertility transition, achieved without coercive policy and ahead of its income level, is the literature's standard illustration of that mechanism. The health system's utilisation shows the same signature: women who regard clinics as theirs to use, who can travel to them and speak in them, convert supply into outcomes in a way that provision alone never achieves. The allocation therefore registers the gender settlement as a constituent part of the second cause, the social transformation from below, rather than as a fifth cause, while noting that scholars dispute the weight to give matriliny itself against schooling policy and reform campaigning. What is not disputed is the direction of the evidence. The model's demand side was feminised early, and any society attempting to learn from Kerala while leaving its own gender order untouched has declined the lesson at the point where the literature says it matters most. The crisis literature returns the compliment in its own register, since its account of educated unemployment is disproportionately an account of young women whose schooling the settlement encouraged and whose employment it failed to supply, a debt the model owes precisely to those who made it work.

## The labour movement beneath the politics

Between the reform associations and the party governments stands a formation easy to overlook in an allocation organised by governments and movements: the organised working class of the coir yards, cashew factories, tile works, plantations, and docks. Kerala's traditional industries employed large numbers in conditions the early labour organisers described in harrowing detail, and from the 1930s unions organised those workers into some of the most durable industrial organisations in India. Cashew processing, staffed overwhelmingly by women, and coir, spread through the backwater belt in small sheds and household production alike, became union strongholds whose struggles set wage floors and working conditions across their regions. Agricultural labourers organised separately and in alliance with the tenantry, and the headload workers of the markets and godowns built a union famous enough to become a byword, for solidarity among its admirers and for restrictive practice among its critics. This organised layer matters to the allocation in three ways. It supplied the communist movement with its mass base and its cadre discipline, converting the second cause's associational habits into industrial power. It raised the wage floor under the rural and urban poor directly, through strikes and legislation the unions pressed successive governments to enact and enforce, so that part of what the table credits to government policy was in fact conceded to organised pressure. And it generated, in time, the phenomenon the crisis literature indicted: labour practices that employers experienced as inflexible and investors as deterrent, so that the same organisation appears in the record as both a cause of the model's equity and a cause of its industrial thinness.

An honest allocation keeps both appearances. The wage floor was a genuine transfer to the poorest households, and its effects on children's schooling and family nutrition are part of how the indicators were equalised. The deterrent effect on footloose industry was also genuine, though the critics' version requires the qualification that Kerala's traditional industries were declining for reasons of technology, resources, and global competition that no labour regime caused, and that investors weighed power supply, land prices, and national policy alongside union strength. The deeper point is structural. The model's political economy rested on an organised working class strong enough to defend its share and a state economy too weak to grow that share by expansion, so distribution became a contest over a slowly growing product. That is the material setting in which the crisis literature's fiscal and employment strands grew, and it explains why the fourth cause arrived as such a relief: remittances expanded the product from outside the contest, relaxing for a generation the zero-sum pressure that the domestic economy imposed on every distributive claim.

## The health system as the model's working machinery

Health is where the model's abstractions become bricks, staff rosters, and the daily experience of households, and it rewards a closer look than summary treatments give it. The physical network descends from the princely medical departments, which built hospitals in the towns and dispensaries in the countryside and ran the vaccination and sanitation services of small states that regarded public health as a visible proof of good rule. After independence the network was extended downward through primary health centres and sub-centres, staffed by doctors, nurses, and field workers in densities the health literature describes as high by Indian standards, and upward through district hospitals and medical colleges. Three features of the system's working life matter for the allocation. The first is utilisation: the population used the facilities, travelled to them, and expected treatment from them, an expectation the demand side of the second cause had built and which distinguishes a functioning system from a map of buildings. The second is staffing culture: the state trained nurses and paramedical workers in large numbers, many of them women, and exported the surplus across India and eventually to the Gulf, so that the health system and the migration stream were staffed by the same educational pipeline. The third is the private sector's growth alongside the public one, accelerating as remittance income rose, until the state's health economy became a mixed system in which public facilities carried much preventive and inpatient care while private hospitals and clinics captured a rising share of curative spending.

The critical literature on health presses two qualifications that belong in any grading. One concerns quality and conduct: reports and studies record absenteeism, informal payments, and the pattern known as dual practice, in which public doctors maintain private clinics, alongside the defensive argument that the system's outcomes remain superior to those of states spending comparable shares. The other concerns the epidemiological transition itself: a population that survives infancy and lives long accumulates the chronic diseases of ageing, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cancers, whose treatment is expensive and prolonged, so that the model's health success generated a cost structure unlike that of a young, high-mortality population. The literature on Kerala's health transition treats this as the fiscal face of victory: the system must now finance the diseases of longevity for a population whose incomes did not rise with its lifespan. Neither qualification overturns the health row of the allocation. Together they specify what the row's credit consists in: a delivery system that converted modest resources into outcomes through density, staffing, and use, and whose later difficulties are substantially the difficulties of its own success arriving at a treasury too small for it.

## Education: the aided sector and the long argument over quality

Education displays the four causes in miniature, because every one of them left an institution in the schoolyard. The princely states funded government schools and aided private ones. The reform movements and churches founded the great majority of the aided sector, so that the typical Kerala school of the twentieth century was privately managed and publicly financed, owned by a caste association, a church, a Muslim educational society, or a local trust, staffed by teachers paid from the state treasury under rules the state set. Communist-led governments, beginning with the ill-fated education bill of the first ministry, sought to discipline that settlement: to standardise teachers' pay and service conditions, restrain the sale of appointments and capitation practices that reformers alleged against some managements, and assert public purposes over private proprietors. The settlement that emerged from decades of conflict was a compromise with a distinct shape: the aided sector survived, its teachers became effectively public servants in pay and security, its managements retained ownership and a role in appointments, and the state's politics absorbed a permanent negotiation between government and the educational interests, in which the churches and associations functioned as powers no front could ignore. Alongside it grew, from the remittance decades onward, a sector of unaided private schools charging fees, drawing the children of migrant and salaried households, and teaching increasingly in English medium, so that the state's school map came to register its class structure with some precision.

The quality debate is the part of the education story the celebratory literature handles worst, and this article follows the critics in insisting on it. Access, in the sense of enrolment and years completed, is not in question; the argument concerns what the access delivered. Assessments and commentaries assembled by education researchers describe uneven learning outcomes, rote-dominated pedagogy in many institutions, and a scramble for professional and technical seats that pushed families toward the unaided sector and toward colleges outside the state. Defenders reply that comparisons of learning are themselves contested, that the public and aided systems carry obligations the fee-charging sector declines, including the education of the poorest and of children in remote areas, and that the state's examination results and its production of graduates for national institutions testify against simple decline. The allocation records the debate rather than settling it, with one grading consequence: the education credit in the first three rows is a credit for access achieved and sustained, and for the social levelling that near-universal schooling performed, and it is not a certificate of pedagogical excellence, which the literature does not uniformly grant. A model whose strongest verified claim is that everyone went to school, in a country where that was nowhere else true at its income level, has claim enough without borrowing virtues its own researchers dispute.

### How should a reader weigh access against quality in the education record?

By keeping the two claims separate and grading each on its own evidence. Access is established by enrolment and attainment across generations and classes; quality is contested in assessments whose methods and baselines are themselves disputed. Conflating them lets celebration borrow quality it has not proven and lets criticism erase access it cannot deny.

## Food, land use, and the economy underneath

The land reform redistributed rights over an agricultural economy whose structure limited what redistribution could yield. Kerala's farms are small, its holdings fragmented by generations of partition, its terrain divided between coastal lowland, midland garden cultivation, and highland plantations. Commercial crops, coconut, rubber, pepper, cardamom, tea, coffee, cashew, occupy much of the cultivated area, while the staple food, rice, is grown on lowland paddy fields that shrank steadily as garden crops, housing, and speculation claimed the land. The state consequently imports the greater part of its foodgrain, a deficit as old as the princely period, when Travancore already drew rice from outside, and the public distribution system's importance is unintelligible without it: distribution was the mechanism by which a chronically food-short state guaranteed its households access to staples at tolerable prices, whatever the harvest or the market. Agricultural wages, pressed upward by union organisation and by the scarcity migration created, rose to levels the farm economy's productivity could not comfortably carry, and paddy cultivation contracted under the combined pressure of wages, conversion, and the pull of more profitable or less labour-hungry uses. The literature on the state's agrarian change records this as a transformation with genuine gains and genuine losses braided together: the labourer's household ate better and schooled its children, the rice bowl shrank, and the landscape filled with homestead gardens and houses financed from the Gulf.

For the allocation, the agrarian record performs a clarifying service about the third cause. Land reform in Kerala was a reform of relations more than of production: it ended a tenancy order and the social power built on it, and it was never, in the event, the prelude to a collective or state-led transformation of farming itself. Holdings remained private, family-operated, and increasingly part-time as households diversified into wages, trade, services, and migration. Judged as agrarian revolution the reform is modest; judged as the abolition of a caste-marked structure of rural domination, it is among the most consequential acts of any Indian state government. The Marxist tradition's own categories help state the distinction: the reform changed the relations of production in the countryside without transforming the forces of production, and the state's subsequent history, commercial, service-heavy, remittance-fed, is what a society looks like when the first change is completed and the second is left to markets and migrants. Readers who come to the case expecting land reform to have industrialised Kerala will misread it; readers who see that it equalised the starting position of rural households before the migration era will have grasped its place in the allocation.

## Comparisons that discipline the story

Comparison is the allocation's external check, and two comparisons do most of the work. The first is with the rest of India. Other states had land reform statutes; few implemented tenancy abolition with Kerala's completeness, and the comparative literature on Indian land reform standardly pairs Kerala with West Bengal as the exceptions that prove the implementation rule, a pairing this series maintains by treating the Bengal case in its own article. Other states had princely territories with creditable provision, and some, Mysore and Baroda among them, invested seriously in education, yet none combined that inheritance with reform movements of Kerala's density, a communist movement of Kerala's electoral weight, and a migration outlet of Kerala's scale. The model's distinctiveness is conjunctural: it is the combination, in sequence, that no other state replicated, which is precisely what the four-cause form asserts. The second comparison runs the other way, against the Indian diaspora's other great sending regions and against remittance economies generally. Remittances flow to many poor regions without producing Kerala's indicators, because the indicators require institutions for the money to amplify: schools worth paying fees to, clinics worth attending, a state whose services set the standard the private sector must meet. Where those institutions are absent, remittance income builds houses and imports consumption goods and leaves the social indicators where it found them. The comparison thus returns credit to the first three rows from the fourth row's own evidence: migration money is a multiplier, and multipliers act on what exists.

A third comparison, internal to the Marxist tradition, concerns the Soviet and socialist-state record treated elsewhere in this series. Kerala's communists governed a state inside a federal democracy they did not control, under a constitution that protected property enough to frustrate them and speech enough to organise them, and they were removable by voters and were removed. The outcomes claimed for their tenure were achieved, where they were achieved, under those constraints, with opposition parties auditing every claim and succeeding them in office. Whatever the verdict on the model's economics, its politics refute the lazy equation of communist government with single-party rule: the Kerala case is the record of a communist movement operating as one competitor among others for two generations, its achievements and failures alike produced in the open and judged at elections it repeatedly lost. That is why the case bears so heavily on the questions this series asks about Marxism in India, and why the exam literature returns to it as the tradition's most examinable Indian specimen.

## What Kerala proves about redistribution, and what it does not

The careful reader can now extract the case's defensible lessons, and the allocation exists to keep the extraction honest. Kerala demonstrates that redistribution works on the dimensions it directly touches. Abolish tenancy under conditions of organised tenantry and landlord power in the countryside genuinely ends. Fund schools and clinics for generations and staff them, and a population will use them into outcomes that income alone would not have bought. Guarantee staple food through public channels in a deficit region and the floor under the poorest household rises measurably in security if not in statistics this article may quote. Mobilise volunteers through movements a society already trusts and residual illiteracy can be hunted down in a campaign season. Devolve plan funds to assemblies of literate citizens and local priorities will be set with a legitimacy central schemes envy. Each of these propositions is supported by the record assembled above, and together they amount to the strongest empirical brief the Indian Marxist tradition possesses for its distributive politics.

The same record refuses the larger inferences sometimes drawn from it. Kerala does not demonstrate that redistribution substitutes for production, since its own settlement ran into the fiscal and employment walls the crisis literature describes and was relieved by an external income its politics did not create. It does not demonstrate that the sequence can be started anywhere at will, since its first two causes are nineteenth and early twentieth century inheritances that a policy cannot legislate into existence on demand. It does not demonstrate that communist government is necessary for such outcomes, since its rivals governed half the period and maintained the settlement, nor that communist government is sufficient, since the movement's strength rested on preconditions it inherited. And it does not demonstrate a route out of the employment question, the question its educated young people pose to every government of the state. What it demonstrates, with unusual clarity because the sequence is unusually well documented, is a division of labour among causes: inheritance supplies capacity, mobilisation supplies demand and discipline, redistribution equalises and completes, and external income, when it comes, multiplies whatever the first three have built. A tradition that reads the case whole will claim the third term proudly and the others gratefully. A tradition that reads it selectively will go on being refuted by its own favourite example.

## The tenancy order the reform abolished

To grade the land reform, a reader must first see the order it ended, because summary phrases about feudal remnants conceal the lived machinery. In Malabar, rights over land were stacked in layers. At the top stood the jenmi, the superior landlord, often a high-caste household, a temple, or a mosque institution, holding the ultimate claim. Below stood intermediary tenure holders who took land on long arrangements and sublet in turn, and at the bottom the actual cultivator, the verumpattam tenant, whose hold on the plot he worked could be terminated and whose rent was supplemented by renewal fees, customary presents, and obligations that blurred rent into tribute. Eviction, or its threat, disciplined the whole chain. Tenants who improved land risked losing the improvement's value at renewal; tenants who resisted exactions risked the plot itself. In the south, Travancore's tenures had been partly regularised by earlier state action, and the royal government's own lands were administered under arrangements that gave many cultivators a more direct relation to the state, one reason the agrarian temperature differed between regions. In the homestead compounds across the state, the kudikidappu dweller occupied the extreme of dependence: a hut on a corner of the landlord's land, held by permission, paid for in labour and loyalty, terminable at will.

The political meaning of this order was that economic and ritual hierarchy reinforced one another at the point of daily life. The landlord was frequently of a caste entitled to deference; the tenant's obligations included marks of subordination that made class power legible as caste power and caste power enforceable as class power. The peasant associations of Malabar, in which communist organisers were central from the 1930s, attacked the order at its instruments: they campaigned against renewal fees, resisted evictions, demanded written receipts, and built the tenantry's confidence that the layers above them were neither natural nor eternal. The associations' work explains why the reform, when legislation finally armed it, could be implemented rather than merely proclaimed: claimants existed in their tens of thousands who knew what they were owed and were organised to file, testify, and persist through the land tribunals. It also explains the reform's limits from the other side. Plantation estates, held under different tenures and employing wage labour rather than tenants, were largely exempted, so the plantation workforce, among the state's most exploited, stood outside the reform's central transaction. Ceiling provisions met the ingenuity of families who partitioned, transferred, and converted land in anticipation, and the surplus actually resumed fell well short of the surplus the statute books imagined. The allocation records the reform as the abolition of a tenancy system, which it was, and not as a general equalisation of landholding, which it was not, and the distinction preserves the grading from both inflation and dismissal.

## How the world found Kerala

The case entered international development writing through a specific door, and the door is worth describing because it shaped the single-cause stories the allocation exists to correct. In the mid-1970s, a study prepared under United Nations auspices, examining poverty, unemployment, and development policy with Kerala as its Indian material, presented the state as evidence that social progress could outrun income growth under public action. Through the following decade the case travelled: development economists cited it against the doctrine that societies must grow rich before they can afford health and education; Amartya Sen and Jean Drèze placed it at the centre of their work on hunger and public action, treating Kerala as the demonstration that democratic politics, a free press, and organised public demand could convert modest resources into survival and literacy. The phrase Kerala model crystallised in this traffic. Each retelling, however, compressed the case toward the teller's thesis. Economists of public action emphasised the state and its services; admirers of the Indian left emphasised the communist governments; critics of planning emphasised the stagnation waiting behind the indicators. The four causes travelled badly because a model, to travel, must be simple, and the sequence that explains Kerala is not simple. It has princes in it, and missionaries, and caste associations pursuing communal advantage, and a communist movement that inherited more than it made, and oil money from Arabia. None of that fits on a slide.

The scholarly literature, as distinct from the travelling version, never lost the sequence. Historians of Travancore kept the nineteenth century in view; the Centre for Development Studies kept the census and survey record accumulating; the migration surveys, from their first rounds, insisted on the external economy's weight. What the allocation adds to that scholarship is not new evidence but a discipline of presentation: the causes set side by side, each bounded by what it cannot explain, so that a reader meeting the case for the first time meets it whole. There is a series interest in the discipline. The articles around this one treat movements and governments that claimed outcomes in many countries, and the habit Kerala teaches, of asking what was inherited and what was added and what arrived from outside, is the habit the whole cluster on Marxism in India requires. The state's admirers within the Indian left have sometimes resisted the habit, hearing in it a diminution of their movement. The record suggests the opposite: a movement whose real achievements survive the subtraction of its inheritances is more formidable, not less, and the subtraction is what this article has performed.

## The margins of the model

An allocation that graded only the model's centre would repeat the celebratory literature's favourite omission. The state's tribal populations, concentrated in the hill districts of Wayanad and in pockets such as Attappady in Palakkad, stand in the record as the model's internal margin. Land alienation stripped tribal households of ancestral holdings through debt, fraud, and settlement by outsiders, and the restoration legislation intended to reverse the alienation was implemented weakly and late, so that the community the land reform era should have reached first was reached least. Indicators among tribal populations, in health, nutrition, and schooling, are described in the research and official literature as lagging the state pattern severely, a lag documented with particular force in reporting on child nutrition in Attappady. Fishing communities along the coast form a second margin: artisanal fishworkers, facing mechanised competition, volatile seas, and indebtedness to merchants, organised their own unions and movements, often in dialogue with and sometimes at odds with the mainstream left, and their indicators and security trailed the state's reputation for generations. Plantation workers, many of them descendants of Tamil migrants recruited under colonial labour systems, formed a third margin, housed by estates, paid at rates set in a separate industrial world, and largely outside the tenancy settlement.

The margins matter to the allocation for a reason beyond completeness. They test each cause and find its boundary. Princely provision reached the hills thinly; reform movements organised the plains' castes more thoroughly than the hills' tribes; communist governments, for all their distributive machinery, did not solve tribal land alienation within their tenures; remittances flowed least to the households with the least to send a migrant out. The pattern teaches the same lesson as the crisis literature from a different direction: the model's mechanisms were powerful within the society that generated them and weakened at that society's edges, among communities whose incorporation into the claiming public was never completed. A grading that says so has not debunked the model. It has located it, in social space as well as in time, and location is what an allocation is for.

## A state government inside a federation

Kerala's governments never commanded the instruments that a national state commands, and the allocation's third row must be read with the federation's constraints in view. A state government in India cannot print money, sets only part of the tax structure, depends on transfers and shared revenues from the union, and operates its signature policies inside national law, as the dismissal of the 1959 ministry demonstrated at the outset. Industrial policy, banking, major infrastructure, and the terms of trade between agriculture and industry lie largely beyond its reach. Within those walls, the room that existed was the room the Kerala settlement occupied: land relations, which the constitution left substantially to the states; education and health, shared subjects in which state initiative could run ahead; food distribution, operated jointly with the union's procurement system; labour welfare, where state legislation built the welfare funds; and, eventually, the design of local government. The model's shape, strong exactly where state power is real and absent exactly where it is not, is partly the biography of Indian federalism. Critics who ask why Kerala's governments did not industrialise the state as a national government might have attempted are asking a question the constitution had already half answered, though only half: other Indian states built more industry within the same walls, and the crisis literature's strand on investment climate addresses the half that remains.

The federal setting also structured the politics of credit that this article has resisted throughout. Every expansion of provision could be claimed by the front in office in Thiruvananthapuram and disclaimed as the union's doing or the predecessor's legacy, and every failure could be exported upward to Delhi's transfers and policies. Voters adjudicated these claims at intervals of a few years for two generations, which is one reason the state's political argument is so relentlessly historical: each front campaigns on a rival allocation of credit for the same settlement. The four-cause allocation offered here is, in that sense, an intervention in an argument the state's citizens have conducted since before the model had a name. Its claim on the reader is not novelty but discipline. It asks the fronts' inherited argument to submit to sequence, mechanism, and the final column of the table, and it is content to be judged by whether the gradings survive the evidence better than the claims they replace.

## The reading rooms: print as infrastructure

Among the second cause's instruments, print deserves a section of its own, because it converted reform from event into environment. The region's newspapers date to the nineteenth century, and by the early twentieth its towns and larger villages supported a culture of reading rooms and libraries that visitors remarked on and the library movement, organised through a state-wide association from the 1940s, extended into the countryside with the explicit aim of putting a reading room within reach of every village. Newspapers were read aloud in tea shops and reading rooms to listeners who could not yet read; pamphlets and cheap editions carried reform argument, nationalist politics, and then socialist literature into the same spaces; the popular science movement later used the network for its campaigns. The political consequences were cumulative. A population that follows public argument in print can be addressed by a movement as a public, with reasons and records, rather than summoned as a crowd. Elections in the state became contests of documented claim and counterclaim to a degree unusual in the India of the period, and governments faced a readership that compared promises against files. Historians of the press in Kerala connect the reading culture back to the mission and princely investment in schooling and forward to the literacy campaign, whose volunteers worked through institutions the library movement had built. The allocation counts print within the second cause, while noting its peculiar status as infrastructure for all the others: the tenantry's associations published, the unions published, the parties published, and the migration surveys themselves were received by a public trained to argue with findings. When the crisis literature emerged in the 1990s, it emerged into that public, which is why the state's own economists could wound the model in print and be answered in print, in a debate the celebratory versions abroad rarely noticed. The model argued with itself, in public, in its own language, for decades. An allocation is simply the most formal version of that argument.

## Welfare funds and the settlement's second generation

The 1970s and 1980s added a layer to the third cause that distinguishes the settlement from a simple expansion of services: statutory welfare funds for whole categories of workers whom neither factories legislation nor tenancy reform had reached. Headload workers, toddy tappers, cashew and coir workers, artisans, fishermen, and others were brought under boards financed by levies on employers, traders, or the trade itself, paying pensions, medical assistance, and benefits in sickness and death. The funds were a characteristically Kerala invention in their breadth, an attempt to socialise risk for an informal workforce through occupational corporatism rather than through a universal tax-financed scheme. Their record, as the labour literature describes it, is mixed in instructive proportions: coverage widened the settlement's floor to workers the classic welfare state misses, while the funds' finances strained, benefits stayed modest, and administration absorbed the politicisation the crisis critics alleged against the whole apparatus. The allocation enters them in the third row as evidence of the repertoire's reach and of its limits in the same exhibit. A movement that could organise headload workers into a statutory fund had achieved an integration of labour into public provision few Indian states attempted. A settlement that financed the integration through levies on declining trades had built its generosity on narrowing ground, and the arithmetic of that narrowing is the crisis literature's home territory.

It is in these decades, too, that the alternation between fronts hardened into the state's permanent constitution of government, with each coalition returning to office pledged to extend the settlement and discovering in office the exchequer's limits. The significant point for the allocation is that the settlement survived alternation intact and grew under both fronts, which complicates attribution in a way the four rows are designed to absorb. Policies initiated by one front, the welfare funds among them, were maintained and multiplied by the other; the distribution system and the schools were nobody's property and everybody's inheritance by the 1980s. What remained distinctively the communist movement's contribution across the whole period was less any single statute than the standing organisation of the settlement's beneficiaries, the unions, peasant associations, and party committees that monitored delivery village by village and converted entitlement into receipt. Students of the case sometimes call this the difference between rights on paper and rights in the hand. The allocation's third row, read carefully, is a claim about that difference, and it is the part of the row least visible to accounts that grade governments by legislation alone.

## The demographic transition: the model's quiet engine

Behind the famous indicators runs a demographic transformation that the literature treats as the model's engine room. Mortality fell first, as public health measures, malaria control, nutrition through distribution, and the spread of clinical care cut deaths in infancy and childhood and from infectious disease. Fertility fell after, and fell fast by historical standards, as educated women married later, as child survival made large families unnecessary for security, as the costs of educating children rose with families' ambitions for them, and as contraception became available through a health system women already used. The result was a population that aged early by Indian standards, with a rising share of elderly dependents, a shrinking share of children, and a working-age bulge that arrived, inconveniently, in the decades when the domestic economy was least able to employ it and the Gulf was most able. Demographers describe Kerala as having completed in a poor economy the transition that rich economies completed with industrialisation, and the description carries the allocation's moral in demographic form: the transition's drivers were the first three causes, schooling, health provision, mobilisation, distribution, while its economic consequences were managed by the fourth. A society that teaches its daughters and keeps its infants alive will transform its demography whether or not its factories multiply. Kerala is the proof, and the proof cuts both ways, since the transformation's dividend depends on employment the model did not generate at home and its costs, in elder care and pensions, fall on the settlement the crisis literature audits.

The elderly deserve their sentence in the allocation, because they are its most literal creditors. A state with an old population and a young one's tax base faces pension commitments, chronic disease burdens, and care needs that its founders did not price. The welfare funds and pension schemes of the settlement's second generation were partial answers, and the remittance household became, in practice, a private pension system, with migrant children funding parents' consumption and care across distance. When the crisis literature speaks of unsustainability it is often speaking, concretely, of this arithmetic. The allocation records demography not as a fifth cause but as the medium through which the four causes acted on one another across time: provision and mobilisation changed the population; the changed population strained provision; migration relieved the strain while deepening the age imbalance by removing the young. Few exhibits in the case better justify the article's method. Single-cause stories cannot even describe a feedback loop, let alone grade it.

## Churches, associations, and the powers between citizen and state

The Kerala settlement was negotiated among organisations as much as enacted by governments, and a section belongs to those intermediary powers, because they explain both the settlement's reach and its characteristic blockages. The churches, Catholic, Orthodox, Jacobite, Mar Thoma, and others, ran schools and hospitals on a scale that made them co-providers of the model and political principals in their own right. The agitation that destroyed the first ministry was substantially organised through church and communal networks, and no subsequent front governed education without treating with them. The caste associations that began as reform movements matured into federations with colleges, hospitals, banks, and parliamentary weight, courted by both fronts and capable of disciplining either. Muslim organisations in Malabar built their own educational empires from the reform era onward, transforming their community's position through schooling while bargaining within the coalition system. The picture that results is not the textbook sequence of state acting on society. It is a dense corporate landscape in which the state financed, regulated, and bargained, communities built and defended, and parties aggregated and alternated. The Marxist tradition's own theory has a vocabulary for this landscape in its accounts of civil society and hegemony, and Kerala is among the places where that vocabulary earns its keep: the communist movement's achievement was to win leadership within a civil society it did not create and could not command, and its defeats, beginning in 1959, came when that civil society's organised powers combined against it.

For the allocation, the intermediary powers complicate the rows usefully. Much of what the table credits to princely provision was delivered by mission and community institutions the princes subsidised. Much of what it credits to reform movements was institutionalised as communal property whose later defence could obstruct the third cause's governments. Much of what it credits to those governments was bargained into existence against the associations' resistance and then administered through the associations' schools. Credit in such a landscape is genuinely joint, and the allocation's four rows should be read as claims about initiating mechanisms rather than about sole authorship. The reader who finishes the article with a picture of four hands on the same institutions, at different times and with different instruments, has understood the case better than the reader who leaves with four separate monuments.

## Traditional industries and the long decline

The crisis literature's industrial strand rests on a substratum of fact that deserves its own exposition, since Kerala's traditional industries were once substantial employers and their troubles long predate the debates of the 1990s. Coir, the fibre of the coconut husk, supported a dense belt of household and shed production along the backwaters, employing women in retting and spinning and men in weaving and trade, and it met mechanised competition, synthetic substitutes, and the exhaustion of its raw material geography across the postwar decades. Cashew processing, concentrated in the Kollam region and staffed overwhelmingly by women, depended on imported raw nuts and on labour costs whose rise, the industry's chronic complaint, pushed processing toward lower-wage regions even as Kerala's workers' organisation set the industry's floor. Handloom, tile making, beedi rolling, and the fisheries each carried their own version of the pattern: labour-intensive production, organised workers, thin margins, and markets that technology or resources were shrinking. The state's industrial history is thus not a blank where factories failed to arrive. It is a landscape of old industries declining while new ones, electronics, petrochemicals, large manufacturing, mostly located elsewhere in India for reasons of scale, power, land assembly, and national licensing that historians of Indian industry distribute across several causes. The communist-led governments' responses, workers' cooperatives in dying trades, welfare funds for their members, attempts at revival boards, were palliative in character, and their defenders and critics divide over whether more was possible. The allocation takes no side in that counterfactual. It notes only that the third cause's repertoire was formed in the politics of organising these very workers, and that a movement whose base was the traditional working class faced the decline of that class's industries as an existential problem, not an abstraction. The service economy and the remittance economy that succeeded the old industries employed the workers' children differently, as nurses, shop assistants, drivers, masons, and migrants, and the settlement's politics followed them into the new occupations with the welfare funds and unions of new categories. Decline and adaptation, not stagnation pure and simple, is the record the allocation grades.

## Nurses, professionals, and the second migration

The first Gulf wave was weighted toward manual and craft labour, and the survey literature charts a second movement layered over it: the migration of the trained. Nurses are its emblem. Kerala's nursing schools, many run by Christian institutions, and its public health system together produced nurses in numbers the state's hospitals could not absorb at home, and those nurses staffed wards across India, then the Gulf, then the health systems of Europe and the English-speaking world. Teachers, technicians, engineers, and later information technology professionals followed cognate paths, some through the Gulf, many directly to metropolitan India and abroad. The second migration altered the fourth cause's character. Remittances from professionals arrived in salaried regularity; the households receiving them invested disproportionately in children's schooling, often in the unaided English-medium sector, and in housing and health care of a standard the public system was pressed to match; and the state's educational ambitions ratcheted upward, as families measured success by the professional exit. Critics within the state describe the resulting equilibrium with some bitterness: a public system that trains for export, a private system that trains for the public system's examinations and the foreign market, and a domestic economy whose most reliable product is the employable graduate. Defenders answer that the graduates are not a product but persons, that their migration is an exercise of the very capabilities the model exists to build, and that the remittances and return skills are the model's dividend collected abroad. The allocation records the second migration inside the fourth row while flagging its difference from the first: it is the row where the model's success and its critique become the same fact viewed from two sides, since every nurse in a Gulf or European hospital is simultaneously the school system's triumph and the health system's loss. Grading the fourth cause means refusing to choose between those descriptions, because the evidence sustains both and a choice would be an editorial, not an allocation.

## Can the Kerala model be replicated?

The replication question is the form in which most readers meet the case, and the allocation supplies a disciplined answer: the model as a whole cannot be copied, because two of its four causes are inheritances, but its transferable elements can be named exactly, and its non-transferable conditions can be named with equal exactness, and confusing the two lists is the standard error of the policy literature. Begin with what does not transfer. A nineteenth-century princely administration that funded mass schooling early cannot be legislated retrospectively. A century of reform movements that organised subordinated communities into claiming publics cannot be summoned by a programme document. Matrilineal customs, mission geographies, a coastline open to the Gulf's labour market at the moment oil revenues crested: these are the model's geology, and a state without them starts from different ground. The mechanisms that produced Kerala's demand side, its disciplined parties of alternation, and its migration outlet are historical products, and the first service an honest account performs for other states is to say so, against the conference literature that presents the model as a kit.

Then name what does transfer, because the third cause is substantially a repertoire other governments can adopt in their own conditions. Land and tenancy reform, where a tenancy order exists and an organised tenantry can be built or found, transfers as a principle: redistribution of rights over land, implemented through tribunals the claimants can actually use, changes rural power durably. Near-universal food distribution in a deficit region transfers as an administrative commitment with known logistics. The campaign method for residual illiteracy transfers wherever movements or associations exist that a government can partner with rather than replace. Decentralisation with real funds, on the People's Plan pattern, transfers as a constitutional and fiscal decision, and its Kerala preconditions, a literate citizenry and staffable local bodies, can be approximated by design even where they cannot be inherited complete. Above all, the alternation discipline transfers as a political fact: the settlement endured because voters could punish its managers without ending it, and any state whose distributive institutions survive changes of government owns the model's most portable asset. The verdict the allocation defends is therefore double. As a template, the Kerala model is a mirage, and those who sell it as one repeat the single-cause error in policy dress. As a quarry of tested mechanisms, each with its conditions stated, it is among the richest cases the development literature possesses, and the four rows of the table are its inventory.

## Reading the two literatures

A reader entering the Kerala literature meets two bodies of writing that seem to describe different states, and a short guide to their habits will prevent misreading of the sections above. The celebratory literature, international and domestic, writes in the grammar of achievement: it selects the indicators, narrates the policies that plausibly produced them, and treats the state as proof of a general proposition about public action or about the left in power. Its characteristic omission is sequence; its characteristic risk is the borrowed virtue, quality, prosperity, sustainability, claimed on the indicators' credit. The crisis literature writes in the grammar of the bill: it selects the fiscal, industrial, and employment record, narrates the settlement as a set of commitments an economy failed to underwrite, and treats the state as a warning about distribution without production. Its characteristic omission is comparison; Indian states generally were fiscally stressed, industrially uneven, and employment-short in the same decades, and a bill that every state received cannot by itself convict one state's model. Each literature is a legitimate audit of one side of the ledger, and each becomes false when it presents its side as the whole account. The scholarly centre of the field, the development studies tradition centred on Thiruvananthapuram and its interlocutors abroad, has generally refused both presentations, which is why this article's attributions run so often to that centre's findings even when its conclusions are the article's own. The allocation is offered as the form in which the two audits can be filed together: celebration assigned to the rows and columns where the evidence supports it, crisis assigned its full weight in the rows it qualifies, and neither permitted to annex the other's territory. Readers who keep the table beside them while reading further in the literature will find most new claims arriving with an address already assigned, and the occasional claim that fits no row is usually the one worth stopping for.

## Housing: the transformation everyone can see

If the four causes had a monument a visitor could photograph, it would be the state's housing stock. Within living memory the typical poor household lived in a thatched or tiled hut on a small plot, frequently the kudikidappu plot whose ownership the land reform secured. Across the remittance decades that stock was rebuilt in concrete and tile on a scale that changed the look of every village and town: multi-room houses with modern roofing, water and sanitation connections, and compound walls, financed by migrant savings, by housing loans that banks extended against expected remittances, and by public housing schemes that both fronts operated for households the private flow missed. The survey and census literatures treat housing as one of the clearest material indices of the transformation, and the allocation assigns it across rows with unusual neatness. The plot beneath the house is the third cause's gift, since homestead ownership preceded the building boom and made it possible for the poorest families to build at all. The money that raised the walls is the fourth cause's. The expectation that a labouring family's house should be solid, serviced, and a fit place to raise schooled children is the second cause's norm become architecture. Even the first cause appears, in the towns' older stock of public buildings and in the administrative habit of treating housing as a public concern. Housing also carries the transformation's ironies in concentrated form. Houses grew as families shrank, so that the state's lanes filled with large dwellings occupied by elderly couples whose children worked abroad; construction became a leading employer of local labour and then of migrant labour from other states; and land prices, driven by remittance demand, rose until the young household without a migrant faced a market its grandparents' reform had never imagined. The allocation records housing as the model's most visible success and as a standing illustration of its method, since no single row of the table can explain a single one of those houses.

## Kudumbashree and the settlement's third generation

In 1998 the communist-led government launched Kudumbashree, a poverty eradication mission built on neighbourhood groups of women, and the programme grew into one of the largest women's collectives of its kind anywhere, organising millions of members into groups that pooled savings, ran micro-enterprises, farmed collectively on leased land, staffed canteens and care services, and supplied candidates and workers to the local governments the People's Plan had empowered. The programme matters to this article less for any single outcome than for what it reveals about the settlement's evolution. Its form is the third cause's repertoire in a new key: mobilisation through trusted local organisation, public funds routed through collective structures rather than individual patronage, and the conversion of women, the demand side's historic carriers, from beneficiaries into the machinery itself. Its timing is equally instructive. Kudumbashree arrived when the crisis literature's diagnosis was already conventional wisdom, and it reads, in part, as the settlement's answer to that diagnosis from within its own tradition: if the formal economy would not employ the educated and the poor, collective enterprise and local services, organised at scale, might employ some of them and dignify the rest of the work. Evaluations of the programme in the research literature credit it with real gains in women's public presence, local political recruitment, and household resilience, while recording the familiar qualifications about enterprise profitability and the weight of unpaid labour inside the collective form. The allocation enters Kudumbashree in the third row as the repertoire's late adaptation, and in the article's larger argument as evidence that the settlement was still generating institutional inventions two generations after the first ministry, a fact neither the celebratory literature's frozen model nor the crisis literature's obituary predicts.

## The environment the model inherited and spent

A series that has just crossed ecological Marxism will ask, properly, what the Kerala settlement did to the natural world it governed, and the honest answer complicates every row of the table. The state's famed landscape, its backwaters, wetlands, paddy fields, and forested highlands, sustained pressures that the model's success intensified. Paddy wetlands, which recharge groundwater, buffer floods, and harbour the fish and bird life of the lowlands, were filled for housing and commercial plots as land values rose on remittance demand, until legislation attempted, with uneven enforcement, to halt the conversion. River sand was mined for the construction boom at rates that lowered beds and eroded banks. The highland forests faced encroachment and plantation pressure; quarrying scarred the midland hills; the backwaters absorbed the waste of towns whose sanitation investment lagged their housing. None of this is peculiar to Kerala, and the state also produced environmental movements of consequence, most famously the campaign that saved the Silent Valley rainforest from a hydroelectric project in the 1970s and 1980s, a campaign in which the popular science movement and poets and scientists of the state combined to move a national decision. The allocation's bearing on the environment is indirect but real. The second cause's organisational density made campaigns like Silent Valley possible; the fourth cause's money made the construction pressures relentless; the third cause's governments stood on both sides of the quarrel across the decades, damming and defending by turns. A development model is also an ecological fact, and the Kerala case, which teaches so much about distribution, teaches by omission that its settlement never developed an ecological arm comparable to its distributive one. Readers arriving from the ecological articles of this series will recognise the general problem in a regional costume: growth of provision and consumption, however equalised, presses on the material world, and the pressing is not abolished by being fairly shared.

## Public finance: the arithmetic underneath

The crisis literature's fiscal strand deserves a slower exposition than summary allows, because it is the point where the model's admirers and critics most often talk past one another. A state government committed to an extensive public payroll, to pensions for its employees, to subsidies on food and transport, and to free or cheap services faces a structural gap wherever its tax base is narrow, and Kerala's base was narrowed by the very pattern this article has described: small industry, agriculture in transition, a large share of consumption financed by income earned outside the state's tax reach. Union transfers filled part of the gap under formulas that rewarded population control, which Kerala had achieved, with smaller shares, a federal irony the state's economists note with feeling: success at the demographic transition reduced the transfers calculated per head. Borrowing filled the rest, and debt service grew into one of the budget's largest items, crowding the capital works and maintenance on which the model's physical stock depends. Successive finance commissions and state white papers rehearsed the arithmetic; successive governments of both fronts deferred its conclusions, because the commitments under pressure, salaries, pensions, subsidies, are the settlement itself in budgetary form, and no front could campaign on dismantling what the voters regarded as their property. The allocation draws one inference from the arithmetic and refuses another. It infers that the third cause's repertoire has a fiscal carrying capacity that the state's economy, unaided, did not reach in the model's classic decades, which is a finding about the repertoire's preconditions. It refuses the inference that the settlement was therefore a mistake, since the counterfactual, a Kerala that had starved its schools and clinics to balance its books, would have purchased solvency at the price of the outcomes the whole world came to study, and the migration income that eventually relieved the arithmetic was itself downstream of the spending. Fiscal stress is a genuine strand of the record. It is not a verdict that dissolves the other strands, and the allocation's table, which gives the crisis no row of its own because it explains no indicator, is the article's way of keeping that proportion.

## The politics of protest: a citizenry that answers back

No account of the demand side is complete without the state's culture of protest, the hartals and strikes and processions through which the organised public audits its governments between elections. The culture descends from every cause in the table: the reform movements normalised public assembly for communities once forbidden the roads; the unions and peasant associations industrialised it; the parties of both fronts deploy it in opposition and deplore it in office; the reading public follows its arguments in print. Its effects on the model run in both directions, and the allocation declines to net them out. Protest defended the settlement repeatedly, defeating retrenchments, forcing the restoration of cut services, and keeping distributive questions at the head of the agenda when governments preferred quieter subjects. Protest also raised the cost of administration and investment in ways the crisis literature itemises, and the same procession that defends a hospital's budget can close the road the ambulance needs. What can be said without partisanship is that the culture is the demand side in its active tense. A population that reads, organises, and answers back is the population the four causes jointly produced, and any government of the state, of whatever front, governs in its presence. Foreign admirers of the model who imagine its politics as serene implementation have mistaken the photograph for the film. The settlement was argued into existence and is argued over still, and the argument is not a defect in the model. On the evidence of this article, the argument is a large part of the model.

## Higher education and the self-financing turn

The decades of the crisis literature also transformed higher education, and the transformation tests the allocation in a newer arena. The public and aided colleges that had carried the first generations of mass higher education faced rising demand from the school system's graduates, fiscal limits on expansion, and a queue for professional seats, in medicine, engineering, and later information technology, that the state sector could not satisfy. From the 1990s, governments of both fronts admitted self-financing institutions, private colleges charging fees that covered costs and more, often run by the same communal and church trusts that had built the aided sector a century earlier. The turn was fought bitterly in the state's politics, with the left's own ranks divided between those who saw capitulation to the market in education and those who saw the only available expansion, and the settlement that emerged reprised the older aided compromise in a harsher key: private provision grew, the state regulated admissions and fees with varying success, and access for poor students came to depend on quotas and scholarships contested in every admission season. The allocation reads the episode as the fourth cause acting on the first cause's institutional landscape. Remittance and salaried households supplied the fees; the communal trusts supplied the colleges; the state supplied the regulation and the argument. It also reads the episode as the crisis literature's most concrete parable: a society that had universalised schooling discovered that the ladder's upper rungs could not be universalised on the same fiscal terms, and improvised a mixed economy of aspiration whose inequalities its own values indict. That the improvisation occurred under alternating fronts, including communist-led ones, is the final evidence this article offers for its central discipline. The settlement's custodians were pragmatists inside constraints, and the causes that explain their room to move are the four in the table, not the programmes in their manifestos.

## Support-led security: Kerala among its peers

The development literature long ago placed Kerala in a small company of societies that achieved high social indicators ahead of wealth, and the company portrait sharpens the allocation by showing which causes travel under other names. Sri Lanka built its indicators on food subsidies, public health, and education under governments of varying colour, with a plantation economy and a welfare commitment dating to the colonial period's end. Costa Rica demilitarised its budget into schools and clinics. The Chinese provinces before the market reforms carried literacy and basic health through collective institutions of a wholly different political kind. Sen's comparative work grouped such cases under the idea of support-led security: survival and literacy advanced by direct public support to health, education, and nutrition, in advance of and partly independent of income growth, as against the growth-mediated route in which rising wealth is expected to purchase the indicators eventually. Kerala fits the support-led family and displays its family traits: the dependence of the achievement on sustained political commitment, the vulnerability of the financing when the economy underneath stagnates, and the tendency of the achievement to generate its own constituencies, who defend the support systems as rights. The comparison also isolates Kerala's peculiarities within the family. No peer combined support-led policy with a communist movement of Kerala's electoral depth; none financed its maturity with a labour export of Kerala's relative scale; none conducted its argument in a newspaper culture of Kerala's density. The family's existence proves the third cause's repertoire is not a local magic. Kerala's position within the family proves the repertoire is not the whole story either, since peers reached similar indicators with different politics and Kerala reached its particular mixture, tenancy abolished, planning decentralised, consumption remittance-fed, only through its own sequence. Comparison, handled this way, is the allocation's friend twice over: it validates the mechanisms and it confiscates the miracles.

## Literature, theatre, and the radical imagination

The claiming public had a culture, and the culture had carriers this article has so far named only in passing. Malayalam literature underwent its own progressive turn in the middle decades of the twentieth century, when writers associated with the progressive literature movement put tenants, labourers, fisherfolk, and women of the subordinated castes at the centre of serious fiction, and novels and stories of that school were read in the reading rooms the library movement built until their characters became the public's neighbours. Popular theatre carried the argument further and faster. The Kerala People's Arts Club and troupes like it staged plays whose songs entered the language, dramatising tenancy, usury, and priestly and landlord power for audiences who encountered the critique as entertainment in the village square. Cinema followed, with a Malayalam film culture of unusual literary ambition whose social films assumed a politically literate audience and repaid the assumption. None of this is decoration on the allocation. A tenantry that has watched its own eviction dramatised, sung, and applauded is a tenantry prepared for the association meeting and the tribunal queue, and a schoolchild who has read the reformers' stories has absorbed the demand side as imagination before she meets it as policy. The cultural front was the communist movement's own chosen instrument in its formative decades, staffed by writers and actors the movement honoured, and it drew on the reform era's print infrastructure and the princely era's schools for its audience. Place culture in the second row, then, with a note that the third cause wielded it deliberately and well, and that the case for the movements' importance would be weaker if the imagination had never been organised. States are not redistributed by statutes alone. They are redistributed by populations who have first been persuaded, in story and song, that the existing distribution is a fact to be changed rather than a fate to be endured.

## The returnees: migration comes home

Every migration stream writes a second story in its returns, and the survey literature and the state's common observation record it faithfully. Return migrants arrive with savings, with skills and work disciplines acquired abroad, with health sometimes spent by the labour that earned the savings, and with expectations calibrated to Gulf wages and Gulf order that the home economy meets unevenly. Some returns are triumphs: the house built, the children professional, the small business opened, the family's status transformed within a generation. Some are quiet defeats: savings consumed by illness, by children's weddings and fees, by businesses that failed in a market the returnee misjudged, leaving the household to send out the next generation in its turn. At the scale of the state, the return flow built the construction and services economy's skilled layer, seeded small enterprise across the towns, and created a constituency with a distinctive political temper, worldly about money, impatient with the home economy's frictions, and courted by every front. For the allocation, the returnees close the fourth cause's account with the same doubleness that marks the whole table. Remittances are usually narrated as a flow of funds; in lived form they are a flow of people, whose departures and returns reorganised the society that the first three causes had made. The nurse who returns to staff the private hospital, the mason who returns to build the neighbour's house, the failed returnee whose son departs in his place: the fourth cause, examined closely, is the Kerala model reproducing itself through the world labour market, with gains and losses entered in the same ledger. An allocation that kept it as a wire transfer would have missed the cause entirely.

## Local government after the campaign

The People's Plan Campaign's surge settled, in time, into the routine of local government, and the routine is the campaign's real test in the allocation. Panchayats and municipalities across the state continued to prepare plans, run gram sabhas of varying vitality, and administer funds and functions, school and hospital oversight among them, that had once belonged to the state departments alone. The research literature on the post-campaign decades records a mixed institutionalisation: local bodies gained genuine fiscal and administrative substance compared with their counterparts elsewhere in India; participation narrowed from the campaign's festival breadth toward the committed and the interested; party competition colonised the local tier as thoroughly as the state tier, bringing both accountability and the spoils politics the crisis critics describe; and women's representation, constitutionally reserved and socially enlarged by the neighbourhood group movement, changed the personnel of local power in ways scholars are still assessing. The allocation's interest is in what the trajectory proves about the third cause's deepest claim. Decentralisation in Kerala did not wither when its founding campaign ended, because the conditions that made it possible, literacy, organisation, competitive politics, a public that reads budgets, persisted after the volunteers went home. Institutions survive their inaugurations where a society can staff them. That sentence, generalised, is close to the article's whole thesis about the case, and it is why the allocation assigns so much weight to the unglamorous second row: movements and publics are the load-bearing walls of every later construction, including the ones built by governments that would prefer to be remembered as the architects.

## The misreadings, corrected in one place

The brief for this article lists the errors that competing coverage repeats, and the allocation now permits each to be corrected in a sentence or two, with its correction's address in the table. The first misreading credits the outcomes to communist government alone; the sequence refutes it, since princely provision and reform mobilisation precede the first ministry by generations, and the correction lives in the table's first two rows. The second misreading, the mirror image, holds that the outcomes owe nothing to communist government; the land reform, the distribution system's coverage, the literacy campaign, and the decentralisation programme refute it, since no other Indian state of the period matched that combination, and the correction lives in the third row. The third misreading describes Kerala as economically prosperous; the model's defining feature is the opposite conjunction, high social indicators at historically low income, and a reader who misses the conjunction has missed the phenomenon the literature exists to explain. The fourth misreading assigns the social indicators to remittances; the indicators were distinctive before migration reached scale, and remittances explain the consumption surface of the later decades rather than the school and clinic system built earlier, a correction that lives in the fourth row's final column. The fifth misreading treats the model as a template awaiting export; the replication section above gives the disciplined version, mechanisms transferable and conditions not, and no correction in this article matters more for readers who arrive with policy intentions. A sixth misreading, common in the tradition's own debates, treats the Kerala experience as a verdict on socialism in general, for or against. A single state inside a federation, governed in alternation, inheriting a princely and reformist past, and financed in maturity by exported labour, is evidence about a specific repertoire under specific conditions. The series places the case among many, and its weight in any general verdict is exactly the weight its conditions can bear, no more and, the allocation insists against the dismissive reading, no less.

## The allocation's verdict

The four-cause allocation can now be stated as the article's namable claim, in the form a reader can carry away and use. Kerala's outcomes are the product of princely-state provision, pre-communist social mobilisation, communist-era redistribution and decentralisation, and migrant remittances, and no single-cause account survives contact with the chronology, since the headline outcomes were already forming before communist government existed and the base under later consumption lies outside the state. Each clause of that sentence has been earned in a section above. The princely clause rests on the documented head start in schools, hospitals, and the administrative idea that provision was the state's business. The mobilisation clause rests on the reform movements' construction of a claiming public, feminised early and organised through associations, print, and cultural fronts. The redistribution clause rests on the abolition of tenancy, the coverage of distribution and services, the campaign that completed literacy, and the devolution that trusted assemblies with funds, all of it administered under alternation and audited by a hostile opposition and a reading electorate. The remittance clause rests on the survey literature's decades of documentation and on the visible transformation of housing and consumption. The final clause, the refusal of single causes, rests on the method the article has practised throughout: ask of every claimed cause what it cannot explain, and stop believing it at exactly that boundary. What remains for the wider argument about Marxism in India is a measured pride and a measured caution, available from the same table. The pride: a communist movement, given an organised society, redistributed land and power more thoroughly than any comparable Indian movement of its century, and completed a social settlement its rivals chose to maintain rather than reverse. The caution: the movement did not create its society, did not solve the productive economy, and was rescued in its settlement's maturity by an external income it neither made nor controlled. Both are the tradition's history in this state. The allocation's four rows are offered as the form in which that history can be held without lying in either direction, and the exam and research paths that follow are built to let each kind of reader test the holding for themselves.

## The election the world watched

The 1957 victory acquired, almost immediately, a second life as an international symbol, and the symbol shaped the case's later historiography enough to deserve a section. A communist party had contested a free election in a populous polity, won it, formed a ministry, and governed under a liberal constitution while the Cold War's categories insisted such a thing was a contradiction. Observers arrived from several directions. Admirers in the international left read the result as proof that the parliamentary road could carry communist politics to office; sceptics read the ministry's programme as the test of whether office would moderate or expose it; the dismissal of 1959 was then read, by the first group as proof that the constitution's tolerance ended where redistribution began, and by the second as the system correcting an aberration. The state's own communists drew the lesson their subsequent conduct expresses: they returned to the electorate, won and lost in alternation for two generations, and built their claim on delivery within the rules rather than on any single tenure. The experiment of the article's title is therefore double. Kerala was an experiment performed by its governments, in redistribution under democracy, and an experiment performed on its governments, by a constitution, an opposition, and a citizenry that dismissed, restored, and audited them in turn. The allocation's restraint about the third cause is partly a product of that double experiment's evidence. A movement that had ruled without interruption for forty years could plausibly be credited or blamed for everything in its territory. A movement that held office roughly half the time, legislating against entrenched interests and under permanent audit, must share its ledger with predecessors, successors, and the society that kept returning its verdicts. The sharing is not a dilution of the movement's importance. It is the precise form its importance took in the only Indian state where it was tested at scale.

## Tribunals and records: how implementation actually happened

Land reform is usually debated at the level of statutes, and statutes are where most Indian states' reforms died. Kerala's difference was manufactured in unglamorous machinery that this section restores to the account. The reform's operative institution was the land tribunal, a local body before which tenants filed for the transfer of ownership, landlords contested, and records were examined claim by claim across the state's villages. Around the tribunals stood the settlement's characteristic supports: revenue officials under instruction and scrutiny, peasant association workers who knew which households held which plots under which arrangements and could walk a claimant through the process, party and union offices that functioned as advice centres, and a press that reported obstruction. The evidentiary basis of tenancy was itself a battlefield, since tenancies had often been oral and the burden of proof could be arranged to defeat the unrecorded cultivator; the movement's local knowledge was the counterweight that made the statute's presumptions usable by the people they were written for. Implementation on this description is not the state acting and society receiving. It is a joint product, statute times organisation, and the multiplication sign is the allocation's whole argument in miniature. Where organisation was thin, among some tribal communities and in the plantation belt's different tenure world, the same statutes yielded less, as the margins section recorded. Where it was dense, in the Malabar tenantry's heartlands, the reform approached the completeness the comparative literature celebrates. The lesson generalises grimly for reformers elsewhere: the distance between a law and its outcome is measured in organisers, and Kerala had spent fifty years, across its second and third causes, manufacturing organisers as its principal industry.

## The coalition decades and the Emergency's shadow

The state's politics after the land reform settled into a pattern whose very repetitiveness is historically informative. Fronts assembled from the communist parties, socialist fragments, communal parties, and regional formations on one side, and from the Congress with its own shifting allies on the other, contested elections that turned on prices, corruption charges, the management of the settlement, and the arithmetic of community representation, and each front's victory was followed by the discovery that the settlement's commitments constrained it exactly as they had constrained its predecessor. The national Emergency of the mid-1970s passed over the state in a distinctive register: Kerala was governed in those years by a coalition that included the communists of one party within the ruling arrangement at the centre's pleasure, while the other communist party opposed, a configuration that divided the movement against itself in ways the national article in this series examines, and that left the state's civil liberties record of the period a subject of its own argument. What matters for the allocation is the continuity beneath the turbulence. Through emergency, coalition collapse, and repeated mid-term realignments, the distribution shops opened, the schools taught, the tribunals' settlement held, and no government of any composition attempted to reverse the land reform or dismantle the service network. A settlement that survives its makers' defeats and its country's authoritarian episode has crossed the line from programme to institution, and the crossing is the strongest evidence available that by the 1980s the model belonged to the society rather than to any party. Attribution debates that continue to assign the model to a front are therefore arguing about a property the record shows in common ownership, which is why this article has graded causes rather than governments throughout.

## Caste after the movements: the unfinished agenda

The reform movements attacked caste and also, in maturing, institutionalised it, and the state's later history cannot be read without holding both truths where the allocation placed them, in the second row. The associations that had demanded education and dignity became, in their prosperous generations, custodians of communal shares: of college managements, of coalition ministries apportioned by community, of government employment claimed as communal entitlement. Caste as ritual hierarchy weakened dramatically across the century, in temple entry, in the decline of distance pollution and public humiliation, in the sheer fact of subordinated communities' organised power. Caste as political arithmetic persisted and in some respects strengthened, since the settlement's benefits were fought over by blocs that organised communally even when their programmes spoke the language of class. The communist movement's own theory promised that class organisation would dissolve caste loyalties into a larger solidarity, and its practice, organising tenants and workers across caste while bargaining with communal leaderships at election time, achieved the promise partially and tactically rather than finally. Scholars of the state divide over the balance sheet, some emphasising the hierarchy's real destruction in the countryside's relations of production, others the adaptation of caste to democratic competition as its second life. The question is taken up on its own terms in the article this series devotes to the caste and class debate, and the present article's obligation is narrower: to record that the second cause's victory was genuine and incomplete in the same institutions, and that the incompleteness is not an embarrassment to be hidden from the allocation but one of its findings. A movement that ends untouchability's public forms and leaves communal arithmetic governing college admissions has transformed its society and not finished transforming it, and Kerala's honesty about that sentence, in its scholarship and its quarrels, is part of what the case teaches.

## The plantation world the reform passed by

The plantation belt of the high ranges runs on a separate history, and its separateness is the allocation's standing reminder that reforms have jurisdictions. Tea, coffee, cardamom, and rubber estates were carved from forest under colonial grants, worked by labour recruited from Tamil districts and from the state's own subordinated communities, and housed in estate lines under managements whose authority, for generations, approached a private government. Tenancy law did not reach the estate worker, whose relation to the land was employment, not cultivation, and the ceiling provisions exempted plantations as going concerns, so the reform that transformed the lowland village passed the high ranges by. What organisation reached the estates came through unions, whose struggles over wages, housing, and the statutory benefits of plantation labour law form a long and only partly successful record, and through the welfare funds and public services that the settlement extended uphill more slowly than across the plains. The estates' later history, price collapses in tea and coffee, closures and abandonments that stranded line-room families without wages or services, belongs to the crisis decades and was met by the same repertoire of protest and palliative that the traditional industries received. In the allocation's accounting, the plantation belt performs the same service as the tribal margins, from the opposite end of the state's geography: it marks the boundary of the third cause's central instrument and demonstrates that the instrument, tenancy abolition, was fitted to one agrarian structure and silent before another. General theories of the Kerala model that speak of land reform as if it had remade the whole countryside are refuted by a single bus ride into the hills, where the estates' lines stand as the reform's unvisited territory, and the allocation's grading, which credits the reform with the tenancy order's abolition and nothing beyond, is written to survive that bus ride.

## The coast: fishworkers and their own movement

The fishing coast supplies the case's most instructive example of organisation arising outside the main currents and correcting them. Artisanal fishworkers, Catholic and Muslim and Hindu by community, worked a dangerous sea in craft whose economics were governed by the merchants who financed boats, ice, and fuel and who took the catch at prices the fisherman rarely set. Mechanised trawling, arriving with export demand for prawns, threatened the artisanal fleet's grounds and the inshore ecology together, and the response, built through the 1970s and 1980s, was an independent fishworkers' movement, animated by activist clergy and organisers of a new type, which federated beyond the state into a national forum and won seasonal trawling bans, welfare provisions, and a recognised place in the state's politics. The movement's relation to the communist-led unions was competitive as often as allied, since the established federations had organised the coast's workers within structures the new movement judged too close to the boat-owning and merchant interests. The allocation draws two lessons. First, the second cause's genus, organisation of the subordinated into claiming bodies, continued to generate new species throughout the model's history, and the model's margins were repeatedly reduced by movements its central actors had not planned. Second, the state's distributive politics should be pictured not as a government dispensing to a passive coast but as an arena in which organised constituencies, including ones in rebellion against the organisers, extracted their shares. Both lessons raise the standing of the demand side in the final grading, and both are reasons the allocation treats mobilisation, rather than any ministry, as the case's most continuous thread.

## Nutrition, and what the indicators leave out

The research literature's care with measurement, which this article's figures protocol follows, extends to a warning about the indicators themselves that belongs in the record. Survival and schooling are counted more easily than nourishment, development, or learning, and the state's record on the harder measures is more argued over than its record on the easier ones. Studies of child nutrition in Kerala have at times found levels of undernutrition that sit awkwardly beside the mortality record, a paradox the literature debates through the quality of diets, the disease environment, and the distribution of food within households. Anaemia among women appears in survey findings at levels the celebratory account would not predict. Mental health, including suicide rates that the literature has long described as elevated in the state, forms the darkest of the qualification literatures, raising questions about the psychic costs of the model's particular modernity: educated aspiration against narrow opportunity, migration's separations, alcohol's toll among men, and the pressures of a status-conscious society under permanent examination. This article asserts none of the contested figures and adjudicates none of the disputes. It records their existence because an allocation that graded the model on its famous indicators alone would be repeating the travelling version's simplification at the scholarly level. The four causes explain the pattern the world cites. The residual literatures, on nutrition, on mental health, on the quality behind the access, define the pattern's shadow, and a complete map of the case, which is what this article has attempted, must show the shadow's outline even where it cannot fill it in.

## Ageing and the care economy

The demographic transition's second act arrives as a question of care, and the settlement's responses to it mark the frontier where the allocation's causes are still being tested. A population with many elderly and few children at home, with the middle generation substantially abroad, must organise the care of its old people through some mixture of family return, paid domestic and nursing labour, public services, and the charity of religious and community institutions. Kerala's mixture leans heavily on the market and the mission: private hospitals and home-nursing agencies, church-run old age homes, and the labour of carers drawn increasingly from poorer households and other states, supervised at a distance by migrant children whose remittances pay the bills. The state's governments have experimented with palliative care networks of genuine distinction, community-based programmes that train volunteers to care for the chronically ill at home, an initiative the health literature cites internationally and that descends, recognisably, from the settlement's own grammar of organised voluntarism applied to a new need. The allocation finds in the care economy a compressed restatement of its whole argument. The need was produced by the first causes' success; the response is being assembled from the second cause's associational habits, the third cause's public programmes, and the fourth cause's money, in proportions no one planned. Whether the assembly holds is the form the crisis question takes for the next generation, and it will be answered, as the earlier questions were, less by manifestos than by whether the society's organising capacity renews itself for a task its founders never faced.

## How the princes paid: the fiscal base of provision

Princely provision was not an act of grace floating free of finance, and its foundations explain both its reach and its ceiling. Travancore's revenues rested on land tax, on customs from a coast open to the pepper and spice trade's long history, and on state monopolies in commodities such as salt and tobacco that small kingdoms could administer at their borders and ports. The kingdom also carried the obligations of a court, an army, and a subsidiary relationship with the British paramount power, whose resident watched its budgets. Within those constraints the dewans who administered the state across the nineteenth century, several of them administrators of genuine modernising intent, chose to spend visibly on schools, hospitals, roads, and public works, partly from conviction, partly because a small state competing for standing among its peers could display progress in no cheaper currency. The choice compounded. Literate subjects staffed the administration that collected the revenues that funded more schools, and the state's bureaucracy became a destination for the educated, which fed families' demand for education in a loop the allocation counts among the first cause's mechanisms. The ceiling was equally structural. A princely exchequer could fund primary schooling and dispensaries; it could not fund an industrial transformation, and it answered to a court whose legitimacy the reform movements were learning to question. When responsible government became the demand, the fiscal question and the political question merged: who would tax, and for whose priorities, was the argument the national movement in the princely states conducted through the 1940s, and the communist movement inherited that argument along with the schools. Provision's history in the region is therefore not a prelude that ends when politics begins. The budget was political from the first rescript, and the four causes are, among other things, four successive answers to the question of who pays for the common life and who decides.

## The missions: a third network beside state and association

Between the princely administration and the caste associations stood a network this article has mentioned only in passing and now restores to its proper weight: the Christian missions. Protestant societies, the Church Missionary Society and the London Missionary Society in the south, the Basel Mission in Malabar, and the Catholic orders across the coast, built schools, printing presses, hospitals, and teacher training institutions from the early nineteenth century, motivated by evangelism and sustained by the conviction that converts and neighbours alike should read. Their schools admitted children the caste order excluded, and in the south the missions' work among subordinated communities became one of the channels through which education reached households the princely system had served last. The Basel Mission in Malabar added a distinctive industrial chapter, founding tile works, weaving establishments, and printing operations that employed converts and modelled a dignity of manual labour its converts carried into the region's culture. Relations between missions, states, and communities were never simple harmony. Princely governments aided and suspected the missions in turn; caste Hindus contested mission schools as engines of conversion; the associations of the second cause learned the missions' institutional techniques, the school, the press, the hospital, the fundraising network, and applied them to communal purposes the missionaries had not intended. The allocation counts the missions within the first cause's supply, since they operated under princely tolerance and often princely subsidy, and within the second cause's prehistory, since their institutions trained the organisers and normalised the schooling of the excluded. Their presence is a further reason the case refuses single authorship. The schoolhouse in a Kerala village of 1900 might be a government building, a mission foundation, or a caste association's pride, and quite often its history ran through all three in succession, which is the four-cause argument written in masonry.

## The teachers: a profession becomes a political class

One occupational group threads through every row of the table and deserves recognition as a mechanism in its own right: the schoolteachers. The aided and government schools employed, by the middle of the twentieth century, a vast salaried class distributed into every village, literate by definition, organised early into associations and unions, and placed by their work at the exact junction of state and family. Teachers carried the reform era's values into households that had never attended a meeting; they staffed the nationalist and then the communist movements in numbers out of proportion to their share of the population; they conducted the surveys, kept the rolls, and taught the classes of the literacy campaign; and as voters and unionists they became a constituency no front could neglect, whose service conditions, won in the struggles that began with the first ministry's education bill, set the pattern for the public payroll whose growth the crisis literature audits. The allocation does not give the teachers a row, because they are not a cause beside the others but a transmission belt among them: princely and mission and association schools produced them, the movements organised them, the governments employed them, and migrant households, headed so often by a teacher's salary or aimed at a teacher's post for a daughter, measured respectability by them. Their ubiquity explains a feature of the case that puzzles outside observers, the speed with which new programmes, from the literacy campaign to the People's Plan's working groups to the palliative care networks, found competent local hands. A society dense with teachers is a society with a standing reserve of organisers in every ward. Kerala's density was built by the first two causes and spent by the rest, and the spending continues: the Gulf's schools and the world's hospitals are staffed in part by the children of those classrooms, carrying the transmission belt abroad.

## The rice question: food politics at the centre of the settlement

Kerala's food deficit made the politics of rice a permanent theatre in which the settlement's promises were tested, and the theatre's history binds the third and fourth causes together. A state that grows only part of its staple must import the rest, and the price and availability of rice in the fair price shops and the open market was, for two generations, the most sensitive index by which ordinary households judged their governments. Food agitations punctuate the state's history: movements over levy and procurement, over the centre's allocation of grain to a deficit state, over the ration's quantity and the shop's honesty, in which the communist movement, in office and out, was a principal actor, sometimes defending the distribution it administered and sometimes besieging the administration that starved it. The political lesson the state's actors absorbed was that food security was the settlement's foundation stone, prior even to land in the daily experience of the poor, since the landless labourer's household eats from the shop and the wage, not from a plot. That priority shaped the distribution system's near-universal design, unusual in an India where rationing was typically confined to cities and the destitute, and it shaped the state's bargaining with the union government into a permanent negotiation over grain. Remittances altered the theatre without closing it. Households with Gulf income exited the ration queue in practice while retaining their cards, bought finer grain in the open market, and shifted consumption toward protein, vegetables, and restaurant food, while the distribution system settled into its role as the floor beneath those without migrants and the insurance of those with them. The allocation reads the rice question as the settlement's connective tissue: the issue on which princely precedent, movement organisation, government commitment, and household strategy met every week at the shop counter, and the issue whose quieting by prosperity is the most concrete meaning the word development has in the state's ordinary speech.

## The stress test: war, return, and the remittance economy's shocks

Dependence is a word the crisis literature uses easily, and the record supplies episodes in which the dependence was tested rather than theorised. The Gulf crisis and war at the start of the 1990s drove a mass return of migrant workers to Kerala within months, an evacuation and homecoming that emptied the airports of the Gulf into the state's towns and presented, in compressed form, every fear the dependence thesis contains: incomes stopped, households that had borrowed against remittances faced their creditors, and the domestic economy was asked to absorb a workforce it had exported because it could not employ it. The state's response, relief and rehabilitation measures, loan rescheduling, the eventual re-migration of most returnees as the Gulf economies reopened, and the survey literature's documentation of the episode, showed a system more resilient than the sharpest critics expected and more exposed than the celebrants admitted. Later shocks, oil price collapses, Gulf nationalisation policies reserving jobs for citizens, and the periodic tightening of labour regimes, repeated the lesson at lower intensity. The allocation's grading of the fourth cause incorporates the tests. Remittance income is entered as a principal pillar of later living standards, and it is entered as a factor with a variance the household does not control, which is precisely what distinguishes it in kind from the first three causes: schools, organisations, and land settlements, once built, do not evaporate when a foreign ministry changes its visa rules. A reader weighing the model for export should weigh that asymmetry with the rest. The part of the Kerala achievement most easily admired from abroad, its consumption, rests on its most fragile cause, while the parts hardest to see, the claiming public and the settled institutions, rest on the causes that took a century to build and have so far survived everything the century since has done to them.

## Testing the allocation against its counterfactuals

The allocation can be subjected to one more discipline before the article closes its argument: the counterfactual audit, in which each row is removed in imagination and the consequences traced. Remove princely provision, and the reform movements organise a smaller, less schooled public, the communists inherit districts without the school network their policies presupposed, and the indicators' head start vanishes; the case becomes a poorer version of the Indian norm, and the first row stands. Remove the reform movements, and princely schools serve a deferential population that underuses them, girls' schooling lags, no claiming public exists to discipline governments or staff campaigns, and the third cause's governments legislate for subjects rather than citizens; the second row stands. Remove communist-era redistribution, and the tenancy order survives into the era of the ballot box, landlord power adapts to democracy as it did in much of India, access to the inherited services remains graded by caste and class, and the indicators, respectable by inheritance, plateau well below the recorded pattern while the Gulf takes the educated abroad all the same; the third row stands, and with it the answer to the strongest objection, since the counterfactual shows the inheritance without the addition producing a visibly lesser case. Remove remittances, and the indicators survive, as their chronology says they must, but the consumption, housing, and private expansion of the later decades collapse toward the domestic economy's austere level, and the fiscal crisis arrives earlier and harder; the fourth row stands, bounded exactly where the table bounds it. Four removals, four distinct deteriorations, no row redundant and no row sufficient: that is what a multi-cause allocation claims, and the audit is offered so that readers can repeat it against any rival grading they prefer. The article's confidence in its table is not that the gradings are beyond argument. It is that the arguments must now take this form, cause by cause and counterfactual by counterfactual, and that the single-cause stories with which the case is usually sold cannot survive being asked the table's final question: what, precisely, can your cause not explain?

## The Gulf's geography inside Kerala

Migration remade the state's internal map as surely as it remade its houses, and the survey literature's district-level record lets the allocation watch the fourth cause at work with unusual clarity. The northern districts of Malabar, historically the state's poorest in provision and harshest in tenancy, sent migrants in the largest relative numbers, drawn by networks that pioneers from particular villages and communities established in particular Gulf towns, so that recruitment ran along channels of kin and neighbourhood rather than through any market in the abstract. Remittance spending in those districts compressed a century of catch-up into a generation: houses, mosques and temples and churches rebuilt at monumental scale, private schools and hospitals founded by trusts flush with migrant subscriptions, land prices driven to levels that astonished the state's own economists. The social consequences cut in the familiar double direction. Communities that caste and tenancy had kept at the margin, including the Mappila Muslims of Malabar whose reform movements had preached education against internal resistance, used migration income to build the institutions of arrival, altering the state's communal balance in ways both fronts' electoral arithmetic registered. At the same time the sending districts exported their working-age men so thoroughly that local agriculture and construction came to depend on in-migrants from Bengal, Odisha, and Assam, workers whose presence recreated, inside the model state, the unprotected migrant condition its own people had fled, a symmetry the state's labour department and unions have spent years learning to govern. The allocation notes the geography because it refines the fourth row's grading: remittances did not fall on the state like weather. They fell where networks led, they amplified the second cause's communal institutions where those existed to receive them, and they exposed, in the in-migrant workforce, the boundary of the settlement's protections at exactly the point where its own history should have made it most alert. Causes interact, and the table's rows, read down a single district's history, are a braid rather than a stack.

## Older migrations: the Gulf in a longer history

The Gulf wave was the largest movement of people out of Kerala, not the first, and placing it in the longer history corrects a foreshortening the allocation exists to prevent. Keralites had migrated for generations before the oil boom: to the plantations and towns of Ceylon and Malaya under colonial labour systems, to Bombay and Madras and Delhi as clerks, teachers, nurses, and soldiers, to the princely and then the national capital's expanding administration. The state's educated unemployment had always exported its surplus, and its households had long experience of the absent earner and the money order. What changed with the Gulf was scale, earnings, and destination: wages at multiples of Indian levels, in economies a few hours away by air, open to the moderately schooled as well as the professional. The longer history matters to the grading in two ways. It shows that migration readiness, the networks, the information, the cultural expectation that a young person's future might lie elsewhere, was itself an inheritance of the first two causes, since a society must be literate and connected before it can be mobile at scale. And it shows that the fourth cause's novelty is narrower than the drama suggests: Kerala had always lived partly on earnings from elsewhere, and the Gulf intensified a structure rather than inventing one. The crisis literature's dependence charge survives the correction in a softened form. A state can be accustomed to migration and still be exposed by its scale, as the stress test of the war years proved. But the allocation prefers the accurate sentence: the fourth cause is an old pattern at a new magnitude, and the magnitude, not the pattern, is what the model's critics are entitled to indict.

## Water, wells, and the quiet determinants

Beneath the headline services runs a stratum of provision so ordinary that summaries skip it, and the health literature insists it belongs in any causal account: water. Kerala is drenched by monsoon and yet chronically short of safe drinking water in its dry months, a paradox produced by terrain that sheds the rain to the sea, by laterite soils, and by settlement dispersed in homesteads rather than concentrated in villages. The traditional answer was the household well, dug into nearly every compound, and public health in the princely and post-independence state alike was substantially a campaign over those wells and their protection, alongside latrine construction, drainage, and the control of the waterborne and mosquito-borne diseases that the climate guarantees. The allocation mentions the stratum because it illustrates, at the humblest level, the case's general law. Outcomes were produced where state programmes met household practice: a chlorination drive works where families maintain their wells, a latrine programme where the reform era had made sanitation a matter of dignity, a malaria campaign where the reading public could be told why the spraying teams must be admitted. Where the meeting failed, in the crowded coastal strips and the hill settlements, waterborne disease persisted as the margins' signature. The famous indicators were assembled from such meetings, repeated millions of times across a century, and no ministry decreed them into being at any single moment. That is the deepest reason the four-cause form fits this case. The outcome has no birthday. It accumulated, cause upon cause and household upon household, and accounts that assign it to a government are mistaking the last visible hand for the whole machinery.

## The cooperative economy and the third sector

Between the state and the market, Kerala built a third economic landscape that the allocation should register, because it carried part of the settlement's weight and displays its grammar in commercial form. Cooperative societies organised credit in the villages, distributed inputs and consumer goods, marketed the produce of smallholders, and in dairy built a federation that collected milk from hundreds of thousands of small producers into a state brand. Toddy workers, weavers, coir workers, and fishers were gathered into cooperatives that governments of both fronts promoted as the alternative to both private exploitation and departmental management, with results that varied from durable success to the familiar pathologies of political capture and loan default. The cooperative idea had roots in the reform era's associational habits and in the national movement's constructive programme, and the communist movement adopted it as the acceptable face of collectivism inside a mixed economy. Within the allocation, the cooperatives matter less as a separate cause than as evidence about the kind of society the first three causes jointly produced: one in which collective forms could be staffed, audited, and argued over by their members, and in which the boundary between movement, market, and state was permanently negotiable. They also matter as a qualification. Where cooperatives were captured by the fronts' local machines, they administered the spoils politics the crisis literature condemns, and their balance sheets record the same dual character as the welfare funds: an instrument of genuine collective provision whose performance depended, village by village, on whether the claiming public was watching. The model's economy, in other words, was never only the sum of its factories and farms. It included a dense institutional underbrush in which the settlement's values were either practised or betrayed daily, and the literature's disagreement about the model's health is often, at ground level, a disagreement about what the underbrush shows.

## Kerala in the Indian left's argument with itself

The case has a second career inside the Indian Marxist tradition, as evidence in the movement's own strategic debates, and a series on Marxism in India must record it. For defenders of the parliamentary and federal road, Kerala is the standing exhibit: proof that communist parties can win office democratically, redistribute within a constitution, and return to be judged, building a record that outlasts their tenures because the society defends it. For the tradition's revolutionary critics, the same record proves the road's limits: two generations in and out of office in one state left national power untouched, capitalism intact, and the movement itself, in their charge, domesticated into an administrator of welfare within a system it was founded to transcend; the Maoist current treated in this series' article on Naxalism drew exactly that moral from the Kerala and Bengal experiences. For the movement's internal reformers, the case poses the question the crisis literature sharpened: whether a left that redistributes without transforming production has built a way station or a terminus. The allocation takes no side in that argument, which belongs to the tradition's continuing life and to other articles. It does insist on the evidence the argument must use. Any verdict on the parliamentary road that cites Kerala must cite the whole table: the inheritance the road's travellers started with, the mobilisation that made their vehicle run, the redistribution they genuinely achieved, the production they did not achieve, and the external income that kept their settlement solvent. Cited whole, the case supports neither triumph nor dismissal. It supports a harder sentence that the tradition's honest members in the state have themselves uttered in various forms: that what was won was real, that what was not won was decisive, and that the distance between those clauses is the agenda the case bequeaths. An allocation cannot settle a movement's strategy. It can prevent the strategy from being debated over a fiction, and for this series that prevention is service enough.

## The evidence base: literatures, not legends

A note on sources belongs in the body of an article that has refused to quote figures, because the refusal could be mistaken for lightness of evidence. The account above rests on literatures with names and addresses. The social history of the princely territories rests on the work of historians of Travancore and Cochin and on the educational and administrative record they mined. The reform movements rest on movement histories, biographies of the reformers, and the political sociology that followed Desai and others in tracing mobilisation's consequences. The communist decades rest on the legislative record, on implementation studies of the land reform by scholars including Herring, on the political science of Heller and others for decentralisation, and on the memoir and debate literature of the participants. The crisis case rests on the Centre for Development Studies tradition, from K. K. George's early statement through the labour research of Kannan and the fiscal analyses of the state's own white papers and commissions. The migration account rests on the survey series conducted from the same centre by Zachariah, Irudaya Rajan, and their colleagues across successive rounds, the most sustained documentation of a migration system anywhere in India. The health and demographic claims rest on the survey and registration literatures as synthesised by Drèze, Sen, Ramachandran, and the public health scholarship. Where those literatures conflict, the article has said so and declined to adjudicate, which is the figures protocol's purpose: not to avoid evidence but to avoid pretending that a reference article stands above the disciplines it reports. Readers who wish to test the allocation will find, in the literatures named, both the material for its defence and the material for its refutation, and the article's grading is offered to that testing without reserve.

## The language of aspiration: Malayalam, English, and the schools

One argument inside the education story deserves extraction, because it condenses the settlement's tensions into a single parental decision. The public and aided schools taught in Malayalam, the mother tongue, and the pedagogical case for early schooling in the language of the home is one the state's educators long defended with pride. Across the remittance decades, however, families increasingly bought English-medium schooling in the unaided sector, judging, rightly or wrongly, that English was the currency of the Gulf professions, of national examinations, and of the metropolitan labour market their children would enter. The shift was class-marked and self-reinforcing: as salaried and migrant households exited, the public system's intake concentrated among the poor, and the political voice defending its quality weakened in the way the crisis literature describes for public services generally. Governments of both fronts responded late and ambivalently, improving English teaching within public schools while defending the mother-tongue principle, and the state's classrooms became a daily referendum on what the model was for: equality of provision in the common language, or mobility through the language of the wider market. The allocation records the episode without a verdict, as a specimen of the settlement in its third generation. The first causes built the common school; the fourth cause's income let families shop beyond it; the third cause's governments were left administering the tension between the equality they preached and the aspirations their own success had taught. A model is not refuted by generating such tensions. Every durable settlement generates them. But an account of Kerala that omits the queue outside the English-medium school has omitted the place where the model's heirs announce, each admission season, what they think the model still owes them.

## A state of towns: the urban pattern

Kerala urbanised without building great cities, and the pattern shapes everything from its politics to its ecology. Settlement runs in a near-continuous ribbon along the coast and the midland roads, village shading into town without the sharp rural-urban boundary that structures Indian statistics and Indian sociology alike, so that the state's census urbanisation and its lived urbanisation tell different stories and both are partly true. Households combine a homestead plot and garden cultivation with wage work, trade, services, and a migrant's remittance, and the categories of farmer, worker, and trader blur within single families across a single week. For the allocation the pattern explains several otherwise puzzling features of the record. Public services could be delivered at high coverage because the population was never scattered in remote hamlets beyond the road's reach; the school, the clinic, and the distribution shop stood within walking distance of a dispersed but connected citizenry. Land reform's homestead provision mattered so much because the homestead was the universal platform of this settlement pattern, the base from which every other activity launched. Industry, conversely, faced a landscape without the concentrated land and labour pools that factory urbanisation elsewhere assembled, and the state's industrial geography remained one of small units strung along the ribbon. Even the politics follows the pattern: the gram sabha and the ward committee govern a public that lives at a density where representatives are neighbours, and the audit culture of the settlement, its processions and its reading rooms, is the culture of a society too interconnected for anonymity. Readers comparing Kerala with states of compact villages and distant towns should add the settlement pattern to the inheritance column, since it precedes every government and conditions every policy, one more entry in the long list of what the third cause did not have to create and could not have created for itself.

## Tourism and the selling of the model

In the final decades of the century the state acquired an industry built from its own reputation, as tourism marketed the backwaters, the hills, and the Ayurvedic tradition to visitors from India and abroad under a slogan that made the landscape a divine possession. Tourism's relevance here is not its size, respectable though the sector became, but its character as the model's self-commodification. The greenery the brochures sold was the homestead garden landscape the settlement pattern produced; the literacy and political stability the operators relied on were the model's own; the houseboats were converted cargo craft of the backwater trade; the Ayurvedic clinics drew on a medical tradition the princely courts had patronised and the state's health culture kept reputable. Even the industry's discontents reprise the article's themes: tourism's enclaves reproduced the margins' exclusions in miniature, its construction pressed on the wetlands the environmental section mourned, and its employment, seasonal and service-bound, joined the long list of work that is not the industrial employment the crisis literature demands. The allocation does not grade tourism as a cause of the model, since it arrived as a consequence. It records the industry as the moment the model's image became one of its exports, alongside its nurses and its workers, and therefore as a source of the travelling version's simplifications: a state sold as a postcard will be read as one, and the four causes, with their tenancy tribunals and their fiscal arithmetic, are precisely what the postcard leaves out. The scholar's Kerala and the brochure's Kerala are the same place at different exposures, and this article has been written, deliberately, at the exposure where the causes show.

## The panchayat as a school of the settlement

Local government in Kerala long predates the People's Plan, and its deeper history rounds out the decentralisation story the third cause claims. Village unions and panchayats existed under the princely administrations and the early republic in attenuated form, and the communist-led governments of the late 1980s had already attempted a district council experiment that the succeeding front dismantled, an alternation-era rehearsal for the greater devolution of the following decade. The pattern across these episodes is the one the allocation predicts: devolution succeeded in proportion to the society's capacity to staff and audit it, and the 1990s campaign differed from its predecessors less in conception than in the funds devolved and the mobilisation that inaugurated it. What two further decades of routine local government added was a training ground. The panchayats and municipalities became the settlement's school of administration, where thousands of citizens, women in reserved seats prominent among them, learned budgeting, contracting, and the management of the schools and clinics now under local oversight, and from which a share of the state's legislators and organisers graduated. Scholars of decentralisation treat this human capital effect as among the campaign's most durable products, and it belongs in the allocation as the third cause's late dividend: an investment in the citizenry's governing capacity whose returns appear in every subsequent programme that found competent local hands. It also belongs in the record as a standing contrast with the fiscal story. The state's exchequer strained while its civic capacity grew, and any balance sheet of the model that counts only money will misprice the asset the panchayats represent. Kerala's governments were often short of funds. They were rarely short of people who knew how a meeting is run, a budget read, and a work audited, and that abundance, built by the second cause and multiplied by the third, is the closest the case comes to a renewable resource.

## The industrial working class that stayed small

A final structural fact completes the frame, and it is the one the Marxist tradition's own categories make most poignant. The classical theory expected the proletariat of large industry to be the agent of transformation, and Kerala's transformation was accomplished without one. The state's industrial workers were real but dispersed across small units and declining traditional trades; its organised strength lay instead among agricultural labourers, tenants turned owners, teachers, government employees, informal workers in statutory funds, and the vast penumbra of the educated unemployed and the migrant. The movement that governed Kerala was therefore a coalition of the labouring countryside and the salaried and aspiring strata, held together by organisations that spoke the language of class while representing a society whose centre of gravity was the homestead, the classroom, and the remittance. Critics inside the tradition have treated this composition as the explanation of the experiment's limits: a movement of small producers and salary earners, they argue, will redistribute consumption and services with passion and hesitate before the expropriations and industrial disciplines that a factory proletariat's programme might have attempted. Defenders reply that the movement represented the working people who actually existed in Kerala, and that fidelity to them, rather than to a demographic the theory preferred, is the case's honour. The allocation records the composition as fact and the argument as the tradition's own, since both bear on how the third cause should be graded. Judged against the programme of classical proletarian revolution, Kerala is a deviation. Judged as the record of what organised labouring people, in a poor and literate society, extracted from their rulers and built for themselves across two generations, it is among the most substantial achievements the Indian left can show, and the tension between those judgements is not resolvable by evidence. It is the tension the series exists to document, in India as in the ecological and European cases that surround this one.

## Chit funds, gold, and the household balance sheet

The household economy that the four causes jointly produced had its own financial instruments, and they deserve a place in the record because they show the society intermediating its own savings long before analysts of financial inclusion discovered it. The chit fund, a rotating savings and credit association in which members subscribe monthly and the pooled sum is auctioned or allotted by lot to one member in turn, was ubiquitous in Kerala's towns and villages, and the state itself entered the business through a public sector chit company founded in the late 1960s, which grew into one of the state's largest financial institutions on the custom of ordinary households. Alongside the chits ran the gold loan: the pawned or pledged ornament, converted at the neighbourhood bank or finance company into school fees, a visa's cost, a medical bill, or the margin money for a house plot, in a society where women's gold functioned as the family's reserve asset and its ceremonial face at once. Remittances passed through these instruments and multiplied them, as migrant savings sought the chit's discipline and the plot's security. The allocation's interest is diagnostic once more. A population that runs its own rotating credit for a century, that staffs a state chit company into a major institution, and that treats a daughter's gold as a bankable reserve is a population whose financial behaviour cannot be described by the vocabulary of backwardness the development literature brought to most poor societies. The habits are the second cause in mufti: associational, literate, record-keeping, and oriented to the long term. They also carry the familiar shadow, since chit failures and gold-loan foreclosures punctuate the record, and the crisis of any household's finances in Kerala is conducted, like its politics, in public and at close quarters. The model's households were never merely beneficiaries of the causes above them. They were financiers of their own advancement, at interest rates and risks they understood, and the four rows of the table meet, at the kitchen table where the chit book is kept, in a single family's arithmetic.

## The knowledge institutions: the state studies itself

Kerala built, alongside its schools, a small archipelago of research institutions whose existence is itself a datum about the model. The Centre for Development Studies, founded in 1970 under the economist K. N. Raj's leadership and housed in Thiruvananthapuram, became the case's principal observatory, training generations of researchers, hosting the debates this article has drawn on, and producing the migration surveys and the crisis literature from inside the society they described. Around it stand institutes for water, for tropical botany, for regional health and earth sciences, and university departments whose journals and conferences made the state's argument with itself a credentialed activity. The allocation counts the archipelago as the second cause's institutional crown and the third cause's instrument: a left government of the 1970s endowed the centre that would later publish the most damaging audits of the left's own model, and the fronts that followed continued to fund the society's self-examination because a reading public expected nothing less. Few exhibits better capture the case's difference from the stereotype of movement rule. The Kerala settlement generated its own critics in its own institutions, paid them salaries, and read them in its newspapers, which is why the crisis literature could be at once internal and authoritative, and why this article could be written from named literatures rather than from legend. A tradition confident enough to endow its auditors has at least one virtue the allocation is happy to record without grading, since it conditions every grade the article has given: the evidence was gathered by the society under examination, in public, over decades, and the four causes are answerable to it in a way that propaganda never is.

## Monsoon, flood, and the governance of water

In 1924 the state's rivers rose in a flood remembered for generations as the deluge of the century, drowning lowlands, remaking channels, and entering the language as the standard against which later inundations are measured. The episode belongs in this article because disaster is the audit no government can schedule, and Kerala's history with water displays the settlement's strengths and exposures in concentrated form. The strengths are the familiar ones: a literate population that can be warned and organised, local bodies and associations that know every lane, a press that carries instructions and afterwards carries the reckoning, and a political culture in which relief is a right claimed loudly rather than a favour received quietly. The exposures are the settlement's own products: a population spread across floodplains and reclaimed wetlands in the continuous ribbon the urbanisation section described, paddy fields filled and drainage narrowed by the building boom, quarried hills and sand-mined rivers whose altered courses the water finds. Each generation's development writes the next generation's flood map, and the state's argument about its model is incomplete until it includes the argument about where the model has been building. The allocation draws the moral in its own terms. The first causes gave the society the capacity to survive its waters with remarkably little loss of life by the standards of poor societies. The fourth cause's money and the third cause's incomplete land-use governance increased the property at risk. Survival and exposure grew together, which is the Kerala pattern in hydrological dress, and the reader who has followed the table this far will recognise it without further gloss.

## A composite village: the causes at ground level

Abstractions about causes can be tested against a single imagined but representative settlement, assembled from the record's typical features, and the exercise is worth performing before the article closes. Take a midland village of the sort that fills the state's ribbon development. Its school began as a mission foundation in the nineteenth century, passed into the aided net under a caste association's management, and stands in a building the panchayat repaired under a decentralised plan. Its older residents include a man whose father held land as a tenant under an intermediary, filed before a land tribunal with the peasant association's help, and received the title that made the family's homestead plot, the one the reform's kudikidappu provision secured, into the ground on which a concrete house now stands, built in stages from a son's wages in the Gulf and roofed in the year a daughter qualified as a nurse. The village reading room holds the newspapers its association founded generations ago and the pamphlets of every movement since. Its ration shop, its health sub-centre, its women's neighbourhood group, its cooperative society's office, and its bus stop stand within a few hundred metres of one another, and the argument at the tea stall outside the reading room, about which front squandered what, is conducted by men and women who can each name the decade in which each institution arrived. Nothing in the village is explicable by a single cause, and every institution in it carries at least two causes in its foundation deed. The composite is not evidence; the evidence is in the literatures the article has cited throughout. It is a check on the imagination, a reminder that the table's rows are not abstractions competing for a prize but layers of one place's accumulated life, and that the people who live among the layers have never had the option of attributing their village to a single author. Historians call such places overdetermined. The residents call them home, and the allocation's four rows are simply the directions from which home was built.

## The consensus beneath the alternation

Beneath the famous alternation of fronts lies a consensus so broad that the state's polemics usually conceal it, and naming the consensus completes the political side of the allocation. No front that has governed Kerala has proposed to reverse the land settlement, to dismantle the distribution network, to withdraw the state from schooling and health, or to repeal the welfare funds and the local government framework; the argument between the fronts is conducted over management, extension, finance, and credit, within a settlement both treat as the electorate's property. The consensus extends to the electorate's expectations, which punish deviation more reliably than they reward innovation, and to the bureaucracy and the intermediary powers, churches and associations and unions, whose institutional lives are woven into the settlement's fabric. Political scientists describe such arrangements as path dependence, and the description is accurate but bloodless. In the state's own idiom the settlement is simply what government is for, an understanding purchased by two generations of mobilisation and delivery, and it explains the paradox that Kerala's politics can be simultaneously furious and stable: the fury is real, and it is contained within walls nobody campaigns to demolish. For the allocation, the consensus is the third cause's final form. A repertoire that began as one movement's programme ended as the common sense of a whole polity, administered by its opponents as faithfully as by its authors, and at that point attribution to the movement alone becomes not merely inaccurate but unintelligible, since the achievement being graded is precisely the repertoire's escape from its authors. Movements rarely know whether to celebrate that escape or mourn it. The Kerala movement has done both, in print, for decades, and the argument is among the most instructive in the Indian left's literature.

## What this case asks of the series

The articles surrounding this one examine movements that seized states, lost them, or never approached them, and the Kerala case contributes a distinctive question to that company. Elsewhere the argument turns on what a movement did with sovereign power. Here sovereignty was never on offer: the movement governed a state inside a federation, under a constitution it could not amend, against interests it could not dissolve, before an electorate that dismissed it at intervals and a press that audited it daily. The question the case poses is therefore about the yield of partial power patiently used, and the allocation is the form of its answer: a tenancy order abolished, a society's access equalised, a literacy completed, a planning decentralised, and a productive transformation unachieved, the whole financed in maturity by the labour of the society's own emigrants. Readers who bring the case to the series' larger disputes, about parliament and revolution, about the state and its transformation, will find it refuses enlistment by either army's pamphlets. It shows a communist movement achieving durable, life-changing redistribution through institutions it shared with its enemies, and it shows the same movement stopped, by the economy's structure and its own electorate, at the threshold of the transformation its doctrine promised. Both showings are documented in the same record and neither cancels the other. The exam guides that close this cluster will ask candidates to handle the case whole, and the standard this article sets for that handling is the standard it has tried to meet: every cause graded, every grading bounded, and the final column never left blank.

## Who acted? The question of agency across the four causes

Every causal allocation conceals a theory of agency, and this article's should be made explicit before it closes. The four rows distribute action among unlike agents: dynastic states deciding from above, subordinated communities organising from below, a political movement converting organisation into legislation, and hundreds of thousands of households pursuing their own strategies across an international labour market. No single subject, not the party, not the people, not the market, not the crown, carries the story, and that is the deepest respect in which the case resists the narrative habits of both its celebrants and its enemies. Movements prefer histories with protagonists, and the Kerala record keeps dissolving its protagonists into coalitions and preconditions. The communist ministry of 1957 acted, decisively, and it acted with instruments the reform associations had forged, on a stage the princely budgets had built, before an audience the reading rooms had trained. The migrant acted, decisively, and acted on credentials the public schools had issued and toward destinations the pioneers' networks had mapped. Even the princes acted inside constraints, of paramountcy, of revenue, of a legitimacy their reforming subjects were redefining beneath them. Agency in this case is real at every level and sovereign at none, and the allocation's refusal to crown a protagonist is not modesty. It is the finding. Traditions that require a single author for social change will find the case unusable, which may be why the case is so often used badly, cropped to whichever author the user arrived with. The series' interest in Kerala, stated plainly, is that it trains the opposite habit: follow the instruments, date the layers, and let the credit fall where the sequence puts it, even when the sequence puts it in several places at once.

## Why delivery worked: trust, scrutiny, and local knowledge

One mechanism recurs inside every row of the table without owning a row of its own, and the article ends its argument by honouring it. Kerala's programmes were delivered into a society that watched them. The beneficiary of a welfare fund knew the board's members; the parent at the school gate knew the teacher's attendance by the neighbourhood's gossip before any inspector reported it; the claimant before the land tribunal arrived with the association's worker who knew the plot's history better than the record did; the reader of the morning paper could compare the minister's claim with the file the opposition had obtained. Delivery systems fail in most places at the last mile, where the entitlement meets the clerk, the queue, and the bribe. In Kerala the last mile was inhabited, by organisations with memories and by citizens with newspapers, and the inhabitation is the scarce resource that the first two causes spent a century accumulating. Governments elsewhere have copied the state's programmes and discovered that the copy lacks the watchers. This is also why the crisis literature's politicisation charge cuts so deep when it lands: the same density that polices delivery can capture it, and the line between the union that monitors the hospital and the union that staffs it with its own is a line the society argues about continuously, in the open, with its usual equipment of print and procession. The allocation cannot grade a mechanism that operates inside all four rows, and it does not try. It records the mechanism as the case's connective tissue and as the final answer to the replication question that policy readers bring: what Kerala had, beneath the programmes, was a public that audited its own provisions, and building that public, by schools and associations and print across a hundred years, was the work on which everything else in this article stands.

## The settlement inside the family

Institutions survive by being inherited inside households, and the Kerala settlement's most reliable transmission mechanism was the family itself. The generation that remembered tenancy taught its children the value of the title deed and the ration card by narrating what preceded them; the generation that marched in the literacy campaign taught its grandchildren that an illiterate elder was a family responsibility rather than a private shame; the migrant's children grew up measuring the house's rising walls against an absent parent's contract dates, absorbing the arithmetic of sacrifice and schooling as the household's common sense. Family strategies carried the settlement's priorities across the decades more faithfully than any party programme: educate the daughters, keep the clinic's appointments, maintain the well and the latrine, vote, read the paper, join the association, send the able young where the wages are and bring the money home to the plot. Sociologists sometimes describe such transmission as habitus and economists as preferences, but in the state's own telling it is simply what a family does, and its invisibility in the statistics is why outside accounts over-credit governments. The allocation's rows describe the public architecture. The family's kitchen-table curriculum is the private architecture that kept the public one inhabited, and any future history of the model that begins with the ministries and ends with the surveys will have missed the room where the model was actually reproduced, one household decision at a time, across four generations.

## A note on exceptionalism

Scholars of the state periodically warn against Kerala exceptionalism, the habit of treating the case as a wonder exempt from the rules that govern other societies, and the warning deserves the article's endorsement in a specific form. Kerala is not exempt from political economy. Its governments faced budgets, its parties faced elections, its households faced prices, and its outcomes were produced by mechanisms, provision, mobilisation, redistribution, migration, that operate everywhere and can be compared everywhere. What is exceptional is only the combination's completeness and the record's documentation: few societies assembled all four mechanisms in sequence, and fewer still argued about the assembly in print for a century while research institutes measured it. The distinction matters because exceptionalism, in its lazy form, is another single-cause story, with the cause renamed as culture or destiny and removed from examination. The allocation has been written against that removal. Every mechanism named here has an address in the comparative literature, from support-led security to land reform implementation to remittance economics, and the article's gradings stand or fall by evidence those literatures can check. If the case teaches a method, the method is the opposite of exceptionalism: assume the mechanisms are ordinary, insist on the sequence, and let the extraordinariness, where it survives, reside in the documented facts rather than in the teller's reverence. A case that can be explained without miracles is worth more to a serious tradition than a miracle, because explanations can be argued with, improved, and used.

## Reading the landscape: what the traveller actually sees

The case's abstractions settle, for a visitor moving through the state, into a legible landscape, and the legibility is itself a summary of the allocation. The traveller passes a continuous ribbon of homesteads, each on its plot, garden trees around a house whose fabric dates its owner's migration history at a glance, the older tile giving way to concrete storeys as remittance decades advanced. At intervals stand the settlement's public furniture in dense array: the government school and the aided school, their boards carrying the names of the associations and churches that founded them; the primary health centre and the private clinic facing one another across the road; the ration shop with its queue of card-holders; the reading room with its glass case of newspapers; the panchayat office flying its flag; the cooperative bank; the union office with its fading mural; the women's group meeting in a borrowed hall. Temples, churches, and mosques rebuilt at monumental scale mark the communal institutions that organised the second cause and banked the fourth. Nothing in the scene announces itself as a model. There is no capital district of development, no showcase project with a foundation stone, and that absence is the point the article has laboured to establish. The Kerala achievement is not a monument but a density, the accumulated presence of ordinary institutions within everyone's reach, built by unlike hands across a century and a half. A traveller who understands why the school, the clinic, the shop, and the reading room stand where they do, and whose money and whose movement put them there, has understood the four causes in their only fully honest form: as a landscape that a society made for itself, layer upon layer, and then argued about in the newspapers the reading room provides.

## The open end: employment and the next argument

Every account of the case, this one included, arrives at the same unfinished sentence, and it is better to end the argument by writing the sentence out than by decorating it. The settlement educated a society faster than its economy learned to employ it, and the surplus of educated labour was absorbed, across two generations, by migration, by the expansion of public and service employment, and by the patience of households that carried their graduates through long waits. None of those absorbers is guaranteed to the next generation on the old terms: Gulf labour markets nationalise and fluctuate, the public payroll is the fiscal problem itself, and household patience is a resource the young increasingly spend on departure rather than on waiting. The state's argument with itself has accordingly shifted its centre, from the defence of the settlement's achievements to the search for an economy worthy of them, conducted by both fronts in the vocabulary of investment, skills, and knowledge industries, and audited by the same public that audited the ration shops. The allocation offers no prophecy about that argument's outcome, since prophecy is not among its instruments. It offers the record on which the argument will be conducted: a society whose capabilities are not in doubt, whose institutions for claiming and delivering are intact, whose fiscal room is narrow, and whose history demonstrates that organised publics can build, across generations, what no single government can decree. Whether the next cause to enter a future edition of the table is a productive transformation achieved at home is the question the case leaves open, and leaving it open, with the evidence arranged, is the correct place for a reference article to stop.

There is a temptation, on reaching the open end, to ask the case for a morale, and the article resists it in favour of a plainer observation. The Kerala record shows ordinary mechanisms, funded schools, organised citizens, redistributed land, exported labour, compounding across a century into an outcome that struck the world as extraordinary, and it shows the same mechanisms meeting their limits in the same decades and the same villages. Nothing in the record required heroes, and nothing in it was accomplished by villains' absence; it was accomplished by princes calculating legitimacy, reformers building schools out of humiliation, tenants filing claims, nurses boarding aircraft, and voters dismissing governments, each acting on reasons the record lets us reconstruct. If the case has a use beyond its region, it is the encouragement that such reconstruction gives: outcomes that look like national character, once dated and disaggregated, turn into sequences that other societies can study without either envy or despair. The study will not flatter the student. It will show how much of the achievement was inherited, how much was fought for, how much was financed from abroad, and how much remains undone, and it will leave the proportions to be argued over with the evidence in hand. That is all the allocation has ever claimed to provide: not a verdict that ends the argument, but an arrangement of the record in which the argument can finally be conducted honestly, cause by cause, decade by decade, village by village.

## Citing the case without abusing it

Reference articles exist to be cited, and this one closes its argument with the terms on which its case may honestly be used, since Kerala suffers more from its citers than from its critics. The first term is dating: any claim about the model must carry its period, because a sentence true of the indicators in the 1970s may be false of the fisc in the 1990s and meaningless about the households of the remittance decades, and the commonest abuse in the literature is the undated present tense, in which a century and a half of sequence is compressed into a timeless tableau. The second term is specification: claims must name their outcome. Kerala is evidence about literacy, survival, land relations, and the politics of provision; it is not evidence about industrialisation, fiscal sustainability, or the quality of learning, except as a warning, and a citation that borrows the famous indicators to certify an unrelated virtue repeats the travelling version's original sin. The third term is inheritance: any use of the case to praise or indict a government must state what that government inherited, since the allocation shows the inheritance doing causal work in every period, and a verdict that omits it is a verdict about a different, imaginary state. The fourth term is the final column: a citation is honest only if it can say what its favoured cause cannot explain, and the writer who cannot fill that column for Kerala has not yet earned the right to fill the others. These terms are not pedantry. They are the difference between a case and a slogan, and the distance this article has travelled, from the puzzle of the opening paragraph through forty sections of sequence and mechanism, is the distance between the two. The four causes allocation is offered, finally, as a citation device: a compact, checkable form in which the whole case can be carried into other arguments without being traduced on the journey, by friend or enemy of the tradition it partly belongs to.

## The balance sheet, drawn in prose

It may help the reader who has followed the argument this far to see the whole grading compressed into a single paragraph of prose, stripped of the table's geometry. Kerala entered the modern age with schools and clinics a princely and missionary century had built, and with a society its reform movements had taught to claim them, to read, and to organise. Into that inheritance came a communist movement that abolished the tenancy order its predecessors had only protested, carried the inherited services toward every household, finished the literacy the schools had begun, and taught the citizenry to plan in its own assemblies, while rival governments, alternating with it for two generations, maintained and extended the same settlement under the electorate's audit. Onto that achievement fell, from the 1970s, the earnings of the society's own emigrants, which raised its consumption to levels its fields and workshops never earned and bound its fortunes to labour markets abroad. The settlement's costs, fiscal strain, thin industry, educated unemployment, and the unfinished business of its margins and its ecology, are entered in the same ledger, in the same ink. That paragraph is the article. Everything else in these pages is its evidence, its boundaries, and its defence against the shorter paragraphs, crediting one cause and damning another, with which the case is usually sold. Readers who carry away only the paragraph will still carry away more of the truth than the slogans carry, which is the standard a summary should meet and the standard by which the article is content to be judged in its turn.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier of this case lies where the allocation's rows are thinnest. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the Kerala model?

The Kerala model names a state's attainment of literacy, longevity, and health well beyond what its modest income would lead anyone to expect, a pattern the research literature records across decades of demographic and survey work. The outcomes were consolidated while communist-led coalitions and their rivals took turns in government from the late 1950s onward, and the case entered development writing as proof that determined public action could deliver schooling and care ahead of wealth. Its defining feature is the conjunction, not either element alone, and the conjunction, high social development alongside low income, is what demands explanation. The model is a pattern to be explained rather than a policy document, and the explanation offered here runs through four causes in sequence.

### Q: How did Kerala achieve its social indicators?

Through a sequence no single government authored. Princely states funded schools and hospitals from the nineteenth century, and missions and communities added institutions of their own. Reform movements organised among subordinated castes built a population that demanded schooling and care, and supplied the organisers of public life. Communist-led ministries, backed by mass movements, abolished tenancy, pushed food distribution toward universality, sustained the public services, finished mass literacy by campaign, and decentralised planning. From the 1970s, Gulf remittances raised household consumption and private spending on education and health. Each stage presupposed the one before: the schools produced the public, the public produced the politics, the politics redistributed, and the migrants were the schools' own graduates. The allocation in this article grades each contribution and states what each cannot explain.

### Q: Did communism cause Kerala's social outcomes?

In part, and the parts can be dated. Schooling and health provision on a substantial scale are older than any communist ministry, and reform movements had mobilised the population before one existed. The communist contribution is best specified rather than debated in the abstract: it was decisive in redistributing land and rural power, in equalising access to the inherited services across caste and class, and in completing literacy and decentralising planning. Migrant earnings sent home from the Gulf, from the 1970s onward, form a separate major factor in the state's later living standards. A movement that inherits an educated, organised society and then dissolves its tenancy order has done something specific and large, and the allocation credits exactly that, neither the invention of the society nor a mere custodianship of it.

### Q: What social reform movements preceded communism in Kerala?

The movements organised from the late nineteenth century among the state's communities. Sree Narayana Guru's movement among Ezhavas, organised through the Yogam from 1903, preached against caste hierarchy and built temples, schools, and colleges. Ayyankali organised Pulaya agricultural labourers in struggles for roads, dress, and school admission. The Nair Service Society, founded in 1914, reformed Nair social practice and built educational institutions, while church schools spread through parishes and mission stations and Muslim reformers pressed education in Malabar. Together with an explosive growth of print, reading rooms, and libraries, these movements produced a mobilised and substantially literate public before communist organisation became a major force, which is why historians treat mobilisation as an inheritance the communists received rather than a condition they created.

### Q: How successful were Kerala's land reforms?

By the standards of Indian land reform, exceptionally successful in their central purpose and limited in their wider ambitions. Legislation recast in the late 1960s and implemented from the start of 1970 abolished tenancy: the intermediary's right to rent was extinguished, cultivating tenants became owners through land tribunals, and hutment dwellers were confirmed in ownership of the plots their homes stood on. Comparative studies rank the implementation with West Bengal's as India's most thorough, resting on an organised tenantry that could file and sustain claims. The limits are equally documented. Ceilings were blunted by exemptions, notably for plantations, and by evasive transfers, so the redistributed area fell short of the rhetoric; the plantation workforce stood largely outside the settlement; and the reform changed relations without transforming production, leaving farming in small private holdings. Its deepest achievement was social: the end of a caste-marked order of rural subordination.

### Q: What was the People's Plan Campaign?

The decentralisation programme launched by the communist-led government of 1996, which placed a substantial portion of the plan budget in the hands of elected local bodies, village panchayats and municipalities and the block and district tiers above them, and made plan-making begin in open village assemblies rather than in the state capital. Assemblies debated local needs; working groups of officials, experts, and volunteers turned the debate into costed projects; trained facilitators helped draft the documents; and committees of beneficiaries watched over the works. Political scientists including Patrick Heller count it among the most ambitious experiments in participatory planning anywhere, and its documentation by T. M. Thomas Isaac and Richard Franke carried it to an international audience. Its recorded limits, thinning participation after the first surge, uneven local capacity, and dependence on a fiscally stressed state exchequer, belong to the same record. It is graded in this article as a real achievement in participatory governance, resting on literacy and organisation that earlier causes had built.

### Q: How important are Gulf remittances to Kerala?

Major, and precisely bounded. From the 1970s, emigration to the Gulf economies brought earnings home on a scale documented over decades by the migration surveys of the Centre for Development Studies, associated with K. C. Zachariah and S. Irudaya Rajan. Remittances transformed household consumption, rebuilt the housing stock in concrete, financed private schooling and hospital care, settled debts, and sustained local trade and construction. They are the principal explanation of the state's later living standards relative to its domestic production. They do not explain the social indicators, which were distinctive before migration reached scale, nor the public institutions through which health and education were largely delivered, and the migrants themselves had been educated in the schools the earlier causes built. Remittances also imported vulnerability, since the flow depends on Gulf labour markets and oil revenues beyond any Kerala government's control, as episodes of mass return demonstrated.

### Q: What is the Kerala model crisis argument?

The argument, developed from the 1990s by development economists associated with the Centre for Development Studies and stated early in K. K. George's study of the model's limits, that the social settlement was economically unsustainable. Its strands are fiscal stress, as salaries, pensions, and subsidies met a narrow tax base and growing debt; industrial thinness and the decline of traditional industries; educated unemployment, documented in K. P. Kannan's labour research, as schooling outran job creation; questions about quality behind the access statistics in schools and health facilities; dependence on remittances rather than domestic production; and a critique of politicisation across public institutions. Defenders answer that the stresses were widely shared among Indian states, that educated unemployment is educational success's shadow, and that migration relieved what it also exposed. This article presents the literature at full strength and records what it establishes for causal purposes: the redistributive repertoire did not generate a productive economy adequate to the society it educated.

### Q: Can the Kerala model be replicated?

As a whole, no, and in its mechanisms, substantially yes. Two of its four causes are inheritances that policy cannot legislate into existence: a nineteenth-century princely commitment to funded schooling, and a century of social reform movements that organised subordinated communities into a claiming public, together with conditions such as matrilineal custom and a coastline facing the Gulf labour market. What transfers is the third cause's repertoire, adopted with its conditions stated: tenancy reform implemented through machinery claimants can use, near-universal food distribution in deficit regions, campaign methods for residual illiteracy where movements exist to partner with, decentralisation carrying real funds to assemblies of citizens able to deliberate, and distributive institutions robust enough to survive changes of government. The error to avoid is the template fallacy, which packs the mechanisms while leaving the conditions behind and then blames the destination when the sequence does not repeat.

### Q: What does Kerala prove about redistribution?

It proves that redistribution works on the dimensions it directly touches, under conditions this article specifies. Abolishing tenancy where the tenantry is organised genuinely ends landlord power in the countryside. Funding schools and clinics across generations, in a society mobilised to use them, buys health and literacy at incomes where the market alone would not. Guaranteeing staple food through public channels in a deficit region raises the floor under the poorest households. It does not prove that redistribution substitutes for production, since the settlement met fiscal and employment limits that external income had to relieve; it does not prove communist government necessary, since rival fronts maintained the settlement for half the period; and it does not prove the sequence available on demand, since its first stages are historical inheritances. The defensible lesson is a division of labour among causes, and Kerala's value to the argument is that its sequence is documented well enough to teach it.

### Q: How does the four-cause allocation grade the communist contribution?

As decisive in redistribution and completion, and derivative in origination. Communist-led governments, pressed and staffed by a movement rooted in the tenantry and the unions, abolished the tenancy order, carried food distribution and public services toward universal use, completed mass literacy through a volunteer campaign, and devolved plan funds to local assemblies. Those are achievements no other row of the table can claim, and comparative evidence within India supports their weight. The same grading refuses the movement credit for the society it inherited: the princely school and hospital network, the reform movements' claiming public, and the print culture that made its arguments audible all precede it, and the strongest objection in the literature, that the outcomes were substantially in place before 1957, is conceded on chronology. Grading by addition rather than by origination is the allocation's core discipline, and the communist row is where the discipline costs and earns the most.

### Q: What should a student separate between outcomes achieved before and under communist government?

The stock from the equalisation. Before communist government, the region had acquired the stock: schools and hospitals funded by princely states and built by missions and associations, a reform-mobilised public, high demand for girls' schooling, and indicators already diverging from the Indian pattern. Under communist-led governments and their alternating rivals, the stock was equalised and completed: tenancy abolished, distribution and services pushed toward universal use across caste and class, residual illiteracy campaigned down, and planning decentralised. A student who keeps the separation clean can answer both hostile and celebratory questions without borrowing evidence across the boundary. A student who merges them will credit 1957 with 1857's work, or erase the land reform because the schools were older, and both errors are the ones examiners on this case are trained to find.

### Q: Why does the remittance question decide so much of the argument?

Because remittances are the one cause that can be used to dissolve all the others, and the sequence is the only defence against the dissolution. If the indicators had followed the money, the model would reduce to an oil-financed purchase and the political history would be decoration. The record shows the indicators distinctive before large-scale migration, the migrants themselves produced by the public school system, and the money flowing into institutions, public and private, that earlier causes built. Remittances therefore decide the argument about the model's later decades, its consumption, housing, and private expansion, while losing the argument about its origins. Debates that treat the remittance question as settled in either direction, everything or nothing, are refusing the chronology, and the chronology is the cheapest evidence in the case: it costs nothing to check and it disposes of the two loudest claims at once.

### Q: How does the four causes allocation order the evidence?

By mechanism, period, and falsifiability. Each cause is assigned the outcomes its mechanism could have produced in the period when it operated: princely provision to the head start in supply, reform movements to the demand and organisation, communist-era policy to redistribution and completion, remittances to the consumption levels of the later decades. The table then states, for every cause, the evidence behind the assignment and the outcomes the cause cannot explain, and the article tests the result three ways: against the state's internal geography, where Travancore, Cochin, and Malabar entered with different combinations of the causes; against comparison with other states and with other remittance economies; and against counterfactual removal, in which each cause is imagined away and the deterioration traced. An allocation that survives those tests is offered as better than the single-cause stories, not as beyond argument, and the invitation to regrade it on better evidence is part of the method.

### Q: What separates a model from the conditions that produced it?

A model, properly speaking, is the transferable residue: the mechanisms that can be detached and re-established elsewhere, such as tenancy reform with usable tribunals, universal food distribution, campaign literacy, and funded decentralisation. The conditions are the untransferable remainder: the princely fiscal history, the reform century, matrilineal custom, the associational density, the migration outlet. Confusing the two produces the template fallacy, in which a state adopts Kerala's programmes without Kerala's public and is surprised by ordinary results, and its mirror image, the counsel of despair in which nothing can be learned from a case whose conditions were special. Every real case has special conditions; the analytical work is the separation. Kerala rewards the separation unusually well because its conditions are so well documented and its mechanisms so clearly dated, which is why this article treats the case as a discipline for causal argument and not only as a chapter of Indian history.
