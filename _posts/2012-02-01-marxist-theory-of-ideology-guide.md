Ask a room of educated readers what ideology means in Marxist theory and most will give the same answer with the same confidence. Ideology, they will say, is false belief: a distorted picture of society that serves the interests of a ruling class and keeps subordinate people quiet by keeping them mistaken. The answer is neat, quotable, and roughly half right, which in this subject is a dangerous proportion. It captures one thing Marx wrote in one book of his youth and misses the larger part of what he wrote across his life, including the considered statement of his mature method in which the same word carries no implication of falsity at all. Worse, the neat answer cannot explain why the literature that followed Marx is a century-long quarrel about the definition itself, why a revolutionary leader could use the word as a compliment, why a sociologist could build a whole discipline on its neutral meaning, and why the sharpest critics of the concept attack not its politics but its logic. Every one of those puzzles dissolves once a prior fact is faced squarely. Marx uses the word ideology in several senses that do not sit comfortably together, and the subsequent argument is mostly an argument about which sense to keep. Sorting the senses is therefore not a preliminary to the theory. Sorting the senses is the article, and the reader who finishes it will hold a rule that settles most disputes before they start: the sense-first rule, which holds that any argument about ideology is unresolvable until both sides state which sense of the word they are using.

![The Marxist theory of ideology explained, the four senses, ruling ideas and the camera obscura image - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-03.webp)

The stakes of the sorting exercise reach well beyond exegesis. Ideology is one of the few technical terms from nineteenth century social theory that escaped into ordinary speech, and it arrived there damaged. In everyday usage the word names whatever the speaker disagrees with: opponents have ideologies, the speaker has principles, policies, or plain common sense. Political journalism uses it as a synonym for a party programme, so that a manifesto becomes an ideology and a pragmatist becomes a politician without one. Academic usage is more careful but no more unified, because each discipline inherited a different Marx, or a different anti-Marx, and built its definition on that inheritance. A student moving between a sociology lecture, a politics seminar, and a history class can meet three incompatible definitions in a single week, each presented as the meaning of the term, and the confusion is nobody's fault. It is the predictable result of a word whose founding author never stabilised it. Marx wrote no treatise on ideology, offered no canonical definition, and used the term across four decades in ways that shift with his polemical targets and his developing theory of society. Reconstructing what he meant therefore means reconstructing several things he meant, locating each in its text, and resisting the temptation to promote one location into the whole map.

## Does ideology mean false beliefs in Marx?

### Does ideology mean false beliefs?

Not always in Marx. He uses ideology pejoratively for inverted thinking, positionally for the ideas of the ruling class regardless of truth, and neutrally in the 1859 Preface for the forms in which people become conscious of conflict. Only the first sense implies falsity.

That short answer contains the article in miniature, and each clause of it needs unpacking. The pejorative use belongs chiefly to The German Ideology, the sprawling manuscript of 1845 and 1846 that Marx and Engels wrote against their former allies among the Young Hegelians and then left unpublished, in their later phrase, to the gnawing criticism of the mice. There ideology names a specific intellectual failure: treating ideas as if they were independent powers that make history, and so producing an account of the world that is upside down, an inversion of the real relation between material life and consciousness. The positional use appears in the same manuscript, in the famous ruling ideas passage, and it asks a different question entirely. Rather than asking whether the dominant beliefs of an age are true, it asks whose beliefs they are, which class produces them, and by what institutional means they become the beliefs everyone else encounters as the obvious truth. The neutral use arrives in the Preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy in 1859, the most deliberate summary of his method Marx ever published, where ideological forms are described as the forms in which people become conscious of a conflict in the economic foundations of society and fight that conflict out. Nothing in that sentence suggests the forms are illusions. A courtroom, a parliament, a theology, and a philosophy are all, in this usage, arenas where a real struggle becomes visible to its participants, and calling them ideological describes their function without grading their truth.

A fourth position completes the set, and it is the most original of the four. In the analysis of commodity fetishism in the first volume of Capital, Marx describes appearances that are neither straightforward truths nor mere mistakes. The social character of human labour appears to producers as a property of the things they exchange, as if value were a natural quality of a coat or a quantity of corn rather than a relation between people. This appearance is not a lie told by anybody, and it is not dispelled by better information, because it is generated by the structure of the activity itself. People who know perfectly well that value is a social relation still confront prices as objective facts every time they shop. The appearance is, in a phrase the later literature developed from this passage, objectively valid for the society that produces it: false as an account of what value ultimately is, true as a description of how value unavoidably shows up. A concept of ideology that cannot accommodate this case will fail on the most sophisticated page Marx wrote about consciousness, which is why any serious treatment needs all four senses on the table before it starts judging among them.

Holding four senses apart sounds like pedantry until the practical payoff appears. Consider the question students are asked most often: is Marxism itself an ideology? Under the pejorative sense the answer must be no, almost by definition, since a doctrine that exposed inverted thinking could hardly confess to practising it, and defenders of Marxism have often answered in exactly that spirit. Under the positional sense the answer depends on which class the doctrine is said to serve, which relocates the argument from truth to allegiance. Under the neutral sense the answer is plainly yes, and harmlessly so, because every articulated worldview through which a social conflict is fought out counts as ideological, Marxism included. Three answers, all defensible, all derived from Marx, and each worthless until the questioner says which sense the question uses. That is the sense-first rule in action, and it applies with the same force to examination essays, to seminar disputes, and to the shouting matches of public debate, where the word is hurled as an accusation whose sense shifts mid-flight.

## Ideology before Marx: a science of ideas becomes an insult

The word had a career before Marx touched it, and the career explains the flavour his earliest usage carries. Ideology was coined in the closing years of the eighteenth century by Antoine Destutt de Tracy, a French philosopher working in the tradition of Locke and Condillac, who proposed it as the name for a new science: the systematic study of ideas, their origins in sensation, and their combination into judgements. The project belonged to the confident rationalism of the Enlightenment's last phase. If ideas could be traced to their sources and analysed with the rigour applied to matter by the natural sciences, then error would lose its hiding places, education could be rebuilt on evidence, and politics might be conducted as a branch of knowledge rather than a contest of prejudices. Tracy and his associates, known as the ideologues, were reformers with institutional influence in the educational settlement of revolutionary France, and for a brief period their science of ideas stood close to the centre of the new order's intellectual life.

The fall was swift and instructive. Napoleon, first as consul and then as emperor, turned against the ideologues as his rule hardened into personal power, blaming abstract theorists for the disasters of revolutionary politics and branding them with the word they had coined for their science. Ideology, in the Napoleonic usage, meant impractical metaphysics: the spinning of systems by men who understood neither war, nor administration, nor the stubbornness of real human material. The insult stuck. A term invented for the most rigorous possible study of thought entered the nineteenth century meaning its opposite, a fog of abstraction that obscured reality instead of clarifying it. Something of both ancestors survives in every later use. The scientific ambition survives wherever ideology is studied as a real structure of belief with causes and effects that can be investigated. The Napoleonic sneer survives wherever the word is used to dismiss an opponent's thinking as unworldly system-building. Marx inherited the sneer ready-made, and his earliest writings deploy it with relish against German philosophers who, in his view, had perfected the art of mistaking their own concepts for the world.

That inheritance matters for reading the texts fairly. When the young Marx calls the Young Hegelians ideologists, part of what he means is Napoleonic: these are men intoxicated by phrases, fighting battles against shadows of their own manufacture while the actual Germany of censorship, poverty, and princely particularism goes unexamined. But Marx quickly bends the inherited insult towards a theory, and the bending is his contribution. For Napoleon the ideologue's fault was a personal and professional failing, a theorist's impracticality. For Marx the inverted thinking of the ideologists has a social cause and a social function. It arises from a determinate position in the division of labour, and it serves, whether its holders intend this or not, to present the existing order as natural, eternal, or divinely sanctioned. An insult becomes an instrument of analysis, and the history of the concept from that point is the history of attempts to sharpen, soften, or discard the instrument.

The word's prehistory also warns against a common classroom error. Because Marx's pejorative sense is the one embedded in the insult he inherited, students often assume the pejorative sense is the concept itself, as if the analysis of ideology were simply the exposure of other people's fog. But an inherited insult is a starting point, not a theory, and Marx's own usage moved on within his lifetime, through the positional claim of the ruling ideas passage and into the neutral register of the 1859 Preface. Tracing that movement is the only honest way to reconstruct the concept, because a theory assembled from one period of an author's work and imposed on the rest will misread every text it touches. The four senses are not four rival theories invented by later commentators. They are four strata in one author's writing, laid down at different times for different purposes, and the later literature is best understood as a set of decisions about which stratum to build on.

## The first sense: inverted thought and the primacy of life over consciousness

The German Ideology, written with Engels in 1845 and 1846, opens the pejorative account with a general claim about the relation between life and thought. The premises of the inquiry, its authors announce, are real individuals, their activity, and the material conditions under which they live. People produce their means of subsistence, and in producing them they produce their social relations, their institutions, and eventually their ideas about all of these. Consciousness, on this account, is from the beginning a social product, interwoven with the material activity of associated individuals, and it can never be anything but the conscious reflection of actual life processes. Language itself is treated as practical consciousness, arising from the need for intercourse with others. The philosophical target is the German tradition descending from Hegel, in which the movement of concepts threatened to become the real subject of history and actual people its instruments. Against that tradition the manuscript insists on a reversal of explanatory direction. It is not consciousness that determines life, but life that determines consciousness, and any inquiry that begins with what people say, imagine, and conceive will end by studying phantoms unless it anchors those representations in the production process that sustains their authors.

Inversion is the manuscript's central image for what goes wrong when the anchor is dropped. In ideology, the authors write, men and their circumstances appear upside down, as in a camera obscura, and they add the crucial clause that this appearance arises from the historical life process just as the inversion of objects on the retina arises from their physical life process. The comparison deserves slow attention, because it is more precise than its later sloganised versions suggest. A retinal image is not a hallucination; the eye genuinely receives the world inverted, and the inversion is a lawful product of optics rather than a failure of the organ. Likewise the camera obscura, the darkened chamber in which a scene outside is projected upside down on a screen through a small aperture, produces a real image that is really inverted. Ideological inversion, by analogy, is not random error or personal stupidity. It is a systematic and explicable reversal: thoughts, which are in fact products of material life, appear as its producers; laws, states, religions, and philosophies, which arise from definite relations among people, appear as independent powers standing over those relations and ruling them. The ideologist is the person who takes the projected image for the scene itself, and then writes histories of the projection.

### What does the camera obscura actually show?

The camera obscura image shows a real scene rendered upside down by the apparatus that projects it, and Marx uses the device to argue that ideological inversion is systematic rather than stupid. Ideas produced by material life appear to rule that life, just as the projected image seems to stand on its own.

Because the inversion is lawful, the pejorative sense carries an implication that softer accounts of false belief miss. Correcting an ideology, on this model, is not like correcting an arithmetical slip, where pointing at the error suffices and the corrected person simply proceeds. The conditions that generate the inversion continue to operate, so the inversion regenerates. A philosophy that treats the state as the embodiment of reason will be produced, in one costume or another, as long as intellectual work is organised as a separate profession reflecting on society from a position insulated from production. This is why the manuscript's polemic is so impatient with purely intellectual refutation. Its authors mock the Young Hegelian belief that fighting phrases with phrases liberates anybody, and the mockery contains a methodological rule that later Marxist criticism repeatedly rediscovered: an idea's falsity, where it is false, is explained by the arrangement that keeps producing it, and criticism that stops at the idea has stopped one step before the explanation. The rule also sets a trap for the critic, since a theory of why other people's thought inverts reality invites the question of what exempts the critic's own thought, a question this article takes up in full when it reaches the reflexivity objection.

The German Ideology remained unpublished in Marx's lifetime, a fact with large consequences for the concept's history. The pejorative account, the camera obscura, and the ruling ideas passage all entered circulation only when the manuscript was finally published in the twentieth century, decades after the neutral usage of the 1859 Preface and the fetishism analysis of Capital had already shaped the tradition. Generations of Marxists therefore formed their understanding of ideology from the later, less pejorative texts and from the polemics of the movement, and the recovery of the early manuscript rearranged the concept's centre of gravity retrospectively. Debates that look, on the surface, like disagreements about the world are often disagreements about which textual stratum counts as canonical, and the sense-first rule disciplines those debates too, by forcing each party to name its stratum.

One more feature of the pejorative sense needs emphasis because it corrects the most durable of all the misreadings. Ideology in The German Ideology is not primarily a theory of lying. The ideologists are not con men who fabricate doctrines they privately disbelieve in order to gull the masses. They are sincere. The philosopher who finds the state rational, the economist who finds the market natural, and the theologian who finds the hierarchy of heaven reflected in the hierarchy of earth genuinely take themselves to be describing the world, and their sincerity is part of the mechanism. A doctrine maintained by cynics would collapse the moment the cynics were exposed; a doctrine maintained by believers reproduces itself through every school, pulpit, and editorial office that staffs itself with the convinced. Marx's point about inversion is a point about how a social position can organise perception so reliably that honesty is no protection against error. That is a far more unsettling claim than the accusation of propaganda, and it is the claim that gives the pejorative sense its enduring sting.

## The second sense: ruling ideas and the question of whose thought prevails

The most quoted sentence in the whole literature on ideology stands in the same manuscript as the camera obscura, yet it advances a different kind of claim. "The ideas of the ruling class are in every epoch the ruling ideas" is not, on its face, a statement about truth or falsity. It is a statement about distribution and power. One class, the authors argue, is the ruling material force of society and at the same time its ruling intellectual force, because the class that controls the means of material production controls, in consequence, the means of mental production: the presses, the pulpits, the universities, the publishing houses, the patronage networks that decide which manuscripts become books and which preachers get parishes. Ideas produced outside that circuit exist, but they lack the machinery of amplification. The dominant ideas of an age are therefore the ideas of its dominant class, not because subordinate people are empty vessels, but because the production and distribution of ideas is an industry with owners, and the owners are the same people who own the other industries.

Notice what this positional sense does and does not assert. It does not assert that ruling ideas are false. Some of them may be perfectly accurate: a ruling class that prizes literacy, punctuality, and the accurate keeping of accounts spreads beliefs that are both convenient to its rule and largely true. It does not assert that ruling ideas are invented by the ruling class out of nothing. Doctrines are typically worked up by professional thinkers, clerics, jurists, and professors, whose relation to the dominant class is one of employment, patronage, and shared formation rather than puppetry. It does not even assert that subordinate classes passively absorb the ruling ideas whole, a stronger thesis that later writers extracted from the passage and that the passage itself, read carefully, does not contain. What it asserts is an asymmetry of production: the ideas that saturate an epoch's institutions, that present themselves as the horizon of the thinkable, will bear the stamp of the class whose resources sustain the institutions. The stamp shows most clearly in what the dominant ideas treat as universal. Each ascending class, the manuscript argues, is compelled to present its own interest as the common interest of all, to dress its particular claims in the costume of reason, nature, freedom, or mankind itself. The costume is the ideological element in this second sense: not falsity exactly, but a systematic misrepresentation of scope, in which the partial passes itself off as the whole.

### What does the ruling ideas passage actually claim?

The passage claims that the class controlling material production also controls the production and distribution of ideas, so the beliefs that dominate an epoch express the outlook of its dominant class. The claim concerns whose ideas prevail and how they spread, and it leaves the truth of those ideas open.

Universalising is a mechanism worth dwelling on, because it recurs at every level of the theory and gives students a transferable analytical tool. A particular interest, honestly avowed, recruits only its owners. The mill owners who want a longer working day gain little by announcing that longer days enrich mill owners. The same demand, translated into the language of national prosperity, the discipline of labour, or the moral dangers of idleness, recruits allies who will never see a mill dividend. Translation into universal form is therefore not decorative hypocrisy laid over a real motive; it is a functional requirement of class rule in any society where rule needs consent, allies, and laws passed by assemblies. Marx and Engels apply the observation historically: the bourgeoisie of the revolutionary era spoke of freedom and equality as such, not of freedom for property owners and equality before a law that protected accumulation, and the universality was both a real historical force, since serfs and guildsmen could take the words seriously and turn them against their authors, and an ideological form, since the words as institutionalised quietly excluded those without property from their full enjoyment. The double character of universal claims, at once emancipatory and veiling, is one of the tradition's most fruitful insights, and it belongs entirely to the positional sense. A belief can be ruling, universal in costume, and substantially true in content, all at the same time, and only a sense-confused analysis will try to convict it of simple falsehood.

### Does an idea have to be false to serve a ruling class?

No, and the distinction matters for every application of the concept. An idea serves a ruling class by fitting its position and spreading through its institutions, and fitness and falsity are different properties. Doctrines of order, property, and improvement can be largely accurate about the world while still presenting one class's arrangement as everyone's natural horizon.

The positional sense also locates ideology in a different place from the pejorative sense. The camera obscura story is about the structure of individual and professional consciousness, about what happens inside the heads of thinkers who are cut off from material practice. The ruling ideas story is about an infrastructure: buildings, budgets, licensing, hiring, and the thousand small decisions by which some doctrines get printed in every parish and others get whispered in kitchens. Later theorists who built on the second sense, above all those who studied institutions and common sense, were developing a sociology of intellectual production rather than a psychology of error. This difference of location answers, in advance, one of the questions the whole tradition keeps asking: whether ideology lives in people's heads or in the practices and institutions that surround them. Marx's texts supply material for both answers because he worked both seams, the seam of consciousness in the early polemic and the seam of distribution in the ruling ideas analysis. A reader equipped with the four senses table later in this article can place any particular claim by asking which seam it works.

## The division of labour behind the illusions

Between the camera obscura and the ruling ideas passage, The German Ideology inserts an explanatory mechanism that binds the two senses together: the division of material and mental labour. So long as production and reflection are carried on by the same people, ideas remain tethered to practice by the daily corrections of physical work; the farmer's theology is disciplined by the weather in a way the professional theologian's is not. Once a society supports a stratum whose entire occupation is the production of ideas, priests first, then jurists, philosophers, and state officials, consciousness gains the practical possibility of emancipating itself from the world in appearance. The professional thinker can now construct systems whose only material is other systems, and the labour of systematising generates its own criteria of success: elegance, completeness, fidelity to a school. Ideology in the pejorative sense is, on this account, the occupational product of unanchored mental labour, and the ruling ideas are the same stratum's product selected, funded, and distributed by the dominant class. One mechanism thus generates both the inversion and the distribution, which is why the manuscript treats priests as the first ideologists and why it regards the professionalisation of thought as a material fact with intellectual consequences rather than a fall from some earlier honesty.

The mechanism retains a bracing plausibility outside its original century. A commentator class paid to produce opinions on every subject by the hour will produce opinions whose confidence exceeds their evidence, not because its members are dishonest, but because the division of labour rewards fluency over verification and punishes the admission of ignorance with silence on the screen or the page. A university discipline rewarded for novelty within a closed citation circuit can elaborate distinctions of vanishing empirical weight with complete professional sincerity. An economics faculty financed through chairs endowed by fortunes made in finance need reach no corrupt conclusions for its shared sense of which questions are serious to drift towards the concerns of its benefactors. In each case the Marxist explanation directs attention away from the beliefs and towards the labour process that manufactures them, and in each case the explanation works in the positional register even where no falsity has been demonstrated. The division of labour account is the bridge between the senses, and writers who miss it typically end up choosing between a crude theory of deception, which the texts do not support, and a bloodless theory of worldviews, which loses the critical edge that made the concept worth having.

There is a historical corollary that the manuscript draws and that students should be able to reproduce. Because the stratum of professional thinkers attaches itself to whichever class is ascending, supplying each new order with its universal costume, the history of dominant ideas tracks the succession of dominant classes, and apparent revolutions in thought, the passage from theology to metaphysics to positive science, for instance, register changes in the social foundations of intellectual production rather than an autonomous progress of the spirit. This is the base and superstructure relationship stated from the side of the producers of ideas, and the fuller structural account is developed in the companion guide to [base and superstructure explained](/2010/02/01/base-and-superstructure-explained/), which sets out how legal, political, and intellectual forms correspond to the relations of production without being mechanically dictated by them. The ideology manuscript adds what the structural formula alone cannot: the names of the occupations, the routes of patronage, and the professional deformations through which correspondence is actually achieved.

## The third sense: the neutral forms in which conflict becomes conscious

Thirteen years after the unpublished manuscript, Marx published the short Preface that generations of readers have treated as his methodological testament, and the word ideology appears in it doing a noticeably different job. The Preface distinguishes the material transformation of the economic conditions of production, which can be studied with the precision of natural science, from the legal, political, religious, artistic, and philosophical forms in which people become conscious of the conflict underlying that transformation and fight it out. Those forms are the ideological forms. Nothing in the sentence brands them as illusions. A legal doctrine through which a property dispute is argued, a political programme through which a taxation struggle is conducted, a theology through which a peasant war understands itself: these are media of consciousness, the available languages in which a society's members can perceive a conflict that originates elsewhere. Some of those languages illuminate, some distort, most do both at once, and the Preface's point is classificatory rather than accusatory. It assigns the forms a place in the architecture of society, between the economic foundation they express and the consciousness of the individuals who think within them.

The neutrality of this usage is not a lapse or a courtesy. It follows from the architecture itself. If every society's conflicts must be fought in some medium of ideas, and the medium is supplied by the inherited culture rather than chosen by the combatants, then ideological forms are as unavoidable as language, and as little to be condemned as such. Condemnation, where it belongs, attaches to particular contents: to a doctrine that misdescribes the conflict it mediates, or to the claim of a form to stand above the conflict entirely, as pure reason or eternal law. Marx's own practice respects the distinction his Preface draws. His political writings analyse rival doctrines, Bonapartist rhetoric, republican constitutionalism, the slogans of 1848, as serious forces to be understood in their social roots, not as mere screens to be torn away in a sentence. The analyst of ideology in this third sense resembles a linguist of social conflict more than a detective of lies, mapping the grammars within which interests become arguments and arguments become movements.

### Why does the neutral sense change how the other senses read?

The neutral sense changes the reading because it shows Marx classifying all the forms in which social conflict is understood, his opponents' and his own tradition's alike, under one heading. Ideology there names a level of social life, like law or art, and levels cannot be false in themselves, though particular occupants of them can be.

Later writers seized on the neutral sense precisely because it permits a comparative sociology of belief. If ideology names the medium rather than the error, then doctrines can be compared by their social origins, their internal organisation, and their effects, without the analysis collapsing into a verdict on their truth, a verdict the analyst may be incompetent to deliver in fields far from home. The political science usage that treats ideology as a structured programme of beliefs, a map of the good society and the means towards it, descends from this stratum of Marx more than from the pejorative one, however little its users may acknowledge the ancestry. So does the usage, common in anthropology and cultural studies, in which an ideology is a people's working model of their world, to be described with the same respect and the same scepticism as any other cultural artefact. Each descendant kept the neutrality and dropped the architecture, retaining the word for the medium while forgetting the economic foundation the medium was said to express. Whether that amputation leaves a usable concept or a husk is one of the standing arguments of the literature, and it cannot even be stated until the senses are separated.

The neutral sense also reframes the question of Marxism's own status in a way the pejorative sense cannot. If ideological forms are the necessary media of conflict, then a theory of society that becomes the self-understanding of a movement is functioning ideologically in the third sense whatever its truth value, and there is no scandal in saying so. The scandal would be a theory that claimed to operate without any medium at all, to see society from nowhere. Some of Marx's defenders have embraced exactly this conclusion, treating the third sense as the mature one and the pejorative sense as a youthful weapon outgrown; others insist the critical bite of the concept is lost if it cannot convict. The quarrel is genuine and continuing, and it is, once again, a quarrel about which sense a whole tradition should adopt, dressed up as a quarrel about the facts. The companion treatment of [false consciousness explained](/2012/02/15/false-consciousness-explained/) takes up the neighbouring term that the pejorative sense left behind, and shows why it, too, means less than the polemics built on it suggest.

## The fourth sense: appearances the structure itself generates

The analysis of commodity fetishism in the first chapter of Capital introduces a phenomenon that none of the earlier senses quite covers, and careful readers have treated it as a fourth position in Marx's thinking about consciousness. Producers who work independently and meet only through exchange find that the social character of their labour confronts them as a property of things. A table, once it steps forth as a commodity, is described as transcending sensuousness, standing on its head and evolving grotesque ideas out of its wooden brain; the wit is at the expense of anyone who thinks the mystery lies in the object rather than in the social form. Value appears as a natural attribute of products, prices appear as magnitudes things possess, and money appears as a thing with powers rather than as the crystallised form of a relation among producers. These appearances are not imposed by any doctrine, church, or press. Nobody teaches them. They are generated afresh, every day, by the practice of exchange itself, and they persist in the minds of people who have read the explanation and accepted it, because the practice that generates them persists too.

The consequences for the concept of ideology are profound, and they cut against both crude versions of the earlier senses. Against the crude pejorative version, fetishism shows an appearance can be unavoidable without being anybody's intellectual failing: the most lucid economist still experiences the price of bread as a fact about bread when standing in a shop, because within the act of exchange the appearance is the operative reality, the only form in which the underlying relation can be transacted. Against the crude positional version, fetishism shows that the most important veil over capitalist relations is not woven by a ruling class at all. It is woven by the relations themselves, impersonally, and it veils the position of the rulers as thoroughly as the ruled: the capitalist who experiences profit as the natural fruit of his capital's productivity is as enclosed in the appearance as the worker who experiences wages as payment for labour rather than for labour power. A theory of ideology adequate to Capital must therefore include structurally generated appearance as a category distinct from both error and ruling class doctrine. The full exposition of this analysis, with its religious analogies and its distinction between the value form and its contents, is given in the guide to [commodity fetishism explained](/2010/08/01/commodity-fetishism-explained/), and readers who master that argument hold the key to the most defensible modern versions of the ideology concept.

Fetishism also supplies the strongest available reply to a familiar sneer: that calling other people's beliefs ideological is a lazy substitute for engaging with them. Whatever may be true of sloganised uses, the fetishism analysis is the opposite of lazy. It does not dismiss the appearance as stupid. It reconstructs, step by step, the social process that makes the appearance necessary, shows exactly which real relation the appearance both expresses and conceals, and predicts that the appearance will survive its own explanation, a prediction the reader can test in any marketplace. Criticism of this kind cannot be answered by pointing out that the critic holds beliefs too, because the analysis is not a claim about the critic's exemption. It is a claim about a mechanism, offered to anyone, including mechanisms that may govern the critic's own perceptions in other domains. Whether all ideological analysis rises to this standard is another matter; the standard itself, set in the fourth sense, is the tradition's best defence of its legitimacy.

A further distinction follows, between appearances that conceal and appearances that merely refract. Not every socially generated form distorts in the same direction or to the same degree. Legal equality before the law, for instance, is in Marx's treatment a real achievement of bourgeois society and a real veil at once: the equality is genuine within the courtroom and systematically misleading as a portrait of the society whose members enter the courtroom with wildly unequal resources. The form tells the truth about one level and lies about the whole. Analyses that grade every appearance as simply false flatten these discriminations and end up unable to explain why some ideological forms command the sincere allegiance of intelligent people across classes. The forms endure because they are anchored in real practices and partial truths. Pure fabrications are brittle; nobody needs a theory to explain why a lie nobody believes fails. The durability of the forms that matter is itself evidence for the fourth sense, since only appearances rooted in the structure of daily activity possess that kind of strength.

The four senses can now be laid side by side. | Ruling ideas | The German Ideology | The dominant class's ideas, amplified by its control of mental production | Not necessarily | Gramsci and the sociology of intellectuals | Dominant ideology |
| Medium of struggle | Preface of 1859 | The forms in which people become conscious of conflict and fight it out | No | Mannheim and political science | Ideology as worldview or programme |
| Structural appearance | Capital, fetishism chapter | Appearances generated by the form of the relations themselves | Partly: true of the surface, false of the whole | Althusser and value-form theory | Ideology as lived relation |

Read down the falsity column and the whole problem of the concept stands revealed in four cells. A word that can mean a doctrine whose defining feature is falsity and, two rows later, a medium whose defining feature is neutrality cannot be used responsibly without a qualifier, and most public quarrels about ideology are collisions between rows. The critic who calls a worldview ideological in the first row's sense has accused it of inversion; the defender who replies in the third row's sense, protesting that every movement has its forms of consciousness, has answered an accusation that, in the form answered, was never made. Both may be right about their own row and the exchange still settles nothing. Examination candidates lose marks in exactly this way, sliding between rows inside a single answer, first convicting a doctrine of falsity and then excusing it as a necessary form, without noticing the slide. The table's practical use is as a brake: it forces the slide into visibility, where it can be either justified as a deliberate movement between senses or corrected as a confusion.

The table also explains the concept's strange career in the century after Marx. Each major theorist of ideology can be located as the champion of one row against the others. The critical tradition that runs through Lukács radicalised the first row, making the exposure of inverted, thing-like consciousness the centre of the theory. Gramsci, writing in a fascist prison about why the working class of the advanced countries consented to its rulers, rebuilt the second row into the theory of hegemony, in which ruling ideas rule not by saturation but by organising a lived common sense, always incomplete and always contested. Mannheim, a sociologist outside the Marxist movement, generalised the third row into a sociology of knowledge in which every standpoint, including the analyst's, is ideologically situated. Althusser, returning to the fourth row, argued that ideology is a material practice embedded in institutions and rituals, representing the imaginary relationship of individuals to their real conditions, and in that sense eternal, coextensive with social life itself rather than a curable disease of it. Four inheritances, four Marxes, and a single word stretched across all of them: the table is the map of why the secondary literature reads like an argument among strangers.

## Sorting the inheritance: typologies of the concept

Faced with this plurality, scholars of the concept have repeatedly tried to sort it, and two sortings have become standard equipment. Raymond Geuss, in a philosophical study of the critical theory tradition published in 1981, distinguished three families of conception. In the descriptive sense, ideology is simply the belief system characteristic of a group, its concepts, attitudes, and preferences, with no evaluation implied. In the pejorative sense, ideology is belief that is false or distorted in ways explained by the interests it serves, and this is the sense critical theory needs for its emancipatory work. In the positive sense, ideology is a system of beliefs a class or movement needs in order to act, valued precisely because it mobilises, as when a revolutionary movement speaks approvingly of its own ideology. Geuss's sorting maps closely onto the four senses table, with his descriptive and positive families dividing the work of the neutral row between them, and his pejorative family covering the illusion row and part of the ruling ideas row. The correspondence is reassuring: independent routes through the same texts converge on the same plurality, which suggests the plurality is in the material rather than in the commentators.

Terry Eagleton, opening a well known survey published in 1991, performed the complementary exercise of counting. His first chapter assembles sixteen definitions of ideology in circulation, ranging from the process of producing meanings and values in social life, through the body of ideas characteristic of a group, to false ideas that legitimate a dominant power and ideas defined simply as whatever the speaker's opponents believe. The list is deliberately exasperating. Its point is that the definitions are not all compatible, that some contradict others outright, an ideology in one definition being the cure for ideology in another, and that no single formulation can be rescued by calling the others loose usage. Eagleton's own preference, developed across the book, leans towards the pejorative family disciplined by the structural insight: ideology as the ways in which meaning serves to establish and sustain relations of domination, with the service, rather than falsity alone, doing the critical work. That refinement matters. A true statement can serve domination, as when an accurate statistic about productivity is deployed to naturalise a distribution of its proceeds, and a false statement can serve nobody at all. Locating the critical charge in the function performed, rather than in the truth value carried, is the most influential repair the concept received in the late twentieth century, and it presupposes exactly the sense-sorting this article performs.

### How many definitions of the word are in circulation?

Eagleton's survey counts sixteen definitions in active use, and the number is less a census than a symptom. Definitions multiply because each discipline and each political tradition selected the stratum of Marx's usage that suited its purposes, then forgot the selection had been made and mistook its part for the whole.

The typologies converge on a lesson about method. Definitions of ideology are not innocent preliminaries. Each one smuggles in a theory: of what beliefs are, of how they relate to interests, of whether the analyst stands inside or outside the field analysed. Choosing the pejorative sense commits the analyst to possessing, at least locally, a non-ideological standpoint from which inversion can be detected, and the history of the concept is littered with the wreckage of that commitment. Choosing the purely descriptive sense abandons the critical question the word was sharpened to ask, namely why the meanings that circulate are these meanings, produced by these institutions, serving these arrangements. The honest options are therefore two: adopt the pejorative sense and pay for it with a worked account of the analyst's own position, or adopt a functional sense and pay for it by specifying the mechanisms through which meaning serves power. The sense-first rule does not choose between these payments. It insists only that the bill be acknowledged before the argument begins, which in practice dissolves a surprising share of the disputes it is applied to.

## What later Marxists did with the word

The tradition's internal history reads, once the senses are separated, as a series of wagers on different rows of the table. Lenin's wager was the boldest, because he flipped the word's valence. In the pamphlet What Is to Be Done?, published in 1902, the question posed to socialists is framed as a choice between bourgeois ideology and socialist ideology, with the middle excluded on principle: since there can be no non-class ideology in a class society, any weakening of socialist ideology means, by that measure, a strengthening of bourgeois ideology. The pejorative shiver is gone. Ideology here is the organised self-consciousness of a class, and the only question is whose. This is Geuss's positive sense operating at full strength inside the movement, and it explains a usage that baffles students who arrive from the pejorative texts: party schools that taught ideology as a subject to be proud of, publishing houses that issued primers in it, and cadres who described their own formation as ideological training without a trace of embarrassment. Judged by the sense-first rule, none of this contradicts Marx, because the Preface's neutral sense licenses it. Lenin's innovation was to draw the conclusion the neutral sense always contained: if every class fights its conflicts in ideological forms, a revolutionary class needs its forms built deliberately rather than inherited by accident.

Karl Mannheim drew the opposite conclusion from the same neutral root, and the confrontation between the two inheritances defined the concept's academic career. In Ideology and Utopia, published in 1929, Mannheim argued that the concept had undergone an internal expansion: from the particular conception, in which an opponent's specific claims are distrusted as disguises of interest, to the total conception, in which an entire age's or class's structure of thought is understood as a function of its social position. Once the total conception exists, it cannot be monopolised. The Marxist who explains bourgeois thought by bourgeois position must expect the same explanation applied to Marxist thought, and Mannheim embraced the consequence, proposing a general sociology of knowledge in which all standpoints are situated and the hope of truth rests not on exemption but on the dynamic synthesis of perspectives, carried, he suggested, by a relatively unattached stratum of intellectuals. The proposal has been attacked from every side, as evasive, as self-refuting, as a liberal evasion of the critical point, yet the problem it answers is real and permanent, and the reflexivity discussion below returns to it. For the sense-sorting purpose, Mannheim matters because he demonstrated that the neutral sense, pursued consistently, transforms ideology from a weapon into a condition, and a weapon everyone must drop or share.

Gramsci's reconstruction of the second sense proceeded from a political puzzle rather than a philosophical one. The puzzle was the resilience of the existing order in the advanced capitalist countries, where economic crisis had not produced the revolutionary collapse that cruder versions of the theory predicted. His answer, developed in the prison notebooks, relocated ruling ideas from the saturation model implied by a hasty reading of the ruling ideas passage to a model of organised consent. Hegemony, in his vocabulary, is the leadership a class exercises through the institutions of civil society, schools, churches, newspapers, associations, and the professions, weaving its outlook into the common sense of the population as a set of lived, fragmentary, half-articulated certainties rather than a doctrine anyone could recite. Because common sense is contradictory and incomplete, hegemony is never finished; it must be maintained by continuous work, and it can be contested by a counter-hegemony built with the same patience. The account explains phenomena the saturation model cannot, above all why subordinate classes can despise their rulers' explicit doctrines while continuing to inhabit their categories, and the complete treatment of this alternative is given in the guide to [Gramsci's hegemony](/2015/01/15/gramsci-hegemony-complete-guide/), which this article's second row points towards throughout.

Althusser's intervention of 1970, the essay on ideological state apparatuses, pushed the fourth sense towards a general theory. His theses can be stated compactly. Ideology represents the imaginary relationship of individuals to their real conditions of existence, where imaginary marks not falsity but the lived, felt quality of the relation, as one lives one's relation to the law or the wage before one theories it. Ideology has a material existence, inscribed in apparatuses and their practices: the school, the church, the family, the media, and the rituals, kneeling, signing, queuing, saluting, through which beliefs are enacted before they are professed. And ideology interpellates individuals as subjects, hailing them into identities they recognise as their own, so that the category of the free subject is itself an ideological product rather than a starting point. The essay's most scandalous implication, drawn out by its author, is that ideology in this sense has no outside and no history of the ordinary kind: a society without lived relations to its conditions is not a society at all. Critics objected that a concept stretched to cover every lived relation loses the power to discriminate, and that the apparatuses essay leaves no room for the class struggle it repeatedly invokes. Both objections have force, and both are, in the vocabulary of this article, row disputes: Althusser universalised the fourth row, and his critics were defending the critical privileges of the first. The companion essay on [Althusser and the ideological state apparatuses](/2012/03/01/althusser-ideology-state-apparatuses/) works through the apparatuses one by one and gives the objections their full weight.

### Where does ideology live, in minds or in everyday practices?

In Marx's texts it lives in both, distributed across the senses. The illusion sense analyses the structure of consciousness in professional thinkers; the ruling ideas sense analyses an infrastructure of production and distribution; Althusser's development locates ideology in rituals and institutions, arguing that beliefs are enacted in practices before they are thought.

The pattern across all four inheritances deserves one explicit statement, because it is the pattern the examination system rewards and the pattern public argument obscures. No major theorist in the tradition simply applied Marx's concept. Each selected a sense, generalised it, and paid the price the selection exacted: Lenin paid with the critical edge, Mannheim with exemption, Gramsci with the saturation claim, Althusser with discrimination. Knowing the prices is what separates a reader who can use the literature from a reader who merely collects its slogans. It also explains why anthologies of ideology theory feel so quarrelsome: they are records of four different research programmes sharing a single inherited word, and the sense-first rule is the only reliable concordance among them.

## The dominant ideology thesis and its evidence problem

One descendant of the ruling ideas passage achieved a textbook life of its own and must be handled separately, because it is frequently attributed to Marx and is not his. The dominant ideology thesis, as its critics christened it, holds that in capitalist societies a more or less unified dominant ideology, propagated through education, media, and religion, secures the consent of subordinate classes by inducing them to accept the existing order as natural and just. In its strong form the thesis makes three claims: that a coherent dominant ideology exists, that it is disseminated effectively to the subordinate population, and that this dissemination explains the stability of the order, the absence of revolutionary consciousness among those who would benefit from revolution. Each claim is empirical, and each has been examined.

The examination was not kind to the strong form. Nicholas Abercrombie, Stephen Hill, and Bryan Turner, in a study published in 1980 devoted to the thesis, argued that in feudal and early capitalist societies the dominant ideology bound the dominant class itself more thoroughly than it penetrated below, serving to integrate rulers and legitimate them to one another, while subordinate classes were held in place chiefly by economic compulsion, the dull pressure of needing wages, rather than by conviction. Their own historical materials showed peasants and workers maintaining robust, sceptical, and often oppositional cultures alongside the official doctrines, aware of the gap between the preached order and the lived one. Ethnographic studies of factory and mining communities repeatedly found the same doubleness: public conformity combined with private irony, a fluent ability to repeat the official account of things and an equally fluent backstage commentary on it. None of this refutes the positional sense of ideology, which never claimed saturation. It refutes a specific strong descendant, and the distinction illustrates the practical cash value of the senses: an empirical finding can demolish the dominant ideology thesis while leaving the ruling ideas passage, with its modest claim about asymmetries of production, essentially intact.

The weaker descendants survive in better health. That the institutions of mental production are unequally resourced, that their output skews towards the outlooks of those who fund and staff them, that subordinate groups must build their counter-publics with inferior means and against the grain of the common sense those institutions maintain: these claims accommodate the ethnographic evidence, since none of them requires anyone to be fooled. Gramsci's version, with hegemony as incomplete work rather than achieved saturation, was constructed precisely to survive in this environment, and its survival is a lesson in theory choice. A theory of ideology that predicts total penetration is falsified by every ironic joke in every factory canteen. A theory that predicts asymmetry, friction, and partial uptake is confirmed by the same jokes, which exist because the penetration is partial and are necessary because it is real.

## Ideology, propaganda, error and the lie

Precision about neighbours protects the concept from the inflation that has repeatedly ruined it. Four different failures of belief circulate in these discussions, and they have different structures, different remedies, and different moral weights. A simple error is a false belief held for ordinary reasons, a misread map or a misremembered date, corrected by information and carrying no social explanation. A lie is a statement known to be false by its maker, intended to deceive, and corrected by exposure of the maker. Propaganda is communication designed to persuade regardless of truth, typically organised and funded, and it may traffic in truths selected for effect as readily as in fabrications; its analysis concerns intention and organisation, not the truth values of its contents taken one by one. Ideology, in any of Marx's senses, differs from all three. It need not be false, as the neutral and positional senses establish. It need not be intended by anybody, as the fetishism sense establishes. And it is never corrected by information or exposure alone, because its roots are positional and structural rather than informational. The diagnostic question that separates ideology from its neighbours is therefore genetic: not is this belief false, and not who benefits from it, though benefit is a clue, but what arrangement of production, profession, and institution keeps generating this belief in this form among these people. A belief with that kind of explanation is an ideological phenomenon. A belief without it is an error, a lie, or a campaign, and calling it ideology adds fog rather than light.

### Is ideology a deception practised on the public?

Not in Marx's account, and the sincerity of its producers is central to the mechanism. The professional thinkers who elaborate ideologies believe their systems; the audiences who inhabit ideological forms find them livable rather than imposed; and the appearances generated by exchange deceive nobody deliberately, since they arise from practice itself.

The deception model fails empirically as well as textually. It predicts that ideologies collapse when their fabricators are unmasked, and the historical record shows doctrines surviving the unmasking of their authors with ease, because the audiences were never depending on the authors' honesty. It predicts cynical producers, and the record shows believers: the priests who feared the doctrines they preached, the economists whose fortunes were ruined by the very market naturalness they taught, the officials who administered systems whose justifications they could recite in their sleep and defended at cost to themselves. Sincerity on this scale is not a puzzle to be explained away but the central datum the theory exists to explain, and the positional answer explains it economically: people formed by an institution, rewarded by it, and surrounded by its products will find its outlook to be the shape of the world, with no decision to deceive ever taken or needed. The lie is the exceptional case in the economy of belief, not the typical one, and a criticism that treats every opponent as a liar has abandoned analysis for abuse.

A corollary follows for the ethics of using the word. To call a belief ideological, in the disciplined senses, is to offer an explanation of its persistence, not to convict its holder of dishonesty or stupidity. The explanation may still be wrong, and it must compete with other explanations, including the possibility that the belief persists because it is true and its holders have noticed. That possibility is the one ideologically intoxicated criticism most often forgets, and forgetting it converts a research programme into a reflex. The method article that follows this one in the series, on [the method of ideology critique](/2012/03/15/ideology-critique-method/), sets out the procedural safeguards: state the belief fairly, establish its falsity or partiality independently of its origins, and only then offer the genetic explanation, in that order, since to reverse the order is to commit the genetic fallacy with a German vocabulary.

## The strongest objection: explaining disagreement away

Every serious account of this theory owes its readers the strongest available objection, stated as its holders would state it, and the strongest objection to ideology theory is not political but logical. It runs as follows. A theory that explains beliefs by the social position of their holders possesses a universal solvent for disagreement. Whenever an opponent argues back, the theorist need not answer the argument; the theorist diagnoses it. The economist who defends the market is not a reasoner with premises to be examined but a voice of the bourgeoisie, and his arguments are not refuted but located. The historian who disputes a Marxist reading of a revolution is exhibiting the consciousness of his stratum. The worker who rejects the doctrine is in the grip of false consciousness, a condition the theory itself defines and from which, conveniently, agreement with the theory is the only exit. A method that can absorb every counterexample as further evidence of the phenomenon it studies has made itself unfalsifiable in practice, however falsifiable its individual claims may be on paper. It has also, the objection continues, abandoned the ethic of argument. To answer a person is to honour the possibility that they are right; to explain a person is to decide in advance that they cannot be. Ideology critique, on this charge, is not a contribution to the conversation about society but a technique for declining to have it, dressed in the vocabulary of depth.

The objection gains strength from the theory's own political history, which must be faced without flinching. Where Marxist parties held state power, the diagnosis of ideological deviation became an administrative instrument. Disagreement with a policy could be classified as the expression of a class-alien standpoint, and the classification ended arguments that evidence might have lost. Doctrines were revised by decree, scientists and artists were instructed in the standpoint their positions allegedly required, and the genetic explanation of belief, offered in the seminar as a tool for understanding priests and professors, operated in the ministry as a tool for silencing engineers and poets. Defenders of the theory reply, correctly, that a concept's abuse by states claiming its authority is a record about those states, and the distinction between a theory and the record of regimes invoking it is one this series maintains throughout. The reply is sound and insufficient. A method whose characteristic move is the relocation of disagreement from the plane of reasons to the plane of positions carries a standing temptation towards that administrative use, and a mature statement of the theory should name the temptation as part of the theory's risk profile rather than treating every abuse as an inexplicable betrayal. Tools are judged partly by what they invite in careless hands, and ideology critique invites, in careless hands, exactly the substitution of diagnosis for answer that its critics describe.

The objection also has a subtler philosophical layer that survives every appeal to good practice. Even the careful critic, who states the opponent's argument fairly and refutes it before offering a genetic explanation, relies on an asymmetry: the opponent's beliefs get explained by position, while the critic's beliefs get explained by their truth. Yet the critic has a position too, an institution, a formation, a salary, a readership, and the theory's own principles predict that these shape the critic's perception as surely as any priest's was shaped. If the shaping is universal, the critic's confidence that this particular explanation is the true one is itself a datum awaiting explanation, and the regress has no natural stopping point. If the shaping is not universal, if some positions permit unshaped perception, then the theory owes an account of which positions those are and how the critic knows that this one qualifies, an account that cannot itself be certified by the exempt standpoint without circularity. Mannheim saw the regress clearly and accepted it as the human condition; the classical Marxist tradition mostly declined to look, and the declining is the weakest page in its record on this subject.

The reply, when it is made at full seriousness, has several components, and they should be distinguished because they defend different senses. First, the genetic explanation of a belief and the evaluation of it are logically independent operations, and nothing in the method licenses substituting one for the other; where practitioners substitute, they commit a fallacy the method itself can name, since Marx's own analyses, of the political economists above all, refute arguments line by line before explaining their authors' horizons. Second, the theory's claims are not in fact unfalsifiable in their specific forms: the dominant ideology thesis was tested against evidence and its strong form fell, which is behaviour no sealed system exhibits, and the fetishism analysis makes predictions, about the persistence of appearances under instruction, that experience could disappoint. Third, the asymmetry the objection targets is not unique to this theory. Every sociology of error, every psychology of bias, every history of science explains some beliefs by their causes and accepts others as knowledge, and the demand that ideology theory alone achieve a God's eye symmetry is a demand no inquiry meets. These replies do not acquit the theory of its temptations. They establish that the objection convicts a malpractice the theory can internally condemn, and that the dispute worth having is the empirical and textual one, about which beliefs, generated by which arrangements, distort in which directions. That dispute is real work, and it cannot be conducted at all until the senses are sorted, since the objection bites with full force only on the pejorative sense and largely dissolves against the neutral one, where explanation carries no sneer.

## The reflexivity problem and the three replies

The brief for this article assigns the full engagement with reflexivity to the method article later in the cluster, and honouring that division, the present section states the problem exactly and maps the replies without pretending to close the question. The problem, in its sharpest classical form, is this. The theory asserts that thought is conditioned by social position, and that the conditioning characteristically distorts. Marxism is a thought, produced from a social position, by identifiable people in identifiable institutions. Either the conditioning applies to it, in which case its claim to truth is compromised by its own account of what such claims are worth, or it does not apply, in which case the theory exempts itself from its central law and the exemption is special pleading. Karl Popper pressed versions of this charge against all historicist sociology; theologians pressed it against Mannheim; undergraduates press it, in corridor language, against every lecturer who teaches the concept, and it deserves better than the corridor answer.

Three replies structure the literature. The standpoint reply, developed most famously by Lukács, holds that social positions differ in their epistemic yield: the standpoint of the proletariat, the class with no interest in veiling the relations that exploit it, affords a less distorted view of the social whole, rather as the view from a valley floor reveals the drainage of a landscape that the view from any single estate conceals. The reply's strength is its refusal of exemption; its weakness is the certification problem, since the theory that identifies the privileged standpoint is itself the theory the standpoint is meant to certify, and historical movements claiming to embody the standpoint have not always exhibited the predicted lucidity. The science and ideology break, associated with Althusser, replies by redrawing the map: within every formation, a practice of knowledge production can break with the lived ideology that surrounds it, as physics broke with the spontaneous ideology of falling bodies, so Marxism's scientific core is not one ideology among others but the knowledge of ideology's mechanisms. The reply's strength is its analogy with the established sciences, which do emerge from common sense by methodical rupture; its weakness is the demarcation problem, since the criteria of a successful break are internal to the practice claiming to have performed it, and rival claimants to scientific status multiply. The partiality reply, the quietest of the three, concedes the conditioning and denies that conditioning entails falsity. All thought is from somewhere; some somewheres see some things truly; the theory claims not a view from nowhere but a view whose distortions are, on specified questions, smaller and more correctable than its rivals', because its explanations have survived specified tests. On this reply Marxism in the neutral sense is an ideology like others, and in the pejorative sense is an accusation it must continually earn exemption from, question by question, rather than a status it possesses.

### Does the theory exempt itself from its own central claim?

The honest answer is that the tradition has tried three routes out of self-exemption and none commands universal assent. Standpoint theory grants one class position a clearer view; the science break claims a methodological rupture with lived ideology; the partiality reply concedes conditioning and argues that situated thought can still be true.

None of the three replies is a refutation of the problem, and a textbook that presented any of them as one would be committing the very sin this article exists to correct, the presentation of one sense's settlement as the theory's settled result. What can be said is that the problem is not unique to Marxism, that every general theory of belief, including the critic's own implicit theory that arguments are best assessed without reference to their origins, must locate its own assertion somewhere in the world it describes. The reflexivity problem is the price of admission for any sociology of knowledge whatsoever. Traditions differ in whether they itemise the bill.

## Applying the sense-first rule

Rules earn their keep in application, and the sense-first rule can be demonstrated on the disputes that recur wherever the word is used. Take first the examination staple: was religion, for Marx, an ideology? Sense-sorted, the question divides into answerable parts. In the pejorative register, religious consciousness is treated in the early writings as an inverted world consciousness produced by an inverted world, the sigh of the oppressed creature and the heart of a heartless world in the famous mixed metaphor of the 1844 Introduction, which grants religion a protest content alongside its consoling function and so refuses the sneer of mere delusion. In the positional register, churches figure as producers and distributors of ruling ideas where their hierarchies are fused with the dominant order, and as something more complicated where they shelter dissent. In the neutral register, theology is plainly one of the forms in which social conflicts are fought out, from peasant wars preached as apocalypse to liberation movements preached as exodus. A candidate who runs the three registers in sequence has answered the question; a candidate who answers it in one register has answered a third of it.

Take, second, the newsroom usage, in which a commentator calls a policy proposal ideological to suggest it is impractical, doctrinaire, or insincere. Translated by the table, the accusation is Napoleonic rather than Marxist: it revives the emperor's sneer at abstract systematisers and borrows Marxist gravity for a charge Marx's senses do not make, since none of the four rows equates ideology with impracticality, and the neutral row equates it with the unavoidable medium of all politics including the commentator's own pragmatism, which is itself a structured position with premises about markets, states, and human motivation that could be set out as a doctrine in an afternoon. The sense-first rule performs a useful act of hygiene here. It asks the accuser to specify the charge: is the proposal false in the way of the first row, or partial in its universals in the way of the second, or merely held by opponents, in which case the word is doing the work of a boo and should be retired in favour of an argument. Much public usage does not survive the question, which is the point of asking it.

Take, third, the internal dispute of the movements that claim Marx's inheritance, in which one faction accuses another of ideological deviation. Sorted by the senses, the accusation in its positive Leninist register is a claim that the deviation abandons the organised self-consciousness of the class, a claim about fidelity that can be argued on the texts and the record. Transposed into the pejorative register, the same words accuse the deviation of inverted thinking in the service of another class, a far graver charge requiring evidence about mechanisms, not mere distance from a line. The political catastrophes of the tradition correlate strongly with unmarked transpositions of this kind, in which a fidelity dispute, conducted in the vocabulary of pathology, acquired the penalties appropriate to treason. The sense-first rule is thus not a scholar's ornament. It is a safety device for arguments whose terms carry histories.

Students revising this material with structured notes, for instance in the sense-tagged summaries the VaultBook study notes at [Marxist study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) are designed to hold, should build the habit mechanically: every quotation filed under ideology gets its row number attached before it gets filed at all. The mechanical habit, practised across a whole syllabus, converts the rule from an insight one applauds into a reflex one uses, and it generalises beyond this concept, since base, class, and state in this tradition all carry plural senses that examinations quietly exploit.

### Can the word be used without the insult attached?

Yes, and it routinely is, in three separate communities: political scientists use it for structured programmes of belief, movements use it approvingly for their own organised self-consciousness, and anthropologists use it for a people's working model of their world. The insult belongs to the Napoleonic inheritance and to the pejorative sense alone.

One further application belongs in a pillar article, because it governs the reading of everything else in the cluster. The articles that follow this one take up false consciousness, the Althusserian apparatuses, and the method of ideology critique in turn, and each inherits a different row of the table as its centre of gravity. A reader who carries the table into those articles will find their apparent contradictions mostly pre-dissolved: the false consciousness discussion is a first row discussion and inherits the first row's problems, the apparatuses discussion is a fourth row discussion and inherits the fourth row's problems, and the method discussion is the attempt to state procedures that let a critic work the first row honestly. Sequencing the cluster in this way is the editorial form of the sense-first rule. Concepts in a tradition are not a heap but a division of labour, and the pillar's task is to make the division visible before the specialised articles begin their specialised work.

## The mechanisms by which ideas do their work

A pillar article owes its readers more than a taxonomy; it owes them the working parts. Across the literature, a stable repertoire of mechanisms recurs wherever ideological functioning is analysed concretely, and naming the repertoire converts the concept from a verdict into a set of testable claims about particular texts, rituals, and institutions. The first mechanism is naturalisation: the presentation of a historically specific arrangement as if it were a fact of nature or a deliverance of eternity. Property regimes, family forms, hierarchies of occupation and nation have all been naturalised in their day, and the tell is always the same grammatical move, from this is how we have arranged things to this is how things are. Naturalisation works because arrangements perceived as chosen invite negotiation, while arrangements perceived as given invite only adaptation. The second mechanism is universalisation, analysed earlier in this article through the ruling ideas passage: the dressing of a sectional interest in the language of the whole, so that opposition to the interest registers as opposition to reason, order, or humanity itself.

The third mechanism is legitimation, the supply of reasons why those who benefit from an arrangement deserve to and why those who bear its costs should accept them as just, inevitable, or redemptive. Legitimating ideas range from the theological, in which earthly rank mirrors a divine order, through the meritocratic, in which rank mirrors certified talent, to the technocratic, in which rank mirrors expertise certified by the institutions the rank-holders control. Each age's dominant legitimation can be read off its honours system and its school examinations, and each is most effective when it contains a measure of truth, since talent is real, expertise is real, and orders do exist; the ideology lies in the inference from the partial truth to the justice of the whole. The fourth mechanism is dissimulation, the concealing or denying of relations that would embarrass the arrangement if seen steadily: the wage form concealing the division of the working day into paid and unpaid portions, the contract form presenting a relation between unequals as an exchange between free parties, the commodity form concealing the labour inside the price. Dissimulation differs from the lie because no one composes it; the forms themselves do the concealing, which is why the fourth sense of ideology, structural appearance, is the natural home of this mechanism.

The fifth mechanism is fragmentation: the division of a potential collective subject into competing fragments whose members experience one another, rather than the arrangement, as the obstacle. Craft against craft, nation against nation, the employed against the unemployed, the established resident against the newcomer: wherever subordinate populations expend their antagonism horizontally, the vertical relations that organise their common condition escape attention without a single doctrine needing to be believed. The sixth mechanism is reification, the treatment of human relations and historical products as if they were things with powers of their own: the market demands, the economy requires, capital flees, as if abstractions had appetites and itineraries. Reification is the everyday linguistic form of the camera obscura inversion, and its detection requires no specialised apparatus beyond noticing when a noun phrase has quietly eaten its verbs and the people who performed them. The seventh mechanism is the manufacture of lived obviousness, the slow depositing of all the above into common sense, where they operate below the threshold of argument as the feel of what everyone knows. Doctrines can be debated; the obvious can only be noticed, and noticing it is the first exercise every serious student of this subject is set.

Each mechanism is a claim that can be investigated, and the investigations have standards. To allege naturalisation, show the historical variation the naturalised account denies: the arrangement existed otherwise, elsewhere, or elsewhen, and the texts that call it eternal can be dated. To allege universalisation, show the sectional benefit and the universal costume in the same document, and show the exclusions the costume performs. To allege dissimulation by a form, reconstruct the relation the form presents and the relation it omits, as Marx's analysis of the wage does, and let the omission carry the charge. Criticism conducted at this level of specification is answerable, corrigible, and cumulative, everything the strong objection accused the theory of not being. Criticism conducted above this level, at the altitude where whole civilisations are convicted of ideology in a paragraph, deserves the objection in full. The mechanisms are therefore the article's practical core: they are what the sense-first rule is for, since each mechanism belongs to a determinate sense and misbehaves when borrowed by another.

## Ideology among its neighbours: culture, worldview, values and discourse

Students lose as many marks at the concept's borders as at its centre, because the syllabus surrounds ideology with near-synonyms that are not synonyms. A worldview, in the usage descending from German philosophy, is the total outlook within which a person or an age interprets experience: broader than ideology in connotation, more cognitive and less interested, carrying no necessary reference to power or to social position. Every ideology implies or presupposes a worldview, but worldviews are attributed to contemplatives and civilisations in contexts where no claim about rule is being made, and the attribution does different work. Culture, in the anthropological sense, is broader still: the whole way of life of a group, its techniques and cuisines and kinship rules as well as its beliefs. To call an entire culture ideological is to evacuate the term, since the critical and positional senses require a contrast class, beliefs or arrangements that are not doing that work, and totalising usage was the precise weakness critics found in the most ambitious version of the Althusserian inheritance.

Values name the evaluative components of belief systems, what a group holds good, worthy, or shameful, and they figure in ideology as ingredients rather than as the dish. Discourse, in the usage associated with Foucault, is the most consequential neighbour, because it was developed partly as a replacement. A discourse is a regulated practice of statement-making, with rules about who may speak, from which institutional site, and with what effects of truth; the concept directs attention to the production of knowledge and its entanglement with power while deliberately refusing the ideology concept's apparent commitment to a truth outside the play of discourses, from which the falsity of a discourse could be judged. The refusal is principled: Foucault held that the true and false distinction, wielded through the ideology concept, smuggled the analyst's own discourse into the position of judge. Marxists replied that refusing the distinction disables the criticism of domination at exactly the point where it matters, the point where a dominated group's account of its condition is truer than the official one. The border dispute is unresolved and instructive, and it maps, predictably, onto the senses: discourse theory accepts the positional and neutral analyses while rejecting the pejorative row's claim to a standpoint outside. A student who can state the border in those terms can handle any comparison question the two vocabularies generate.

Myth and narrative form a final border worth patrolling. A society's founding stories, of exodus, revolution, frontier, or liberation, function ideologically in every sense when they naturalise a present distribution by giving it a sacred or heroic past, and the functional analysis of myth overlaps the analysis of ideology so extensively that some writers treat them as one subject. The distinction worth keeping is formal: myths are narratives, carried by story, character, and rite, while ideologies in the strict textual senses include doctrines, arguments, and forms of consciousness that may contain no story at all, such as an accounting convention or a jurisprudential principle. The forms overlap in their social work and differ in their machinery, and precision about machinery is, as throughout this article, the difference between analysis and gesture.

## Reading the primary texts without drowning

The pillar closes its analytical work with guidance on the texts themselves, since the four senses are, finally, an index for reading. The German Ideology rewards slow reading of its first part, on Feuerbach, where the premises, the camera obscura, the division of labour, and the ruling ideas all stand within a few dozen pages of one another; the remainder, a vast polemic against figures few readers now know, can be sampled for tone once the argument is secure. Readers should know that the manuscript's textual condition is imperfect, assembled by editors from drafts, deletions, and fair copies, so that arguments built on a single contested sentence of it are weaker than arguments built on its repeated architecture. The 1859 Preface is ten pages that repay a hundred readings, and the exercise that disciplines all others is to state, for each of its famous sentences, which stratum of the architecture it describes and what would count as evidence against it. The fetishism section of Capital is the hardest of the three and the most rewarding, best approached after its author's analysis of the value form has been followed line by line rather than summarised, because its conclusions about appearance are earned by the derivation and sound like mere assertions without it.

Across all three texts, one reading habit protects against the commonest error, which is quotation mining: the extraction of a striking sentence, its detachment from the sense in force in its passage, and its deployment as Marx's view of ideology tout court. The habit produced the textbook definition this article opened with, and it produces most of the concept's public confusion. The corrective habit is citation with coordinates: text, date, sense, and the mechanism alleged. A claim about ideology that cannot supply its coordinates has not yet become a claim about Marx, whatever else it may be, and examiners, editors, and honest opponents are entitled to send it back for completion.

## Worked cases: the senses applied to history

Abstract taxonomy persuades few readers; the senses justify themselves on cases. Consider first the divine right of kings, the doctrine that monarchical authority descends from God and that resistance to it is sin. In the pejorative register, the doctrine is inverted thought in textbook purity: a human arrangement, kingship, maintained by identifiable interests and enforced by identifiable soldiers, represented as the reflection of a celestial arrangement that no one arranged. In the positional register, the analysis shifts to production: the doctrine was elaborated by court theologians and jurists, distributed through established churches whose livings depended on the crown, and universalised in catechisms that presented obedience to this king as obedience to order as such. In the neutral register, divine right is one of the forms in which the period's conflicts over taxation, succession, and church property were fought out, a medium in which both absolutists and their parliamentary opponents were obliged to argue, the opponents characteristically answering theology with theology. In the structural register, the analyst notes how much of the doctrine's plausibility was generated by the practice of a world in which authority was in fact inherited everywhere, in guild, household, and manor, so that hereditary monarchy appeared as the political instance of a universal form rather than as an anomaly requiring defence. Four analyses of one doctrine, none redundant, each answering a different question, and a student who can run all four on a fresh case has learned the concept.

Consider, second, the idea of the self-made man, the doctrine that fortunes are the natural wages of talent and effort and poverty the natural wages of their absence. The pejorative analysis notes the inversion: the outcome of a distribution process, shaped by inheritance, schooling, credit, and the ownership of assets, represented as the revelation of inner qualities that the process in fact produced and rewarded selectively. The positional analysis asks after production and benefit: the doctrine is amplified by the institutions of a commercial society, flatters its beneficiaries with a biography they can believe, and universalises a sectional experience, since the mobility that some genuinely achieve is predicated of the whole. The neutral analysis observes that the doctrine is a form in which real conflicts, over taxation, welfare, and the regulation of markets, are fought out by all parties, including its critics, who must argue in its vocabulary of desert even to oppose it. The structural analysis points to the daily appearance that sustains it: the market does present each outcome as a price, a wage, a valuation, apparently measuring the worth of the person or product it prices, so that the doctrine feels less like a teaching than like a report. Here again the senses divide the labour, and the characteristic textbook failure, announcing that the self-made man is an ideology and moving on, performs none of the four analyses while claiming the authority of all of them.

Consider, third, a harder and more instructive case: the belief, widespread among subordinate populations themselves, that existing arrangements are broadly fair. The strong dominant ideology thesis predicted this belief and explained it by saturation. The ethnographic record complicated the prediction, finding instead a divided consciousness: the same worker who endorses fairness in the abstract gives a fluent account of particular unfairnesses at work, and the same citizen who trusts the institutions in general narrates specific encounters with them as rigged. The senses handle the complication gracefully. Positionally, the institutions of mental production do set the abstract vocabulary in which fairness is discussed, and the vocabulary's terms, opportunity, desert, competitiveness, are the ruling ideas' residue. Structurally, the wage form and the contract form generate the appearance of fairness daily, since an exchange of equivalents is what the surface honestly shows. Pejoratively, the inversion is local rather than total, appearing precisely where the abstract vocabulary is used to override the concrete account. The divided record is thus not an embarrassment for the concept sorted by senses. It is an embarrassment only for the crude descendant that required unanimity, and the episode should be taught as the model of how empirical work disciplines this theory when the theory is stated in falsifiable parts.

## Errors this article exists to prevent

Certain mistakes recur in examination scripts and published journalism with such regularity that they can be listed in prose and corrected in advance. The first is the definition by slogan, in which ideology is defined as false consciousness and false consciousness is then never distinguished from ideology, so that two terms collapse into one another and neither does any work; the companion guide to false consciousness exists precisely to pry them apart. The second is the attribution of the strong dominant ideology thesis to Marx, convicting him of an empirical claim he never made in terms the evidence would have embarrassed, and then either defending the claim past its death or discarding the whole concept with it. The third is the conspiratorial short circuit, in which the existence of an interest is treated as proof of a deception, and the analyst moves from who benefits to who is lying without passing through any mechanism. The fourth is the party programme confusion, imported from political journalism, in which ideology names the manifesto of a movement the writer opposes and never the premises of the writer's own camp.

The fifth error is the asymmetric exemption, in which the analyst's own beliefs are treated as knowledge by courtesy while everyone else's are treated as ideology by analysis; it is the reflexivity problem enacted as a habit, and examiners are trained to notice it. The sixth is level confusion, the treatment of a neutral-sense phenomenon, the forms of consciousness of an age, as though it were a pejorative-sense specimen, a particular inverted doctrine, so that the student writes of the ideology of an entire century as if it were one lie with one author. The seventh is the genetic shortcut, in which a belief's social origin is offered as its refutation, sparing the writer the labour of establishing that the belief is false, partial, or functional in the first place. Each error is a sense error in disguise, a borrowing of one row's authority to do another row's work, and the fact that the entire bestiary can be generated from the table is the strongest internal evidence that the table captures something real about the concept's structure.

There is also an eighth error, the mirror of the others, committed by the theory's enemies rather than its students: the assumption that because the word has been used as a solvent for disagreement, every use of it is that solvent, and the concept can be dismissed by citing its abuses. Dismissal by abuse would retire half the vocabulary of social science, including power, interest, and class itself, and it fails the same test the strong objection fails, by refusing to distinguish the senses in which a term does disciplined work from the registers in which it does mischief. The mature position, which this pillar exists to establish, holds both truths at once: the concept has a disgraceful polemical history and a respectable analytical core, and the sorting of senses is what keeps the second available without denying the first.

## How an ideology holds: consent, habit and the absence of alternatives

The mechanisms describe what ideological forms do to the world they represent. A complementary question asks what they do to the people who live inside them, and the literature's answers sort into three grades of holding, which should never be conflated. The strongest grade is belief: the subject accepts the form's account as true and can defend it. The middle grade is habit: the subject does not so much accept the account as enact it, turning up, signing, queuing, deferring, and experiencing the enactment as the simple shape of a day; Althusser's insistence on ritual and apparatus belongs to this grade, and it explains why forms survive the collapse of their own credibility, since a practice does not need to be believed to be continued, only to be the practice there is. The weakest and most pervasive grade is the absence of alternatives: the subject may disbelieve the account and resent the practice, yet cannot coordinate on another, because the available languages, organisations, and meeting places are all furnished by the order in question. Political stability in the record is usually a compound of the three grades in proportions that vary by class and institution, and the analytical error to avoid is inferring the first grade from the observable fact of stability. Quiet is not consent, and consent is not conviction, and a theory of ideology that cannot tell them apart will misread every stable society it examines, including, especially, the societies whose rulers congratulated themselves on the loyalty of the quiet.

Habit deserves a section of its own within this one, because it is where the concept of ideology meets the concept of practice, and the meeting has been the most fertile ground in the field for two generations. A belief that must be renewed by argument every morning is fragile; a belief sedimented into the timetable, the architecture, the forms of address, and the design of forms is renewed by the world itself. The school day teaches punctuality, hierarchy, and individual assessment more reliably through its organisation than through any lesson about them, and the lesson is learned by the body before it is available to the mind. The queue at the benefits office teaches the relation between citizen and state in posture and waiting time. The open plan office teaches a theory of transparency and availability that no handbook states. Analysts influenced by the fourth sense of ideology have learned to read such arrangements as texts, asking of a building or a procedure the genetic question the method prescribes: what relation does this arrangement present as natural, and what relation does its smooth functioning require its participants not to notice? The reading can be done well or badly, and its quality is measurable by the same standard as elsewhere, by whether the claimed relation can be reconstructed independently and the claimed concealment exhibited rather than asserted.

The absence of alternatives, finally, connects the theory of ideology back to the theory of class with which the preceding cluster of this series was concerned. A class becomes a collective actor only when its members acquire languages in which their shared condition can be stated and organisations in which it can be acted on, and the production of those languages and organisations is precisely the counter ideological work Gramsci assigned to the patient construction of a counter-hegemony. Where that work has not been done, the ruling senses prevail by default rather than by persuasion, and the analyst should describe the situation in the vocabulary of default, not in the vocabulary of deception or consent. Much of what passes for the power of ideology in the world is, on inspection, the power of an unopposed infrastructure, and recognising this redirects political as well as analytical attention, from the refutation of beliefs towards the construction of the institutions in which different beliefs could be produced, tested, and lived.

## The concept in the seminar room: particular and total

One distinction from Mannheim's work has become so standard in teaching that a pillar article must state it cleanly, because it organises the reflexivity problem into a usable form. The particular conception of ideology distrusts specific assertions by an opponent: this claim is interested, that statistic is selected, this history is apologetic. It operates locally, leaves the opponent's general framework standing, and is practised by every competent controversialist including those who have never heard the word ideology. The total conception distrusts a framework: it holds that an age or a class thinks in categories that are themselves a function of its position, so that even its honest inquiries are pre-formed by what its position lets it see. Marx's pejorative sense is a total conception armed with a materialist explanation of which positions exist and why. Mannheim's sociology of knowledge is the total conception generalised and disarmed, its explanatory edge retained and its class content broadened into standpoint as such. Most confusion in student work arises from sliding between the two conceptions mid-argument, deploying particular suspicions as if they established a total claim, or retreating from a total claim to a particular one when challenged and advancing again when the challenge passes. Naming the conception in force is the seminar room version of the sense-first rule, and students who acquire the habit find that a large fraction of the literature's famous paradoxes are level errors of exactly this kind.

The total conception also clarifies what is genuinely scandalous in the theory and what is not. That interests colour particular claims is a platitude of the debating chamber. That categories themselves are historical, that what counts as a fact, a cause, a person, or an explanation varies with the organisation of the society doing the counting, is a strong philosophical thesis with consequences far beyond politics, reaching into the history of science and the philosophy of language. Marx's version adds a mechanism and a motor: categories are historical because the practices they organise are historical, and they change when the practices do, which is why the categories of political economy, value, labour, capital, present themselves to his analysis as the theoretical expressions of definite relations of production rather than as eternal furniture of thought. On this reading, the critique of ideology and the critique of political economy are one project at two levels, and Capital's patient reconstruction of how the categories of the market misdescribe the relations of the factory is the total conception executed with a rigour the programmatic texts only promise. Readers who want the execution rather than the promise should go there, and go slowly.

## Why the word will not die

Obituaries for the concept of ideology have been published at regular intervals for half a century, and the concept has outlived each of its obituarists, a fact that itself requires explanation. The end of ideology was proclaimed in the postwar decades by sociologists who believed that affluence and technocratic management had drained politics of doctrine; the proclamation was itself promptly analysed as an ideology, the self-image of a managerial order presenting its preferences as the absence of preferences, and the episode taught the field a durable lesson about premature funerals. The concept survives because the phenomenon it names in its several senses survives: beliefs are still produced by institutions with owners, still universalise sectional interests, still become the media of conflicts their holders did not choose, and still crystallise into appearances that practice regenerates daily. Each sense names a permanent feature of social life under conditions of division, and a vocabulary that abandoned the word would have to reinvent it within a generation under another name, as indeed the successor vocabularies of discourse, framing, narrative, and common sense have repeatedly done while disclaiming the inheritance.

Survival is not vindication, and the article's last analytical word should be one of discipline rather than celebration. The concept of ideology, sorted by its senses, equipped with its mechanisms, and restrained by the procedural safeguards against explaining disagreement away, remains the sharpest instrument the social sciences possess for a specific and recurring question: why these beliefs, in this form, among these people, now. Asked without the sorting, the question degenerates into the accusation with which this article began, ideology as the name for other people's falsity, a usage Marx inherited from an emperor and improved beyond recognition in his best pages. The four senses, the table that holds them, and the rule that one must be chosen before an argument can begin are the improvement made usable. Everything that follows in this cluster, on false consciousness, on the apparatuses, and on the method of critique, presupposes that the instrument has been picked up by the right end.

## Testing a claim of ideology: what evidence would settle it

Because the strong objection charges the theory with absorbing all evidence, the pillar should state, sense by sense, what evidence tells against an ideology claim, so that the claims of this article are hostage to fortune in the way the objection demands. Against a pejorative claim, that a doctrine inverts its subject matter, the telling evidence is the doctrine's success on its own ground: if the allegedly inverted account predicts, organises, and survives attempts at refutation by the ordinary standards of its discipline, the inversion charge weakens to the point of withdrawal, since inversion was alleged precisely as a failure of representation. Against a positional claim, that a doctrine's dominance expresses a ruling class's position, the telling evidence is distributional and biographical: doctrines that dominate against the funding, staffing, and interest of the dominant institutions, or whose producers are drawn from and accountable to subordinate strata, embarrass the claim in proportion to their centrality. The career of liberation theology within churches whose hierarchies opposed it, and of abolitionist doctrine within a republic whose economy rested on slavery, are the standard hard cases, and a positional theory that cannot process them without calling them exceptions has been falsified at its centre rather than at its edges.

Against a structural claim, that an appearance is generated by a practice and will persist while the practice does, the telling evidence is dissociation: appearances that vanish while the practice continues unchanged, or that persist intact where the practice has been abolished, break the claimed connection. The history of economic categories supplies natural experiments of a rough kind, since forms of production have been transformed within living memory in several societies, and the fate of their associated appearances is a matter of record rather than speculation. Against the neutral sense no evidence is required or possible, since it asserts a classification rather than a mechanism, and this is precisely why the neutral sense cannot do critical work alone: a sense that nothing could embarrass also convicts nothing. The four rows of the table thus differ in their evidential exposure as much as in their content, and part of stating one's sense is stating what would make one stop. Critics who demand falsifiability from the theory are entitled to that statement. Theorists who supply it find, in practice, that their claims become smaller, more numerous, and more often right, which is the ordinary biography of a research programme growing up.

The same discipline applies to the mechanisms. A claim of naturalisation is embarrassed when the allegedly naturalised arrangement turns out to be defended by its holders in frankly historical terms, as a chosen and revisable settlement, since naturalisation alleged the denial of historicity. A claim of universalisation is embarrassed when the sectional interest cannot be identified independently of the doctrine said to express it, for then the analysis has inferred the interest from the doctrine and the doctrine from the interest in a circle with no outside point. A claim of reification is embarrassed when the thing-like language proves to be a shorthand its users unpack correctly on demand, speaking of the market's demands and meaning, precisely, the aggregated decisions of identifiable actors under identifiable constraints. None of these embarrassments is exotic. They are the ordinary defeats of ordinary claims, and a literature that records them, as the record of the dominant ideology thesis shows this literature doing, is not the sealed system of the objection's portrait.

## The concept's travels: four national careers

The senses also have geographies, and a brief tour of them explains why international literatures on ideology so often talk past one another. In France, where the word was born as a science and disgraced as an insult, the concept's modern career ran through Althusser and the structuralist generation, for whom ideology named the inescapable medium of subjecthood, and through the reaction against that generation, for whom the word became synonymous with the dogmatism of a party intellectual culture. French usage consequently retains a philosophical altitude: ideology there is a question about the constitution of the subject before it is a question about the content of beliefs. In Germany, the word's career ran through the sociology of knowledge and the Frankfurt tradition, in which the critique of ideology merged with the critique of mass culture and of instrumental reason, producing a literature in which the administered world's entertainments and sciences alike fall under suspicion of performing the inversion the young Marx diagnosed in philosophy. German usage retains a systematic ambition: ideology is a moment in a total account of a society's self-understanding, and partial uses feel unfinished.

In the Russian and Soviet career, the positive sense reigned institutionally: ideology was the name of the doctrine a movement taught its members and a state taught its citizens, complete with academies, curricula, and examinations, and the word's pejorative charge was reserved for the doctrines of class enemies, bourgeois ideology being a fixed phrase of the period's official language. The coexistence, within one state vocabulary, of ideology as the proudest possession and ideology as the enemy's disease is the reflexivity problem in institutional form, and it was managed, as such problems usually are, by an exemption: the doctrine of the ruling party was classified as science, and ideology was what the others had. In the Anglophone career, finally, the word was domesticated by political science into the neutral study of belief systems, their constraint, their distribution across electorates, and their distance from the pragmatic self-image of the political culture using the term, a usage in which calling a position ideological often means merely that it is systematic. Four careers, four centres of gravity, and a single word whose translation between literatures requires the sense-first rule as much as its translation between centuries does. A reader who knows which national literature a text belongs to can predict, with useful accuracy, which row of the table its author will treat as the concept itself, and can discount accordingly.

The travels have a lesson for the series as a whole. Concepts in a living tradition behave like the classes this series analysed in its previous cluster: they are not essences travelling unchanged through history but relations, reconstituted in each setting by the institutions that carry them and the conflicts they are used to fight. The history of the word ideology is itself a small confirmation of the neutral sense's deepest claim, that ideas have careers because societies have structures, and that tracing the career of an idea without tracing the structures is the idealist error the whole theory exists to correct. The pillar article of a cluster is entitled to that reflexive flourish once, and having made it, should return to work.

## The worked example Marx left: political economy as ideology

The theory of ideology has one fully worked application from its author's own hand, and students meet the concept most profitably there: the critique of political economy, the discipline whose categories Capital anatomises. Marx's treatment of the economists is the method in miniature, and it respects the safeguards this article has been assembling. The classical economists, Smith and Ricardo above all, are treated with open respect as genuine investigators who pushed the analysis of value and distribution to the limit of their horizon. Their errors are then specified as errors, on the merits, in the theory of value and of wages. Only after the specification comes the genetic explanation: the horizon itself, the unexamined assumption that capitalist relations are the natural and final form of production, is explained by the economists' position as the theoretical voice of a class whose world those relations were. The order of operations is the whole lesson. Refutation first, explanation second, and the explanation offered for the limits of an inquiry whose achievements are fully acknowledged. Nothing is explained away, because everything has first been answered.

The later, vulgar economy of Marx's polemics represents the degenerate case in which the order collapses, and his contempt for it is instructive precisely because it is discriminating. In the vulgar writers, as he presents them, inquiry has been replaced by apologetics: the categories are no longer investigated but arranged so as to flatter the arrangement they describe, and the trinity formula, capital yielding interest, land yielding rent, labour yielding wages, presents the revenues of the three great classes as springing from three natural sources, like fruit from separate trees, concealing the common root in unpaid labour. The analysis of the trinity formula is the fourth sense applied to a doctrine: the formula is not a lie, since revenues do arrive in those forms, and it is not a mere error, since it organises the real appearances of the system with perfect fidelity to their surface. It is the surface, systematised and mistaken for the depth. A student who understands why the trinity formula is the paradigm of ideological thought in Marx has understood more of the concept than a student who has memorised every definition in Eagleton's catalogue, because the paradigm shows all four senses cooperating in a single object: the illusion of independent sources, the positional service to the classes whose revenues are naturalised, the neutral status of the formula as the form in which a whole society understood its distribution, and the structural generation of the illusion by the forms of revenue themselves.

The example also disciplines the reflexivity problem in practice. Marx's critique of the economists assumes that a writer situated inside a class society can nonetheless produce analyses that are true of it, since he is in the act of doing so, and it locates the difference between inquiry and apologetics not in the writer's address but in the writer's procedure: whether the appearances are taken as the starting point of an investigation that reconstructs their hidden connections, or as the finishing point of a system that arranges them for admiration. Procedure, not position, is the operational test, and it is available to any reader of any standpoint, which is as close as this tradition comes to dissolving the reflexivity problem rather than merely answering it. The method article in this cluster builds its safeguards on this foundation, and the pillar records the foundation here: explain positions, judge procedures, and never let the first operation swallow the second.

## The ideologist as a social type

Behind the senses stands a figure, the professional producer of ideas, whose social situation does more explanatory work in Marx's writings than any doctrine attributed to him. The ideologist, as the texts construct him, is defined by a paradox of dependence and independence. Materially, he depends entirely on the order he describes, for his living: the priest on the tithe and the living, the professor on the chair and the ministry that fills it, the journalist on the proprietor and the advertiser, the jurist on the state whose law he expounds. Intellectually, his professional self-image requires independence: the doctrine must present itself as the product of pure inquiry, obedient only to the evidence and the argument, and the producer must believe in that presentation for the product to carry conviction. The paradox is not hypocrisy but structure, and its resolution in practice is the whole art of ideological production: the skilled ideologist experiences his dependence as the natural distribution of honour to merit and his conclusions as the deliverances of an unclouded reason, and both experiences are sincere. Criticism that aims at the man will always miss this, denouncing as corruption what is in fact a labour process, and criticism that aims at the labour process, at the appointments, the funding, the syllabuses, the career penalties for heterodoxy and the career rewards for fluency within the paradigm, engages the phenomenon at the level where it is actually produced.

The type has proliferated beyond anything the nineteenth century supported, and the proliferation is itself a datum for the theory. Societies of the present type employ idea producers in numbers that would have astonished Marx: in universities, media, publishing, public relations, think tanks, consultancies, cultural institutions, and the vast apparatus of content manufacture that attends every other industry. The theory's questions scale with the proliferation. Whose resources sustain each stratum of production, and what range of conclusions can each stratum reach without endangering its reproduction? Which questions does each stratum find serious, and which does it find unaskable, not by prohibition but by the subtler mechanism of the uncommissioned manuscript and the uninvited speaker? Where does a stratum's product universalise a sectional experience, and where does it perform the rarer service of stating the experience of those who cannot commission statements? These are researchable questions, with archives, accounts, and appointment books for evidence, and they represent the positional sense of ideology developed into an empirical sociology of intellectual life. The pillar's claim for the concept rests finally here: not on its power as an accusation, which is cheap and corrupting, but on its power as a research programme into the manufacture of the obvious, which is expensive, corrigible, and unfinished.

## A reader's protocol for ideological analysis

The senses, mechanisms, and safeguards assembled so far can be compressed into a protocol that a reader can apply to any specimen: an editorial, a textbook chapter, a speech, a syllabus, a museum's arrangement of its rooms. The protocol is stated here as continuous prose because its steps interlock, and because a checklist would suggest a mechanical certainty the method does not possess. Begin by establishing the specimen's account on its own terms, in a summary its author would accept. This is not politeness but instrumentation: an ideology analysis of a position nobody holds is an analysis of nothing, and the tradition's besetting sin, the refutation of straw, is best prevented at the first step by making the summary pass the author's own test before any criticism is allowed to begin.

Proceed, second, to the evaluation the specimen invites on its merits. Are its claims true, its inferences valid, its evidence representative, its categories coherent? The evaluation must be conducted with the ordinary tools of the relevant discipline and must reach a verdict that could, in principle, come out in the specimen's favour. Where the verdict is favourable, the ideological analysis changes its question: not why is this false, but why is this truth, of all the truths available, the one selected, amplified, and installed as the obvious one, since selection and amplification are positional facts even when content is sound. Where the verdict is unfavourable, specify the failure exactly, as Marx specified the failures of the economists, in the value theory and not in the biography. Vague falsity is not yet an ideological finding; it is an invitation to find the mechanism.

The third movement is the genetic one, and it is permitted only now. Ask who produces specimens of this kind, under what institutional arrangements, with what careers at stake; ask what the specimen's categories render invisible, and whether the invisibility is systematic, always hiding the same relata in the same direction; ask which of the mechanisms from this article's repertoire the specimen exhibits, naturalisation, universalisation, legitimation, dissimulation, fragmentation, reification, or the depositing of all of these into common sense; and ask what practice, if any, generates the specimen's appearances afresh each day, so that refuting the specimen would leave its source intact. Each question has evidence appropriate to it: appointment records and funding streams for production, comparative history for naturalisation, the document's own exclusions for universalisation, the reconstructed relation for dissimulation. An analysis that can cite its evidence at each step has earned the word ideology as a conclusion. An analysis that begins with the word has earned nothing.

The fourth movement is reflexive, and it closes the protocol by turning it on the analyst. State the sense in force, the analyst's own institutional position, and the evidence that would embarrass the analysis, as this article modelled in its section on testing. The reflexive movement is not a confession ritual; it is a quality control, ensuring that the asymmetry the strong objection condemns is at least visible in the finished work, where readers can discount for it. Applied across a field, the protocol converts ideology critique from a genre of denunciation into a genre of inquiry with shared standards, and it is the protocol, more than any definition, that the cluster's method article will formalise. Readers who practise it on this article itself, hunting its selections, its universals, its invisible relata, and its author's position as a paid producer of syntheses about other people's beliefs, will find material enough. The article's defence is the one the protocol prescribes: its claims are stated with their senses, its strongest objection is stated before its replies, and its evidence is open to inspection. That is all the exemption any analysis may honestly claim, and the theory, properly understood, claims no more for itself than that.

## A fourth worked case: law as an ideological form

Law repays the fourfold treatment particularly well, because it is the ideological form whose workings are most completely documented and whose claims to stand outside ideology are most emphatic. In the neutral register, law is unambiguously one of the forms the 1859 Preface lists first among those in which conflict becomes conscious: the property dispute, the labour dispute, and the dispute over the franchise all arrive in the courtroom translated into a grammar of rights, persons, and remedies, and the translation is not a disguise laid over the conflict but the conflict's public mode of existence. In the positional register, the analysis turns to production: the judiciary is recruited from a narrow stratum, trained in institutions that stratum controls, and professionally rewarded for a style of reasoning in which the existing distribution of property figures as the silent premise of every argument about its incidents. No instruction from any ruling class is required, and none is alleged; the selection of personnel and the formation of professional common sense do the work the ruling ideas passage describes.

In the pejorative register, the critical charge fastens on a specific inversion. The legal form presents the parties before it as abstract persons, equal bearers of rights, and within the form the presentation is accurate: the court genuinely does not ask whether the litigant is a landlord or a tenant when it applies the law of contract. The inversion begins when the form's internal equality is taken as a description of the society whose members enter the court, so that the freedom of the contract between the owner of a factory and the owner of nothing but labour power is celebrated as the meeting of two liberties. The form tells the truth about the courtroom and misleads about the world, which is the graded, partial distortion the fourth sense was developed to describe, and law belongs there too: the ceremony, the robe, the architecture, and the enforced etiquette of the courtroom generate an appearance of impartial majesty that participants experience directly, whatever their private theories, and that appearance is regenerated by every sitting of every court regardless of what anyone believes about it.

The legal case also supplies the tradition's critics with their fairest test, because law is the form in which subordinate classes have won real victories, protections against arbitrary eviction, limits on the working day enforced as rights, franchises extended by statute. A crude positional theory, in which law is simply the ruling class's instrument, cannot explain why the instrument keeps cutting its owners, and the history of such defeats for property administered through law is long and documented. The sorted theory handles the record without strain. The neutral sense predicts that the form is a genuine terrain of struggle, usable by all parties who can master its grammar; the positional sense predicts the asymmetry of the terrain, the cost of mastery, and the selection of its custodians; the structural sense predicts the appearance of equality that the terrain generates and that limits what victories within it can mean. A theory whose senses jointly predict both the victories and their limits has earned its complexity, and a student who can reproduce this paragraph's movement on a fresh institution, the school, the hospital, the platform, possesses the concept in working order rather than in quotation.

## The affective hold: why ideological forms are loved, not merely held

One dimension of the phenomenon remains underdeveloped in the classic texts and indispensable to any modern statement: attachment. Beliefs that organise a life are not held the way timetables are held. They are loved, defended with heat, and mourned when lost, and an analysis that treats their holders as information processors who happen to have faulty inputs will fail to predict any of this. The forms analysed in this article supply their holders with goods that are not reducible to error: an intelligible world, a honoured place within it, a past that explains the present, enemies and friends sorted into legibility, and rituals in which belonging is bodily renewed. A subject who surrenders such a form on the presentation of a counterargument would be surrendering an identity, an income of respect, and a community, in exchange for a proposition. The exchange rate explains the resistance, and it explains why the most effective historical challenges to dominant forms have never been mere refutations. They have been counter-forms: alternative congregations, unions, parties, schools, and festivals in which a different account of the world arrives already furnished with belonging, honour, and rite. Gramsci's insistence that a counter-hegemony must be built, institution by institution, rather than announced, is the tradition's deepest acknowledgment of the affective dimension, and Althusser's rituals point the same way from the other side: what the knees learn, the head is slow to unlearn.

Attachment also explains a pattern that purely cognitive accounts mishandle, the intensification of belief under refutation. Where a form is load-bearing for identity, contrary evidence is processed as persecution, and persecution confirms the form's own narrative of a hostile world arrayed against the faithful. The phenomenon is visible in sects whose prophecies fail and whose membership, in documented cases, emerges more devoted, having converted the failure into a test passed. It is visible in professional strata whose paradigms absorb anomalies for decades, because the anomaly threatens not a hypothesis but a career's accumulated capital of expertise. It is visible, the reflexive protocol obliges this article to add, in traditions of criticism themselves, whose members can experience the criticism of their critical instruments as an attack on the community that taught them to see. A theory of ideology that cannot model attachment will keep being surprised by the durability of what it refutes, and a practice of criticism that ignores attachment will keep supplying its objects with the persecution that renews them.

The affective dimension returns the analysis, by a long route, to the fourth sense. The appearances generated by exchange are not loved; they are inhabited, which is a quieter and stronger relation, and the difference between the two registers suggests a typology of holding that the literature has not fully exploited. Doctrines are believed and can be argued with. Identities are inhabited and can only be succeeded. Appearances are transacted and can only be transformed by transforming the practice that generates them. Much futile criticism consists of applying the method of one register to the objects of another: arguing with an identity as if it were a doctrine, or denouncing an appearance as if it were a lie, or awaiting the transformation of a practice from the refutation of its accompanying doctrine. The senses, once again, are not a scholastic refinement. They are the difference between criticism that engages its object and criticism that performs for its own congregation, and the distinction is available to any reader who has worked through the table with which this article's argument began.

A final implication concerns the tone of the whole enterprise. If ideological forms are loved and inhabited rather than merely mistaken, then the people inside them are not the theory's patients, and the critic is not its physician. The holders of the forms examined in this article, the theologians, economists, officials, believers, and sceptics alike, are people solving the universal problem of living inside a world they did not make, with the materials their positions supplied. Some of their solutions conceal the relations that govern them, and the concealment has beneficiaries, and the beneficiaries' institutions work to keep the concealment in place; all of that can be said, and shown, without a gram of contempt for the concealed. The traditions of ideology critique that retained this respect produced the durable work surveyed here. The traditions that replaced it with contempt produced denunciations that dated faster than the doctrines they denounced. The sense-first rule, the mechanisms, the protocol, and the reflexive discipline are this article's attempt to keep the enterprise on the durable side of that line.

## Ideology and utopia: the paired forms

Mannheim's title yokes ideology to a partner concept that a pillar article should not omit, because the pairing sharpens both terms. Utopia, in his usage, names the mentality of strata whose thought bursts the bounds of the existing order and orients itself towards a world that does not yet exist, as ideology names the mentality whose thought defends and veils the world that does. The distinction is functional and positional rather than literary: the same text can be ideology for the order it protects and utopia for the order it imagines, and yesterday's utopia, the programme of an ascending class, hardens into tomorrow's ideology when that class takes possession of the order it must then defend. The pairing disciplines a common laziness, in which ideology is predicated only of conservatives and utopia only of radicals, when in fact both are positions any doctrine can occupy in sequence as its bearers move through the structure. It also extends the reflexivity discipline in a useful direction: a movement examining its own mentality should ask not only which of its beliefs are ideological in the senses of this article, but which have completed the passage from utopia to ideology, from the imagination of a different world to the defence of the world the movement now administers. The question is uncomfortable in direct proportion to the movement's success, and the traditions that learned to ask it in time are, on the record, the ones whose doctrines aged slowest.

## For students and exam candidates

The four senses are the answer structure for any ideology question, and scripts should be built on them openly. Open with the refusal of the single definition, state the senses with their texts, and let the question's wording select the emphasis: questions about falsity belong to the first sense and The German Ideology, questions about power and consent to the second, questions about Marxism's own status to the third and the reflexivity replies. Quote sparingly and with coordinates, text, date, sense, mechanism. The errors that cost most marks are the slogan definition, the attribution of the strong dominant ideology thesis to Marx, and the slide between senses inside one paragraph. A closing evaluation that names the sense in which the concept survives its critics, and the evidence that disciplines it, reads as maturity rather than fence sitting.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The misconception that arrives in every politics class is the party programme confusion: students for whom ideology names the manifesto of a movement they oppose, and never the premises of their own camp. The fastest correction is biographical and Napoleonic: give them the word's birth as a science and its disgrace as an insult, and let them watch their own usage descend from the emperor rather than from Marx. Seminar time is best spent on the camera obscura passage read slowly, on the ruling ideas passage read against the charge of saturation, and on one exercise in which the class must run all four senses on a single shared specimen, a school textbook's account of a national foundation works well, before anyone is allowed to evaluate. Assessment should reward the naming of senses and punish the unattributed slogan, since the naming is the skill the whole topic exists to teach.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

For working researchers, the usable inheritance is the positional sense developed as a sociology of intellectual production: follow the funding, the appointments, the syllabuses, and the career penalties, and treat the doctrines as products of a labour process before treating them as positions in an argument. Historians will find the neutral sense the safest instrument, since it classifies the forms in which past conflicts were fought without licensing anachronistic verdicts on their truth. Journalists face the word as a temptation: calling a position ideological in print implies, to most readers, the pejorative charge of falsity or bad faith, and professional honesty requires either substantiating that charge with a mechanism and evidence or choosing a plainer word. The genetic question, what arrangement keeps generating this belief in this form, is a reporting assignment in disguise, and the newsrooms that assign it produce the strongest work in this area.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is ideology in Marxist theory?

Ideology in Marxist theory is not one concept but a family of four, held together by a single word Marx used across his career. In The German Ideology it names inverted thought that treats ideas as independent powers; in the same work it names the ruling ideas of a dominant class; in the Preface of 1859 it names, neutrally, the forms in which people become conscious of social conflict; and in Capital it names appearances generated by the structure of exchange itself. Any responsible use begins by stating which sense is in force, which is the sense-first rule this article defends.

### Q: Does ideology mean false beliefs?

Only in one of its senses. The pejorative sense of The German Ideology does treat ideology as inverted, and therefore misleading, thought. The ruling ideas sense is positional and leaves truth open, since a dominant class's ideas may be accurate while still serving its position. The neutral sense of the 1859 Preface implies no falsity at all, naming the media in which conflict becomes conscious. The fetishism analysis is mixed: appearances true of the surface and false of the whole. Collapsing the four into the first is the commonest error in the literature.

### Q: How many senses of ideology are there in Marx?

Four can be distinguished in the texts, though Marx never lists them himself. The illusion sense and the ruling ideas sense both stand in The German Ideology of 1845 and 1846, the neutral sense in the Preface of 1859, and the structural appearance sense in the fetishism chapter of Capital. Commentators group them differently: Geuss sorts conceptions into descriptive, pejorative, and positive families, while Eagleton counts sixteen definitions in circulation. The fourfold sorting used here follows the texts in chronological order and assigns each sense its location, its implication for truth, and its later developers.

### Q: What is the camera obscura metaphor?

The camera obscura was a darkened chamber in which an outside scene was projected upside down through a small aperture, and Marx uses it in The German Ideology for the inversion he finds in ideological thought. Ideas, which are in reality products of material life, appear within ideology as life's producers and rulers. The metaphor's precision matters: a projected image is real, and its inversion is lawful, produced by the apparatus rather than by anyone's stupidity. Ideological distortion is therefore systematic and explicable, and it persists while the apparatus, the division between mental and material labour, persists.

### Q: What does the ruling ideas passage mean?

The passage claims that the ideas of the ruling class are in every epoch the ruling ideas, because the class that controls the means of material production also controls the means of mental production: presses, pulpits, universities, and patronage. Its subject is distribution, not truth. The ruling ideas need not be false, and the passage does not claim that subordinate classes absorb them whole. Its sharpest sub-claim concerns form: a rising class must present its particular interest as the common interest of all, dressing sectional claims in universal costume, and that dressing, rather than deception, is the ideological operation the passage exposes.

### Q: Where did the word ideology come from?

The word was coined at the end of the eighteenth century by the French philosopher Antoine Destutt de Tracy, who proposed it as the name for a science of ideas, a systematic study of how ideas arise from sensation and combine into judgements. Napoleon then turned the coinage into an insult, blaming the ideologues for the impractical abstractions of revolutionary politics. Marx inherited the word with the insult attached, which is why his earliest use is pejorative, aimed at German philosophers he accused of mistaking their concepts for the world. The scientific ambition and the sneer both survive in later usage.

### Q: Is ideology deliberate deception?

No, and the point is central to Marx's account. The ideologists of The German Ideology are sincere: professional thinkers who genuinely believe the systems they produce, formed by institutions that reward that belief and insulate it from the corrections of material practice. A doctrine maintained by cynics would collapse on exposure, whereas doctrines maintained by believers reproduce themselves through every school and pulpit staffed by the convinced. Even the appearances analysed in Capital deceive nobody deliberately, since they arise from the practice of exchange itself. Deception exists in politics, under the names of lying and propaganda, but ideology names a deeper and less voluntary phenomenon.

### Q: Can Marxism itself be an ideology?

The answer depends on the sense, which is why the question is the standard test of the sense-first rule. In the neutral sense of the 1859 Preface, yes, harmlessly: Marxism functions as a form in which social conflict becomes conscious, as every articulated worldview does. In the positional sense, the question becomes whose self-consciousness it is, and Lenin answered by embracing socialist ideology as a positive possession. In the pejorative sense, the tradition has offered three defences against self-conviction: the standpoint reply, the claim of a break between science and ideology, and the partiality reply, which concedes conditioning and denies that conditioning entails falsity.

### Q: Is ideology in people's heads or in institutions?

Marx's texts support both locations because the senses distribute the phenomenon differently. The illusion sense analyses consciousness, the structure of thought in professional thinkers cut off from practice. The ruling ideas sense analyses an infrastructure of production and distribution, presses, schools, and churches. Althusser developed the institutional location furthest, arguing that ideology has a material existence in apparatuses and rituals, that people enact their beliefs in practices before professing them, and that schools and families form subjects as reliably as doctrines form opinions. A complete analysis follows the belief from the institution that produces it to the head that holds it and back again.

### Q: How does ideology help reproduce class rule?

Chiefly without needing to be believed in full. Ruling ideas set the vocabulary in which public questions are posed, so alternatives must argue in terms furnished by the order they challenge. Institutions of mental production amplify outlooks compatible with the dominant class and starve others of means. Forms like the wage and the contract present unequal relations as exchanges between equals, doing their concealing work at the level of daily practice. Naturalisation converts chosen arrangements into apparent facts of nature, and fragmentation turns the antagonism of subordinate groups sideways against one another. Rule is reproduced by the compound of these mechanisms, not by any single doctrine's persuasiveness.

### Q: How does the camera obscura image explain Marx's view of ideology?

The image explains ideology as inversion with a cause. In a camera obscura the scene is genuinely present but upside down, and Marx's point is that thought about society can be genuinely about society while standing its relations on their heads, treating laws, states, and philosophies as the movers of a history they in fact reflect. Because the inversion is produced by an apparatus, the division of labour that separates professional thinking from material practice, criticism aimed only at the image leaves the apparatus working. The metaphor therefore converts a complaint about error into a research programme about the conditions that manufacture error reliably.

### Q: What should a student separate before calling a belief ideological?

Four separations earn their keep. Separate the belief's truth from its function, since a true belief can serve a class position and a false one can serve nobody. Separate the belief from its neighbours, error, lying, and propaganda, which have different structures and remedies. Separate the senses of ideology itself, and state which one the charge uses. And separate the evaluation of the belief from the explanation of it, conducting the evaluation first, on the merits, so that the genetic explanation answers a specified failure rather than replacing the argument. A charge that survives all four separations is analysis; one that skips them is atmosphere.

### Q: Why does ideology in the neutral sense not imply that a belief is false?

Because the neutral sense classifies rather than accuses. In the Preface of 1859, ideological forms are the legal, political, religious, artistic, and philosophical forms in which people become conscious of a conflict in society's economic foundations and fight it out. A medium cannot be false in itself, though particular messages carried by it can be. A courtroom is not true or false, though verdicts are; a theology is a language of struggle before it is a set of propositions. Calling a form ideological in this sense assigns it a place in the architecture of society, between the foundation it expresses and the consciousness that thinks within it.

### Q: How do ruling ideas relate to the division of labour in The German Ideology?

The division between material and mental labour is the mechanism that makes ruling ideas possible. Once a society supports a stratum whose occupation is the production of ideas, priests, jurists, philosophers, and officials, consciousness can appear to emancipate itself from practice, and systems can be built whose only material is other systems. That stratum attaches itself to the dominant class, which funds and distributes its product, so the ideas that saturate an epoch are produced by professionals and selected by power at once. The ruling ideas passage states the distribution; the division of labour passage explains the producers, and the two together form the manuscript's complete account.

### Q: What distinguishes ideology from a simple mistake or a deliberate lie?

Three things: its cause, its distribution, and its resistance to correction. A mistake is held for ordinary reasons and yields to information; a lie is known to be false by its maker and yields to exposure. An ideological belief is generated by a position within the division of labour or by the forms of social practice themselves, it is shared by a stratum or a class rather than idiosyncratic, and it survives information and exposure because the arrangement producing it continues to produce it. The diagnostic question is therefore genetic: not whether the belief is false, but what keeps manufacturing it in this form among these people.
