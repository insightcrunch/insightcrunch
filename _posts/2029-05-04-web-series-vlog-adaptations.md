Web series and vlog adaptations of Romeo and Juliet ask a precise question of the play: what happens to a tragedy built on public violence and private longing when the only stage is a webcam and the only audience is a subscription feed? The documented record gives a short, lopsided answer. Between 2012 and 2016 the literary web series became a proven adaptation form, with an Emmy at its professional end and festival honours in its school and student branches, yet the form's practitioners chose Austen's novels and Shakespeare's comedies again and again, and the whole era produced exactly one full vlog retelling of the Verona tragedy: *Jules and Monty*, an eighteen-episode series made by students at Tufts University in 2014. That imbalance is not an accident of taste. It is the format rendering a verdict on the play. The vlog is a soliloquy machine, the most exact device adaptation has ever found for the play's interior life, and it is structurally incapable of the play's public machinery: two households, a street, a feud, a tomb. Every documented exhibit in the InsightCrunch web-series survey confirms one half of that sentence or the other, and the single tragedy in the catalogue confirms both halves at once, succeeding for ten episodes of courtship and straining through eight episodes of fall.

![A student vlogger recording a video diary episode in a dorm room - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-101.webp)

The argument of this article is therefore a verdict with two clauses. First, the web series restored something the mainstream screen had spent a century thinning out: the experience of being alone with a character's mind while events gather elsewhere, which is the experience the play's soliloquies were built to produce. Second, the same form amputated the causal machinery that makes the play a tragedy rather than a romance with a sad ending, and *Jules and Monty* shows the amputation happening in its own episode map, its own shifts of camera grammar, and its own ending, in which nobody dies and a farewell video stands where the tomb should be. The critics divide over that ending, and the division is instructive. Myles McNutt, the television scholar who followed the series as it aired, treated the missing deaths as a puzzle of genre classification. The reviewer for PopOptiq, surveying the literary web series in 2014, treated the ending as a plain failure that left a bitter taste. Both readings are documented below, and the evidence adjudicates between them: the failure is real, but it is structural rather than a lapse of talent, and McNutt's frame explains the reviewer's experience better than the reviewer's frame explains the series.

## How The Lizzie Bennet Diaries Proved the Vlog Format

The vlog adaptation was not invented for Shakespeare, and its rules were fixed before any Shakespearean used it. *The Lizzie Bennet Diaries*, created by Hank Green and Bernie Su and produced by Pemberley Digital, ran on YouTube from April 9, 2012 to March 28, 2013, in one hundred episodes of between two and eight minutes. Lizzie Bennet, a graduate student, addressed the camera from her bedroom, reported the events of Jane Austen's *Pride and Prejudice* as diary entries, acted out the other characters in costume sketches with her sisters, and withheld her Darcy from the frame until episode sixty. The story also lived outside the videos: characters kept social accounts, answered followers in character, and let plot turns detonate between episodes in the audience's own feeds. In 2013 the series won the Primetime Emmy for Outstanding Creative Achievement in Interactive Media, Original Interactive Program, the first Emmy ever awarded to a web series, and the award honoured the interactive architecture as much as the drama. A form that had begun as a wager about attention had been certified by the oldest institution in American television.

### What exactly is a vlog adaptation?

A vlog adaptation retells a canonical story as a sequence of short video diaries, filmed as if the characters themselves were posting them, in period dress replaced by bedrooms and laptops. Events the camera cannot attend are reported by the narrator after the fact.

Other characters enter only when they visit the room, and the audience follows the posts in something close to the characters' own time. The novel or play underneath supplies the plot; the diary supplies the voice.

One device of the founding series deserves isolating, because every successor had to answer it. Lizzie Bennet, alone in her room, solved the problem of the absent interlocutor by performance: she re-enacted conversations in costume, playing her mother, her sisters and Darcy himself in sketches for the camera, a device the audience came to know as her costume theatre. The device is a confession of the form's limit disguised as a joke. A diary cannot admit a second voice, so the diarist impersonates the world, and every other character exists at the strength of her mimicry. *Nothing Much To Do* answered the device with multiplication, giving Benedick his own channel and the Watch its third, and *Jules and Monty* answered it with the braided pair of diaries, the first design in the form in which the beloved is not impersonated, reported or withheld but heard in the first person. The tragedy's adaptation is, in this one respect, the form's technical summit: nobody in the boom built a better instrument for two minds. The instrument simply could not also be a city.

The format's founding bargain deserves precision, because every later exhibit, *Jules and Monty* included, signed the same contract. A vlog series trades coverage for intimacy. The camera is confined to wherever a character plausibly records, which means the narrator's room, which means that anything happening elsewhere, a ball, a proposal, a duel, arrives as testimony rather than spectacle. Austen's novel could bear that trade almost without loss, because *Pride and Prejudice* already moves by report, letter and conversation; its great events are mostly things people say about things that happened. The epistolary skeleton under the novel made the translation nearly frictionless: a letter is a vlog that waits for the post. Pemberley Digital then industrialised the discovery. *Emma Approved*, developed by Bernie Su, ran seventy-two episodes from October 2013 to August 2014, recast Emma Woodhouse as a lifestyle coach whose professional self-display justified the camera, and won the same interactive Emmy. *Frankenstein, MD*, created by Bernie Su, Brett Register and Lon Harris with PBS Digital Studios, ran twenty-four episodes in the autumn of 2014 and bent the diary into a lab notebook, Victoria Frankenstein presenting her experiments as a science show, which carried Mary Shelley's novel of a mind narrating its own catastrophe with surprising aptness. Three prose sources, three full runs, one format: by the end of 2014 the vlog adaptation was a going concern with a house style, a proven audience and an awards shelf.

Two of the form's discoveries inside those runs matter for everything that follows. The first is that absence is a narrative resource a diary can spend like money. Lizzie's Darcy does not appear on camera until the sixtieth episode, and the withholding, sustained across months of uploads, converted a casting decision into the series' central suspense: the audience fell for a voice's report of a man long before the man was permitted a face. The spin-off vlogs granted to Lydia Bennet extended the lesson downward through the cast, proving that a secondary channel could carry plot the main diary could not witness, and the architecture of rival and subsidiary feeds entered the form's permanent toolkit. The second discovery is darker, and *Frankenstein, MD* states it plainly: a narrator who controls the record controls the story's moral weather. Victoria Frankenstein's science show presents her experiments as instruction, and the audience watches a catastrophe assemble inside a frame its narrator insists is educational. The diary form, that is, does not merely report events. It grants its keeper the power to characterise them, and every adaptation that uses the form inherits that power along with the intimacy.

*Jules and Monty* inherited both discoveries and inverted the first. Where Pemberley Digital withheld its hero for sixty episodes, the Tufts series puts both lovers on camera from the opening fortnight and braids their testimonies, because a tragedy of mutual ignorance cannot be told by one diarist: the form's scarcity, for this play, is not faces but spaces, rooms the lovers cannot share and events neither can witness whole. The second discovery the series inherited intact, and it powers the best of the middle episodes, in which Monty's cheerful record and Jules's guarded one diverge over the same days, and the audience, subscribed to both, watches two incompatible characterisations of one courtship harden into the conditions of a fall.

What the form had not faced was verse, and what it had not faced was a plot whose engine is public. Austen's dangers are social and internal: embarrassment, misjudgement, a compromised sister, a proposal refused. The camera in a bedroom can hold all of those, because all of them can be spoken. The Verona tragedy runs on a different fuel. Its Prologue promises "the two hours' traffic of our stage" (Prologue, line 12) and delivers brawls, a masked ball, a street killing, an exile, a sleeping potion and a double suicide in a vault, a sequence of events that happen to bodies in shared spaces, most of them witnessed by crowds. A form built to report events from a bedroom was always going to find this play the hardest canonical text on the shelf, and the production record shows that the form's pioneers, offered the whole of literature, walked past the tragedy for three years before a pair of sophomores picked it up as a writing exercise.

## Jules and Monty: The Tufts Production Record

*Jules and Monty* began, by its co-creator's account, as homework of the loosest kind. Imogen Browder, a sophomore at Tufts University, proposed a web series after watching *The Lizzie Bennet Diaries*, and with Edward Rosini, her fellow sophomore, she worked out the premise across what the Tufts Daily described as breakfast meetings: set the play at college, and see how the characters and the plot would change. Browder's own summary to the campus newspaper was disarming: "It kind of started as a writing exercise." The pair wrote the series and played the leads, Rosini as Romeo "Monty" Montgomery, a second-year student, Browder as Juliet "Jules" Caine, a first-year student, and they took the project to Tufts University Television, the campus station, which adopted it as its largest production in some time. Evey Reidy and Andy De Leon directed; Reidy also played Nancy Mills, the confidante. Eighteen episodes appeared on YouTube between March 10 and May 5, 2014, most of them between five and eight minutes long, which puts the complete series close to the length of a single stage performance of the play it adapts, the Prologue's own "two hours' traffic" (Prologue, line 12) recovered almost by accident.

### Why does the series frame the vlogs as a class assignment?

The vlogs exist because a communications course requires them: Monty announces in the first episode that the videos are a class project, and Jules is drawn into the same assignment through her roommate. The frame is set in the opening minutes and never leaves the series.

The assignment is the series' enabling fiction, the reason two private people keep recording themselves, and it quietly replaces the play's machinery of go-betweens with an institution, a syllabus, a deadline, a grade, that orders the lovers to keep testifying to the camera whether testifying is wise or not.

The setting translates the play's geography into campus jurisdiction. The lovers attend the fictional University of Verona, and the two houses become fraternities: Jules's family house is Kappa Alpha Psi, headed by her older brother Cliff, styled in the cast's own shorthand the Lord of Kappa, while Monty belongs to the rival Mu Tau Gamma. The feud is thus a Greek-row war of parties, pranks and honour, fought by young men whose allegiance is chosen at rush and enforced by house pride, which is a sharper translation than it first appears: fraternity loyalty, like the loyalty of the play's serving-men and cousins, is inherited by joining and defended without a stated cause. Around the pair the series builds the play's full complement in undergraduate dress. Mark Matthews, Monty's roommate, carries Mercutio's function and wit. Rose, Monty's girlfriend at the outset, gives Rosaline a face and a grievance the play never grants her. Nancy Mills, Jules's roommate and a nursing major, stands in the Nurse's place, the joke of the casting folded into her degree subject. Ty Johnson carries Tybalt's heat and family duty, Cliff absorbs old Capulet's authority and menace into an elder brother, Pierre, an exchange student from Paris, fills the County's slot as the approved suitor whose attentions curdle into something possessive, and Ben, Monty's other companion in the episode descriptions, holds Benvolio's place as the circle's would-be peacemaker. The Tufts Daily summarised the method exactly: part oratory narration, part found footage, a series that alternates between the lovers' two channels of testimony and scenes the camera happens to catch.

The making deserves a paragraph of its own, because the production model is part of the adaptation's meaning. The script was not a fixed text handed to a crew: the Tufts Daily's account describes drafts circulating among the station's writers, a substantial collaborative rewrite, and Andy De Leon, the station's head of scripts, carrying the material to the wider membership before production. The station supplied what the pair could not, cameras, lighting, crew, and its director of photography, a first-year student named Ben Taylor, called the series the station's biggest production in a while and its flagship for demonstrating what the campus could make. The timing sharpened the experiment: the same semester, the university's Experimental College was running a course on YouTube as a business and creative practice, so the campus was simultaneously studying the platform as curriculum and using it as a stage. No earlier adaptation of the play had been made inside an institution that was also, in another classroom, theorising the medium of its release. The series' makers were, in the strictest sense, examined on their method while they practised it, and the double position suits the material oddly well: a play about young people performing assigned roles inside an institution, remade by young people performing an assignment inside one.

Two production facts govern everything the series achieves and everything it fails to achieve. The first is the alternation: Browder and Rosini narrate in turn, so the story reaches the audience as two diaries braided together, each lover holding the camera in private and neither holding the whole plot. The second is the hybrid camera. The reviewers noticed it at once: where the diary form would oblige the series to report its crises after the fact, *Jules and Monty* repeatedly breaks its own frame and films pivotal action directly, in found-footage style, with a moving camera across multiple locations. PopOptiq's reviewer, no friend of the series' second half, conceded that it took more risks than its peers on precisely this point. The concession matters, because the moments where the series abandons the vlog are the moments where the play's machinery engages: a party that goes wrong in episode eleven, a confrontation in episode fourteen that the creators prefaced with a content warning. The series' episode map, read cold, already contains the article's verdict in miniature. The first ten episodes, the courtship, live inside the diary form and flourish there. The last eight, the fall, keep escaping the form, and the escapes grow longer as the plot grows heavier.

The verse survives in the series under a rationing rule that reviewers documented with delight. Reviewers of the series noted that Shakespeare's language surfaces at chosen pressure points, when the characters are in love or drunk, as if the pentameter were a substance the modern undergraduates could only metabolise with lowered inhibitions. Scout Magazine's listing treasured the spectacle of Mark, drunk, delivering Mercutio's taunt from the play, "Come, come, thou art as hot a Jack in thy mood as any in Italy" (3.1), to a party crowd. The device is a small masterpiece of adaptation logic. What the films of the play's teen descendants achieved by setting, and what Baz Luhrmann's film achieved by stylisation, the series achieves by intoxication: the verse is marked as the language people fall into when their guard is down, which is a defensible theory of the play itself, whose lovers speak their truest lines when the social self is suspended, in a garden at night, in a bedroom at dawn, in a vault beyond society altogether.

## The Episode Map: Eighteen Posts Across Two Months

A survey can grade a series; only the episode map can show where the load-bearing walls stand. The documented descriptions of the eighteen episodes, kept by the episode guides and the contemporary reviews, trace a shape that no summary quite captures. The first episode, released on March 10, 2014, is Monty's alone: a halting introduction in which he names himself, calls the assignment a class project and a blog thing, and promises to settle on a name later. The comedy of the frame is the point. Romeo enters the play mid-sentence in a love affair with Rosaline and a quarrel with the morning; Monty enters his series apologising to a lens, and the series' whole method, testimony in place of action, is confessed in the apology. Jules arrives in the second episode, and the guides record the detail the reviewers kept returning to: she is camera-shy, a Juliet who must learn to be looked at, introduced not at a feast but through her roommate's enthusiasm for the same class project. The feud's first skirmish in the series is thus an argument about whether to post at all.

The middle of the map belongs to the form working at full strength. Mark hijacks Monty's channel in one early episode, announcing in its description that there are things Monty would never tell the camera and that the assignment requires them to be seen: Mercutio as an unauthorised editor, the friend who understands the medium better than its keeper and treats the diary as a stage that can be stolen. The ninth episode, as McNutt noted, tells most of the courtship through montage, the lovers' shared footage assembled into the relationship neither diary could narrate alone, the closest the form comes to the shared sonnet of the feast. And an episode from the later run preserves the series' most quoted description, written by the friend who posted it: the lovers had left the camera running one morning, and after the pain of the surrounding weeks the poster judged that everyone could use a little brightness. The aubade of Act 3, the lovers' dawn debate over the lark and the nightingale, survives here as found footage and an editor's mercy, the play's most private duet recovered by the one device the diary form possesses for two voices, an accident of recording.

The fall occupies the map's last third and changes its grammar. Episode eleven is aftermath: its description speaks in a friend's alarm, reporting a party that went wrong in ways nobody expected, Nancy brought home, hopes expressed for Monty and for Ty. The event itself, the series' equivalent of the street fight that kills Mercutio and Tybalt, reaches the record as report and worry, the diary form handling the catastrophe exactly as Austen's form taught it to, at one remove, in the past tense. Episode fourteen, released on April 21, is the confrontation between Cliff and Jules, posted with its description in the voice of the friend who released the footage and hoped to be forgiven for it, some things being impossible to leave unseen. The warning the team attached to that episode belongs to the map as surely as any plot point: the series' midpoint is the moment its makers publicly renegotiated the terms on which violence could enter a diary. The eighteenth episode, on May 5, closes the map with Jules's packed boxes and her farewell. Between the first apology and the last goodbye stand eight weeks of uploads, a complete tragedy processed through a school assignment, and the map's proportions are the reviewer's proportions: ten episodes of ascent inside the form, eight of descent against it.

The Tufts Daily's summary of the method, part oratory narration and part found footage, names a distinction the map makes visible at every turn, and the distinction is evidentiary. A vlog testifies: a speaker, after the event, in a chosen frame, tells the subscribers what happened and what it meant, with all the unreliability the play assigns to its reporters, the Nurse's embroidery, Benvolio's partial account of the street fight, the Friar's self-exculpating narrative to the Prince. Found footage witnesses: the camera was present, the event unfolded inside the frame, and the audience judges the bodies directly. The play keeps the two kinds of knowledge rigorously apart, testimony for the interior, spectacle for the public act, and assigns its catastrophe to the gap between them. The series, obliged by its premise to prefer testimony, keeps being driven back to witness at the exact points where the play is public: the party, the confrontation, the morning after. Read as a ratio, the map is a graph of the tragedy's resistance. The courtship runs at nearly pure testimony, two voices and a montage. The fall runs at a rising proportion of witnessed footage, as if the plot were physically expelling the diary from its own series. No other adaptation of the play wears its central problem so legibly in its form, and the legibility is a gift to criticism: the argument of this article could be drawn, almost without prose, from the map's changing ratio alone.

The calendar deserves its own sentence, because serial rhythm is one of the form's unexamined instruments. Eighteen episodes across the eight weeks from March 10 to May 5 means the story arrived in instalments of roughly two a week, each a few minutes long, separated by days in which the audience lived alongside the characters' silence. The play compresses its catastrophe into five days of stage time and famously breaks its own chronology doing it; the series stretches the same catastrophe across two months of real time and lets the audience age with it. Suspense in the theatre is a held breath. Suspense in a feed is a subscription maintained, and the difference explains the map's emotional physics: the courtship, which improves with duration, luxuriates in the intervals, while the fall, which depends on momentum and the terrible speed of the play's last acts, bleeds pressure at every gap between uploads. A form that releases its tragedy in instalments has already decided, before any ending is chosen, that the catastrophe will arrive as a series of reports rather than a blow.

## The Soliloquy Machine: What Direct Address Does to the Play

The deepest affinity between the vlog and this play is not technological but structural. Shakespeare's tragedy is, to a degree unusual even among his works, a machine for producing soliloquy. Juliet alone carries some of the longest private speeches in the canon: the epithalamion of Act 3, "Gallop apace" in most modern paraphrase, where she begs the night to come and imagines Romeo cut "out in little stars" at his death (3.2); the potion soliloquy of Act 4, where she interrogates her own terror line by line, "What if it be a poison which the friar / Subtly hath minister'd to have me dead" (4.3, lines 24 to 25), before drinking on a reasoning process the audience has been forced to share. Romeo answers in kind, from the garden soliloquies of Act 2 to the defiance of Act 5, "Is it even so? Then I defy you, stars!" (5.1, line 24). On the early modern stage these speeches were delivered outward, to a crowd standing in daylight, and the convention made the audience the character's confidant by default. Four centuries of darkened theatres and proscenium frames slowly converted that confidence into eavesdropping: the modern audience overhears a soliloquy, where the first audience received one.

The vlog reverses the conversion. A vlogger speaks alone, to a lens, in a room, for an audience that will receive the speech privately, on a phone, at the moment of choosing to press play. Direct address is not a style in the form; it is the form's entire ontology, and it reproduces the soliloquy's original contract with a fidelity no stage revival can match. Subscribers are confidants by subscription. They are told what the narrator tells nobody else in the story, they hold knowledge the other characters lack, and they are addressed as intimates while remaining powerless to intervene, which is exactly the emotional position the play constructs for the witnesses of Juliet's potion speech. When *Jules and Monty* alternates its narration between the two lovers, it builds the play's signature effect into its architecture: the audience alternates confidants, holding Jules's fears and Monty's hopes in parallel, watching two diaries converge on a catastrophe that neither diarist can see whole. Dramatic irony, which the stage achieves by showing the audience more than the characters know, the series achieves by subscription, granting the audience both channels and each character only one.

### How does the balcony scene change when the window becomes a webcam?

The balcony scene is already a scene about mediated address: Romeo speaks from hiding to a woman who believes she is alone, and Juliet's "O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?" (2.2, lines 33 to 34) is a soliloquy overheard, not a speech delivered. The webcam performs the same geometry with the signs reversed.

The vlogger believes the audience is absent, a lens, a future, and speaks with a candour the character would never risk in company, while the audience, present at every word, occupies Romeo's place in the orchard, unseen, unanswering, falling in love with a voice that does not know it is heard. A full reading of [the balcony scene in Act 2 Scene 2, its language and staging](/2023/07/21/balcony-scene-act-2-scene-2-analysis/) shows how much of its charge depends on that asymmetry, and the web series is the first adaptation form to rebuild the asymmetry as its default setting rather than as a single night scene.

The gains register most in Jules herself. The series' reviewers singled out the arc of Browder's performance in terms that map precisely onto the soliloquy argument: the Jules of the second episode is camera-shy, halting, uncomfortable under the lens, and across eighteen episodes she grows fluent, composed, determined to finish the record she started. Juliet's play traces the same arc in five acts, from the girl who speaks barely a dozen lines in her first scene to the woman who reasons her way alone through the potion speech. The vlog gives that growth a visible instrument: competence at the camera is competence at selfhood, and the series can show Juliet's education happening in the grain of the form, episode by episode, without a line of exposition. Monty's corresponding gift is the play's other interior register, improvisation. Rosini's reviewers praised a goofy, unwhining Romeo, a lover whose diary is funnier and less hopeless than the text's, and the diary form licenses the difference: a Romeo who performs himself for an imagined audience is a Romeo whose melancholy has somewhere to go, converted into bits, riffs and roommate banter with Mark, until the plot removes the audience he was performing for.

The braided diary also solves, quietly, the play's most persistent staging problem: Romeo's disappearances. The text keeps its hero offstage for the whole of Act 4 and strands him in Mantua for the catastrophe's approach, and productions routinely feel the sag of a tragedy whose male lead has been reduced to receiving news. A two-channel diary cannot lose its hero in that way. Banishment, in the series' grammar, is simply the condition of testifying from somewhere else, and the exile stretch of the plot, which the stage must fill with the Friar's errands and the fathers' arrangements, the series fills with the banished man's own record of banishment. The form thereby promotes the play's most passive act into testimony, and it is no accident that the farewell which ends the series belongs to Jules rather than Monty: the braided structure had spent eighteen episodes teaching the audience to receive catastrophe from whichever lover the plot had isolated, and the final isolation is hers. Against that, the form's handling of the play's third great interior voice should be recorded as a demotion. The Nurse's garrulity, those tidal speeches of memory and evasion in Acts 1 and 2, is the play's prose equivalent of a feed, one consciousness unspooling association in real time, and Nancy's vlogs inherit the function at reduced pressure, because a peer's chatter, however warm, cannot carry the Nurse's freight of a whole adult life spent inside another family's house.

Against these gains, one loss is recorded in the same reviews, and it is the hinge of the whole adaptation. The lovers of the play meet inside a shared sonnet, fourteen lines exchanged mouth to mouth at the Capulet feast, "If I profane with my unworthiest hand / This holy shrine, the gentle sin is this" (1.5, lines 92 to 93), a form that makes two voices into one artefact. A diary form cannot do that. Two vloggers can answer each other across episodes, quote each other, stitch and respond, but the form's atom is the single voice in the single room, and its love scenes are therefore correspondences, not encounters. *Jules and Monty* papers over the gap with montage, most fully in its ninth episode, where the relationship is told through assembled fragments, and with the found-footage excursions that let the lovers share a frame. The papering is skilful and the reviewers liked the montage. Yet the structural fact stands: the form that perfects the play's solitude cannot natively stage its union, and the series must keep leaving its own form to let its lovers be together.

## The Machinery Problem: Feud, Households and the Fight the Camera Cannot Hold

Everything the vlog perfects is private. Everything the tragedy needs in its second half is public, plural and causal. The play's catastrophe is not produced by feelings but by machinery: a feud that supplies violence on schedule, two households that supply authority and surveillance, a friar whose plan has moving parts, a postal system that fails. The feud in particular is not an atmosphere but a workforce, serving-men, cousins, retainers, a prince with a statute, and a reading of [the feud between the Capulets and Montagues, why the houses hate and how the hatred organises Verona](/2022/04/01/capulet-montague-feud-explained/) shows a civic system, not a mood. The diary form has no crowd. Its world is a corridor of bedrooms, each containing one speaker, and the households that crush the play's lovers can enter that corridor only one visitor at a time, as guests in the frame.

### Why does the feud need more rooms than a dormitory has?

A feud is a relation between groups, and groups are the one thing a vlog cannot photograph. The camera can hold a narrator and a visitor, perhaps a roommate wandering through the background, but a house, a faction, a street full of armed kinsmen exceeds the frame by definition.

Fraternity war is the series' ingenious compression of the problem, substituting rush, parties and house honour for bloodline, yet even the compression must be reported more often than shown, because a party with two houses in full cry is precisely the scene the diary form cannot afford to stage.

The compression works while the feud is a premise and fails as the feud becomes a plot. In the early episodes the fraternities function as the lovers' social weather, the reason the romance is deliciously forbidden, and a vlog can carry weather in a sentence. From the party episode onward, the feud must do the play's causal work: injuries must be real, alliances must harden, Cliff's authority must become coercion, Pierre's suit must become a threat with a deadline. The series responds by reaching for its second camera grammar, the found footage of the party gone wrong in episode eleven, where the episode's own description, written in a friend's alarmed voice, hopes that Monty is all right, and Ty too. Violence, when it comes, comes as footage rather than testimony, because testimony would shrink it to anecdote. The pattern culminates in episode fourteen, in which Cliff confronts Jules and strikes her. The creative team prefaced the episode with a content warning, and Browder defended the decision in public, arguing in substance that a team which had felt the scene's force in the room owed its audience a warning before a montage-shaped expectation was broken by an assault. The exchange, documented by McNutt, is the format's conscience made visible: a form that recruits its audience as confidants acquires obligations that a stage, which recruits witnesses, does not. Nobody warns the groundlings. The Prince's statute has no trigger warning. The vlog's intimacy is a contract, and the contract limits what the form may do with the play's brutality at the very moment the plot requires brutality to escalate.

Beneath the feud sits a deeper incompatibility, and it is mechanical rather than visual. The play's catastrophe turns on a failed delivery: Friar John's letter, quarantined in a plague house, never reaches Mantua, and Romeo buys his poison on the strength of a servant's accurate report of a staged funeral. The tragedy is, at its hinge, a story about a message that does not arrive. A platform built for delivery abolishes that hinge. A posted video is published to every subscriber at once; a message in a feed carries its timestamp and its evidence; nothing in the world of the vlog can be both sent and unseen. The form therefore cannot import the play's accident and must re-motor its catastrophe from mishap to decision, from a letter lost to a relationship abandoned, which is why the series' ending is a departure rather than a misunderstanding at a tomb. Estrangement is the only irreversible act a feed cannot undo with a follow-up post, and even that irreversibility is borrowed, since a channel can always resume. The play's machinery runs on the friction of a world where words travel at walking pace and arrive too late. The web series inhabits a world where words arrive instantly and are answered in the comments, and in that world the tragedy has to find another engine or stop, which, at eighteen episodes, is what it honourably does.

The subsidiary machinery fails in quieter ways that the survey makes legible. The play's Friar is a one-man postal service, pharmacy and confessional, and the series has no equivalent figure, because no undergraduate institution concentrates those powers; the communications course, which orders the testimony, is the nearest device, an authority that joins the lovers by assigning them the same task and endangers them by making the record public. Paris, in the play a suitor with almost no interior life, exposes the form's selection effect from the other side: a vlog adaptation can only cast characters who could plausibly hold a camera and a confidence, and Pierre survives as a presence in other people's footage, dwindling, as one reviewer complained, into a creeper at the edge of the story. The form does not merely abridge the play's population. It silently recasts the play as a world in which only the introspective exist at full size, which is a fair description of the internet and a falsification of Verona.

The households themselves shrink in the translation, and the shrinkage is documented in every cast account the production left behind: no parent of either lover appears in them. Old Capulet's authority devolves onto an elder brother, Cliff, whose power over Jules is real but fraternal, a seniority of years and house office rather than the play's absolute paternal jurisdiction, and old Montague vanishes from the record entirely. The change reads as campus realism, since undergraduates do live beyond their parents' daily reach, and it quietly disarms the tragedy's cruelest mechanism. The play's Juliet is crushed between a father's command and a friar's gamble, with the whole weight of a household economy behind the command: a marriage market, an inheritance, a name. Cliff can strike his sister, and episode fourteen makes the blow land, but he cannot marry her off, disinherit her or set a Thursday deadline with a count; the institutional depth that makes the play's coercion fatal is precisely what a student world cannot reproduce, and the series' catastrophe is correspondingly demoted from doom to rupture. What the feud loses in the dormitory is not noise or numbers. It loses jurisdiction.

One further absence completes the machinery audit: the Prince. Escalus is the play's purely public voice, a figure who exists only as sentence and statute, opening the action with a law and closing it with a judgement, and he has no diary equivalent, because authority of his kind does not confess, vlog or explain itself in a bedroom. The series' world is correspondingly stateless, its disputes refereed by no power above the fraternities, and the place the Prince occupies in the play is filled, in the series' real existence, by the platform itself: the host that distributes the testimony, counts its audience, attaches its warnings and can, in principle, remove the whole record. The parallel is worth stating exactly. Verona's prince fails to keep his peace and arrives, twice, too late, to pronounce over the consequences. The platform likewise neither prevents nor redeems anything in the story; it merely hosts, measures and preserves, and its judgements, the view counts McNutt recorded, the recommendation lists, the library guides, are delivered after the fact, over a closed channel, in the Prince's own posture of belated summary. The form did not abolish the play's authority. It outsourced the office to an infrastructure that performs it as statistics.

The comparison with the play's other modern descendants sharpens the point. The teen films that descend from the play, surveyed in the series' account of [the play's modern teen-film descendants and their high-school Veronas](/2029/04/27/teen-film-descendants/), could keep the machinery by keeping the crowd: a school is a city in miniature, with corridors for streets and cliques for houses. The web series traded the crowd for the confidant. What it bought with the trade is the subject of the sections on either side of this one; what it paid is measured in the ending, where the machinery has to produce a catastrophe and finds, instead of a tomb, a door closing.

## The Ending: A Tragedy Without Death

Every adaptation of the play is finally judged at the tomb, and the survey in this article is organised so that each exhibit's ending testifies. *Jules and Monty* ends on May 5, 2014, with an eighteenth episode whose description is written in Jules's own hand: the boxes are packed, she is leaving the university for good, a few loose ends remain, and the sign-off thanks the internet for the fun. Nobody dies. McNutt, writing on the day the finale appeared, named the fact with a classifier's precision: the series follows the tragic narrative of the play, albeit without death. Scout Magazine's later listing called the finale still tragic, still a little improbable, but open-ended, and suited to the new setting. PopOptiq's reviewer, writing two months after the finale, delivered the prosecution: the team plainly felt unable to stray so far from the source as to give the lovers a happy ending, and the thing chosen instead left a bitter taste in the mouth.

The disagreement is genuine and worth adjudicating slowly, because the ending is where the format's whole logic comes due. Consider what the play's ending requires: two bodies, a vault, a watch, two households and a prince assembled over the corpses, and a settlement spoken aloud, "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3, lines 309 to 310). The catastrophe is public property; its meaning is manufactured by the crowd that survives it. A vlog series can supply none of that assembly. Its stage is a bedroom and its public is a feed, and a suicide recorded for a class assignment does not merely strain credulity; it violates the contract the series had spent eighteen episodes signing with its audience, the contract visible in the episode fourteen warning, under which the confidants in the feed are owed care rather than spectacle. Friar Laurence's warning, "These violent delights have violent ends" (2.6, line 9), is a line the format can quote but cannot obey, because the violent end is the one exhibit the diary cannot post.

The reviewer's subsidiary complaint, that eighteen episodes should have been half or twice as many, deserves a verdict too, since it is the one most often repeated against the series. Twice the length would not have saved the ending. The machinery problem compounds with scale: more episodes of a feud are more parties, more confrontations, more footage fleeing the diary, until the series becomes the television drama its budget could not support. Half the length would have saved the ending's logic at the courtship's expense, cutting the ten strong episodes that justify the experiment. Eighteen episodes is not a miscalculation. It is the natural size of this plot in this form, the point at which testimony about a courtship is complete and testimony about a catastrophe has not yet begun to repeat itself, and the reviewer's arithmetic, like the reviewer's bitterness, describes the form while aiming at the team.

What the series posts instead is banishment, generalised to both lovers and softened into departure. Romeo's exile is the one catastrophe in the play that consists purely of absence, and absence is the native catastrophe of a feed: a channel that stops, a face that leaves the frame, a last video whose description reads like a note on a door. Jules's farewell converts the tomb into a final upload, and the emotional fact the play buries in a vault, that the lovers' world ends, is preserved in the only currency the form holds, the end of the record. The farewell also surrenders the play's second ending, the one that follows the deaths: the reconciliation of the houses. Shakespeare's catastrophe is civic before it is private; the bodies in the vault purchase a settlement, Montague offering his hand and a statue in pure gold, Capulet answering in kind, the Prince closing the account over both. The settlement requires the corpses as its currency, since nothing less has ever been able to stop the feud, and it is spoken by the fathers, in public, over the evidence. A series without deaths and without fathers has nothing to settle with and nobody authorised to settle. The fraternities of the University of Verona are never reconciled in the record; the feud does not end, it is abandoned, losing its object when Jules leaves the jurisdiction. The difference is the whole distance between the play's world and the feed's. In Verona, private catastrophe is converted, at terrible cost, into public peace. In the series, private catastrophe is converted into an exit, and the public world, the houses, the campus, the comment threads, continues unreconciled around the empty channel. Modern audiences have often found the play's golden statues grotesque as a price for peace. The series discovers the opposite discomfort: a world in which the price is never paid because the currency, witnessed death, is the one thing the medium cannot mint.

Judged as dramaturgy, the substitution is intelligent, and Scout's verdict of tragic but open-ended registers its intelligence fairly. Judged as tragedy, the reviewer's bitterness also registers something real: the play's ending is not sad because the lovers are separated but because the separation is irreversible and witnessed, and a departure leaves both properties behind. Jules can come back next semester. Juliet cannot. The series knows the difference, and the knowledge is why its last episodes feel, in the reviewer's phrase, like a fall that never quite lands.

The ending's reception history adds a footnote that cuts in the series' favour. In the years after the finale, the recommendation lists that mapped the literary web series kept returning to the Tufts production with a formula that amounts to a scarcity verdict: Scout Magazine's survey of the field remarked that a list of the genre would not be complete without at least one adaptation of this play, and supplied *Jules and Monty* as the one. Mic's fourteen-series census of literary YouTube reached the same shelf by the same road. The series had become, by default of the whole industry, the representative of its play in the medium, a position no other student production of the era occupies for any canonical text. Scarcity is not quality, and the article's verdict does not pretend otherwise. But scarcity on this scale is evidence about the form, not about the eighteen episodes: when an entire adaptation economy produces one specimen of a kind, the specimen's compromises describe the economy's limits, and the farewell that disappointed the reviewer is the most informative ending in the survey precisely because a stronger team, a larger budget and a longer run would have met the same wall at the same place, the moment a diary is asked to contain a tomb.

The adjudication, then: McNutt is right about the cause and the reviewer is right about the effect, and the cause explains the effect so completely that the two verdicts collapse into one. The final eight episodes are weaker than the first ten not because the student team tired, the acting, by every account, grew stronger as the camera work grew more assured, but because the plot's centre of gravity moved from the territory the form owns, two minds in two rooms, to the territory the form rents, parties, violence, family coercion, consequence. A tragedy is a machine for converting private feeling into public wreckage. The vlog is a machine for the reverse conversion. *Jules and Monty* ran the play through the second machine and produced, predictably and honourably, a romance with the wreckage reported, warned about, and finally replaced by a goodbye. That is not a happy ending, and it is not the play's ending. It is the format's ending, and the survey's other exhibits show the same signature wherever a source's catastrophe had to be posted rather than staged.

The sequel record closes the argument from the far side. Comedy worlds in the boom kept going: the Candle Wasters carried the *Nothing Much To Do* company forward into a university sequel, and Pemberley Digital's audience followed Austen from one novel to the next. *Jules and Monty* has no second season in its record. Its makers, rebranded as Neat-O Productions, made other series, an original genre piece called *Wavejacked* and a Greek-mythology campus adaptation, *Pantheon University*, and McNutt's 2017 account of their trajectory treats the Verona series as the finished first chapter of a student career rather than an interrupted run. Rosini's own professional record adds the fitting coda: he and Browder went on to adapt Shakespeare plays for the Boston Shakespeare Project, work that carried a fellowship and a teaching troupe performing for schoolchildren. The tragedy's makers ended where the series itself ended, in a classroom. A feed can in principle continue any world indefinitely, and the comedies used that licence; the tragedy's world was a countdown disguised as a diary, and when the diary closed, the world had been spent. The form's open-endedness, its most advertised virtue, turns out to be a property of happy stories.

## Why the Boom Chose Austen and the Comedies

The asymmetry in the catalogue is stark enough to demand explanation. In the boom years the form produced, at the professional end, one hundred episodes of *Pride and Prejudice*, seventy-two of *Emma* and twenty-four of *Frankenstein*, and at the independent end a single New Zealand company, the Candle Wasters, produced three complete Shakespeare series in three years: *Nothing Much To Do* in 2014, seventy-six episodes retelling *Much Ado About Nothing* across three interlinked channels from a New Zealand high school; *Lovely Little Losers* in 2015, carrying the same company of characters to university inside *Love's Labour's Lost*; and *Bright Summer Night* in 2016, compressing *A Midsummer Night's Dream* into a single Wellington house party. Against that shelf stands one tragedy, eighteen episodes long, made by sophomores as a writing exercise. The web did not lack ambition or Shakespeare. It lacked, specifically, the tragedies, and the reasons are written into the sources themselves.

### Why did the Candle Wasters build three series from comedies and none from a tragedy?

The Candle Wasters' documented Shakespeare slate is three comedies, and the pattern is a rational reading of the form by its most decorated practitioners. Comedy's catastrophes are social, a shaming, a broken vow, a night of mistaken identity, and social catastrophe is reportable: it can be narrated to a camera the morning after, which is the vlog's only tense.

Tragedy's catastrophes are physical and final, and a form limited to testimony must either shrink them into anecdote or break its frame to film them, the two failures that mark the last third of *Jules and Monty*.

Begin with the sources' raw materials. Austen's novels are built from the very units the vlog consumes: letters, visits, conversations, revisions of judgement delivered in a single voice. Her narrators already behave like vloggers, confiding assessments to an intimate audience while the plot happens in drawing rooms the audience rarely enters. The comedies Shakespeare wrote for a speaking world behave similarly. *Much Ado About Nothing* is a play about report, eavesdropping, slander and reputation, a plot in which the central catastrophe, Hero's disgrace, is an act of narration performed at a wedding and undone by a watchman's overheard account. Jane Wanninger, presenting the series to the Shakespeare Association of America, described the vlog as a participatory visual narrative form and *Nothing Much To Do* as a particularly successful translation of the play into a teen-driven medium, and the success is overdetermined: a play about people talking about each other, adapted into a form of people talking about each other, loses almost nothing in transit. Even Hero's feigned death, the comedy's one excursion to the tomb, is a death by press release, a fiction circulated to produce repentance, and a feed is the ideal instrument for circulating it.

The wider shelf of the wave tells the same story in a different accent. When PopOptiq surveyed the literary web series in the summer of 2014, the five productions worth reviewing were *The Lizzie Bennet Diaries*, *Emma Approved*, *Jules and Monty* and two more prose adaptations: *The New Adventures of Peter and Wendy*, which set Barrie's Peter Pan in small-town Ohio with Wendy as a columnist, and *AnnewithanE*, drawn from *Anne of Green Gables*. Four novels and a novel-derived fantasy against one verse tragedy: the wave's centre of gravity was prose fiction, and specifically the prose of courtship, growing up and small communities, stories whose worlds fit inside a bedroom and a feed. Shakespeare entered the boom through comedy's side door, carried by a school company in New Zealand, and the single attempt on the tragedy came from the same amateur stratum, a campus station rather than a funded studio. No professional producer of the era, not Pemberley Digital with its Emmys, not PBS Digital Studios with its first scripted commission, chose a Shakespeare play at all. The professionals chose Shelley and Austen. The canon the web actually adapted was the canon of the confiding narrator, and Shakespeare's most confiding plays are his comedies of talk.

The tragedy's raw material points the other way. Its language is the first obstacle. A prose source can be paraphrased into dorm-room speech without a visible seam; the play's verse announces itself in every line, and a vlog that keeps it whole sounds like a recitation, while a vlog that drops it whole sounds like a different work. *Jules and Monty* found the rationing solution, verse at the pressure points, drunkenness and desire, and *Nothing Much To Do* chose the opposite pole, modern language throughout with the play's wit rebuilt as banter and musical numbers. Both solutions are admissions that the verse cannot simply be carried. Stephen O'Neill, whose *Shakespeare and YouTube* appeared in 2014 as the boom crested, framed the platform's Shakespeare as a field of user-generated abundance shadowed by a reduced Bard, the plays surviving as clips, speeches, auditions and fragments more readily than as wholes. The series record bears the frame out with unusual neatness: YouTube filled with the play's speeches, balcony scenes performed to webcams in bedrooms across the world, while the play entire was attempted once, by the only team willing to accept the reduction as the price of the attempt.

Luke McKernan, the British Library's curator of newsfilm and the Candle Wasters' most persistent institutional champion, called their work a new form of Shakespearean production that needed to be championed, fresh, illuminating and connected to a young audience by the medium itself. The honours followed the comedies: *Bright Summer Night* took Best Drama at the 2017 Hollyweb Festival and a Young Jury Award at the Carbello Interplay Festival in 2018, *Lovely Little Losers* drew a Best Web Series nomination at the 2016 NZ Web Fest, and the team was named SPADA's New Filmmaker of the Year for 2017. No comparable honours attach to the record of the era's single tragedy. The festival circuit, like the format, rewarded what the form does well. None of this diminishes *Jules and Monty*; it locates the series correctly, as the control experiment of the boom, the one production that measured the form against the hardest canonical plot and published the measurements.

## Who Pays for a Vlog Shakespeare?

The episode counts in the survey look like measures of ambition until they are read as measures of patronage, and the economics of the boom explain the tragedy's thinness as precisely as the poetics do. Pemberley Digital was a professional shop: its flagship's audience was large enough that a Kickstarter campaign for a DVD edition, seeking sixty thousand dollars, passed its goal in under six hours and raised, by a team member's account, almost half a million. *Emma Approved* was made in association with Kin Community, a commercial network for women creators, and *Frankenstein, MD* brought a broadcaster into the form, PBS Digital Studios staking its first scripted series on the format and on Pemberley Digital's house style. The Candle Wasters began as students and were professionalised by their own success: *Bright Summer Night* was crowdfunded on Kickstarter to a little over twenty-five thousand dollars from four hundred and fifty backers, and the company's later work drew funding from New Zealand on Air and Google, the standard patrons of a national screen culture. Each of these arrangements is a mission statement wearing a budget. A public broadcaster funds education, a national agency funds youth culture at home, a commercial network funds an audience it can sell, and a fandom funds the world it wants continued.

*Jules and Monty* was funded by a campus television station's existing infrastructure: cameras, lights, crew and a channel, the in-kind patronage of a university. The distinction shows in the artefact. Eighteen episodes against one hundred is not chiefly a difference of nerve; it is a difference of payroll, of how many weeks of writing, shooting and editing an institution will underwrite, and of whether the underwriting renews. O'Neill's description of the platform as patron, distributor and archive needs the corollary the boom's record supplies: the platform patronises everyone equally and therefore no one for long, and the series that ran to length were the ones that found a second patron with a mission. Austen found commerce and public broadcasting. The comedies found a national film culture. The tragedy found a student activities budget and a semester, and produced, inside those limits, the most formally interesting failure of the era. Patronage is not a footnote to the survey. It is the survey's hidden column, the one that predicts the episode counts before a word of any script is read. The afterlife of the funded series underlines the point in a way the upload counts cannot. In December 2016, a syndication deal between Sinclair Broadcast Group and Canvas Media Studios carried the Pemberley Digital catalogue, the Austen series and *Frankenstein, MD* among them, into television, the vlogs re-edited into half-hour episodes without closing credits and presented as broadcast drama. The form's founding artefacts thus completed a full circuit of the media economy, from a bedroom camera to an Emmy to a syndication package, and the circuit was available only to the properties a professional patron had built to catalogue standards. No such second window opened for the campus tragedy or was ever likely to. The distinction is not between good and bad work. It is between work an institution could sell twice and work an institution could only show once, and it maps, with uncomfortable exactness, onto the distinction between the boom's comedies and novels and its single tragedy.

## The Shape of the Makers

Read the survey's maker column as a census of who was permitted to adapt at all, and a second pattern emerges beneath the patronage. Two professional companies account for the prose shelf. One four-woman collective accounts for the whole of the Shakespeare comedy shelf. One campus station, animated by two sophomores, accounts for the tragedy. The form's celebrated democratisation is real, since three of those four makers were amateurs at the moment of making, and the platform asked no permission of any of them. Yet the distribution of genres across the makers is not random. The nearer a maker stood to professional infrastructure, the safer the source: a broadcaster's first scripted commission was a science-education Frankenstein, a commercial network backed a courtship comedy, and the single attempt on the culture's most dangerous love story was left to the makers with the least to lose, students whose failure would cost a campus station a semester rather than a company its slate. Risk, in the vlog economy, flowed downhill to the unpatronised, and the play that most needed a risk-taker got exactly the risk-takers its danger deserved.

The makers' identities belong in the same census, because the form's demographics shaped its canon. The Candle Wasters were four young women from New Zealand, working first as students. Pemberley Digital's commercial patron for *Emma Approved* was a network built for women creators, and its Austen properties addressed an audience the industry had persistently undervalued. *Jules and Monty* began with a woman's proposal, Browder's, after an Austen series, and split its narration between a woman and a man. O'Neill's study of the platform insists that YouTube Shakespeare be read with attention to the politics of identity, to who gets to hold the camera and be believed, and the boom's record gives that insistence a concrete object: the literary web series was substantially a form built by young women and students, adapting an inherited canon on their own terms, in bedrooms, with equipment a school could lend. The tragedy's place in that economy was never going to be settled by the tragedy's merits. It was settled by who was making, who was paying, and which stories the makers recognised as theirs to tell. The play of the two households was, in the end, adapted by the demographic the households least imagined as authors, and the adaptation's tenderness toward Jules, its camera-shy heroine learning fluency across eighteen episodes, is the demographic fact turned into form.

The division is not decorative. The survey's most sophisticated instrument was built for its least completable story, and the table's columns, device against ending, state the era's whole dilemma in two words per row: the better the diary, the worse the tomb.

Read down the final column and the survey argues the article's thesis without assistance. Every comedy and every novel on the table ends in the neighbourhood of its source's ending, because those endings are settlements, marriages, reconciliations, exposures, that can be announced to a camera. The tragedy alone ends in substitution, its ending traded for the one final event a feed can represent without lying: somebody stops posting. Read across the episodes column and the second argument appears. The prose adaptations run long, seventy-two to one hundred episodes, because a diary can always add another day; the Shakespeare comedies run long too, seventy-six episodes for *Nothing Much To Do*, because talk replenishes itself. The tragedy's eighteen episodes are not a failure of stamina. They are the measure of a plot that had to happen in the world, at parties and in confrontations, in a form that can only testify, and testimony about a feud exhausts its credit quickly.

The survey's discipline deserves one further note, because the field invites inflation. The lovers' names attach themselves to hundreds of uploads that no survey can honourably count: bedroom recitations of the balcony scene, school projects abandoned at three episodes, sketch channels borrowing the feud for a week. Those artefacts belong to the history of the play's readership, and O'Neill's account of the platform gives them their due as user-generated Shakespeare. They do not belong in a census of adaptation, which is a claim about wholes: a source carried from its beginning to an ending by an identifiable maker. By that standard the vlog era adapted the play exactly once, and the singularity is the finding on which this article's verdict rests. The flagship's documentation is, for a student production, unusually complete, and the completeness deserves noting as part of the record: a campus newspaper covered the production twice, before release and during it; a television scholar analysed it within days of its finale and returned to it three years later; the episode guides preserved its descriptions entire; and a university library guide carried it into teaching collections. Most student work of the era survives as the uploads alone. This series survives as an archive, which is why a survey can grade it on evidence rather than on memory, and why its compromises can be argued over at all. A form that could produce a hundred episodes of Austen in a year produced eighteen episodes of Verona in the same years and then stopped, and no account of the web series that omits the stopping can explain the form.

## The Chorus in the Comments: Participation and the Audience's New Office

The play opens with a hired voice instructing the audience in its duties. The Chorus of the Prologue begs the crowd to attend with patient ears, promises that the actors' toil will mend whatever the bare stage misses, and hands the spectators a summary that spoils the ending in advance: "A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life" (Prologue, line 6). The web series is the first adaptation form to rebuild that office instead of merely inheriting the text. A vlog's audience is addressed as a body, thanked, warned, recruited and answered; characters maintain accounts between episodes and reply to followers in character; the story's intervals are filled with the audience's own production of comments, theories and fan works. The participatory layer is not marketing wrapped around the series. It is the series' Chorus, distributed into thousands of hands, performing the Prologue's functions, orientation, apology, interpretation, on a rolling basis.

### Who performs the work of the Chorus in a web series?

The audience does, organised by the platform. Subscribers receive each episode as a personal address, comment beneath it in the hearing of the characters' world, follow the cast's accounts between uploads and carry plot knowledge from channel to channel that no single character holds.

The creative team conducts that chorus through warnings, replies and in-character posts, and the result is the Prologue's bargain rebuilt as an interface: attention and patience exchanged for a story whose gaps the community agrees to mend.

The episode fourteen warning shows the office working at full stretch. When the team prefaced Cliff's assault on Jules with a notice, and Browder defended the notice against the charge of spoiling the scene, the argument on both sides was conducted in public, on the series' own channels, with the director Evey Reidy adding the theatre community's context. The audience was not merely informed; it was consulted, and its replies became part of the production's documented record, quoted by McNutt in his genre analysis within a fortnight. No stage production of the play generates that archive. The closest precedent is the printed paratext of the early quartos, and the distance between a title page and a live comment thread is the distance between the play's first participatory century and its second.

The scholarly record of the series is itself a product of that chorus, and the fact is worth pausing on. The fullest critical account of *Jules and Monty* was written by a television scholar who followed the production in public, on the same blogging platforms the production used, exchanging posts with its makers while the episodes were still appearing; the trigger-warning debate survives because the participants conducted it where it could be quoted. Print-era adaptations leave reviews and memoirs. The web series leaves its own conversation, time-stamped and searchable, a paratext that grows around the work like scaffolding that was never struck. Criticism of the form therefore works from an archive the form generates about itself, and the survey in this article, assembled from episode guides, campus reporting, platform criticism and the makers' own posts, is an exhibit of the condition it describes: a tragedy whose documentation is as participatory as its telling.

The warning also exposes the feed's strangest inheritance from the play: its spoiler ethics. The tragedy is the most thoroughly spoiled canonical text in the language, since its Prologue announces the lovers' deaths before the first scene and dares the audience to keep watching anyway, and for four centuries the dare has worked, because foreknowledge in a tragedy is not a leak but a lens. The feed runs on the opposite folk ethic, in which foreknowledge is theft and a warning is a negotiated settlement between the teller and the told. Browder's defence of the episode fourteen notice was, without anyone naming it so, an argument between the two ethics: the team's instinct that the audience must be prepared collided with the objection that preparation spoils, and the compromise, a warning that names the category of harm and hides the blow's address, is the Prologue's own compromise rebuilt for an age of comment threads. The play spoils its ending and withholds its path. The series warned of its violence and withheld its agent. In both cases the audience's office is identical: to watch what it has been told is coming, and to find that knowing is not the same as being ready.

The audience the form actually gathered deserves an honest census. McNutt, revisiting the series in 2017, recorded the scale without varnish: the premiere drew more than twenty thousand views, the finale more than eight thousand, numbers that would shame a fringe theatre and vanish inside a television rounding error. Yet the afterlife of those numbers is longer than their size suggests. A university library guide at Simmons University lists the series, alongside *Nothing Much To Do*, as a teaching text for students meeting the play, with a plain warning about strong language; the series is assigned, in effect, by the same kind of institution that assigned its making, a campus. The class-assignment frame turns out to be prophetic. The vlogs were coursework inside the fiction and became coursework outside it, and the play, which entered the classroom centuries ago as a text to be read, re-enters it here as a feed to be followed, episode by episode, in something close to the rhythm of its first serial audiences.

## Verse Against the Webcam: The Language Problem

The brief's complication, social-media format against the verse, is the point where the survey's evidence and the play's text collide most directly, and it deserves a section of its own. The problem is not that the verse is old. The problem is that the verse is social. Shakespeare's lovers speak a language engineered for a stage full of listeners, heightened, patterned, quotable, a public instrument even at its most intimate: the shared sonnet of the first meeting is a formal dance performed in a crowded room, and the balcony scene is a negotiation conducted in speeches built to carry across a yard. The vlog's language is engineered for the opposite condition, one voice, one lens, the illusion of unstudied privacy. Put the verse in a vlogger's mouth whole and the illusion curdles into recitation, the one register the form cannot survive, because a diary that sounds rehearsed is a diary exposed as theatre. *Jules and Monty* understood the trap and answered it with the rationing rule the reviewers documented: the original language surfaces when the characters are drunk or in love, the two states in which a modern undergraduate might plausibly speak in quotations, and the device flatters the play even as it rations the text, implying that the pentameter is what feeling sounds like when the ordinary self lets go.

A fairer frame for the problem comes from the play itself, which is already stratified by register. Shakespeare writes prose for the serving-men who open the play with their obscene wordplay, prose for much of Mercutio's fencing with Romeo, prose for the Nurse's tidal recollections, and reserves the pentameter for formal feeling, for sonnets, sentences and soliloquies. The play's world, that is, already knows the difference between the language of company and the language of the heart, and distributes them by scene. The vlog flattens that distribution into a single register of confiding speech, the prose of intimacy, and then must promote the prose stratum to carry the verse stratum's loads: the sonnet's fusion, the sentences' authority, the soliloquy's altitude. *Jules and Monty*'s rationing rule can be restated in these terms as a refusal of the flattening, a decision to let the verse stratum erupt through the prose at the points where the play itself lets feeling overrule decorum. *Nothing Much To Do* accepted the flattening and rebuilt the wit in the prose register, songs included, which is why its Shakespeare sounds translated rather than quoted. Neither solution is a failure of nerve. They are the only two doors in the wall, and the survey's Shakespeare entries divide between them without a third option appearing in the era.

The comparison with the play's loudest screen descendant is instructive, and it cuts against the usual ranking of fidelity. The account of [Baz Luhrmann's Romeo + Juliet, its pop Verona and its intact text](/2028/08/04/baz-luhrmann-romeo-plus-juliet-1996/) describes a film that kept nearly every line and changed nearly everything else, trusting the verse to survive guns, billboards and a beach. The web series performs the mirror operation: it keeps the situation entire, the feud, the families, the sequence of desire and disaster, and lets the verse go except at the pressure points. Neither operation is the faithful one; they are faithful to different organs. Luhrmann preserves the play's voice and transplants its body. *Jules and Monty* preserves the play's nervous system, who knows what, who confides in whom, who is deceived, and lets the voice fall to a murmur. Critics who rank adaptations by lines retained will always score the film higher, and critics who rank by structure will note that the diary's confidences reproduce the soliloquy's work, which the film's voiceover-free spectacle largely abandons. The survey's position is that the second ranking measures something the first cannot see.

The set speeches migrate in the same direction, toward whoever owns a channel and an audience. Mercutio's Queen Mab speech is the play's purest set piece, a riff performed for company, detachable from the plot and famous apart from it, and the diary form has exactly one native slot for such a performance: the friend's takeover, the episode in which the supporting player seizes the means of narration. Mark's hijack of Monty's channel, announced in its description as a rescue of material Monty would never tell the camera, is that slot filled by the right character, the wit of the circle treating his friend's diary as a stage that can be stolen for an aria. Even the aubade finds its accidental equivalent. The dawn parting of Act 3, the lovers' two-voice debate over lark and nightingale, is the play's one scene of married dialogue, and the series' nearest approach to it is the episode its poster salvaged from a camera left running through a morning, two voices caught together by an accident of the apparatus and released as brightness in a dark fortnight. The pattern across both cases is consistent: what the play performs, the series can only capture, and its captures are at their best when the apparatus, not the script, does the finding.

What neither form recovers is the verse as event, language that does things in public: Mercutio's wit killing him in company, the Prince's sentences closing over the city, the lovers' sonnet fusing two speakers into one poem in front of both houses. The web series can quote those moments, and its Mark does, drunk, to general delight. Quotation, however, is the verse reduced to souvenir, and O'Neill's reduced Bard is never more visible than here, in the gap between a line treasured as a clip and a scene inhabited as a world. The honest summary is that the format treats the play's language the way the play's characters treat the feud: as an inheritance honoured in flashes and evaded in practice, present everywhere as pressure and almost nowhere as speech. One carrier of heightened feeling does survive the translation entire, and the reviewers noticed it without naming its function: the music. Reviewers praised the songs threaded through the episodes as a large part of the series' texture, and the praise identifies the verse's understudy. Where the pentameter is rationed to drunkenness and desire, the soundtrack performs the office the verse performed on stage, marking the moments when ordinary speech is no longer sufficient and feeling must be scored to be believed. The substitution is the era's quietest admission about where its heightened language went.

## Final Judgment: What the Web Gave the Play, and What It Withheld

The series thesis for this run of articles holds that the cliché keeps the play on stage and screen while the web restores it as participatory media, and the catalogue assembled here supports the thesis with one correction. The web restored the play's participatory *address*, the confidant audience, the spoiled ending announced in advance, the community labour of interpretation, and it restored the soliloquy to the centre of the adaptation economy for the first time since the age of the closet drama. What the web withheld was the play's world: the crowd, the houses, the street, the vault, the whole public machinery by which private love is converted into civic catastrophe. The restoration and the withholding have the same cause, a camera that can go anywhere a person can be alone and nowhere a society gathers.

On the critics' disagreement, the record permits a settled judgment. McNutt's genre reading, that *Jules and Monty* is a tragedy migrating into a medium with no shelf for tragedy, survives every check the episode map offers, and his subsidiary claim, that the series matters as proof of what web distribution let students attempt, is corroborated by the teaching afterlife the series found. The PopOptiq reviewer's disappointment survives the same checks, because the last eight episodes demonstrably strain, flee the diary for found footage, and close on a substitution the reviewer was entitled to find bitter. Where the reviewer errs is in aim: the disappointment is laid on the team's choices, when the evidence shows the choices were forced by the form's contract, the same contract that made the first ten episodes the freshest courtship the play had received in years. O'Neill's frame, abundance shadowed by reduction, turns out to be the description under which both verdicts fit: the boom generated more ways to meet the play than any previous decade and exactly one whole tragedy, whose wholeness cost it the tomb.

The verdict on the web-series form, as an instrument for this play, is therefore double and final. As a reading of the lovers, the vlog adaptation is the most penetrating popular form the play has found since the nineteenth-century closet editions, because it rebuilds the soliloquy's contract from the inside and lets a Juliet educate herself in public, five minutes at a time. As a reading of Verona, it is a beautiful falsification, a city of bedrooms without streets. The road not taken is visible inside the survey itself. The comedy line was already travelling toward the ensemble: *Nothing Much To Do* multiplied its channels, and *Bright Summer Night* dissolved the diary into party footage, a whole crowd carrying the story for a night. A tragedy built on that trajectory, houses with channels of their own, the feud conducted as a war of feeds, is imaginable, and elements of the design sit in the record waiting. It would solve the crowd and lose the confessional, becoming a drama that happens to be distributed online rather than a diary that happens to be drama, and the boom's makers, offered that trade at every step, declined it every time. Their instinct was sound. The diary was the discovery; the crowd was the price; and the one team that paid the price in instalments has left the clearest account of what the price buys. Future practitioners will solve the crowd problem with bigger budgets and ensemble formats, and the solutions will cost the intimacy that made the form matter. *Jules and Monty*, eighteen episodes made by sophomores for a campus station, remains the experiment that found both edges of the instrument, and a form is known by its edges. The play survived the encounter, as it survives every encounter, diminished in the body and strangely enlarged in the voice, still waiting for the medium that can hold its two hours entire. There is a historical rhyme worth naming in closing. Every era that has democratised access to the play has done so by privatising it: the prose tale for the nursery, the closet edition for the solitary reader, the classroom text for the pupil alone with the page. The vlog wave belongs in that lineage and surpasses it, because its privatisation kept the one thing print always surrendered, a face and a voice performing the confidence in real time. What the lineage shares, across two centuries of media, is the trade this article has measured in episode counts and ending substitutions: the play made portable by being made inward, Verona exchanged for a voice. The web did not invent that trade. It merely ran it at industrial scale, published the results in public, and left the record where a survey can count it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who created Jules and Monty, and where was it made?

Two Tufts University sophomores created it: Imogen Browder and Edward Rosini, who also wrote the series and starred as Jules Caine and Romeo "Monty" Montgomery. They took the project to Tufts University Television, the campus station, which produced it as its largest undertaking in some time, with Evey Reidy and Andy De Leon directing. Browder had the idea after watching *The Lizzie Bennet Diaries*, and the pair shaped the college setting across a series of working breakfasts before recruiting the drama department and the station's crews. It remains the documented flagship of Shakespeare vlog adaptation: a complete retelling of the play made entirely by students, on a campus, for YouTube.

### Q: How long does a full viewing of Jules and Monty take?

Roughly two hours. The series runs to eighteen episodes, most of them between five and eight minutes long, released between March 10 and May 5, 2014. That total lands almost exactly on the measure the play sets for itself in its Prologue, "the two hours' traffic of our stage" (Prologue, line 12), which no feature film of the play has ever matched for economy. The resemblance is a coincidence of format rather than a design target, but it underlines the vlog era's efficiency: the whole tragedy, told as testimony, occupies the span of a single stage performance, while the prose adaptations around it ran to seventy-two and one hundred episodes.

### Q: Why do the characters in Jules and Monty keep vlogs?

Because a communications course makes them. Monty announces in the first episode that the videos are a class project, a blog-like assignment he is still naming as he records, and Jules enters the same regime through her roommate Nancy, who takes to the assignment with far more relish. The device is the series' enabling fiction: it explains why two private people keep testifying to a camera, gives the testimony a rhythm of deadlines, and supplies an institution in place of the play's go-betweens. Where the tragedy uses the Nurse, the Friar and a failed letter to move information, the series uses a syllabus, and the lovers are bound together first by coursework and only later by love.

### Q: What do the fraternities Kappa Alpha Psi and Mu Tau Gamma stand in for?

They stand in for the two houses. Jules's family fraternity, Kappa Alpha Psi, is the Capulet interest, headed by her older brother Cliff, while Monty's Mu Tau Gamma carries the Montague allegiance, so the ancient grudge becomes a Greek-row war of parties, pranks and house honour. The translation is more exact than it looks. Fraternity loyalty is adopted rather than born, yet it is defended with the same unreasoning completeness as the loyalty of the play's serving-men, who fight over a gesture in the street without being able to state the feud's cause. The campus version also restores something the play assumes: a world small enough that everybody's allegiance is public knowledge.

### Q: Why is the Nurse figure a nursing major named Nancy Mills?

Because the series casts by undergraduate logic: Nancy Mills is Jules's roommate and closest friend, and her nursing major is the production's quiet joke about her function, the confidante with practical knowledge of bodies, remedies and secrets. Played by Evey Reidy, who also co-directed and served as the production's artistic director, Nancy absorbs the Nurse's roles of messenger, adviser and comic counterweight, translated from servant to peer. The change matters. The play's Nurse is an employee whose loyalty is bought and whose betrayal in Act 3 is a servant's prudence; Nancy's loyalty is friendship, uncomplicated by wages, and her centrality in the vlogging, she is the keenest poster of the circle, gives the Nurse's garrulity a native medium at last.

### Q: Where does Shakespeare's language survive inside Jules and Monty?

At the pressure points: the reviewers of the series documented that the original verse surfaces when the characters are drunk or in love, as if the pentameter were a register the undergraduates fall into when inhibition drops. Scout Magazine singled out Mark, the Mercutio figure, delivering Mercutio's taunt "Come, come, thou art as hot a Jack in thy mood as any in Italy" (3.1) while drunk at a party. The rationing is a theory of the play disguised as a production economy. Shakespeare's characters likewise speak their heightened language when the social self is suspended, in gardens, bedrooms and vaults, and the series simply assigns the verse to the two modern states, desire and drink, that license a borrowed tongue.

### Q: What happens in episode 14 of Jules and Monty, and why did it carry a warning?

Episode 14 stages the confrontation in which Cliff, Jules's brother, strikes her during an argument, the series' translation of old Capulet's violence against his daughter in Act 3. The creative team prefaced the episode with a content warning, and Imogen Browder defended the decision publicly on the ground that a team which had felt the scene's force while making it owed the audience notice before a montage-shaped expectation gave way to an assault. The exchange, documented by the critic Myles McNutt within days, became a small landmark of web-series ethics: a form that recruits subscribers as confidants accepted obligations of care that stage productions, which recruit witnesses, have never carried.

### Q: What does Jules do in the final episode in place of the tomb scene?

She posts a farewell and leaves. The eighteenth episode, released on May 5, 2014, is framed by Jules's own description of the moment: the boxes are packed, she is leaving the University of Verona for good, a few loose ends remain, and the sign-off thanks the internet for the fun. Nobody dies in the series, a fact the critic Myles McNutt recorded in the formula that it follows the play's tragic narrative albeit without death. The tomb is replaced by an ending native to the feed, a channel closing, a face leaving the frame, banishment generalised to both lovers and softened into departure, with the irreversibility of the play's vault traded away for an open door.

### Q: Which Emmy did The Lizzie Bennet Diaries win, and why was the win a first?

It won the Primetime Emmy for Outstanding Creative Achievement in Interactive Media, Original Interactive Program, in 2013, and it was the first Emmy awarded to a web series. The category choice is the point. The Academy honoured not a piece of filmed drama that happened to live online but the interactive architecture itself: the character accounts, the audience exchanges, the story distributed across platforms and unfolding in the followers' own feeds. A form invented by practitioners of online video had been certified by American television's oldest institution on the form's own terms, and the certificate named the very quality, participation, that separates the vlog adaptation from every screen form before it. Pemberley Digital's *Emma Approved* repeated the win two years later.

### Q: Why did a Jane Austen novel prove the vlog format before any Shakespeare play?

Because Austen's fiction is already built from the vlog's units. *Pride and Prejudice* advances by letter, visit, report and revised judgement, narrated in a single confiding voice, while its largest events, an elopement, a proposal, a disgrace, happen offstage and arrive as testimony. A diary camera loses almost nothing of such a book. The Verona tragedy advances by brawl, ball, duel, exile and suicide, events that happen to bodies in public and reach the audience as spectacle. Hank Green and Bernie Su were also established online creators with an audience in place, while the Shakespeare attempts came from students and school companies. The form went where its grammar fitted, and its grammar was epistolary before it was ever dramatic.

### Q: Which three Shakespeare plays did the Candle Wasters turn into web series?

Three comedies: *Much Ado About Nothing*, as *Nothing Much To Do* in 2014; *Love's Labour's Lost*, as *Lovely Little Losers* in 2015; and *A Midsummer Night's Dream*, as *Bright Summer Night* in 2016. The New Zealand company, four young women working from Wellington, became the form's most decorated Shakespeare practitioners, with *Bright Summer Night* taking Best Drama at the 2017 Hollyweb Festival and the team named SPADA's New Filmmaker of the Year. Their slate is also the asymmetry of the era in miniature: offered the whole canon across three productions, the most accomplished web Shakespeareans of the boom chose comedy every time, and no tragedy at all.

### Q: Across how many channels does Nothing Much To Do divide its story?

Three. The Candle Wasters' *Much Ado About Nothing* spreads its seventy-six episodes across three interlinked YouTube channels: one carrying Beatrice and Hero's vlog, one carrying Benedick's rival channel, and a third, the Watch, offering a wider view of the Messina High School world. Plot-critical material sits on every channel, so the audience assembles the story by subscription, holding perspectives no character holds entire. The scholar Jane Wanninger, presenting the series to the Shakespeare Association of America, treated it as a model of the vlog as participatory visual narrative, and the three-channel architecture is the clearest technical advance the Shakespeare web series made on the single-diary design of *The Lizzie Bennet Diaries*.

### Q: Why did the literary web-series boom favour comedy over tragedy?

Because comedy's catastrophes are reportable and tragedy's are not. A shaming, a broken vow, a night of mistaken identity can be narrated to a camera the morning after, in the vlog's only tense, while a duel, a poisoning and a double suicide must be shown or else shrink into anecdote. The sources divide the same way: Austen's novels and Shakespeare's comedies run on conversation, report and reputation, the diary's native materials, while the tragedy's engine is public violence between groups. The production record ratifies the logic without exception. Every long-running literary web series of the boom adapts a comedy or a novel of manners, and the era's single tragedy is also its shortest Shakespeare production, eighteen episodes against seventy-six for *Much Ado About Nothing*.

### Q: What happens to a soliloquy when it is retold as a vlog?

It recovers its first audience. On the early modern stage a soliloquy was delivered outward to a crowd in daylight, making the audience the character's confidant; darkened theatres later converted that confidence into eavesdropping. A vlog restores the original contract, since the form is nothing but direct address: one voice, one lens, a subscriber who has chosen to listen and who holds knowledge the other characters lack. Juliet's potion soliloquy, "What if it be a poison which the friar / Subtly hath minister'd to have me dead" (4.3, lines 24 to 25), is the play's purest specimen, a mind reasoning aloud in terror, and the diary camera is the first adaptation technology that receives such a speech in its native posture rather than translating it into voiceover or dream.

### Q: Why can the tomb scene not be vlogged?

Because the tomb is the least private and least reportable scene in the play. It requires two bodies, a vault, a watch, a fight at the door, two households and a prince assembled over the corpses, and its meaning is manufactured by the crowd that survives it. A diary camera can hold one living speaker in one room; it cannot hold a corpse, a congregation or a civic settlement, and a suicide staged for a class assignment would break the contract of care the form signs with its subscribers, the contract *Jules and Monty* made explicit when it warned its audience before a single act of violence in episode 14. Every documented vlog adaptation therefore substitutes at the tomb: a farewell post, a closed channel, an ending by absence.

### Q: What do comments, likes and character accounts add that a stage Chorus cannot?

Continuity and reply. The play's Chorus appears twice, at the Prologue and before Act 2, begs for patient ears and vanishes; a web series' audience performs the choric office continuously, orienting newcomers, debating motives under each episode, following the characters' accounts between uploads and carrying knowledge across channels. The creative team conducts that chorus with warnings, answers and in-character posts, and the exchanges become part of the production's record, as when the episode 14 warning debate was quoted by critics within a fortnight. The Prologue's bargain, attention exchanged for a story whose gaps the audience agrees to mend, is rebuilt as an interface, and the audience's labour, which the theatre consumes silently, the feed preserves and displays.

### Q: What keeps Jules and Monty available to watch, and what threatens that survival?

A platform's hospitality keeps it available: the series lives where it premiered, on YouTube, under the stewardship of its uploaders' accounts, free to any viewer with the link. Stephen O'Neill's *Shakespeare and YouTube* named the platform's triple office exactly, patron, distributor and archive, and the third office is the fragile one. No broadcaster's vault, no publisher's reprint programme and no festival print guarantees the series; its survival depends on an account remaining active and a corporation's servers continuing to serve an eighteen-episode student production from 2014. The contrast with the play is the point. The tragedy survived four centuries in print, a medium of copies; its web descendant survives at the pleasure of a single host.

### Q: Did Jules and Monty win any festival awards?

No festival or academy award appears in its documented record. The honours the series collected were of another kind: adoption by Tufts University Television as its flagship production, sustained critical attention from television scholars who treated it as a landmark of student web production, and a teaching afterlife in university library guides that recommend it to students meeting the play. The era's prizes flowed elsewhere, an Emmy to Pemberley Digital's Austen series and festival drama awards to the Candle Wasters' *Bright Summer Night*. The absence is itself evidence for the survey's argument: institutions rewarded the forms and genres the vlog does well, and the boom's one tragedy was honoured chiefly by being studied.

### Q: What does the InsightCrunch web-series survey catalogue, and how are its entries graded?

The survey catalogues the documented literary web series of the vlog era, seven productions between 2012 and 2016, and grades each on its production record: maker, platform, source, episode count, governing device and ending decision. Inclusion requires evidence, a named maker and a verifiable run, so fan uploads and name-borrowing sketches are excluded, and two entries carry partial records flagged in their cells rather than smoothed away. Read down its final column, the survey's grading turns into an argument: every comedy and novel on the table ends near its source's ending, announced to a camera, while the single tragedy ends in substitution, its tomb traded for a farewell post.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch web-series survey to argue that the vlog restores the soliloquy while surrendering the tomb?

By arguing from the table's two patterns rather than from impressions of individual series. The episodes column shows testimony flourishing wherever a source thinks aloud, one hundred episodes of Austen, seventy-six of *Much Ado*, while the ending column shows every production that faced a physical catastrophe substituting at the grave. Set *Jules and Monty* between those columns: its alternating diaries rebuild the soliloquy's contract of confided knowledge, its found-footage escapes mark the feud escaping the form, and its farewell finale is the tomb surrendered in documentary form. An essay that keeps the claim tied to the survey's counts and credits can defend the paradox that defines the era, a medium that gave the play back its voice by taking away its world.
