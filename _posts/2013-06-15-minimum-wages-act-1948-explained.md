Ask what the Minimum Wages Act 1948 guarantees and most accounts give the same answer: a legal floor below which no employer may push a wage. That answer is half right, and the half it misses is the half that governed the working lives of most Indians for seven decades. The Act makes the obligation to pay a minimum wage unusually absolute. It overrides contracts, it disregards the employer's capacity to pay, and the Supreme Court has held that paying less than the minimum is forced labour within the meaning of the Constitution. But the obligation switches on only for work that a government has chosen to list. A worker whose occupation stands in the Schedule to the Act, or in an employment added to it by notification, holds a right that no bargain can remove. A worker in an occupation nobody has scheduled holds no statutory minimum wage at all, whatever the poverty of the wage actually paid. Coverage, not the level of the rate, was always the real question in this statute, and the whole history of the Act, from its drafting in the 1940s to its replacement by the Code on Wages in 2019, is the history of that scheduling gate and the long argument about whether to remove it.

This is a pillar article on the statute. It sets out what the Act requires and of whom, explains the two mechanisms by which a rate comes into existence, reconstructs the need-based benchmark against which every rate is judged and found short, states the constitutional principle that makes the duty absolute, and gives an honest account of the enforcement record, which is where the statute is weakest. It also explains why a country with this Act had thousands of different minimum wages and no national minimum wage, and what changed when Parliament replaced the scheduling model with a universal floor. The wider field of Indian labour legislation, of which this Act is one of the founding statutes, is mapped in [the guide to India's labour law framework](/2013/05/01/indian-labour-law-framework-guide/), and this article stays inside its own ground: one Act, its machinery, its case law and its limits.

## What existed before the Act, and why it failed

Statutory wage floors arrived in India as an answer to a named evil: sweated labour. The expression belonged to the industrial vocabulary of the early twentieth century and described trades in which workers, unorganised and easily replaced, were driven by competition among themselves to accept wages below subsistence. The Royal Commission on Labour, appointed in 1929 under John Henry Whitley and reporting in 1931, examined Indian industrial conditions and recommended the creation of machinery for fixing minimum wages in selected industries where organisation was weak and wages were unduly low. The recommendation produced no central statute in the 1930s. Wage regulation remained a provincial patchwork, and the industries where bargaining power was weakest stayed outside any floor.

The international template existed. In 1928 the International Labour Conference adopted Convention No. 26, the Minimum Wage-Fixing Machinery Convention, which required ratifying states to create machinery through which minimum rates could be fixed for workers in trades where no effective regulation of wages existed and wages were exceptionally low, and Recommendation No. 30, which set out how such machinery should work. The machinery idea mattered more than any number. A minimum wage, on this design, is not a figure announced by a legislature. It is the output of an institution: representatives of employers and workers, with independent members, enquiring into conditions in a trade and advising a government that then fixes the rate by legal instrument. The Indian Act of 1948 reproduces that institutional design almost exactly, and understanding it as machinery, rather than as a rate, is the key to understanding everything that follows.

The Second World War industrialised parts of India quickly and disordered its labour markets. Wartime inflation cut real wages while the cost of living index climbed, and disputes over dearness allowance filled the industrial tribunals. The tripartite machinery built during the war years, the Indian Labour Conference and the Standing Labour Committee, gave employers, workers and governments a standing forum, and within that forum the case for a permanent statute hardened. A draft bill was considered by the Indian Labour Conference in 1945, and the Standing Labour Committee took up the proposal for separate legislation covering the unorganised sectors the following year. The Minimum Wages Bill moved through the legislature of a country becoming independent as it was drafted, and the resulting Act, the Minimum Wages Act, 1948, Act 11 of 1948, received the assent of the Governor-General on 15 March 1948 and came into force the same day. Its preamble states its entire ambition in one line: an Act to provide for fixing minimum rates of wages in certain employments. The word "certain" carries the whole future of the statute. Parliament chose a floor with a gate in it.

The road to the statute ran through the tripartite machinery that wartime India had built for labour questions. The Standing Labour Committee, at its meeting in October 1946, considered the statutory fixation of minimum wages and concluded that separate central legislation was necessary, taking up for detailed consideration the definitions of minimum wage, employment and employer, the circumstances in which fixation machinery should apply, and the appointment of an advisory committee to report on the arrangements required. A draft Minimum Wages Bill had been placed before the Indian Labour Conference in 1945, and by the time the Constituent Assembly's India legislated in the first months of 1948 the design questions had been argued for half a decade: whether the law should be central or provincial, whether it should name its employments or cover all work, and whether the wage should be set by committees of the interests or by the state after enquiry. The Act's answers, a central statute on a concurrent subject, a schedule of named employments, and tripartite committees under a duty owed by government, were each the product of that argument, and each carried the argument forward into the Act's working life rather than settling it.

Three months after the Act commenced, the Central Advisory Council of Labour appointed the tripartite Committee on Fair Wages, in November 1948, and its report of 1949 supplied the conceptual vocabulary that Indian wage law has used ever since. The Committee distinguished three levels of wage. The living wage, the highest, would enable a worker to provide for himself and his family not merely the bare essentials of food, clothing and shelter but a measure of comfort, education for children, protection against ill health and insurance against misfortune; the Committee treated it as the ultimate goal, to be approached as national income rose, and Article 43 of the Constitution later directed the state to endeavour to secure a living wage for all workers. The minimum wage, the lowest of the three concepts, had to provide not merely for bare subsistence but for the preservation of the worker's efficiency, including some measure of education, medical requirements and amenities. Between the two stood the fair wage, above the minimum and below the living wage, its level governed largely by the capacity of the industry to pay. This three-level structure is not decoration. The courts built the doctrine of the Act directly on it, holding repeatedly that capacity to pay, central to a fair wage, is irrelevant to a minimum wage, and that the minimum wage fixed under the Act must be measured against need rather than against an employer's balance sheet.

## The architecture of the Act

The Act is short, thirty-one sections and a Schedule, and its weight is carried by a handful of definitions and a single structural decision. Section 2 defines the terms on which everything turns. An "employee" is any person employed for hire or reward to do any work, skilled or unskilled, manual or clerical, in a scheduled employment in respect of which minimum rates have been fixed. The definition reaches beyond the factory gate: it includes an out-worker, a person to whom articles or materials are given out to be made up, cleaned, altered, finished or otherwise processed for sale, where the work is done in the out-worker's home or in premises outside the giver's control. That inclusion mattered in 1948 and matters still, because it draws home-based piece workers, the beedi roller working at a kitchen table, the woman stitching garments for a contractor, inside the definition of the protected worker. Members of the Armed Forces of the Union are excluded.

An "employer" is defined with matching breadth. It covers any person who employs one or more employees in a scheduled employment, whether directly or through another person, and whether on behalf of himself or any other person, and it then names the individuals who count as the employer in particular settings: in a factory, the person named as manager under the Factories Act, 1948; in a government employment, the person or authority appointed for supervision and control, or the head of the department; under a local authority, the appointed supervisor or the chief executive officer. The drafting answers the oldest evasion in labour law, the claim that the real employer is somebody else, by attaching the duty to the person who controls the work and the payment. Where labour is supplied through a contractor, the statutory definition reaches the person who employs through another, although the detailed scheme of liability between a principal employer and a contractor, including who must pay when the contractor defaults, is worked out in [the Contract Labour (Regulation and Abolition) Act, 1970](/2013/07/01/contract-labour-act-1970-explained/) and belongs to that article.

"Wages" means all remuneration capable of being expressed in money which would be payable if the contract of employment were fulfilled, and it expressly includes house rent allowance. It excludes the value of house accommodation, light, water and medical attendance, any other amenity or service excluded by government order, the employer's contributions to a pension or provident fund or a scheme of social insurance, travelling allowance, sums paid to defray special expenses entailed by the nature of the employment, and gratuity payable on discharge. The exclusions do practical work. When a court asks whether an employer has paid the minimum, it compares the minimum rate with the wages so defined, and payments falling inside the exclusions cannot be counted toward the floor. Section 2 also defines the "cost of living index number" and the "competent authority" appointed to ascertain it, the machinery by which the price-linked part of a wage is measured, and "scheduled employment", the definition that decides the reach of the whole Act: an employment specified in the Schedule, or any process or branch of work forming part of such employment.

### What exactly does the Act require an employer to do?

Where a notification fixing minimum rates is in force for a scheduled employment, Section 12 requires the employer to pay every employee wages at a rate not less than the minimum rate fixed for that class of employees in that employment, without deductions except as authorised, and within the time and conditions prescribed.

Around that core duty the Act fixes hours and rest days, requires overtime at the overtime rate, compels registers and records, and arms inspectors and a claims authority to enforce it.

The division of power is the second structural decision. Labour sits in the Concurrent List of the Constitution, and the Act splits fixation between two levels of government through the definition of "appropriate Government" in Section 2(b). The Central Government is the appropriate government for any scheduled employment carried on by or under the authority of the Central Government or a railway administration, and for mines, oil-fields, major ports and corporations established by a Central Act. For every other scheduled employment, the State Government is the appropriate government. Each government fixes and revises rates for its own sphere, the Centre's schedule eventually carrying forty-five scheduled employments, in agriculture, mining, construction, railways and the like, while the schedules of the states together grew to well over a thousand entries. Two governments in the same territory, dozens of governments across the country, each with its own schedule, its own rates, its own zones and its own revision calendar: the multiplication of Indian minimum wages is not an accident of administration. It is the direct product of this definition and of the scheduling gate it serves.


## The definitions that decide cases

Because the Act operates through lists, its definitions carry unusual weight, and Section 2 repays close reading. The definition of wages in Section 2(h) is drawn widely and then cut back by exclusions, and both halves matter in litigation. Wages means all remuneration capable of being expressed in terms of money which would, if the terms of the contract of employment were fulfilled, be payable to a person employed in respect of his employment or of work done in it, and it expressly includes house rent allowance. It then excludes the value of house accommodation, the supply of light, water and medical attendance and other excluded amenities; any contribution paid by the employer to a pension fund or provident fund or under a scheme of social insurance; any travelling allowance or the value of a travelling concession; any sum paid to defray special expenses entailed by the nature of the employment; and any gratuity payable on discharge. The line the definition draws is between payment for the work and provision around the work. A house rent allowance counts toward the floor. The house itself does not. The Airfreight decision turned on exactly this line, holding that sums inside the definition are counted as one package toward the minimum while the excluded items cannot be used to make up a shortfall.

The definition of employee in Section 2(i) follows the same pattern of breadth with deliberate inclusions. A person counts if employed for hire or reward to do skilled or unskilled work, manual or clerical, in a scheduled employment for which minimum rates have been fixed. Three extensions deserve notice. The definition includes a person employed to do work in an unskilled as well as a skilled category, although unskilled work needs no certificate and its inclusion forecloses any argument that the Act protects only trades. It includes an employee declared to be one by the appropriate government, a residual power to settle doubtful cases by declaration. And it includes the out-worker, defined as a person to whom articles or materials are given out by another person to be made up, cleaned, washed, altered, ornamented, finished, repaired, adapted or otherwise processed for sale, at home or in other premises not under the control of the person who gave the work out. That clause, written in 1948, describes the putting-out system of the beedi, garment and agarbatti trades with complete accuracy, and it is the reason home-based production is inside the Act at all. The single exclusion, members of the Armed Forces of the Union, marks the boundary of the civilian labour statute.

The definition of employer in Section 2(e) is built for evasion-resistance. It includes a person who employs one or more employees directly or through another person, on his own behalf or on behalf of any other person, and then names the office-holders who count in the organised sector: in a factory, the person named as manager under the Factories Act; in an employment under government control, the person or authority appointed for supervision and control, or the head of the department where none is appointed; in an employment under a local authority, its chief executive officer. The through-another-person clause is the one that bites in practice, because scheduled work in India runs heavily through contractors, and the definition allows the claim under Section 20 to be directed at the person who engaged the labour through the intermediary rather than stopping at the intermediary's door. Read with the case law on the Asiad works, where payment through chains of contractors did not dilute the obligation, the definition is the statutory root of the principle that contracting out the hiring does not contract out the floor.

The remaining definitions set the administrative cast. The competent authority under Section 2(c) is the officer appointed to compute the cost of living index, the cost of living allowance and the cash value of concessions, the technician of Section 4. The prescribed authority and the inspectorate appear in the working sections. Cost of living index number is defined by reference to the index notified for the class of employees concerned. None of these definitions is ornamental. In a statute where protection depends on classification, the definitions are where classification is won and lost, and the volume of reported decisions turning on Section 2, rather than on the rate itself, is the proof.

## The coverage gate: scheduled employment

Everything in this Act is downstream of the Schedule. As enacted in 1948 the Schedule had two parts. Part I listed twelve employments: work in woollen carpet making or shawl weaving establishments; rice mills, flour mills and dal mills; tobacco manufactories, including beedi making; plantations growing cinchona, rubber, tea or coffee; oil mills; employment under any local authority; construction and maintenance of roads and building operations; stone breaking and stone crushing; lac manufactories; mica works; public motor transport; and tanneries and leather manufactories. Mines entries were added to Part I by later amendment. Part II contained a single entry of enormous weight: employment in agriculture, in any form of farming including the tilling of soil, dairy farming, the production and cultivation of any agricultural or horticultural commodity, the raising of livestock, bees or poultry, and forestry and timbering operations. Between them the two parts describe the India of 1948 accurately: the sweated factory trades in one list, the land in the other.

Section 3(1)(a) obliges the appropriate government to fix minimum rates for employments specified in Part I or Part II and for employments added to either part by notification under Section 27. Section 27 is the gate's hinge. The appropriate government may add any employment to either part of the Schedule, in respect of which it is of opinion that minimum rates should be fixed, after giving not less than three months' notice of its intention by notification in the Official Gazette. Scheduling is therefore an act of executive discretion preceded by a notice period, and the protection of an entire occupation can wait upon a government's opinion that its turn has come. The record of scheduling, occupation by occupation and state by state over seven decades, is the true coverage map of the Act, and it is a map drawn by hundreds of separate decisions rather than by the statute itself.

Even inside the gate there is a second, quieter gate. Section 3(1A), inserted by amendment in 1957, provides that the appropriate government may refrain from fixing minimum rates for any scheduled employment in which there are in the whole state fewer than one thousand employees engaged, and must fix rates only when it finds, after inquiry, that the number has risen to one thousand or more. A tiny trade in a small state can be scheduled and still lawfully left without a rate. The provision was a concession to administrative practicality, and like Section 27 it locates the reach of the Act in executive judgment rather than in the worker's need.

The courts were drawn into the gate repeatedly, because coverage litigation is what a scheduling statute manufactures. Much of the case law under the Act is not about the level of a wage at all. It is about whether a given activity falls inside a scheduled entry: whether a process or branch of work forms part of a listed employment within Section 2(g), whether an establishment's activity is the activity the entry names, whether a hospital, a club, a religious institution or a government department carrying on a listed activity is inside or outside. Every such case was decided on the words of an entry, and behind each stood a group of workers whose entire statutory protection turned on the classification of their employer's business. The Act generated this litigation by design. A universal statute asks one question, what is the wage. A scheduling statute asks a prior question in every case: does the statute speak to this work at all.

The consequences of the gate are sharpest at the bottom of the labour market, where the work is most stigmatised and the workers least organised. Employment under a local authority stands in Part I, which reaches the sanitation work of municipalities, but much of the cleaning economy runs through contractors, informal arrangements and occupations that no schedule names with precision. The removal of human waste by hand, the lowest and most degrading work in the Indian labour market, was addressed in the end not by scheduling it under this Act but by a dedicated prohibitory statute, and the modern law on that subject is treated in [the article on the manual scavenging legislation](/2017/04/15/manual-scavenging-act-2013-explained/). The pattern repeats across the unorganised economy. Where work is performed in homes, in small clusters, under names that match no entry, the Act's floor exists on paper for scheduled India and does not exist at all for the rest.

Section 26 contains the exemptions, and they cut in the opposite direction, removing protection the Schedule has granted. The appropriate government may direct that the Act shall not apply to the wages payable to disabled employees. It may, for special reasons, direct that the Act or any of its provisions shall not apply to any class of employees in a scheduled employment or to any locality, for a specified period and subject to conditions. It may also direct that the Act shall not apply to classes of employees in receipt of wages above a prescribed limit, on the ground that fixing a minimum for them is unnecessary. Nothing in the Act applies to wages payable by an employer to a member of his family living with and dependent on him. Each exemption is defensible in isolation, a safety valve for cases the floor fits badly. Together they complete the picture of a statute whose coverage is negotiated at every joint: the Schedule decides what enters, Section 3(1A) decides what is too small to count, Section 26 decides what may leave.

## 1957: the Act learns its own limits

Nine years in, Parliament rebuilt the fixation provisions, and the Minimum Wages (Amendment) Act, 1957, Act 51 of 1957, is the most instructive amendment in the Act's history because every change records a failure of the original design. The original Section 3 had commanded fixation for Part I employments by a fixed date, with revision by a further fixed date, and governments had missed the dates in large numbers, leaving the validity of late fixations in doubt. The 1957 amendment replaced the calendar commands with the standing duty and the five-year review ceiling in the form the section still carries, kept unrevised rates alive until revision, and enacted Section 31, which validated rates fixed in the windows the section recites, in 1952, 1954 and the years following, notwithstanding that they had been fixed after the dates the original section prescribed. The same amendment inserted Section 3(1A), the thousand-employee threshold below which a government may refrain from fixing, and omitted the original Section 6, whose place in the scheme the committee and board provisions of Sections 7 to 9 had absorbed. Read as a whole, the amendment of 1957 converted a statute of deadlines into a statute of continuing duties, trading the clarity of a date for the durability of an obligation that survives its own breach. The trade explains the Act's later history in miniature: nothing in the revised scheme ever again made a fixation void for lateness, and nothing ever again gave a worker a remedy when revision was late. Durability was bought with enforceability, and the price was paid, as the revision record shows, by the workers whose rates stood still.

## How a minimum wage is actually set

Section 3 imposes the duty and its rhythm. The appropriate government shall fix the minimum rates of wages payable to employees in the scheduled employments, and shall review the rates at intervals it thinks fit, such intervals not exceeding five years, and revise them if necessary. The five-year figure is a ceiling on the interval, not a guaranteed timetable, and the section softens its own command with a saving: where for any reason a government has not reviewed the rates within five years, nothing prevents it from reviewing them afterwards, and until revision the old rates continue in force. A missed revision therefore breaches the scheme's expectation without breaking the wage; the worker keeps the stale rate, and the statute absorbs the delay. Section 31 of the Act, a validation provision, records how often fixation in the 1950s had to be rescued retrospectively after governments missed the statutory deadlines the original Section 3 had set, with three separate amendment Acts validating rates fixed in the belief that the power existed. Late revision is not a modern failure of the Act. It is present in its first decade, written into its own text.

Section 3 also gives the fixation its granularity, and this granularity is the second engine of multiplication. Under Section 3(2), a government may fix a minimum time rate, a minimum piece rate, a guaranteed time rate for piece workers, and an overtime rate. Under Section 3(3), different rates may be fixed for different scheduled employments, for different classes of work in the same employment, for adults, adolescents, children and apprentices, and for different localities, and rates may be fixed by the hour, the day, the month or a larger prescribed wage period. A single scheduled employment in a single state can therefore carry separate rates for skilled, semi-skilled and unskilled work, for several zones within the state, and for monthly and daily paid staff, before dearness allowance is layered on top. When commentators report that a large state has several hundred distinct minimum wage rates, the figure is produced by multiplying exactly these variables. Section 3(2A) adds a further pause: where an industrial dispute about wage rates in a scheduled employment is pending before a tribunal, or an award is in operation, rates fixed or revised under the Act do not apply to the employees concerned during that period, a provision that keeps the statutory floor and the adjudicated wage from colliding head-on.

The machinery that produces the rate is in Section 5, and it offers the government a choice between two methods. The first is the committee method: the government appoints as many committees and sub-committees as it considers necessary to hold enquiries and advise it on the fixation or revision. The second is the notification method: the government publishes its proposals by notification in the Official Gazette for the information of persons likely to be affected, specifying a date not less than two months from the date of the notification on which the proposals will be taken into consideration, receives representations, and then decides. Where the government proposes to revise rates by the notification method, it must also consult the Advisory Board. Under either route the process ends the same way, with a notification in the Official Gazette fixing or revising the rates, which, unless the notification otherwise provides, comes into force on the expiry of three months from the date of its issue. The two methods embody different theories of legitimacy. The committee method treats the rate as the negotiated product of the interests affected, advised by enquiry. The notification method treats it as an administrative determination tested by public objection. Both are slower than the wage movements they track, a structural lag no drafting can remove.

The tripartite composition of the machinery is fixed by Sections 7, 8 and 9. Every appropriate government appoints an Advisory Board under Section 7 to co-ordinate the work of the committees and sub-committees and to advise the government generally on fixing and revising minimum rates. The Central Government appoints a Central Advisory Board under Section 8 to advise the Central and State Governments and to co-ordinate the Advisory Boards. Section 9 prescribes the composition of each committee, sub-committee and Advisory Board: persons nominated by the appropriate government representing employers and employees in the scheduled employments, equal in number, together with independent persons not exceeding one-third of the total membership, one of the independent persons being appointed chairman. The design is the ILO machinery of 1928 translated into Indian sections: equality between the two sides of industry, an independent element capped at a third, and a chairman drawn from the independents. Its strength is that a rate emerging from such a body carries the agreement, or at least the participation, of those who must pay and receive it. Its weakness is that the machinery is advisory. The government appoints, the committees advise, and the government fixes, which is why the Supreme Court, in U. Unichoyi and Others v State of Kerala, treated a committee-based fixation as something a court should ordinarily refuse to re-examine on its merits: the rate had been recommended by a body on which both sides sat and agreed.

The co-ordinating function of the Advisory Board is the least visible and most consequential part of the design. A state fixes rates employment by employment, committee by committee, and without a body charged with seeing the whole table, the rates for comparable work in different employments drift apart in ways nobody chose: the unskilled rate in one scheduled trade settles above the semi-skilled rate in another, a revision for a politically visible employment outruns the revision for an invisible one, and the relativities that workers experience as fairness or its absence are produced by accident. Section 7 gives the board the duty of preventing that drift, and the Central Advisory Board under Section 8 carries the same function one level up, advising both levels of government and co-ordinating the state boards, which is the channel through which such uniformity as the system possesses, in allowance practice and in the advisory national floor, has travelled. The boards are also where the system's memory lives. Governments change and committees are appointed for a fixation and dissolved, but the board continues, holds the record of past relativities and past bargains, and supplies the employee and employer members who understand the state's whole wage table rather than one trade's corner of it. When the boards meet rarely, or are reconstituted late, or are bypassed by a government fixing rates by the notification method with the board consulted as a formality, the fixation for each employment becomes a separate negotiation ignorant of the others, and the multiplication of rates that Section 3 permits turns from a design for sensitivity into a source of incoherence. The Act's machinery sections are usually read as procedure. They are better read as the constitution of a small wage-fixing republic, with the board as its continuing institution and the committees as its occasional assemblies, and the health of that republic, state by state and decade by decade, is most of what determines whether the floor in a schedule bears any relation to the floor in the schedule beside it.

### When does a newly fixed or revised rate take effect?

The final notification under Section 5 takes effect on the expiry of three months from the date of its issue, unless the notification itself provides otherwise. Between proposal and operation the Act builds in the two-month representation window of the notification method, the deliberation of committees under the committee method.

A further three months then run before the notified rate binds, so a revision can lawfully take the better part of a year to reach a pay packet.

The full sequence, with the actor and the failure point at each stage, is the findable artifact of this article.

## What a minimum wage is made of

### How is a minimum wage rate made up?

A rate fixed under the Act may take one of three forms under Section 4: a basic rate plus a cost of living allowance that moves with the cost of living index; a basic rate, with or without that allowance, plus the cash value of essential commodities supplied at concessional rates.

The third form is a single all-inclusive rate combining all three elements. The allowance and the cash value of concessions are computed by the competent authority. In practice most notifications fix a basic rate and attach a variable dearness allowance revised against the consumer price index, so the wage a worker receives is a sum of a fixed part and a moving part.

A fixed rate under the Act is not necessarily a single number. Section 4 permits the minimum rate of wages to consist of one of three structures. It may be a basic rate together with a special allowance, called the cost of living allowance, adjusted at intervals and in the manner the appropriate government directs so as to accord as nearly as practicable with the variation in the cost of living index number for those workers. It may be a basic rate with or without the cost of living allowance, together with the cash value of concessions in the supply of essential commodities at concession rates, where authorised. Or it may be an all-inclusive rate allowing for the basic rate, the allowance and the cash value of concessions together. The competent authority under Section 2(c) computes the allowance and the cash value of concessions at the intervals the government directs.

In practice the second element became the living part of the wage. Most governments fix a basic rate and attach a variable dearness allowance, revised at regular intervals, commonly twice a year in the central sphere, by reference to movements in the consumer price index. The basic rate changes only on a Section 3 revision, which the five-year ceiling governs loosely; the allowance moves with prices between revisions, which is the Act's answer to inflation inside a slow statutory process. A minimum wage quoted in a notification is therefore a sum with a moving part, and a worker comparing a pay packet against the rate has to compare against the basic and the allowance for the correct period together.

The Supreme Court settled how the comparison works in Airfreight Ltd v State of Karnataka, decided on 4 August 1999. The Karnataka notification fixed minimum wages inclusive of dearness allowance up to a stated index point, with allowance payable for future increases, and the employer argued about how the components should be counted. The Court held that the minimum rate fixed under the Act is remuneration payable to the worker as one package: where a rate is linked with the cost of living index, the amount paid by way of dearness allowance is not an independent component to be stacked on top, but part and parcel of the process of computing the minimum wage itself. An employer whose total pay package exceeds the notified minimum does not owe the allowance again as a separate item; equally, in deciding whether the minimum has been paid, amounts falling within the exclusions in the definition of wages in Section 2(h), such as the value of house accommodation, are not to be counted toward it. The floor is a single figure, reached by adding what counts and ignoring what does not.

### Can minimum wages be paid in kind instead of cash?

The default rule in Section 11 is that minimum wages payable under the Act shall be paid in cash. Payment wholly or partly in kind is permitted only where it has been the custom to pay in kind and the appropriate government, considering it necessary, authorises it by notification.

The government may also authorise the supply of essential commodities at concessional rates. The cash value of wages in kind and of concessions is then estimated in the prescribed manner, so that even an authorised payment in kind is measured against the cash floor.

Two further provisions complete the anatomy of the rate. Section 10 empowers the government to correct clerical or arithmetical mistakes in a fixation order by notification, placing every such correction before the Advisory Board for information, a small provision that acknowledges how error-prone multi-zone rate notifications are. And Section 11's insistence on cash connects to a much older struggle: payment in kind, the truck system of the nineteenth-century industrial world, tied the worker to the employer's goods and prices, and the cash rule is the Act's refusal to let the floor be discharged in anything the worker cannot spend elsewhere.

## Hours, rest days, overtime and the pay packet

The Act is a wage statute that also regulates the working day, because a daily rate means nothing without a definition of the day it buys. Section 13 empowers the appropriate government, for any scheduled employment in which minimum rates have been fixed, to fix the number of hours of work constituting a normal working day, inclusive of specified intervals; to provide for a day of rest in every period of seven days, with remuneration for the rest day; and to provide for payment for work on a rest day at not less than the overtime rate. These matters are typically carried into the rules made under Section 30, which prescribe the normal working day, conventionally nine hours for an adult in most state rules, and the weekly rest. Section 13(2) applies the regime only in qualified form to employees on urgent or emergency work, preparatory or complementary work, essentially intermittent work, work that must be completed before the duty ends, and work dependent on the irregular action of natural forces, the standing exceptions of any hours law.

Section 14 fixes overtime. Where an employee whose minimum rate is fixed by the hour, the day or a longer wage period works on any day beyond the hours constituting a normal working day, the employer must pay for every hour or part of an hour worked in excess at the overtime rate fixed under this Act or under any law of the appropriate government for the time being in force, whichever is higher. The section expressly preserves Section 59 of the Factories Act, 1948, which gives factory workers double the ordinary rate of wages for hours beyond nine in a day or forty-eight in a week, so a factory worker takes whichever protection is greater. In many state rules the overtime rate under this Act is itself fixed at double the ordinary rate, but the statute's own formula is comparative: the better of the two applicable rates.

The surrounding sections answer the disputes a rate generates in daily use. Section 15 protects the worker engaged for a day who is given less than a full day's work: he is entitled to wages as if he had worked the full normal working day, unless his failure to work is caused by his own unwillingness rather than the employer's failure to provide work, or the case is one prescribed otherwise. Section 16 requires an employee who does two or more classes of work, each with a different minimum rate, to be paid at not less than the rate for each class for the time spent on it. Section 17 secures the piece worker: where an employee is employed on piece work for which a minimum time rate but no minimum piece rate has been fixed, the employer must pay at not less than the minimum time rate, and Section 3(2)'s guaranteed time rate exists for the same purpose, to stop piece rates being set so low that a full day's output earns less than the time rate. Section 12, already noted, states the payment duty itself, and Section 18 imposes the paperwork on which enforcement depends: every employer must maintain registers and records of employees, work performed, wages paid and receipts given, must exhibit notices of the prescribed particulars where the employees work, and may be required by rules to issue wage books or wage slips. A minimum wage that cannot be proved from a record is a promise without evidence, and Section 18 is the Act's attempt to make every payment leave a trace.

### What records must an employer keep, and why do they decide cases?

Section 18 requires every employer to maintain registers and records giving particulars of the employees, the work performed by them, the wages paid to them and the receipts given for those wages, in the prescribed form. The rules may require notices showing the minimum rates fixed, abstracts of the Act and the rules, and the name and address of the inspector to be exhibited in the factory, workshop or place where employees work, and may require wage books or wage slips to be issued. In a Section 20 claim the employer's registers are usually the only contemporary evidence of hours and payment, which is why their absence, or their falsity, so often decides the case.

The record duty runs in one direction only, from employer to state, and the worker who wants proof of his own pay has to build it himself. A worker who keeps his wage slips, a copy or photograph of the notification fixing the rate for his employment and class, and any papers from a claim or a union complaint, holds the materials a Section 20 application is made of. Keeping those wage records together in one private and encrypted store, rather than in a folder that anyone handling the papers can read, is the same protective discipline described in [this account of how journalists use an encrypted vault to protect their sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/), applied to a different kind of exposure: the papers that prove underpayment are also the papers an aggrieved employer would most like to see disappear.

## The need-based benchmark: what the minimum ought to contain

The Act fixes no formula for the level of a rate. It builds machinery and leaves the number to it, which made the argument about adequacy an argument about norms developed outside the statute. The norms came in two instalments. The Committee on Fair Wages had already held that a minimum wage must do more than keep a worker alive: it must preserve his efficiency as a worker, with some measure of education, medical facility and amenity built in. The Fifteenth Indian Labour Conference, meeting in 1957, converted that principle into arithmetic. Its resolution on a need-based minimum wage took the standard working-class family as comprising three consumption units for one earner. It calculated the minimum food requirement on a net intake of 2,700 calories per average Indian adult, on the nutritional norms associated with Dr W. Aykroyd. It estimated clothing at a per capita consumption of eighteen yards a year, giving seventy-two yards for the average worker's family. It took housing at the rent corresponding to the minimum area provided under the government's industrial housing scheme. And it provided that fuel, lighting and other miscellaneous items of expenditure should constitute twenty per cent of the total minimum wage. Those five elements, family size, food, clothing, housing and a twenty per cent miscellaneous share, became the standard test by which unions argued and tribunals reasoned, although the Act itself nowhere enacts them.

The Supreme Court endorsed the 1957 norms in U. Unichoyi, observing that it is an error to think the minimum wage is only what a worker requires to cover his physical needs and keep himself above starvation, since the wage must ensure not only the sustenance of the employee and his family but also preserve his efficiency as a worker. Three decades later the Court added the sixth element. In Workmen Represented by Secretary v Management of Reptakos Brett and Co Ltd, decided in 1991 with Kuldip Singh J writing for the Court, the five components were supplemented by a further component: children's education, medical requirements, minimum recreation including festivals and ceremonies, and provision for old age and marriage should together constitute another twenty-five per cent of the total minimum wage. The Court's conclusion deserves to be carried in full in any honest account: a wage structure answering the six components is nothing more than a minimum wage at subsistence level, which employees are entitled to receive at all times and under all circumstances, and an employer who cannot pay it has no right to engage labour and no justification to run the industry. The need-based benchmark, five parts from a conference resolution and a sixth from a judgment, is the standard against which actual notified rates have been criticised ever since, and the gap between the benchmark and the rates governments have been willing to notify is the standing indictment of the fixation process by the labour movement. The statute created the machinery. The benchmark came from outside it, and the machinery was never bound to obey the benchmark. That gap between the norm and the notification is where the argument about the level of Indian minimum wages has always actually lived.

Why the gap persists is worth stating without euphemism, because the reasons are structural and both sides of industry state them openly. A government fixing rates weighs the benchmark against the employment it must answer for and the small establishments whose closure a high rate is said to threaten; it also weighs the fiscal echo, since a rise in the agricultural and unskilled rates feeds into public works wage bills and procurement costs across the state. A committee, for its part, receives the need-based calculation as a claim to be negotiated rather than a conclusion to be applied, and the independent members who hold the balance have no instruction in the Act preferring the 1957 norms to the employers' capacity evidence. The norms bind nobody because nobody with power under the Act ever adopted them as a rule of decision: not the statute, which prescribes machinery and not amounts, not the committees, which advise, and not the courts, which in Unichoyi declined to test a committee-made wage structure against a standard of the courts' own. The benchmark's authority is moral and forensic, the authority of a number everyone cites and no one must obey, and the Satpathy exercise of 2018 and 2019 repeated the pattern exactly: an expert body applied the need-based method with modern data, produced a figure, and the figure entered the debate without entering the law.


## The courts make the floor absolute

The constitutional attacks came first and failed completely. In Bijay Cotton Mills Ltd v State of Ajmer and Edward Mills Co Ltd v State of Ajmer, both decided in 1955, employers challenged the Act as an unreasonable restriction on the freedom to carry on trade under Article 19(1)(g) of the Constitution. The Supreme Court upheld the Act: a law fixing minimum wages in sweated employments, where the bargaining power of workers is minimal, is a reasonable restriction in the public interest, and the hardship of an individual employer does not displace the policy. The validity of the Act was, after those decisions, treated as settled, and when tile manufacturers from Kerala renewed the challenge in U. Unichoyi, the Court recorded that in view of the two 1955 decisions the constitutional validity of the Act could no longer be in doubt.

The capacity doctrine followed from the Fair Wages Committee's three levels. In Messrs Crown Aluminium Works v Their Workmen, reported in 1958, the Court worked through the distinction between the minimum wage and the fair wage and held that the capacity of the employer to pay, decisive in fixing a fair wage, has no place in fixing the statutory minimum: the minimum is the irreducible level below which no industry can be permitted to work its labour, and an employer unable to pay it has no right to carry on the enterprise on cheaper terms. Unichoyi restated the point as a headnote proposition: any hardship that may be caused to employers by the wages fixed under the Act, or their incapacity to pay the same, are irrelevant considerations in fixing such wages. The same Court explained the Act's object in words that Airfreight later adopted: the Act purports to prevent the exploitation of labour, and in an underdeveloped country with unemployment on a large scale, labour may offer to work even on starvation wages; the policy of the Act is to prevent the employment of such sweated labour, and what is prescribed is the minimum wage which a welfare state assumes every employer must pay before he employs labour. In Kamani Metals and Alloys Ltd v Their Workmen, the Court added the sentence by which the doctrine is usually remembered: the minimum wage is independent of the kind of industry and applies alike to big and small, it must be paid irrespective of the extent of profits, the financial condition of the establishment or the availability of workmen on lower wages, and it sets the lowest limit below which wages cannot be allowed to sink in all humanity.

The statute carries the same absoluteness in its private law. Section 25, the contracting-out provision, declares that any contract or agreement, whether made before or after the commencement of the Act, by which an employee relinquishes or reduces his right to a minimum rate of wages or to any privilege or concession under the Act, is null and void in so far as it purports to reduce the minimum rate. A worker's signature on an agreement to accept less is therefore legally worthless. The floor is not a default term that the parties can vary. It is a prohibition addressed to both of them, and it exists precisely because the Act's drafters knew that a hungry worker will sign anything.

The third movement in the case law took the floor out of labour law altogether and into fundamental rights. In People's Union for Democratic Rights v Union of India, the Asiad workers case of 1982, the Supreme Court considered construction workers on the Asian Games projects in Delhi who were being paid less than the statutory minimum, often through chains of contractors. Bhagwati J held that force in Article 23 of the Constitution, which prohibits begar and other similar forms of forced labour, is not confined to physical or legal compulsion: it includes compulsion arising from hunger, poverty, want and destitution, and labour or service provided for remuneration less than the minimum wage is forced labour, because the worker provides it under the force of economic circumstance. In Sanjit Roy v State of Rajasthan, decided on 20 January 1983, the Court applied the principle against the state itself. Rajasthan had enacted the Rajasthan Famine Relief Works Employees (Exemption from Labour Laws) Act, 1964, which exempted famine relief works from the Minimum Wages Act, and paid workers on drought relief works less than the minimum. The Court struck the exemption down as violative of Article 23: whenever labour or service is taken by the state from any person, the state must pay at least the minimum wage, and it cannot take advantage of the helpless condition of persons affected by drought and scarcity to extract their labour for less. It said, in the sentence that carries the doctrine, that no work of utility and value can be allowed to be constructed on the blood and sweat of persons reduced to helplessness by drought and scarcity conditions. After these two decisions, payment below the floor is not merely a statutory offence. It is a constitutional wrong, enforceable against the state and, through Article 23's horizontal reach, against private employers as well.

What the courts did not do is equally instructive. They did not take over the level of the rate. Unichoyi refused, ordinarily, to examine the merits of a wage structure fixed on the recommendation of a committee on which employers and employees were represented and agreed, and the fact that comparable industries paid less did not show the notified rates to be excessive. Judicial review polices the floor's existence, its absoluteness and its coverage edges. The number itself remains with the Section 5 machinery, which is why litigation has been so much more successful at establishing that a minimum must be paid than at establishing what the minimum should be.

Two of the leading cases repay a closer look at their facts, because the doctrine reads differently when the workplaces are visible. U. Unichoyi arose from tile manufacture in Kerala, an industry of small works scattered across the Malabar coast, where the state had fixed minimum wages by the committee method and the employers attacked the rates as excessive and the fixation as bad. The Court's refusal to substitute its own assessment rested on a fact about process: the committee that recommended the rates had employers' and employees' representatives on it, they had participated, and a wage structure produced by that enquiry, with both sides heard, was not to be unsettled by a court comparing it with wages in other industries or other regions. The judgment's famous headnote, that the Act is a social welfare legislation and hardship to the employer is irrelevant to the fixation, is therefore paired with an institutional modesty: the Court policed the floor's existence and let the tripartite machinery own the number. Crown Aluminium, decided on the other side of the fair wage line, arose in industrial adjudication rather than under the Act, and its work for the minimum wage cases was to fix the boundary between the concepts. A tribunal fixing a fair wage must weigh capacity; a government fixing a statutory minimum must not. The later cases cite the two lines of authority together, Unichoyi for deference to the machinery and Crown Aluminium for the irrelevance of capacity within it, and between them they left very little room for an employer to attack a fixation on its merits. What remained litigable, and what employers therefore litigated for seventy years, was everything around the number: scheduling, classification, the appropriate government, the composition of committees, and the arithmetic of the package after Airfreight.

The Article 23 cases changed the parties as much as the principle. People's Union for Democratic Rights was a public interest petition about the construction of the Asiad Village and the stadia for the 1982 Asian Games in Delhi, projects of the Union Government executed through public authorities and layers of contractors, on which workers recruited from distant states were paid less than the Delhi minimum, with deductions taken by the intermediaries who had brought them. The Court's holdings reached past the contractors to the principal employers and the state: the obligation to pay the minimum could not be shed by interposing a contractor, the workers could enforce it through the fundamental rights chapter because Article 23 runs against private persons as well as the state, and the state authorities' failure to enforce the labour statutes on their own projects was itself a constitutional failure. Sanjit Roy then closed the exemption route: a state could not do by a special Act exempting famine relief works what the Constitution forbade it to do directly, and the plea that relief workers were not ordinary employees, or that the works were charity rather than employment, failed because the state had taken their labour and owed the floor for it. Together the two cases made the minimum wage the only Indian labour standard with a fundamental-rights remedy behind it, which is why, decades later, the first instinct of a public interest petition about underpaid workers is still to plead Article 23 alongside Section 20.

## Enforcement: inspectors, claims and a five-hundred-rupee fine

The enforcement architecture has three limbs: inspection, a summary claims process, and prosecution. Each limb is weaker than the norm it serves, and the proportions are themselves the finding of this section.

Section 19 provides for inspectors, appointed by notification by the appropriate government for defined local limits. An inspector may enter, at all reasonable hours and with assistants who are government or public authority servants, any premises where employees in a scheduled employment are employed or where work is given out to out-workers, in order to examine registers, records of wages and notices; may examine any person found there whom he has reasonable cause to believe is an employee; may require information from persons giving out work and from out-workers about the persons to and from whom work is given and the payments for it; and may seize or copy registers and records relevant to an offence he has reason to believe has been committed. Every inspector is a public servant, and a person required by an inspector to produce a document or give information is legally bound to do so. The design is thorough. Its constraint is arithmetic: inspectorates are small, workplaces are numbered in the millions, and the employments where underpayment is likeliest, in homes, fields and small workshops, are the hardest to enter and the least likely to keep the Section 18 records an inspection examines.

Section 20 builds the claims route, deliberately informal. The appropriate government appoints an authority for a specified area: a Commissioner for Workmen's Compensation, a Labour Commissioner, an officer not below that rank, or any other officer with experience as a judge of a civil court or as a stipendiary magistrate. An application may be presented by the employee himself, by a legal practitioner, by an official of a registered trade union authorised in writing to act on his behalf, by an inspector, or by any person acting with the authority's permission. That list of permitted applicants is the Act's recognition that the underpaid worker is the person least able to litigate alone: the union official and the inspector are built into the procedure as substitute claimants. The application must be presented within six months from the date on which the minimum wages or other amount became payable, but the authority may admit it later if the applicant satisfies it that there was sufficient cause for the delay. The authority hears both sides, holds such further inquiry as it thinks necessary, and may direct the employer to pay the amount by which the minimum wages payable exceed the amount actually paid, together with compensation as it thinks fit not exceeding ten times the amount of the excess. For claims of other kinds under the section, the compensation ceiling is ten rupees. Section 21 permits a single application on behalf of any number of employees, with the compensation ceiling applied to the aggregate, so a workforce can claim as one. The direction of the authority is final under Section 20(6), the authority has the powers of a civil court for evidence and compelling witnesses and documents, amounts directed to be paid are recoverable as if they were a fine, and Section 24 bars a civil suit for wages that form the subject of a Section 20 application or could have been recovered by one. The route is fast by the standards of Indian litigation and costs little. Its traps are the six-month window, which a worker who does not know the notified rate will usually miss before condonation becomes an argument, and the quality of proof where Section 18 registers were never kept. This account of the machinery is general information about how the Act works, not advice on an individual claim, and the limitation rule in Section 20 makes prompt advice on a real case essential.

Prosecution is the third limb, and it is the most hedged. Section 22 makes it an offence for an employer to pay less than the minimum rates fixed for an employee's class of work, or less than the amount due under the Act, or to contravene a rule or order under Section 13, punishable with imprisonment for a term which may extend to six months, or with fine which may extend to five hundred rupees, or with both, with a direction that the court, in imposing a fine, take into account any compensation already awarded under Section 20. Section 22A punishes contraventions for which no other penalty is provided with a fine which may extend to five hundred rupees. Section 22B then gates the court door: no court may take cognizance of the principal underpayment offence unless an application under Section 20 on the same facts has been presented and granted wholly or in part, and the appropriate government or an authorised officer has sanctioned the complaint, which must then be filed within one month of the sanction; other offences require the complaint or sanction of an inspector, with a six-month limit for Section 22A. The gate deserves a paragraph of its own, because it reverses the usual order of criminal law. A worker cannot set the prosecution in motion at all: he must first win, or partly win, a civil claim on the same facts, then persuade the government to sanction a complaint, then see it filed within a month of the sanction, a sequence in which every step can fail for reasons unrelated to the underpayment. The design was defended as protection against vexatious prosecution of employers, and Section 20 itself carries a penalty, up to fifty rupees, for an application the authority finds malicious or vexatious, so the Act hedges the worker's route as carefully as the employer's exposure. The practical consequence is that prosecutions for underpayment have always been rare relative to the scale of underpayment, and the criminal limb functions less as a deterrent in daily use than as a reserve power whose existence is meant to stiffen settlement before the Section 20 authority. An honest account of the Act's sanctions therefore ranks them in the order the statute's own structure produces: the compensation order first, the constitutional doctrine behind it, and the prosecution a distant third. The five-hundred-rupee maximum fine stands in the Act unrevised in amount since 1948, a figure that once had weight and that inflation has reduced to a token. Whatever deterrence the criminal limb supplies comes from the compensation order under Section 20, which can reach ten times the shortfall, and from the Article 23 doctrine, not from the fine. Section 22C extends liability to persons in charge of a company, Section 23 lets an employer charged with an offence bring the actual offender before the court and escape liability on proof of due diligence, and Section 22D requires amounts that could not be paid because the employee has died or cannot be traced to be deposited with the prescribed authority rather than kept.

The honest assessment is the one the brief for this article insists on and the record supports. Compliance in the unorganised sector, where most Indian workers work, has always been weak. The reasons are structural rather than accidental. The floor applies only to scheduled work, so the first failure is coverage. Rates change by notification and differ by employment, class and zone, so the second failure is knowledge: a worker cannot claim a rate he cannot state, and the publicity duty the rules impose is discharged by notices at workplaces that may not exist. The claims window is six months against employers who control the records. Inspectors are few. And the penalty for the deliberate cheat is a fine fixed in the money of 1948. Against that, the Act's defenders can point to the compensation multiplier, to the union and inspector routes into Section 20, and to the constitutional doctrine that converts underpayment into forced labour. Both accounts are true, and they describe the same statute: a norm stated in the strongest terms Indian law knows, carried by machinery that has never matched its strength.


## Why the floor is federal

The division of fixation between the Centre and the states is sometimes treated as an accident of drafting, and it is better understood as the Act's central political settlement. Labour was a subject on which both levels of the new Indian state legislated, and a minimum wages law had to choose between a single national fixation, machinery the India of 1948 did not possess and prices it did not share, and a divided fixation that put each employment under the government closest to it. Section 2(b) records the choice and its logic. The Central Government takes the employments that belong to the Union's own sphere of activity: employments carried on by or under the authority of the Central Government, railway administrations, mines, oil-fields, major ports, and corporations established by a Central Act. The residuary and far larger sphere, agriculture, factories in general, shops, services, and everything else, falls to the states. The line follows the constitutional geography of industry rather than the geography of need, which is why a mine worker and the worker in the canteen outside the mine gate can stand in different spheres, and why the Act's first jurisdictional question, whose employment is this, so often precedes the wage question.

The settlement bought local knowledge at the price of uniformity, and both sides of the bargain were real. A state government fixing the agricultural rate for its own districts knows the seasons, the crops and the prevailing wages in a way no central fixation of 1948 could have known them, and the committee machinery of Section 5 works best at that scale, with the employers and unions of the trade in the room. Against that, the division guaranteed from the first day that the floor would be a map rather than a line, that the same work would carry different legal prices across a state border, and that a race between states competing for investment with cheap labour was a standing possibility the Act did nothing to prevent. The advisory national floor of 1996 and the statutory floor wage of the Code are the two attempts, seventy years apart, to correct the second consequence while keeping the first benefit: leave fixation divided, but put a bottom under the division. That the correction required first a non-binding exhortation and then a constitutional-era codification, and that the 1948 Parliament did not attempt it at all, measures how completely the federal settlement was accepted by the generation that wrote the Act. They had built a Union out of provinces with very different economies, and a wage law that ignored the difference would have been uniform in the Gazette and fictitious in the field.

## The national floor that never was

Why did India, with a minimum wages Act from 1948, never have a national minimum wage? The first reason is constitutional design: labour is a concurrent subject, fixation was divided between the Centre and the states through the appropriate government definition, and each government fixed rates for its own sphere, at its own pace, by reference to its own prices and politics. The second reason is economic geography: the cost of the 1957 basket differs so much between a metropolitan zone and a poor rural district that a single statutory number was either meaningless in one place or impossible in the other, and every committee that examined the question met that spread. The third reason is the scheduling gate itself: a universal national floor cannot coexist with a statute that applies only to listed employments, because the floor would still stop at the gate.

What India had instead was a floor beneath the floors, and it was advisory. On the recommendation of the National Commission on Rural Labour of 1991, the Central Government introduced the concept of a National Floor Level Minimum Wage in 1996 as a non-statutory measure, a figure states were urged not to fall below, intended to narrow the disparity between state rates and to introduce something like uniformity. It has been revised from time to time on the advice of the Central Advisory Board. The Ministry of Labour and Employment told the Rajya Sabha, in an answer given in February 2024, that the floor was last revised from Rs 160 to Rs 176 per day with effect from 1 June 2017. The figure binds nobody. A state fixing a rate below it breaks no provision of the 1948 Act, because the Act contains no floor wage at all.

The adequacy debate produced its own landmark figure. An expert committee chaired by Anoop Satpathy of the V. V. Giri National Labour Institute, appointed by the Ministry of Labour and Employment in January 2018 to determine the methodology for fixing a national minimum wage, reported in 2019 and recommended a need-based national minimum wage of Rs 375 per day, or Rs 9,750 per month, at July 2018 prices, for a family of 3.6 consumption units, with an additional house rent component for urban workers. The recommendation was not accepted. Placed next to the advisory floor of Rs 176 a day, the Satpathy figure is the clearest single statement of the distance between what the need-based method produces and what governments have been prepared to require: the evidence-based estimate stood at more than double the operative floor. Trade unions have treated that gap as the measure of official unwillingness. Employer organisations have treated the Satpathy method as detached from the paying capacity of the small establishments that employ most Indians. The number did its work either way, by making the gap impossible to describe as small.

The variation the floor was meant to discipline remains the system's defining feature. Because fixation is divided, because schedules differ, because zones and classes multiply within each schedule, and because revision dates stagger, two workers doing the same work in neighbouring states, or in the same state under different governments' spheres, can stand under entirely different legal minima. The Act does not even pursue internal equality of treatment across its own schedules: Section 3 permits different rates for different employments, classes, localities and age groups as a matter of design. What it prohibits is only payment below the applicable rate. Equality of the rate itself, across a border or across a street where spheres change, is a value the statute never adopted, and the debate about a national minimum wage is in substance a debate about whether to adopt it.

One form of equality the Act does contain is between men and women. The rates are fixed for classes of work in a scheduled employment, not for the sex of the worker doing the work, and the Act prescribes no lower rate for a woman performing the same class of work as a man; its machinery treats them equally. The wider guarantee of equal remuneration for the same work or work of a similar nature, and the prohibition on discrimination in recruitment, is the work of a separate statute, treated in [the article on the Equal Remuneration Act, 1976](/2016/02/01/equal-remuneration-act-1976-explained/), and the two protections should not be confused: the 1948 Act sets the height of the floor, the 1976 Act polices one ground on which an employer might set two floors.

## The succession: the Code on Wages, 2019

The reform that the brief for this article calls the single most significant in the subject's history was not a rise in any rate. It was the removal of the schedule. The Code on Wages, 2019, Act 29 of 2019, which received the President's assent on 8 August 2019, consolidates four enactments: the Payment of Wages Act, 1936, the Minimum Wages Act, 1948, the Payment of Bonus Act, 1965 and the Equal Remuneration Act, 1976. Its minimum wages chapter rewrites the two decisions on which the 1948 Act was built. First, coverage: the Code applies its wage provisions to employees generally, and the concept of scheduled employment is abolished, so the fixation duty no longer waits on a list. The question that generated seven decades of classification litigation, whether this work is scheduled, falls away, and the disputes move to the level and the definition of wages instead. Second, the floor: Section 9 of the Code empowers the Central Government to fix a floor wage, taking into account the minimum living standards of workers, with different floor wages permitted for different geographical areas, and provides that the minimum rates fixed by the appropriate governments shall not be less than the floor wage. The advisory National Floor Level Minimum Wage is thereby converted into a statutory concept, and states retain the power, and the duty, to fix higher rates above it, rates already higher than the floor being protected against reduction to the floor. The revision discipline is retained in the same form as Section 3 of the old Act: review and revision at intervals not exceeding five years. The Code also supplies a single definition of wages in Section 2(y), with a ceiling on the excluded allowances, which changes what counts toward the floor across all four of the consolidated subjects.

The position of the Code has to be stated with care, because enactment and operation are different events. The Code has been passed and notified, but its provisions are brought into force by notification in stages, and the Ministry of Labour and Employment has stated in Parliament that the relevant provisions of the Code on Wages had not come into force, the 1948 Act continuing to govern fixation, claims and penalties in the meantime. A reader relying on either regime for a live question should confirm the commencement position before acting on it, because the transition, when completed for minimum wages, replaces the machinery described in this article while preserving much of its substance: fixation by the appropriate government, tripartite consultation, the five-year revision ceiling, and a claims route for underpayment. What does not survive the transition is the gate. The successor statute, its floor wage and its uniform definition are treated in their own right in [the article on the Code on Wages, 2019](/2013/07/15/code-on-wages-2019-explained/), whose questions this article does not take.

The contrast between the two regimes can be stated as a series of substitutions, each aimed at a weakness this article has recorded. For the Schedule, the Code substitutes universal application: the minimum wage chapters speak to employees as such, and the seventy-year jurisprudence of classification, the process-or-branch-of-work cases, the hospital and club and institution cases, loses its subject matter. For the absent national floor, the Code substitutes the Section 9 floor wage, statutory rather than advisory, with the states free and expected to fix above it. For the fragmented definitions of wages across the old statutes, it substitutes the single definition in Section 2(y), under which the allowances excluded from wages are capped at fifty per cent of total remuneration, closing the device by which a pay packet could be structured so that the basic wage, the part to which the floor and other computations attached, shrank to a fraction of the whole. For the fixation duty, it retains the appropriate government and the five-year revision ceiling of Section 6, an admission that the federal division of wage-fixing was not itself the failure. What the Code does not substitute is a new enforcement philosophy: inspection, claims and penalties continue in recognisable form, which is why the assessment offered in this article, that the norm has always been stronger than the machinery, is likely to survive the succession as a description of the problem the machinery serves. Codification settled the coverage question that 1948 left open. It left the delivery question, of knowledge, records, inspectors and claims, where the old Act left it.

## The economics, stated honestly in both directions

No account of this Act is complete without the argument about whether a legal floor helps or harms the workers it names, and the stance of this article is that the statute's text and the compliance record can be stated with confidence while the employment effects remain genuinely contested. The argument against a high floor, pressed by employer organisations before wage committees for decades, runs as follows. Labour at the bottom of the Indian market is abundant and much of it is low in measured productivity. A statutory rate set above what the least productive worker's output will support does not raise that worker's income; it removes the job, or drives it into informality where no floor applies, or substitutes family labour and machinery. The burden falls hardest on the small establishments that dominate Indian employment and on the regions least able to bear it, which is why employer bodies resisted the Satpathy figure and why governments, which must answer for employment as well as wages, have fixed rates below the need-based benchmark in every decade.

The argument for the floor, in the form the Fair Wages Committee and the courts gave it, is that the bottom of the labour market is not the auction of the textbook. A worker facing hunger does not bargain; he accepts, and his acceptance at a starvation wage is the market failure the Act exists to correct. Unichoyi's answer remains the classical statement: in a country of mass unemployment, labour may offer to work even on starvation wages, and the Act's policy is to prevent the employment of such sweated labour in the general public interest. On this view a floor also does positive work. It prevents the efficient employer from being undercut by the sweating employer, it directs competition toward productivity rather than toward cheaper misery, and it sustains the worker's efficiency, which the need-based norms treat as an input into output rather than a welfare extra. The international empirical literature has not delivered the simple verdict the older theory predicted: the best-known study, by David Card and Alan Krueger in 1994, compared fast-food employment in New Jersey, which raised its minimum wage, with neighbouring Pennsylvania, which did not, and found no fall in employment, a result that pushed much of labour economics toward models in which employers hold wage-setting power and a floor transfers income more than it destroys jobs. Later research is divided by level, by sector and by country, and India's informal economy, where the floor is honoured in the breach, fits none of the models cleanly.

This article does not adjudicate that debate, because the statute can be understood without adjudicating it. What the law does is clear: it fixes a rate for scheduled work, makes the duty absolute, and enforces it weakly. What the evidence shows about compliance is also clear, and it cuts across the economics. Where a large share of the protected workforce is in practice paid below the floor, the argument about whether the floor is set at the right level is, for those workers, an argument about a number they never receive. The operative Indian question has never been only where the floor should stand. It has been whether the floor reaches the worker at all, through the gate of the Schedule, through the worker's knowledge of the rate, and through a claims process he can use. The Code on Wages answers the first of those three. The other two survive every codification.

One further economic feature of the Indian case deserves statement because it explains the shape of the Act itself. In much of rural India the labour market for a village or a cluster of villages has few buyers: a handful of landholders and contractors hiring most of the labour on offer, with migration as the worker's only exit. Economists call such a market a monopsony, and in a monopsony the wage settles below the value of the work even without any conspiracy among employers, because each worker's alternative is another employer offering the same depressed rate or the cost of leaving. A statutory floor in such a market does different work from a floor in a competitive one: it substitutes for the missing competition, and the risk that it prices workers out of jobs is at its lowest precisely where employer power is at its highest. The framers of 1948 did not use the vocabulary, but their chosen fields of application, agriculture, plantations, and the sweated trades where a few buyers faced many sellers, are a monopsony map of the Indian economy drawn from experience. The counter-argument retains its force at the other margin: where the buyer is a marginal small employer facing real competition in his product market, a floor set without regard to his costs can close him, and the Act's exemption power in Section 26, used for special reasons and limited periods, is the statute's own acknowledgement that the two margins coexist.

## The Act among its neighbours

No labour statute works alone, and the 1948 Act is woven into three others at points its own text marks. The first neighbour is the Payment of Wages Act, 1936, which regulates the manner and timing of payment, the wage period, the day of payment and the deductions an employer may lawfully make, for the employments it covers. The two Acts divide the subject between them: the 1936 Act assumes a wage agreed or awarded and polices its payment; the 1948 Act fixes the lowest wage that may be agreed and polices its level. A worker can be paid punctually, in cash, without an unlawful deduction, and still be paid unlawfully little, which is the gap the 1948 Act exists to close, and the Code on Wages consolidated the two statutes into one code for exactly that reason. Section 12's own saving, that the duty to pay not less than the minimum is subject to deductions the Act authorises, is the seam where the two statutes meet.

The second neighbour is the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947, and the relationship is managed by avoidance. Wage fixation by industrial adjudication and wage fixation under the 1948 Act can both speak to the same workforce, and Section 3(2A) prevents the collision by suspending the Act's fixation and revision for employees in a scheduled employment while an industrial dispute about their wages is pending or an award governing them is in operation. The theory is that a workforce strong enough to be in adjudication has a wage-setting process of its own, and the statutory floor, designed for the unorganised, steps aside rather than undercutting the tribunal. The cost of the courtesy is a class of organised workers whose statutory floor is frozen for the duration of proceedings and awards, sometimes for years, with the adjudicated wage as their only protection in the interval.

The third neighbour is the Factories Act, 1948, enacted the same year, whose Section 59 gives the factory worker double the ordinary rate for overtime beyond nine hours in a day or forty-eight in a week. Section 14(2) of this Act preserves that entitlement expressly, and Section 14's own comparative formula, the higher of the two applicable overtime rates, generalises the courtesy: wherever another law of the appropriate government gives a better overtime rate, the worker takes the better. The pattern across all three relationships is the same and it is characteristic of the first generation of Indian labour legislation, statutes drafted as modules, each aware of the others, connected by savings and non-obstante clauses rather than by a single code. The Code on Wages, 2019, which merges four of the modules, is the repudiation of that architecture; the labour codes' long commencement, during which the modules continue to govern, is a reminder of how much law the modular system accumulated in seventy years.

## The floor in the fields: agriculture in Part II

Part II of the Schedule contains one entry, and it is the largest workplace in India. Employment in agriculture reaches the tilling of the soil, dairy farming, the production, cultivation, growing and harvesting of agricultural and horticultural commodities, the raising of livestock, bees and poultry, forestry and timbering operations, and any practice performed by a farmer or on a farm as an incident to farming operations. State governments fix the agricultural rate, because agriculture falls in the state sphere, and the agricultural minimum is in most states the rate that touches the largest number of workers and the rate whose enforcement is hardest. The work is seasonal, dispersed across millions of small holdings, paid in a mixture of daily, piece and harvest-linked forms, and carried on between parties who are often neighbours and sometimes kin, in which setting the figure of the inspector and the machinery of Section 20 sit awkwardly. The agricultural worker is also the worker the National Commission on Rural Labour was describing when it recommended a national floor in 1991: rural rates were the lowest, the disparities between states the widest, and the case for a floor beneath the floors began in the fields rather than in the factories.

Agriculture also exposes the limit of the wage form itself. A daily rate assumes a day of work offered and accepted, and much agricultural underpayment takes the form not of a rate below the notification but of days not offered, hours stretched within a notional day, and piece arrangements at harvest whose conversion into a daily equivalent nobody computes. Section 17's protection for piece workers and Section 15's protection for the worker given less than a full day speak to these forms, but each requires a claim, and a claim requires the worker to know the rate, to keep count of days, and to survive the six-month window in a labour market where next season's hiring depends on this season's goodwill. The statute's text reaches the field. Its machinery was designed in towns.

## Out-workers: the home as a workplace

The out-worker clause of Section 2(i) deserves a section of its own because it locates a large part of Indian manufacturing inside the home. The putting-out system, in which a merchant or manufacturer distributes raw material to workers who process it at home and return the finished article for sale, was the dominant form of production in the beedi trade and remains widespread in garment stitching, agarbatti rolling, papad making, and the assembly of small goods. The worker in such a system has no factory to inspect, no attendance register, no fellow workers in one place, and a rate quoted per thousand pieces or per gross rather than per day, which is why the Act's protections for this class are specific: the definition includes the out-worker by name, Section 17 guarantees the time rate where no piece rate is fixed, Section 3(2) authorises a guaranteed time rate for piece work, and Section 19 arms the inspector with power to require information from the person giving out the work about the persons to and from whom work is given out and the payments made for it. Section 19 is the only inspection power in the Act drafted for a workplace that is somebody's house, and its information-requiring form, rather than a power of entry into homes, shows the drafters balancing the floor against the threshold of the dwelling.

The practical weakness is conversion. A piece rate complies with the Act only if a worker of ordinary diligence, working the normal day, earns at least the time rate, and testing that proposition requires time-and-output evidence that neither party keeps. Where governments have fixed piece rates for home-based trades, the rates are commonly fixed per unit of output with the time-rate comparison left to assertion. The out-worker's inclusion in 1948 was conceptually ahead of its time; the International Labour Organization adopted a convention on home work only in 1996. Its enforcement remains the thinnest part of the Act's operation, because every element of the machinery, scheduling, inspection, records and claims, assumes a workplace the out-worker does not have.

## Two spheres, hundreds of schedules

The appropriate government division produced two wage-fixing systems under one Act. In the central sphere, the Central Government fixes rates for the employments in its list, which grew by scheduling to forty-five scheduled employments on the Ministry of Labour and Employment's own count, covering among others construction, mining, agriculture on central farms and establishments, loading and unloading, housekeeping and sweeping in central establishments, and the security and courier trades as they were added. In the state sphere, every state and union territory maintains its own schedule, and the employments scheduled across all states together run well over a thousand, the figures published at different times differing because scheduling is continuous and no single consolidated count is authoritative. A worker's floor therefore depends first on a jurisdictional question, whose sphere the establishment falls in, and only then on the rate. Disputes about the appropriate government, whether an establishment belongs to the Centre or the state, form their own line of litigation under the Act, and a claim filed against the wrong sphere's rate fails on a point that has nothing to do with the wage paid.

The state schedules also record each state's economic history. The original Part I entries, beedi, mica, lac, tanneries, plantations, read as a census of the sweated trades of 1948. Later additions track the changing economy: hotels and restaurants, shops and commercial establishments, private hospitals and nursing homes, brick kilns, stone quarries, domestic work in the states that scheduled it, and employments in newer services. Each addition required the Section 27 process, three months' notice and a notification, and each was usually the product of a campaign by the workers of that trade or a direction from a court or a commission. Read in sequence, the schedules are the closest thing Indian labour law has to a roll of the occupations that succeeded in making themselves visible to the state. The corollary is uncomfortable and is the series thesis for this cluster stated from the other side: the occupations absent from the schedules are, in the main, the ones whose workers could not organise, petition or litigate their way onto the list.

## Revision in practice: how five years becomes ten

Section 3(1)(b) sets a ceiling of five years on the interval between reviews. The record of compliance with that ceiling is the weakest part of the fixation system, and the Act's own text anticipates the failure twice over: the saving clause keeps the old rate alive when the interval is missed, and Section 31 validates the fixations of the 1950s that missed the original deadlines. Between revisions, the dearness allowance carries the whole burden of price defence, and where a government revises the allowance regularly the real floor erodes slowly, while the basic rate, the part that expresses the judgment about need, can stand unrevised for long stretches. Successive committees and the Parliamentary debates on the Code returned to the same complaint, that revisions announced as five-yearly arrived late, applied prospectively from notification rather than from the date they fell due, and left the worker to absorb the intervening inflation in the basic component. The three-month delay built into Section 5 between notification and commencement adds a further lag at the end of every revision, so that even a punctual revision reaches the pay packet months after the decision. The Code on Wages retained the five-year ceiling in Section 6, which suggests the drafters regarded the interval as sound and the failure as one of administration rather than of law. Workers' organisations drew the opposite conclusion from the same record: a duty whose breach carries no consequence for the government and no arrears for the worker is a duty in name. The statute supports the second reading as easily as the first, since the only person Section 3 obliges is the government and the only remedy the Act gives for its default is none at all.

## Reading a rate notification

A reader who wants to know the lawful wage for a particular job has to work through a document built for administrators, and the skill is worth describing because so many claims fail at this stage. A typical state notification names the scheduled employment, then tabulates rates by class of work, commonly unskilled, semi-skilled, skilled and highly skilled, with clerical and supervisory categories in some schedules. It then divides the state into zones, because Section 3 permits locality rates, so the same class carries different figures in a metropolitan zone, in other municipal areas and in the rest of the state. It states whether the rates are daily or monthly, monthly figures usually being the daily rate multiplied by twenty-six, the conventional count of paid days that folds the weekly rest day into the month. It then deals with dearness allowance, either folding an allowance up to a stated index point into the tabulated figure, on the Airfreight pattern, or tabulating the allowance separately with the index and the date from which it runs. Finally it states the date from which the notification operates, which under Section 5 is three months after issue unless the notification provides otherwise. The errors that recur in practice are each a misreading of one of these elements: the wrong zone, the wrong class, a monthly figure compared against a daily payment, an allowance treated as already included when it is tabulated separately, or a superseded notification relied on after a revision. The Act places the duty of exhibiting the rates at the workplace on the employer through the rules under Section 18, and the inspectorate's failure to police that duty is one reason the notification, a public document in the Gazette, remains effectively private knowledge in much of the unorganised sector.

The index machinery behind the allowance deserves a word, because it is the part of the system that works most nearly as designed. The cost of living index numbers against which allowances are computed are compiled and published by the Labour Bureau, the Government of India's labour statistics office, for industrial workers and for agricultural and rural labourers, from price data collected in centres across the country. The competent authority appointed under the Act takes the published index, applies the formula the notification prescribes, points of index movement against a stated amount per point or a percentage neutralisation, and notifies the resulting allowance for the coming period. The process is technocratic, regular and largely uncontested, in striking contrast to the basic rate, which waits on committees, boards and governments. The contrast carries a lesson about the Act's design: the element of the wage that was delegated to a formula moves like clockwork, and the element reserved to negotiation moves like politics. Reformers who have proposed indexing the whole minimum, or fixing the basic rate by a statutory formula drawn from the need-based norms, are in substance proposing to move the basic rate from the second world into the first, and the resistance they meet is the resistance of every interest that benefits from the negotiation, including governments that prefer a wage they control to a wage a formula commands.

## A claim, walked through

The machinery of Sections 19 to 22 is easier to judge when it is followed through a single case in outline, using only the steps the Act prescribes. A worker in a scheduled employment, paid a daily amount below the notified rate for his class and zone, learns the correct rate, from a union, an inspector, or the notice exhibited under the rules. He counts the shortfall over the days worked, knowing the claim must be presented within six months of each amount becoming payable, so the oldest months fall away first. He may present the application himself to the Section 20 authority for the area, or a trade union official authorised in writing may present it, or an inspector may, which matters where the worker fears dismissal for complaining, although the Act gives the authority no interim power to protect his job. The authority issues notice, hears the employer, and examines such records as exist; where the employer kept no registers, the worker's account, supported by co-workers, carries the weight the missing Section 18 records would have carried. If the claim succeeds, the direction orders payment of the excess for the period within limitation, with compensation the authority fixes up to ten times that excess, and the direction is final, with the amount recoverable as if it were a fine imposed by a magistrate. Only then does the criminal limb open, since a prosecution for the underpayment requires the granted application and government sanction, filed within a month of the sanction. The walk-through shows the design plainly: the Act trusts the summary civil recovery and distrusts its own prosecution, and a worker's realistic remedy, in the statute as in practice, is the compensation order rather than the conviction.

## Committees in session: how a wage gets negotiated

The committee method of Section 5 is where the Indian minimum wage is actually argued out, and its procedure explains both the legitimacy and the slowness of the outcomes. A government proposing to fix or revise rates for a scheduled employment appoints a committee for that employment, with employer and employee representatives in equal number and independent members up to one-third, an independent member in the chair. The committee holds enquiries: it receives memoranda, hears the associations of the trade, examines cost of living data collected by the labour bureau machinery, and in the stronger traditions visits workplaces. The employers' case is built on capacity, the workers' case on the need-based norms, and the independent members hold the balance, which is why the composition rule in Section 9 matters so much in practice. A committee dominated numerically by either side ceases to be tripartite in anything but form, and the one-third cap on independents, with the chair reserved to them, is the statutory device for keeping the negotiation honest.

The committee's product is advice. It reports its recommended rates to the government, the Advisory Board co-ordinates the recommendations of the several committees so that rates across employments in the state do not drift into absurdity relative to one another, and the government then notifies the rates it accepts, which need not be the rates recommended. That last step is where the system is most often criticised from both directions: unions complain that governments trim committee recommendations downward under employer pressure applied after the report, and employers complain that governments accept recommendations the industry cannot bear for fear of the political cost of trimming them. The Supreme Court's deference in U. Unichoyi rests on the committee stage, not the notification stage: the Kerala rates survived challenge because a representative committee had enquired and advised, and the Court treated that enquiry as the safeguard. Where a government uses the notification method instead, publishing its own proposals and hearing objections, the safeguard is thinner, which is why the Act insists that revision by that route must still go before the Advisory Board. The two methods are often run in sequence in practice, a committee's enquiry informing proposals that are then notified for objections, and the full cycle, from appointment to a notification in force, is measured in years rather than months in all but the most routine allowance revisions.

## Children, adolescents and apprentices

Section 3(3) authorises different minimum rates for adults, adolescents, children and apprentices, and the provision is a reminder that the Act of 1948 was written for a labour market in which the employment of the young was regulated by differentiation rather than by prohibition. The Act itself bans nothing: it does not forbid the employment of a child in a scheduled employment, and it does not set a minimum age. What it does is price the categories differently, permitting a lower rate for a child or an adolescent than for an adult in the same class of work, and a rate for an apprentice that reflects the training element in the engagement. Prohibition and hours regulation for the young lived in other statutes, the Factories Act and the mines and plantations legislation, and a general child labour statute came much later. The consequence for the floor is that the lowest lawful rates in many schedules have historically been the children's rates in employments such as agriculture and the household trades, and the campaign against child labour in India was fought largely outside this Act, which supplied only the pricing.

The differentiation also created a standing incentive problem that the courts and committees recognised: a materially lower adolescent or child rate invites substitution of younger for adult workers, which is why the differentials in most schedules narrowed over time and why the apprentice category is hedged in the rules by limits on the proportion of apprentices and the duration of apprenticeship. The Code on Wages carried the Section 3(3) differentiations forward in its own fixation provision, retaining different rates for different classes of work and geographical areas, so the structure of a rate table with age and skill gradations survived codification. A reader comparing schedules across decades can watch the child rates thin out and disappear from employment after employment, a change produced by scheduling practice and by the child labour legislation rather than by any amendment to this Act's text, which still carries the 1948 categories undisturbed.

## What the Act does not do

A statute is defined as much by its silences, and the silences of this Act map the reform agenda that the Code on Wages later took up in part. The Act contains no national rate and no floor beneath state rates, the gap the advisory National Floor Level Minimum Wage papered over and Section 9 of the Code addresses. It contains no indexation duty: the cost of living allowance of Section 4 is a permitted structure, not a commanded one, and a government may fix an all-inclusive rate and let inflation work on it between revisions. It contains no consequence for a government that misses the five-year revision ceiling, and no arrears mechanism by which a worker recovers the difference between the rate paid during a delayed revision and the rate eventually fixed. It contains no protection for the claimant's employment: a worker who files under Section 20 has a remedy for the wages and none in this Act against dismissal in retaliation, a gap the walk-through of a claim makes visible. It contains no interest provision for delayed payment, the compensation of up to ten times the excess doing that work in the authority's discretion instead. And it contains no provision reaching the largest single determinant of low wages, the absence of work itself: the floor prices a day of labour, and a worker offered two days a week at a lawful rate is poorer than a worker offered six days below it, a comparison the statute cannot make because employment levels lie outside its scheme entirely. Each silence was a choice the drafters could defend in 1948, when the administrative state was thin and the Act's ambition was already large. Read together, the silences explain why the Act could be simultaneously a landmark and, for the majority of the workers it was written for, a promise honoured mainly in the breach.

## The workers the floor reaches last

Three classes of worker test the Act hardest, and the pattern of their difficulty is the same in each case: the law covers them, and something between the law and the workplace fails. The first is the inter-state migrant, the figure at the centre of the Asiad case. A migrant worker is employed in a sphere and a schedule not his own state's, under a rate notified in a language and a Gazette he will never see, frequently through a contractor who recruited him at a distance and whose deductions and advances bind him before the first wage is paid. The Act's answer, the through-another-person employer definition and the inspector's and union's standing under Section 20, assumes institutions at the destination that the migrant, by definition a stranger there, is least placed to use, and his claim must still be filed within six months at the place of work he may already have left. The second is the woman worker in the scheduled trades. The rate for her class of work is the same as a man's, and in that formal sense the Act is sex-blind, but the classification of work into skilled and unskilled, and the assignment of women disproportionately to the classes rated lowest and to piece-rated home work, means the floor she stands on is the lowest the schedule offers, and her underpayment is the hardest to prove where the work is done at home on pieces. The third is the worker in an employment that was scheduled late or never, whose position this article has described from the start: for him the Act is not a weak remedy but no remedy, and his wage is set by the market the Act was written to correct. A statute's coverage can be measured by its text or by its failures, and the failures of this one concentrate, with a regularity that cannot be accidental, on the workers with the least power in exactly the sense the preamble's generation understood: the migrant without local standing, the woman in the home-based trades, and the unscheduled.

## Counting the wage: periods, the rest day and the month

The Act's arithmetic deserves a closer statement, because disputes about the floor are very often disputes about the count rather than the rate. Wages under the Act may be fixed by the hour, by the day, by the month or by such other larger wage period as may be prescribed, and the choice of period interacts with everything else. A daily rate prices the normal working day that Section 13 and the rules define, and an employer who engages a worker for part of a day owes, under Section 15, the full day's wage unless the shortfall is the worker's own unwillingness. The weekly rest day that Section 13 provides for is a paid element of the structure, remuneration for the day of rest being part of what the section authorises the government to prescribe, which is why monthly rates in Indian notifications are conventionally built on twenty-six days: the month is counted as the working days plus the paid rest days, and a monthly-paid worker who is docked for the rest days has been underpaid even if every working day was paid at the daily rate. Overtime arithmetic runs off the same base, the hourly equivalent of the daily rate divided across the normal hours, and a piece rate is lawful only in the shadow of the time rate it must match under Section 17. Each of these conversions is a place where an employer who pays the notified figure for the notified unit can still pay less than the Act requires, by counting fewer days, docking the rest day, or stretching the day. The inspection and claims machinery exists to police the conversions as much as the rate, and the worker checking a pay packet against a notification needs the conversions as much as the headline figure, which is a further reason the publicity duty in the rules matters: a rate without its arithmetic is only half a floor.

## Verdict

The Minimum Wages Act, 1948 is best read as two statutes in one binding. The first is a doctrine: a wage floor set by need, absolute against contract and against capacity, crowned by a constitutional holding that payment below it is forced labour. That doctrine, built by the Committee on Fair Wages, the Fifteenth Indian Labour Conference, Crown Aluminium, Unichoyi, Reptakos Brett, People's Union for Democratic Rights and Sanjit Roy, is as strong a statement of labour's claim against the market as Indian law contains. The second statute is an administration: a schedule that had to be extended occupation by occupation, committees that advised and governments that delayed, inspectors too few, a claims window of six months and a maximum fine priced in the rupees of 1948. That administration never matched the doctrine, and the gap between them is the subject's real history. The series thesis for this cluster is that a statute whose obligation is absolute but whose coverage is discretionary tells you nothing from its text about who is protected, and that the record of scheduling is where the answer lives. The seventy-year record bears the thesis out. When reform finally came, it did not come as a higher rate. It came, in the Code on Wages, as the removal of the schedule and a floor wage binding on every state, which is a concession, written into law, that coverage was the whole question all along.

For the student of Indian legislation, the Act's deepest lesson is about the difference between a right and a route. The right the Act confers is stated without qualification and defended by the courts without retreat: no contract can waive it, no balance sheet can answer it, and the Constitution stands behind it. The route to the right runs through a Gazette notification the worker may never see, a classification his employer's business may not match, a six-month window that runs whether or not he knows the rate, and records in the keeping of the person who underpaid him. Indian labour law is full of statutes in which the right outruns the route, and this Act, the oldest of the wage statutes and the template of the rest, is where the pattern was set. To read it whole, doctrine and machinery together, is to understand why the argument about Indian minimum wages has never been settled by the strength of the principle, and why every generation of reform, from the amendment of 1957 to the Code of 2019, has been an argument about the route rather than about the right.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who fixes the minimum wage in India?

Under the Minimum Wages Act, 1948, minimum wages are fixed by the "appropriate government" for each scheduled employment. The Central Government is the appropriate government for employments carried on by or under its authority, for railway administrations, mines, oil-fields, major ports and corporations established by a Central Act. The State Government is the appropriate government for all other scheduled employments. Each government fixes and revises rates for its own sphere, acting under Section 3 either through committees appointed under Section 5 or by publishing proposals for objections, advised by an Advisory Board. There is no single fixing authority, which is why rates differ across the country.

### Q: What is a scheduled employment and why does it limit coverage?

A scheduled employment is an employment specified in the Schedule to the Act, in Part I or Part II, or added to either part by the appropriate government by notification under Section 27 after three months' notice. The Act's duties arise only for scheduled employments: the obligation in Section 3 to fix rates, and the employer's obligation in Section 12 to pay them, both attach to work the Schedule names. An occupation that no government has scheduled is outside the Act altogether, however low its wages, so the statute's protection depends on an executive listing decision. Coverage, decided occupation by occupation, is therefore the Act's built-in limit.

### Q: Does India have a national minimum wage?

India has never had a statutory national minimum wage under the Minimum Wages Act, 1948, because the Act contains no national rate and divides fixation between the Centre and the states for their own spheres. What exists is the National Floor Level Minimum Wage, a non-statutory figure introduced in 1996 on the recommendation of the National Commission on Rural Labour, which states are urged not to fall below but which binds nobody. The Ministry of Labour and Employment informed the Rajya Sabha in February 2024 that this floor was last revised to Rs 176 per day with effect from 1 June 2017. A binding national floor arrives only with the floor wage under the Code on Wages, 2019.

### Q: How often must minimum wages be revised?

Section 3(1)(b) of the Act requires the appropriate government to review the minimum rates it has fixed at intervals not exceeding five years, and to revise them if necessary. Five years is a ceiling on the interval, not a promise of a rise: a review may leave rates unchanged, and if a government misses the interval the old rates continue in force until revised, since the section expressly preserves its power to review after the period has expired. In practice revision has often been late. Separately, the dearness allowance element of most rates is adjusted at shorter intervals against the cost of living index, which is how rates move between statutory revisions.

### Q: What is a need based minimum wage?

A need-based minimum wage is a minimum calculated from the measured needs of a standard worker's family rather than from bargaining or from an employer's capacity. The Fifteenth Indian Labour Conference of 1957 set the norms: a family of three consumption units for one earner, food at 2,700 calories per adult, clothing at eighteen yards per person a year, housing at the rent for the minimum area under the government's industrial housing scheme, and fuel, lighting and miscellaneous items at twenty per cent of the total. The Supreme Court in the Reptakos Brett case added a further twenty-five per cent for children's education, medical needs, recreation and provision for old age and marriage.

### Q: Can an employer pay below the minimum wage with the worker's consent?

No. The worker's consent is legally irrelevant. Section 25 of the Act provides that any contract or agreement, made before or after the Act commenced, by which an employee relinquishes or reduces his right to a minimum rate of wages or any privilege under the Act is null and void in so far as it purports to reduce the minimum rate. A signed agreement to accept less does not protect the employer, and the payment remains recoverable under Section 20 and punishable under Section 22. The rule exists because consent given by a worker who needs the job is not treated by the Act as a free bargain.

### Q: Why do minimum wages differ so much across states?

Because the Act divides fixation between governments and multiplies the variables within each fixation. The Centre fixes rates for its sphere and each state for its own, at different times and by reference to different prices and politics. Within a single scheduled employment, Section 3 permits different rates for different classes of work, for adults, adolescents, children and apprentices, and for different localities, and rates may be fixed by the hour, day or month, with dearness allowance layered on top. A single state can therefore lawfully carry several hundred distinct rates. The differences are not administrative error; they are the product of the statute's design.

### Q: What is the floor wage and how does it bind states?

The floor wage is the statutory national baseline created by Section 9 of the Code on Wages, 2019. The Central Government fixes it taking into account the minimum living standards of workers, and may fix different floor wages for different geographical areas. Section 9 provides that the minimum rates fixed by the appropriate governments shall not be less than the floor wage, so a state may fix any rate above the floor but none below it, and a state whose existing rate is already higher may not reduce it to the floor. It converts the older advisory National Floor Level Minimum Wage into a legal limit, and operates once the Code's provisions are in force.

### Q: How can a worker claim unpaid minimum wages under Section 20?

The worker applies to the authority appointed under Section 20 for the area, an officer such as a Labour Commissioner or an officer with experience as a civil court judge or stipendiary magistrate. The application may be made by the worker, a legal practitioner, an official of a registered trade union authorised in writing, an inspector, or another person with the authority's permission. It must be presented within six months of the date the wages became payable, though the authority may admit a later application where the applicant shows sufficient cause. After hearing both sides, the authority may direct payment of the shortfall plus compensation up to ten times the excess.

### Q: What happens to an employer who pays less than the minimum wage?

Two consequences run in parallel. Before the Section 20 authority, the employer can be directed to pay the full shortfall together with compensation up to ten times the amount underpaid. Separately, paying less than the minimum rate is an offence under Section 22, punishable with imprisonment up to six months, or a fine up to five hundred rupees, or both, though a prosecution for underpayment can be launched only after a Section 20 application on the same facts has been granted wholly or in part and the government has sanctioned the complaint. The compensation order, not the small fine, is the sanction with real weight.

### Q: What is variable dearness allowance?

Variable dearness allowance is the price-linked element of a minimum wage. Section 4 of the Act permits a rate to consist of a basic rate plus a cost of living allowance adjusted to accord as nearly as practicable with variations in the cost of living index number. In practice governments fix a basic rate for an employment and revise the allowance at regular intervals, commonly twice a year, by reference to the consumer price index, so that the real value of the minimum is defended between the five-yearly statutory revisions. The Supreme Court in Airfreight Ltd v State of Karnataka held that the allowance is not a separate addition but part of the single package that constitutes the minimum wage.

### Q: Does the Minimum Wages Act cover domestic workers?

Only where a government has brought domestic work inside the Act. The Act applies to scheduled employments, and domestic work is not in the Schedule as enacted; a state government must add employment in domestic work to its schedule under Section 27 before any minimum rate can be fixed for it. A number of states have done so, and in those states a domestic worker has a notified minimum and the Section 20 claims route. In a state that has not scheduled domestic work, the Act gives the domestic worker nothing, which is the scheduling limit operating on the largest and most informal workforce in the country.

### Q: Who counts as an employee under the Minimum Wages Act?

Section 2(i) defines an employee as any person employed for hire or reward to do any work, skilled or unskilled, manual or clerical, in a scheduled employment for which minimum rates have been fixed. The definition expressly includes an out-worker, someone given articles or materials by another person to process for sale, working at home or in premises outside that person's control, which brings home-based piece workers inside the Act. It also includes any person the appropriate government declares to be an employee. Members of the Armed Forces of the Union are excluded. The definition follows the work and the scheduling of the employment, not the label the parties use.

### Q: What is the difference between a minimum wage, a fair wage and a living wage?

The three levels come from the Committee on Fair Wages of 1948. The minimum wage is the floor: it must provide not merely bare subsistence but enough to preserve the worker's efficiency, with some provision for education, medical needs and amenities, and it must be paid regardless of the employer's capacity. The fair wage sits above it and below the living wage, rising toward the living wage as the industry's capacity to pay allows, capacity being the governing consideration at this level. The living wage is the goal at the top, a wage enabling a decent standard of life, which Article 43 of the Constitution directs the state to endeavour to secure.

### Q: What overtime rate must be paid under the Minimum Wages Act?

Section 14 requires an employer to pay, for every hour or part of an hour worked beyond the hours constituting a normal working day, at the overtime rate fixed under the Act or under any other law of the appropriate government in force, whichever is higher. The Act itself works comparatively rather than naming a single multiple, and Section 14 expressly preserves Section 59 of the Factories Act, 1948, so a factory worker keeps the double rate that provision gives for work beyond nine hours in a day or forty-eight in a week. Work on a weekly rest day must likewise be paid at not less than the overtime rate.

### Q: What powers does an Inspector have under the Minimum Wages Act?

An inspector appointed under Section 19 may enter, at all reasonable hours, any premises where employees in a scheduled employment are employed or work is given out to out-workers, and examine registers, records of wages and notices, requiring their production. The inspector may examine any person found there who is believed to be an employee, require information from persons giving out work and from out-workers about the parties to the work and the payments for it, and seize or take copies of registers and records relevant to an offence believed to have been committed. An inspector is a public servant, and obstructing these functions carries consequences under the Penal Code provisions the Act invokes.

### Q: Can the government exempt an employer from the Minimum Wages Act?

Yes, within limits the Act itself sets. Under Section 26 the appropriate government may direct that the Act shall not apply to wages payable to disabled employees; may, for special reasons, direct that the Act or any of its provisions shall not apply to any class of employees in a scheduled employment, or to a locality, for a specified period and subject to conditions; and may disapply the Act for classes of employees earning above a prescribed limit, where fixing a minimum for them is considered unnecessary. Nothing in the Act applies to wages an employer pays a family member living with and dependent on him. Exemptions are notified directions, not private arrangements.

### Q: How are piece-rate workers protected by the Minimum Wages Act?

The Act fixes three kinds of rate under Section 3(2): a minimum time rate, a minimum piece rate, and a guaranteed time rate, which applies to piece workers to secure them a minimum on a time basis. Where a worker is employed on piece work for which a minimum time rate but no piece rate has been fixed, Section 17 requires payment at not less than the minimum time rate. The design prevents the oldest evasion of a wage floor, setting a piece rate so low that a full day's output earns less than the time rate, and it works with the definition of employee, which expressly covers out-workers producing at home.

### Q: Do men and women get the same minimum wage under the Act?

Yes. Minimum rates under the Act are fixed for scheduled employments and for classes of work within them, and the fixation machinery sets one rate for a class of work regardless of the sex of the worker performing it; the Act prescribes no separate or lower rate for women. The rate that binds the employer is the rate for the employment, the class and the zone. The distinct and wider guarantee of equal remuneration for the same work or work of a similar nature, extending beyond minimum rates to wages generally, is the subject of the Equal Remuneration Act, 1976, which the Code on Wages, 2019 has since consolidated.

### Q: Why are so many workers still paid below the minimum wage?

Because the statute's strength is in its norm and its weakness is in everything needed to deliver it. Coverage stops at the Schedule, so many workers have no rate at all. Rates differ by employment, class and zone and change by notification, so many workers cannot state the rate they are owed. The claims window under Section 20 is six months, the records on which proof depends are the employer's to keep, inspectorates are small against millions of workplaces, and the maximum fine for underpayment has stood at five hundred rupees since 1948. The result, recorded by every serious assessment of the unorganised sector, is widespread underpayment despite an absolute legal prohibition.
