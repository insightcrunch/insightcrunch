The RTE 25 percent quota is the most quoted number in Indian education law and the least examined one. Section 12(1)(c) of the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 obliges every unaided non-minority school to fill at least a quarter of its entry class with children from disadvantaged groups and weaker sections in the neighbourhood, free of charge, and the Supreme Court has upheld that obligation against constitutional challenge. Yet in state after state the seats the provision creates have gone unfilled in large proportion, and the argument about why has been conducted largely in slogans: schools are accused of prejudice, governments of indifference, and the provision itself of being unworkable. The record supports a colder diagnosis. The entitlement is legally sound and judicially secure. What fails is the machinery around it, above all the money: reimbursement fixed by formula below the fee a school actually charges, paid years late when it is paid, and eligibility definitions written by states in terms that exclude many of the children the provision was drafted for. The scheme fails at the till, not at the door.

## The RTE 25 percent quota: what Section 12(1)(c) actually obliges a school to do

The obligation is misquoted so often that the text has to be set out before anything built on it can be trusted. The full statutory treatment of the Act, its entitlements and its prohibitions is the work of [the complete guide to the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009](/2015/01/01/right-to-education-act-2009-guide/), and what follows here is only the reservation provision and the machinery that provision depends on. Section 12 distributes the duty of free education across the four kinds of school the Act recognises. Government and local authority schools must provide free and compulsory education to every child admitted. Aided schools must provide it free to a proportion of admitted children at least equal to the proportion that their annual aid bears to their annual recurring expenses, subject to a minimum of twenty-five per cent. Clause (c) then reaches the schools the state does not fund at all: a school of a specified category, and an unaided school not receiving any kind of aid or grant from the appropriate government or a local authority, must admit in class one, to the extent of at least twenty-five per cent of the strength of that class, children belonging to weaker sections and disadvantaged groups in the neighbourhood, and provide free and compulsory elementary education to them till its completion. A proviso extends the same regime to the pre-school class where the school imparts pre-school education, so the entry point is the youngest class the school runs and not always class one.

Three features of that text decide almost everything that happens downstream. The first is that the percentage attaches to the strength of the entry class, so the obligation is an intake obligation renewed with every admission cycle, and the children admitted under it carry the entitlement forward through each succeeding class until elementary education is complete. A school does not discharge the duty once; it builds a stock of quota children cohort by cohort, a quarter of each year's intake, for eight years of each child's schooling. The second feature is that the duty is expressed as a floor, at least twenty-five per cent, and not as a ceiling or an exact share, which matters when states experiment with the design, because nothing in the clause forbids a state from requiring more of its own schools or structuring the process differently. The third feature is the pair of words the whole implementation argument turns on: in the neighbourhood. The Act uses the expression without measuring it. How far a neighbourhood extends, and therefore which children may claim a seat in which school, is left entirely to state rules, and states have answered the question in radii that differ enough to change the effective size of the entitlement from one side of a state border to the other.

### How is the 25 percent figure calculated for a school's entry class?

The figure is a share of the total strength of the entry class, meaning every section of class one, or of the pre-school class where the school begins there, taken together. A school with four sections of forty children has an entry strength of one hundred and sixty and owes at least forty seats.

The computation runs on sanctioned class strength rather than on the number of general category applications received. Because the obligation is computed on strength, a school cannot shrink it by discouraging applications or by reporting a smaller intake than its recognition permits, and education departments in states with centralised admission systems compute the seat matrix themselves from the school's recognised intake. Because the entitlement then travels with the child, the same school's total quota population in a steady state approaches a quarter of the whole elementary enrolment, which is the figure school managements have in mind when they calculate the financial exposure, and it is considerably larger than the single year's intake figure that dominates public discussion.

The reimbursement provision is Section 12(2), and its formula is the second most misquoted element of the scheme. A school providing free education under clause (c) is reimbursed the expenditure incurred by it to the extent of the per-child expenditure incurred by the state, or the actual amount charged from the child, whichever is less, in the manner prescribed by rules. Two provisos cut that entitlement down further. The first caps the reimbursement at the per-child expenditure incurred by a government school, so a state whose own spending per child is low has written its cap into the statute itself. The second proviso withdraws reimbursement altogether to the extent that a school is already under an obligation to provide free education to a specified number of children because it received land, building, equipment or other facilities free of cost or at a concessional rate. A school built on concessional public land, of which there are many in the planned cities, may therefore owe the seats and be entitled to nothing for filling some or all of them.

### Can a school that got its land cheaply still claim reimbursement?

For the seats covered by the free education obligation attached to its land or building concession, no. The second proviso to Section 12(2) removes the reimbursement entitlement to the extent of that pre-existing obligation, so a school that took concessional land on condition of educating a specified number of children free is paid nothing for those children.

Reimbursement remains available only for quota admissions beyond the number the concession already obliges it to teach free. The practical consequence is a two-tier population of unaided schools standing under the same statutory sentence. A school on commercially acquired land is reimbursed, late and below its fee, but reimbursed. A school on concessional institutional land, which describes a large share of the established schools in Delhi and in planned urban developments, carries the same admission duty with no payment attached for the obligation seats, a position its managements have challenged as confiscatory and which administrators defend as the price written into the original allotment. Both descriptions are accurate about different halves of the arrangement, and the friction the proviso generates is a standing feature of every admission season in the cities where concessional allotments were common.

The definitions complete the delegation. A child belonging to a disadvantaged group and a child belonging to a weaker section are defined in Section 2 by reference to groups and income limits that the appropriate government specifies by notification. The central Act names the raw materials, Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, socially and educationally backward classes, groups disadvantaged by social, cultural, economic, geographical, linguistic or gender factors for the one, and a parental income limit for the other, and then hands the operative lists and thresholds to each state. Almost every implementation failure described later in this article passes through that act of delegation: the Centre wrote an entitlement whose beneficiaries are identified by twenty-eight different notification regimes, whose neighbourhood is measured by twenty-eight different radii, and whose price is fixed by twenty-eight different per-child expenditure figures. The quota is one provision. Its administration is a federation of schemes sharing a section number.

## The admission machinery the states built around the quota

The Act itself prescribes almost nothing about how a quota admission is to happen. It forbids a capitation fee and forbids subjecting a child or parent to any screening procedure under Section 13, it requires admission at the commencement of the academic year or within a prescribed extended period under Section 15, and it obliges every school to furnish information the government requires under Section 12(3). The process by which a child actually moves from a household to a seat, the application window, the proof demanded, the method of choosing among applicants when applications exceed seats, the publication of allotments, was constructed by states under their rule-making power in Section 38, and the quality of that construction explains more of the variation in outcomes than any difference in the wealth or politics of states.

The mature version of the machinery, arrived at independently by several states after early admission cycles collapsed into confusion, has a recognisable shape. The education department notifies an admission calendar and opens a single online application window, replacing the older practice under which a parent applied separately at the office of every school. The department computes each school's quota seats from its recognised intake and publishes a seat matrix. Parents register the child, state preferences among schools within the permitted neighbourhood radius, and upload or later produce documents proving category and residence. Where applications for a school exceed its quota seats, allotment is by computerised lottery rather than by any assessment of the child, which is the only selection method compatible with the statutory bar on screening. Allotment lists are published, parents report to the allotted school within a fixed period with original documents, and further rounds are run against the seats that remain after each round's reporting failures and rejections.

Delhi's administration of the quota shows the machinery in its most developed form. The Directorate of Education runs the admission for the EWS and disadvantaged group categories through its own portal and a computerised draw, and its neighbourhood rule is a ladder of distance bands: admission is offered first to eligible children residing within one kilometre of the school, then, if vacancies remain, to those within three kilometres, then within six, and only after the six kilometre band is exhausted to children living beyond it. The same guidelines specify the proof for each category in advance, down to the rank of the officer who may sign an income certificate, a detail that reads as bureaucratic fussiness until it is set against the experience of states where the acceptable proof was left undefined and individual schools decided for themselves which papers sufficed.

Maharashtra runs a centralised online admission for the state as a whole, in which a parent may name up to ten schools, with schools within one kilometre considered first and schools up to three kilometres away considered for the vacancies that remain, allotment again by lottery. The Maharashtra portal's published figures for a single admission cycle illustrate the demand pattern of a large state with a functioning portal: in the cycle reported for Pune district, over fifty-four thousand applications were filed for a little over eighteen thousand seats across nine hundred and sixteen schools, while Sindhudurg district received one hundred and sixty-five applications against two hundred and seventy-one seats. The same machinery produces excess demand in the metropolis and empty seats in the coastal district, which is a fact about where unaided schools and poor households are respectively located, and no redesign of the portal will remove it.

Set against these systems, the early design in many states left the entire transaction at the school gate. A parent learned of the entitlement by word of mouth, obtained forms from each school, submitted papers whose sufficiency the school itself judged, and waited for a list the school published or did not publish. Every discretionary gap in that design was a point at which a reluctant school could fail an application without refusing it: a document declared inadequate, a deadline communicated late, a neighbourhood boundary drawn so that the applicant's lane fell outside it. The migration to state-run portals is the single clearest administrative improvement in the scheme's record, because it moved the discretionary decisions from the party with an interest in refusal to a department with at least a formal interest in filling the seats, and it generated, for the first time, application and allotment numbers that could be checked.

Documentation is where the machinery meets the household, and it is the point of maximum loss. The proofs the scheme demands, an income certificate or a ration card in the below poverty line or Antyodaya categories, a caste certificate, proof of residence, proof of the child's age, are documents that the poorest households are the least likely to hold in current and correct form. An income certificate requires an application to a revenue officer and a wait; a caste certificate for a migrant family's child may exist, if it exists, in another state whose categories the admitting state does not recognise; a family in informal housing may have no residence proof naming the lane it lives in. Delhi's notified lists, which accept a below poverty line ration card or an Antyodaya card as proof of income in place of a certificate, show one state reducing the burden by accepting documents households already hold. States that insist on the full certificate chain convert an entitlement into a test of documentary capacity, and the applicants filtered out at that stage are disproportionately the children the provision named first.

### What happens to a quota seat that remains unfilled after admissions close?

In the systems that states have built, it ordinarily lapses for that admission cycle rather than passing to another child. States differ on the edges: some run successive lottery rounds and mop-up rounds deep into the admission season, and the treatment of a seat vacated mid-year by a departing child is governed by state rules rather than by the Act.

The durable position is that a quota seat is an entry class entitlement computed for a cycle, and a seat unclaimed in that cycle is not a bankable asset the school or the department carries forward.

That lapse rule interacts harshly with the calendar. Quota admissions run on the state's notified schedule, and a family that secures its documents after the final round has no remedy within the scheme for that year, because the entitlement the child holds is to be considered in the process rather than to a seat held open indefinitely. Section 15 of the Act permits admission within an extended period prescribed by rules and protects a late-admitted child by requiring the school to let the child complete studies with special training where needed, but the protection operates on admissions that happen; it does not resurrect a lapsed cycle. The administrative lesson states draw from this is that the number of rounds and the length of the window are themselves policy choices about how many children the scheme will reach, dressed as scheduling decisions.

One more piece of machinery deserves notice because it is invisible in the statute: the seat matrix depends on schools truthfully reporting their recognised intake, and children can only be allotted against seats the department knows exist. Unrecognised schools stand outside the scheme altogether, since recognition under Section 18 is the hook on which the whole of Section 12 hangs, and in settlements where the affordable private schools are unrecognised budget schools, the neighbourhood may contain no institution against which the entitlement can be exercised at all. The quota redistributes places in recognised unaided schools. Where the local market in schooling is informal, the provision has nothing to redistribute, a structural limit no admission portal can cure.

## The evidence on filling, and why the numbers quarrel with each other

Every figure in this field carries its source, its year and its base, and no two bases are merged here, because the counting discipline is the only thing standing between a reader and the single national percentage that this subject attracts and cannot support. The same discipline governs [the enforcement record assembled for the anti-terror statutes](/2011/07/15/anti-terror-law-enforcement-record/), where headline figures dissolved on inspection into incompatible series, and the quota's numbers behave identically. A fill rate is a ratio, and both of its terms are contestable: the numerator depends on which admissions a data system captured, and the denominator depends on how many seats the system believed existed, and in the early years of the scheme the official District Information System for Education captured both badly, including schools reporting more quota children than a quarter of their class strength could contain.

The most careful national reconstruction remains the State of the Nation report on Section 12(1)(c) published in 2015 by the RTE Resource Centre at the Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad with the Central Square Foundation, the Accountability Initiative and the Vidhi Centre for Legal Policy, which worked from the District Information System for Education data for 2013-14 and then cleaned it. On the raw returns, some 23.1 lakh seats were available under the provision and some 7.05 lakh were filled, a fill rate of 30.53 per cent. After the researchers capped each school's reported admissions at the statutory quarter of its class strength and removed the returns that could not be true, the same year produced a fill rate of 15.12 per cent. Both numbers are the 2013-14 national fill rate, computed from the same administrative system by the same team, and the distance between them is a measurement of the data system rather than of the scheme. Anyone quoting a single figure for the country without naming which of these constructions produced it is quoting an artefact.

The state pattern inside that report is more informative than either national number. The median state's seat fill rate stood at 13.33 per cent and the median school participation rate, the share of unaided schools admitting at least one child under the provision, at 32.05 per cent, which means the typical failure was double: most schools took nobody, and most seats everywhere went empty. Uttar Pradesh, the state with the largest stock of seats, recorded a fill rate below one per cent: the same report estimated 6,37,149 seats available in the state and traced only 3,061 admissions approved by Basic Education officers up to June 2015. Andhra Pradesh filled under five per cent. Against those figures stand states where the machinery was worked seriously: Chhattisgarh filled 63.1 per cent of its 36,685 seats in 2013-14, 23,147 admissions, although its school participation rate of 26.84 per cent shows the filling concentrated in a minority of schools, and Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh together accounted for roughly half of all admissions recorded nationally. Delhi's administration reported 35,264 children admitted in 2013-14, a volume figure from a small territory with a dense stock of unaided schools and a centralised draw, and it is not commensurable with any state's fill rate because the denominators are built differently.

The continuation data carry a warning of the same kind. The District Information System returns for children continuing under the provision from 2013-14 into 2014-15 implied a dropout of 68.65 per cent in a single year, a figure the 2015 report itself described as almost certainly a gross exaggeration produced by inconsistent school reporting between the two rounds. The honest statement the data support is narrower: continuation was not being tracked reliably enough to measure attrition at all in the scheme's early years, which is itself an implementation finding, since a state that cannot count the children it admitted cannot follow them, support them or pay for them accurately. Later administrative series, state admission portals and parliamentary answers have added year-by-year numbers for individual states, and this article uses them only in that form, attributed to their source and year, because the bases differ enough that assembling them into a national trend would manufacture a precision the underlying systems never had.

What the evidence establishes without strain is the shape of the outcome. Fill rates in most states, in most years for which a defensible series exists, have sat far below the statutory quarter of entry strength that the provision presupposes; a small number of states have reached or approached respectable filling where the state built a portal, published the seat matrix, fixed the reimbursement and policed the calendar; and the gap between the best and worst states is so wide that national averages conceal the only useful information, which is that the difference is administrative. The entitlement is uniform. The outcomes are a map of state capacity and state choices, and the failure points at which the seats leak away are identifiable one by one.

## Why a seat goes unfilled: the failure points in sequence

A quota seat is not lost at one dramatic moment. It is lost along a chain that runs from the notification defining who is eligible, through the documents a household can produce, the map that decides which schools count as its neighbourhood, the application and lottery, the school's conduct at the point of admission, the state's payment for the seat once filled, and the child's persistence in the school across eight years. A break at any link produces the same statistic, an empty seat, which is why aggregate fill rates mislead reformers into treating one problem. The diagnosis has to be run link by link.

Read as a chain, the table rearranges the usual argument. The two links that attract political attention, school resistance and the quarrel over the entitlement's existence, are real but sit in the middle of the chain. The links that quietly determine the outcome are the first two and the last two: who the state says is eligible, what proof the state demands, what the state pays and when, and what happens to the child once inside. A reform programme that addressed documentation and reimbursement alone, leaving every speech about the provision untouched, would fill more seats than a decade of litigation reaffirming the entitlement, because the entitlement has never been the scarce resource. Seats are. Money and definitions are what make seats usable.

The chain also explains the otherwise puzzling geography of the record. A state does not need to excel at every link; it needs to avoid catastrophic failure at any one of them. Chhattisgarh's high fill rate in 2013-14 was achieved with a school participation rate under twenty-seven per cent, because the schools that did participate filled deeply. Uttar Pradesh failed at the first links, with definitions and approval processes that produced three thousand admissions against more than six lakh estimated seats. The variation is not a mystery of culture or of wealth. It is the arithmetic of a chain in which one broken link zeroes the product of all the others.

## The money: a formula that pays below cost, and pays it late

Every other failure in this scheme can be, and has been, argued about as a question of attitude. This one is arithmetic. Section 12(2) entitles the school to the lower of two figures: the per-child expenditure incurred by the state, and the actual amount charged from the child. The per-child expenditure of a state is the cost of running its own school system divided across its enrolment, a figure dominated by teacher salaries in schools that charge nothing, carry no capital cost of urban land, and in many states struggle to meet the Act's own norms. The fee of an urban unaided school is set in a market where land, salaries and compliance costs are what they are. Where the school's fee exceeds the state's per-child figure, which describes most established urban schools, the reimbursement is the state's figure, and the difference between the two is a loss the school is required by law to absorb on every quota seat, every year, for the eight years each admitted child remains. The statute calls the arrangement reimbursement. A school's accountant calls it a mandated cross-subsidy from the fee-paying parents of the other three quarters of the class, and both descriptions are correct, which is why the argument about it never resolves.

The strongest version of the schools' case deserves to be stated as its holders state it, because the stance of this series is that a grievance does not become illegitimate by being held by an institution. The associations of unaided schools, among them the National Independent Schools Alliance and state level federations that have carried the litigation, argue that the state has conscripted private capacity to discharge a duty the Constitution places on the state itself, and has priced the conscription at the cost of its own cheapest provision rather than at the cost of the service actually delivered. They point out that the shortfall is not absorbed by an abstraction: it is recovered from the fees of other children, many of whose families are themselves far from wealthy, so the scheme operates in part as a transfer from lower middle class parents to a public obligation, arranged without a vote on any tax. They point to delay as a second, unlegislated reduction: a reimbursement that arrives two or three years after the expenditure is worth less than its face value and has to be financed in the interval by the school. And they point to the concessional land proviso as the limiting case, in which a school may be paid nothing at all for seats it is forbidden to leave empty.

The state's answer, also at its strongest, begins from the text. Parliament chose the formula knowing that per-child expenditure in government schools would usually be the lower figure, and the choice was a deliberate fiscal design rather than an oversight: the state buys a seat in an existing school at the price of a seat in its own system, and declines to underwrite the fee structure of the private market. Finance departments add that the per-child figure is not static, that it rises with government teacher pay revisions, and that a school which finds the rate ruinous retains the general category seats whose fees it sets freely. On delay, the official answer is process: claims must be verified against enrolment, schools submit defective claims, and funds move through the sharing arrangement between the Centre and the states under which elementary education expenditure is jointly borne, so a delay in the central release becomes a delay at the school gate. Each of these propositions is true in places. None of them answers the school's underlying point, which is that the gap between price and cost is not a defect of administration but a term of the statute, and that a state which wishes the seats filled by willing schools has set the price below the level at which willingness is rational for the marginal school.

The litigation over money shows the courts holding the two halves of this position apart. In Tamil Nadu, where admissions under the quota for the 2025-26 academic year had not even been opened by the middle of May 2025, the state told the Madras High Court that it had borne the reimbursement bill of Rs 188.99 crore for 2022-23 alone and was pursuing the Centre for a share it put at over Rs 2,150 crore under the Samagra Shiksha arrangement, within which it said the Union had failed to release Rs 342.69 crore representing its sixty per cent share for 2021-22 and 2022-23. A bench of Justice G. R. Swaminathan and Justice V. Lakshminarayanan, in the petition brought by V. Eswaran, refused to let the intergovernmental quarrel reach the schools: the state government, the Court held, has a non-derogable obligation to reimburse private unaided schools, and non-receipt of funds from the Union government cannot be cited as a reason to wriggle out of that statutory obligation. The Court observed that the funds payable towards the Act's obligations need not be linked to the National Education Policy, and directed adherence to the statutory timelines, and Tamil Nadu then carried the funding question to the Supreme Court, which issued notice on its plea, a suit between the state and the Union on the same finances being already pending. The entitlement of the school was affirmed in the strongest available language. The price and the delay, the things schools actually litigate about year to year, were left exactly where the statute put them.

That pattern, a ringing affirmation of the duty to pay and silence on the adequacy of the amount, repeats across the reimbursement case law of the state High Courts, where school associations have challenged notified rates as arbitrary, unreasoned or computed on stale enrolment, and have more often won directions for timely payment and reasoned fixation than any revision of the formula itself. The judicial position amounts to this: the state must pay, must pay on time, and must be able to explain its figure, but the figure is the state's to fix within the statutory cap. A school that wants the cap moved has to persuade a legislature, and no legislature has moved it, because raising the rate is a budget line with no constituency organised enough to claim it, while the schools that bear the loss are few enough in each constituency to be safely disappointed. This is the sense in which the scheme's central flaw is fiscal rather than ideological. The argument is not about whether poor children should sit in these classrooms; that argument was settled in 2012. It is about who pays the difference between two numbers in a formula, and it is conducted in budget documents rather than in speeches, which is why it never gets settled at all.

### When did the 25 percent obligation begin to operate in admissions?

The Act commenced on 1 April 2010, but the obligation's practical life begins with the judgment of 12 April 2012 that upheld it, since admissions for the cycle then under way were in progress under a cloud of challenge and the Supreme Court directed that its ruling operate prospectively.

From the 2012-13 admission cycle onward, no unaided non-minority school could plead uncertainty about the duty, and the implementation record assessed in this article is the record from that cycle forward.

Arrears deserve separate treatment from rates, because a state can pay an inadequate rate punctually or an adequate rate ruinously late, and the record contains both. The claim process in most states requires the school to file child-wise claims, which the district administration verifies against enrolment and attendance before the money moves through the treasury, and each stage is a queue. School associations in several states have placed aggregate arrears figures before courts and legislatures; those aggregates are the claimants' own compilations and are treated here as such, not as audited totals. What is not in dispute, because state governments have conceded it in affidavits and in assembly answers across the period, is that payment lags measured in years have occurred in multiple states and that schools have responded predictably, by treating the quota seat as a liability to be minimised at the admission stage. A reimbursement system that pays late teaches the regulated population that compliance is expensive, and the lesson is visible in the participation figures: a department cannot exhort schools to admit children in April while its treasury is two cycles behind in paying for the children admitted in earlier years, and expect the exhortation to be the operative fact.

## The courts: a settled entitlement and contested machinery

The constitutional litigation over Section 12(1)(c) was over quickly, and everything since has been litigation about the machinery. In Society for Unaided Private Schools of Rajasthan v Union of India, decided on 12 April 2012, a three judge bench of the Supreme Court upheld the Act, including the reservation obligation, as it applied to unaided non-minority schools. Chief Justice S. H. Kapadia and Justice Swatanter Kumar, for the majority, treated the obligation as a reasonable restriction in the interest of the child's right under Article 21A, reading the provision as child-centric legislation through which the state discharges its own duty partly through private capacity, and held that the Act did not apply to unaided minority schools. Justice K. S. Radhakrishnan dissented on the central point: in his view the obligation could not be imposed on unaided schools at all, minority or non-minority, because compelling an unaided institution to provide free education transferred the state's burden onto a private party in violation of the freedom of occupation. The doctrinal path that made both the majority's conclusion and the minority exemption possible runs through the Eighty-sixth Amendment and the cases on unaided institutions that preceded the Act, which [the account of the Eighty-sixth Amendment and the road to the education right](/2015/01/15/86th-amendment-right-to-education/) traces in full.

### Why did the Supreme Court say the state could place this duty on private schools?

Because it located the right in the child rather than in the institution. The majority reasoned that Article 21A obliges the state to provide free and compulsory education, that the state may choose the means of discharging that duty, and that requiring unaided schools to carry a quarter of their entry class free is a permissible and proportioned means.

The school, on this reading, is not expropriated; it is regulated in the service of a right held by the child in front of it, and the reimbursement the statute provides softens the restriction the majority was prepared to sustain.

The exemption then widened. In Pramati Educational and Cultural Trust v Union of India in 2014, a five judge Constitution Bench held that the Act does not apply to minority institutions at all, aided or unaided, because to compel such an institution to admit children under the statute's scheme would abridge the right of minorities under Article 30(1) to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice. The practical consequence was larger than the doctrinal one. Minority institutions constitute a substantial share of the unaided school sector in many cities, and the holding removed their seats from the pool the provision distributes, shrinking the entitlement's urban reach precisely where unaided supply is densest. Parents encounter the exemption as an unexplained refusal at a school gate; constitutionally it is the resolution of a genuine collision between two rights, each held against the state, and the article of faith that it is a loophole does not survive a reading of the bench's reasoning. The position has not been left entirely undisturbed: in Anjuman Ishaat-E-Taleem Trust v State of Maharashtra, decided on 1 September 2025, a two judge bench of Justice Dipankar Datta and Justice Manmohan expressed doubt about the breadth of Pramati, observing that a holding directed substantially at Section 12(1)(c) had exempted minority institutions from the entire Act, and referred the questions, including whether Pramati requires reconsideration and whether the provision might be read down instead, to a larger bench. The exemption therefore stands as the law, with its foundations formally questioned and its reconsideration pending before a bench yet to be constituted, and any honest account of the quota's coverage must carry both halves of that sentence.

Below the constitutional level, the litigation has moved to the two things the opening sections of this article identified as the real determinants: state definitions and state money. The Bombay High Court's treatment of Maharashtra's neighbourhood engineering is the clearest instance. By a notification of 9 February 2024 the Maharashtra government amended its rules to exempt a private unaided school from the quota where a government or aided school stood within one kilometre of it, a device that would have withdrawn the obligation from most urban schools at a stroke while describing itself as an efficiency measure. In Aswini Kamble v State of Maharashtra, a bench of Chief Justice Devendra Kumar Upadhyaya and Justice Amit Borkar quashed the notification on 19 July 2024 as ultra vires the Act and Article 21A, holding in substance that subordinate legislation cannot be permitted to exceed or contradict what the parent Act provides and that the Act attaches no such condition to the obligation. The schools' association carried the matter to the Supreme Court as Association of Indian Schools v State of Maharashtra, and a bench of Chief Justice D. Y. Chandrachud with Justices J. B. Pardiwala and Manoj Misra dismissed the appeal, leaving the quota unconditional on the presence of a government school nearby. A parallel Maharashtra dispute over distance itself, arising when the state confined applications first to one kilometre and then to three, produced the Aurangabad bench's direction that a distance limit which can leave a child with no reachable unaided school defeats the purpose of the Act and should be removed, a proposition the state was driven to answer again when a one kilometre limit was reimposed for a later cycle.

The third stream of litigation concerns what free means once the child is inside. Parents in Delhi established in Social Jurist v Government of NCT of Delhi, in the Delhi High Court in 2013, that the free education the Act promises is not exhausted by waiving tuition: the entitlement carries the textbooks, writing material and uniforms without which attendance is a formality, a reading later applied by the Gauhati High Court when schools demanded that quota parents pay for books and uniforms and the state undertook to reimburse the cost under Section 12(2). Each such judgment extends the real cost of the seat beyond the tuition figure the reimbursement debate usually assumes, which is one more reason the two numbers in the formula understate the school's exposure, and one more entry in the ledger of costs the scheme distributes by silence.

Taken together, the case law yields a stable map. The entitlement is valid and unconditional within its field; the field excludes minority institutions, on a holding whose breadth is under formal question; states may not rewrite the obligation by rule, exemption or radius; the state must reimburse, punctually, and cannot plead its own funding disputes in defence; and free education includes the material incidents of attendance. What the courts have not done, and structurally cannot do, is set the reimbursement rate, appropriate the money, write the eligibility notifications, or run an admission portal. Every operative failure identified in this article lies in that second list, which belongs to governments. The litigation record is therefore best read not as the scheme's battleground but as its boundary fence: it marks the ground on which administration was free to succeed, and mostly did not.

## Definitions as destiny: the federal layer of the scheme

Education sits in the Concurrent List, and Section 12(1)(c) is a central command executed through state definitions, state rules and state money, so the provision a child meets is always the state edition of it. The division of competence that produces this layering, and the history of education's move from the State List to the Concurrent List, is the subject of [the analysis of education in the Concurrent List and its federal consequences](/2015/05/01/education-concurrent-list-federalism/); the implementation point here is narrower. Parliament deliberately left the two operative definitions, disadvantaged group and weaker section, to notification by the appropriate government, and left neighbourhood to state rules, which means that the three questions deciding whether a given child gets a seat, who counts, what must be proved, and how near is near enough, are answered twenty-eight different ways under one section number.

The variation is not cosmetic. Delhi's notified definition, as recorded in the 2015 State of the Nation review, treats a child as belonging to the weaker section where the parents' total annual income is less than one lakh rupees from all sources, and builds its disadvantaged group from the Scheduled Castes, the Scheduled Tribes, other backward classes outside the creamy layer, children with special needs and disabilities, orphans and transgender children, with the disadvantaged group categories expressly excused from producing an income certificate. Other states set the income ceiling at different levels, compose the disadvantaged list differently, and treat categories such as children of widows, children with HIV affected parents, children of sanitation workers and first generation learners inconsistently, so that a household eligible on one side of a state boundary is ineligible on the other without any difference in its circumstances. A ceiling fixed in nominal rupees and left unrevised also shrinks in real terms every year it stands, a quiet annual contraction of the entitlement that no legislature votes on and no court reviews, and states differ in whether they have revised the figure at all since their first notification.

The contrast with reservation at the next level of the system is instructive. In higher education, reservation operates overwhelmingly inside institutions the state funds and controls, administered within a framework in which the regulator created by [the University Grants Commission Act, 1956](/2015/02/15/ugc-act-1956-explained/) and the funding relationship between the state and the institution give the public authority direct leverage over admissions, fees and compliance. Section 12(1)(c) attempts something institutionally harder: reservation inside institutions the state does not fund, does not appoint, and cannot direct except through the recognition power, paid for at a rate the state sets unilaterally and policed by departments whose other duties include promoting the same private sector. That the higher education model fills its reserved seats more reliably is not evidence of greater virtue in its administrators. It is evidence that reservation is easy to enforce against your own institutions and hard to enforce against somebody else's, and that the RTE scheme chose the hard version because the children it targets live where the unaided schools are.

States have also used the definitional power defensively, and the Maharashtra exemption struck down in 2024 shows the technique in its pure form: redefine the field so that the obligation attaches to fewer schools, and the fill rate can be improved on paper by shrinking the denominator while fewer children are admitted in fact. The same incentive operates in reverse in the reporting of success, where a state that narrows eligibility or confines the neighbourhood will record a higher proportion of its seats filled than a state that defines generously. Comparisons between states that ignore the definitions underneath the figures are therefore unsound in a way that favours the restrictive state, and the denominator discipline this series applies to enforcement statistics applies with full force here: a fill rate is only as honest as the eligibility notification and the seat matrix beneath it.

## Inside the school: admission is not the same thing as integration

The provision's purpose was never only access. The case made for it from the Central Advisory Board of Education's deliberations on the free and compulsory education Bill through the parliamentary debates was a mixing case: that a school system sorted by parental income teaches children their place before it teaches them anything else, and that placing children of the neighbourhood's poor households inside the neighbourhood's fee charging schools would interrupt that sorting. The 2012 majority's child-centric reasoning carries the same premise. It follows that the scheme can fill its seats and still fail its purpose, if the children admitted under it are educated apart, and the question of what happens inside the school is therefore not a refinement of the implementation inquiry but its centre.

The honest state of the evidence is that the question is open and under-examined. Studies and official reviews of particular states and cities have documented the full range of arrangements: schools in which quota children are distributed through the ordinary sections and take part in the common life of the school, and schools in which they have been grouped into separate sections, separate shifts or afternoon batches, sometimes defended as remedial grouping and sometimes explicable only as segregation with a timetable. Journalistic accounts and civil society studies have collected instances of children being marked out by uniform differences, excluded from activities carrying charges, or seated and taught apart, and school managements have answered with instances of integration support, bridge teaching and fee paying parents who withdrew their children in objection to the mixing itself. What does not exist is a national measurement of the phenomenon, any more than a national measurement of attrition exists, and this article asserts no national pattern. It asserts the narrower proposition that a scheme whose stated object is mixing cannot be evaluated on admissions alone, and that neither state admission portals nor the Act's monitoring architecture, which runs through the child rights commissions under Sections 31 and 32, was built to observe what happens in the classroom after the allotment letter is issued.

The segregation question also disciplines the standard criticism of schools. Where a school admits its quarter and teaches them apart, the failure is the school's and the department's jointly, and no reimbursement grievance excuses it. Where a school admits its quarter, teaches them with the rest, and loses money on every such child for eight years while its reimbursement arrives two years late, the vocabulary of prejudice does no explanatory work at all, and the school's complaint is the scheme's design flaw speaking. Public argument habitually merges these two schools into a single villain. The implementation record separates them, and the separation matters for policy, because the first school needs enforcement and the second needs a price, and a state that cannot tell them apart will supply neither.

When the Maharashtra schools' appeal against the quashing of the one kilometre exemption reached the Supreme Court, Chief Justice Chandrachud's observations from the bench returned to the mixing premise directly: children from the economically weaker sections must attend good schools, he said, because the children already in those schools will otherwise grow up inside a cocoon of privilege without understanding the country they live in. The remark was an observation in argument and not a holding, and it is recorded here as the judiciary's clearest statement of what the provision is for. Measured against that purpose, an admission system that produces a quarter of every entry class on paper and a separated cohort in the classroom has achieved the statistic and missed the statute.

## The class eight cliff

The entitlement ends where elementary education ends. Section 12(1)(c) obliges the school to provide free and compulsory elementary education till its completion, elementary education means classes one to eight, and the child admitted at five or six years of age therefore holds a right that expires at about fourteen, in a school that in most cases continues to classes ten and twelve. The child does not change schools because the family chose a better option. The child reaches the boundary of the statute in the middle of a school career, at exactly the point where secondary schooling becomes more expensive, where board examination fees and coaching pressures begin, and where a poor household's calculation about a child's labour against a child's schooling is most finely balanced. The scheme integrates a child into a school for eight years and then prices the child out of it.

The responses available within the design are all partial. A school may retain the child on its own fee terms, and some schools do, whether from commitment, from reputation, or because the child's presence has become unremarkable; nothing obliges it to. A state may extend support beyond the statutory floor from its own funds, and the floor in the Act does not forbid generosity, but such extensions are creatures of budget and notification rather than of right, and a family cannot enforce them as it can enforce Section 12. The national education policy of 2020 recommended extending the entitlement itself to cover the years from three to eighteen, which would abolish the cliff by moving the boundary to the end of schooling; the recommendation requires amendment of the Constitution and of the Act and has not been enacted, so the cliff stands in the law as drafted. Parents meeting it describe the experience consistently in the casework of the organisations that assist quota families: the admission in class one felt like an arrival, and the fee demand in class nine arrives as an eviction notice from a community the child has belonged to for eight years.

The cliff is the scheme's most avoidable flaw, because unlike the reimbursement gap it does not even present a hard fiscal choice in its sharpest form. A bridge entitlement covering the two years to the class ten examination for children who entered under the quota would be a bounded commitment with a countable population, every member of it already identified in the state's own admission data. That no such bridge has been legislated at the centre, and that state versions remain exceptional, returns the analysis to the scheme's recurring condition: the children it concerns have no organised voice in the rooms where the follow-through decisions are taken, and a flaw that harms only them can persist indefinitely at negligible political cost. The entitlement was won in a moment of constitutional settlement. Its unfinished edges are being left to the arithmetic of indifference.

## The parent's route through the scheme, and where it breaks in practice

For the parent, the scheme is a sequence of dated events, and it is worth following one household through it because the failure points in the table are abstractions until they are located in a calendar. The sequence begins with information: the family must learn that the entitlement exists, that their child is of entry age in the relevant year, and that applications in their state open on a notified date. Awareness travels unevenly; departments advertise, but the consistent finding of state reviews is that word of mouth through anganwadi workers, school staff, local organisations and other parents carries the entitlement into the settlements where it matters, and a family outside those networks can miss the window without ever having refused anything. This account describes the general position under the Act and state rules; it is not advice on an individual admission, and a family facing a refusal should use the grievance route described below promptly, because admission calendars close on fixed dates and a seat lost in one cycle cannot be recovered inside that cycle.

Registration follows, online in the portal states and at facilitation centres or schools elsewhere. The family assembles the file: proof of the child's age, for which the Act itself, in Section 14, forbids denial of admission for want of a birth certificate and accepts other prescribed documents; proof of residence; proof of category, a caste certificate or its equivalent where the claim is as a disadvantaged group; and proof of income where the claim is as a weaker section. Each document is a small administrative project, and the projects are sequential: a certificate applied for after the window opens may arrive after it closes. Families that succeed in large numbers are disproportionately families assisted by an organisation, a school that wants the admission, or a local official who treats the file as his work rather than the applicant's problem, which is why assisted application centres recur in the fix column of the failure table.

Allotment is the moment of maximum transparency in the portal states: the lottery is computerised, the list is published, and the family receives a school it may have listed or, in oversubscribed cities, a school it listed fifth. Then the second filter operates: reporting. The family must reach the school within the window with original documents; the school verifies; discrepancies between the application and the originals, a name spelled differently, a caste name recorded in a variant form, an address proof in a grandparent's name, become grounds on which admissions fail after allotment, and each failed reporting returns the seat to a later round while the child's year is spent. Where the allotted school is beyond walking distance and the family cannot fund daily transport, the allotment is a paper admission, and the seat will show in the statistics of the cycle in which it was allotted and in no later statistic at all, a gap between allotment and enrolment that the published figures of most states do not separately disclose.

The grievance route exists and is underused in proportion to the grievances. Section 32 of the Act gives any person with a grievance about the right to education a written complaint to the local authority, which must decide within three months after hearing the parties, with an appeal to the State Commission for Protection of Child Rights, and Sections 31 and 32 together lodge monitoring and inquiry in the national and state child rights commissions. A parent allotted a seat and refused at the gate, charged a fee the Act forbids, or asked for a document the notified list does not contain, therefore holds a defined remedy with a defined clock. The practical obstacles are the usual ones: the complaint must be written, the hearing must be attended in working hours, and the admission calendar does not pause while the machinery deliberates, so the remedy vindicates the right more reliably than it recovers the seat. Organisations that run admission helpdesks in the large cities have learned to run the grievance and the mop-up round in parallel, and their casework is the best available map of where the route breaks for an unassisted family.

The record a family keeps decides many of these disputes before they begin. The allotment turns on the application as filed; the refusal dispute turns on what the school said and when; the reimbursement of incidental charges turns on receipts. A parent who keeps the application acknowledgment, the lottery result, the admission letter and every subsequent letter and receipt in one ordered file holds the whole evidentiary basis of any complaint, and the discipline is worth adopting from the first day: [keep the application, the lottery result, the admission letter and the correspondence together in an encrypted file on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/), where the record of the admission survives a lost phone, a flooded room and the eight years over which a quota child's file may be needed. Schools and departments act differently toward a family that can produce the paper, and commissions decide on paper. The scheme's paperwork burden is a defect of the scheme; the family's answer to it is a file.

## The fiscal critique: a transfer to private schools, or a purchase of capacity?

The deepest objection to the scheme is not the schools' objection and deserves separate statement, because it comes from the provision's own side of the argument. The critique, made by educationists and by teacher organisations among others, holds that Section 12(1)(c) is a public subsidy to private schooling dressed as a right: the state pays private managements for seats, normalises the proposition that quality education is a private product the state purchases, and relieves itself of the harder duty of making its own schools worth attending, while the money spent on reimbursement is money not spent on the government schools where the overwhelming majority of poor children actually study. On this view the quota's beneficiaries are a fortunate minority selected by lottery from the poor, and the scheme purchases visible integration for a few at the price of entrenching the two-tier system it claims to dissolve. The per-child comparison is turned against the scheme: a state that pleads poverty in its own classrooms finds funds to pay private fees, however discounted, for the children the lottery favours.

The defence answers at the same level. The capacity the scheme uses exists; the classrooms, teachers and seats in unaided schools are built and staffed, and the marginal cost of filling an empty seat in class one is lower than the capital and recurring cost of creating the same seat in the public system at urban land prices. The children the quota reaches are in the neighbourhoods where government schools are most oversubscribed or most absent, and the alternative to a purchased seat is not a seat in a good government school but, in many settlements, no seat in any school with the Act's norms at all. The defence adds the mixing premise: the scheme's object was never only a seat count, and the value of poor and rich children in one classroom is a public good that a transfer to government schools, however generous, does not produce. And it notes the direction of the subsidy argument's logic: that the state spends too little per child in its own schools is an indictment of the state's per-child figure, which is the same figure the critique's proponents elsewhere demand be raised, and raising it would raise the reimbursement cap with it, reconciling the two positions into a single demand for a higher public price per child.

The evidence does not close this argument, because the argument is partly about what the state is for, but it disciplines the factual claims inside it. The reimbursement outlay is, in the scale of state elementary education budgets, a small line: Tamil Nadu's figure of Rs 188.99 crore for a full year's reimbursement in 2022-23, offered by the state itself in litigation, is the size of a modest programme within a school education budget that runs to tens of thousands of crore, and no plausible accounting makes the quota the cause of the government school system's underfunding, which predates it by decades and is measured in teacher vacancies and unfilled norms of an entirely different order. Equally, the defence's marginal cost argument holds only for seats that exist empty; as the quota population compounds cohort by cohort toward a quarter of the elementary stock of the unaided sector, the commitment ceases to be marginal, and the honest version of the defence concedes that the scheme is a standing purchase of a quarter of private elementary capacity at a public price, to be judged as such. Judged as such, its record is the record already set out: a sound purchase order, an administered price below cost, and payment in arrears.

## The other limbs of Section 12 in operation

Clause (c) dominates discussion, but the implementation record of the section's other limbs explains some of its behaviour. Aided schools owe free seats under clause (b) in proportion to their aid, subject to the twenty-five per cent floor, and since their recurring costs are substantially met from public funds, the obligation operates there with little of the friction the unaided sector generates: the money argument that defines clause (c) is largely absent, and admissions under clause (b) are correspondingly less litigated, though correspondingly less visible in the data, because aided admissions are administered within the ordinary admission process rather than through a separately counted quota entry in many states.

Specified category schools, the third limb of clause (c), occupy a distinct position. The category covers schools of the kind specified in the Act's definitions, the Kendriya Vidyalayas, Navodaya Vidyalayas and Sainik Schools being the standard instances, institutions of the central government with their own admission regimes. Their inclusion in clause (c) alongside unaided schools means the quota reaches into the most sought after publicly run schools in the country, a fact of real distributive significance in the cities where a Kendriya Vidyalaya is the strongest school in its neighbourhood, and their admissions have to reconcile the quota with the transfer based and category based priorities their own founding schemes prescribe. The point for the implementation record is that the provision's reach is wider than the private school frame in which it is usually debated, and that some of the most contested quota admissions in practice are contests for places in the state's own elite institutions.

The recognition boundary completes the map of what the section can reach. Section 18 forbids a school to function without a certificate of recognition, and Section 12's obligations attach to schools within the recognised universe. The early years of the Act saw closures and closure threats against unrecognised budget private schools, the low fee schools of poor settlements, many of them the only private provision their neighbourhoods had, for failure to meet the Schedule's infrastructure norms. Whatever the merits of the norms, the interaction with the quota is direct and rarely noticed: every unrecognised school driven out of a settlement removes both a school poor children attended for fees their families had chosen to pay and a potential site of quota admissions, while the recognised unaided sector into which the quota channels children may have no presence in that settlement at all. A map of quota availability is, among other things, a map of where recognised private schooling exists, and the blank spaces on it are not randomly distributed.

## The monitors: commissions, information and the uses of a published number

The Act's monitoring design assigns the examination of the right's implementation to the National Commission for Protection of Child Rights and the corresponding state commissions, empowers them to inquire into complaints with the powers of a civil court for that purpose, and routes individual grievances through the local authority under Section 32 with the commission as the appellate port. The design is heavy on inquiry and light on sanction: a commission can investigate, summon, recommend and publicise, and the record of the quota's administration has been shaped by that publicity function more than by any penalty. It was commission monitoring and the research it encouraged that kept the gap between entitlement and admission in public view during the years when state reporting systems were recording numbers nobody could rely on, and the 2015 national review, though produced by academic and policy institutions rather than by a commission, worked in the same register: the manufacture of a defensible number as an act of governance.

The information duty in Section 12(3), under which every school must provide such information as the government or local authority requires, is the least discussed provision in the section and the one on which the entire evidentiary edifice rests. Seat matrices, reimbursement claims and fill rates are all downstream of school returns, and the wild discrepancies the 2015 review found between the District Information System figures, state Sarva Shiksha Abhiyan figures and figures supplied in answer to right to information applications are, at origin, discrepancies in what schools reported and what departments recorded. The migration to portal based admission is best understood as a repair of Section 12(3) by other means: when the department runs the allotment, it no longer needs to ask the school how many quota children it admitted, because the department's own system is the record. States that still run the quota on school returns are running it on the honour system, and their published fill rates should be read with that provenance attached.

## Where the scheme fills its seats, and what those places have in common

The counter-reading that the scheme is simply unworkable cannot survive its own record, and the places where filling is respectable are the control group that identifies the causes. Chhattisgarh's 63.1 per cent fill rate in 2013-14, on the cleaned national data, was achieved in a state nobody would describe as administratively luxurious; what it had was a working admission process, a defined seat stock and departments that processed admissions and claims as a routine rather than as a campaign. Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh, which together supplied about half of all recorded admissions in the same period, are large states with deep stocks of unaided schools and functioning state processes, and their volumes demonstrate the obverse of the Uttar Pradesh finding, where a comparable stock of seats produced almost no admissions under a process that filtered applicants at the approval stage. Delhi's volume of admissions, 35,264 children in 2013-14 on its own reporting, rests on the most complete version of the machinery: notified definitions with the proof specified officer by officer, a single portal, a computerised draw, distance bands that widen until the child is placed, and a directorate that publishes the calendar and answers for it.

Lay the high filling systems side by side and the common elements are unglamorous. The definitions were notified early and kept stable, so parents and schools could learn them. The application was made in one place, with help available, and the proof demanded was calibrated to documents households hold. The allotment was removed from the school's discretion. The calendar was announced, repeated across rounds, and defended against slippage. And the reimbursement, whatever its adequacy, moved through a claims process schools regarded as real, so that admitting a child created a receivable rather than a donation. No item on that list requires a constitutional argument, a new statute or a larger budget in any proportion that matters. Every item is a decision a state education department can take inside its existing powers, which is the most damning finding in the whole implementation record: the difference between the states that fill the seats and the states that do not is not money, demography or politics in any combination the data will support. It is whether the department treated Section 12(1)(c) as a system to be operated or as an announcement to be survived.

## Who pays whom: the funding architecture behind the arrears

Section 7 of the Act makes the Centre and the state governments jointly responsible for providing the funds the Act requires, with the central share determined by reference to the estimates the Act prescribes and the balance carried by the state, and in operation the money has moved through the elementary education programme architecture, Sarva Shiksha Abhiyan and then Samagra Shiksha, under a sharing pattern of sixty per cent Centre and forty per cent state for most states. Reimbursement to schools is a state payment made out of that shared stream, and the architecture explains a feature of the arrears record that school level accounts miss: a school's claim can be delayed at three independent points, the district's verification, the state's release, and the Centre's release to the state, and the delay that reaches the school is the sum of whichever queues were occupied. The Tamil Nadu litigation of 2025 put the arithmetic on the record, with the state quantifying both its own outlay of Rs 188.99 crore for 2022-23 and the central share it said had not arrived, and the Madras High Court's answer severed the chain at the point that matters to the school: the state's duty to pay the school does not wait on the Centre's duty to pay the state, because Section 12(2) obliges the reimburser named in the Act and the intergovernmental account is a separate ledger.

The deeper point is about how the obligation was funded in design. A reimbursement entitlement written into a statute, uncapped in aggregate and driven by admissions the state does not control, sits awkwardly inside annual programme budgeting, where the elementary education envelope is negotiated in advance and the quota line must compete inside it against teacher salaries and school grants with stronger administrative protection. States consistently budgeted the line by the previous year's claims rather than by the seats the law required them to fill, so a successful admission season generated its own arrears, and departments privately treated a modest fill rate as a fiscal convenience. No conspiracy is needed to explain the pattern. A finance department presented with an open ended entitlement priced by a formula will fund it residually unless a court or a legislature forces the issue, and the courts, as the Tamil Nadu case shows, will force the payment of claims while leaving the budgeting habit intact. The utilisation figures that result, claims paid against claims filed, against seats allotted, against seats available, are the four denominators of the scheme's financial record, and they decline in that order in every state whose data permit the comparison.

## The costs the waiver does not reach, and the seat that does not travel

Free education under the quota waives the fee. It does not waive the cost of being educated. Transport to a school beyond walking distance is the largest item, and the neighbourhood design assumes it away by placing the child near home; the assumption fails exactly where the neighbourhood contains no covered school and the widening bands place the child across the city, and Maharashtra's allotment framework acknowledged the fact by warning parents allotted a school beyond three kilometres that they would have to arrange and fund the journey or the school bus themselves. Beyond transport sit the charged activities, the excursions, activity fees and materials that schools levy outside the tuition head; the litigation over books and uniforms settled the core of the entitlement generously, but the penumbra of small charges is where the daily experience of being the quota child in a fee paying classroom is negotiated, and where a household's poverty is most legibly displayed to the child. Studies of particular cities have recorded schools recovering such charges from quota parents in plain breach of the scheme and parents paying them for fear of the child's place, a practice the grievance route exists to correct and rarely does, for the reasons already given about written complaints and fixed calendars.

Portability is the quietest defect of all. The entitlement is administered by states, defined by state notification and exercised through state machinery, and it does not travel. A family that migrates for work across a state boundary, the precise population whose children the disadvantaged group definitions are built to capture, loses the admission it holds and must re-enter the new state's process in a later cycle, against that state's categories, with documents issued by the first state that the second may not recognise for its own lists. Within a state, a change of residence across the city can strand the child between a school no longer near and a neighbourhood entitlement that attaches only at entry. The scheme was designed on the image of a settled household in a defined neighbourhood choosing among its local schools. The labour market that produces its beneficiaries is a market of movement, and the mismatch between the design's geography and the population's geography is one more reason the seats leak at the application stage before any question of school willingness arises.

## The demand side: eligible families who do not apply

Implementation analysis habitually counts the seats and the schools and forgets to ask why an eligible family might not present itself, and the application data, where portals make them visible, show that non-application is a mass phenomenon rather than a residual one. Maharashtra's portal figures for the cycle already cited contain the pattern in miniature: one district with applications at three times the seat stock and another with applications below the seats available, in the same state, under the same rules, in the same week. Supply side explanations cannot produce that pattern by themselves. The households differ in information, in documentation, in distance to the nearest covered school, and in their assessment of what the quota seat is worth to them, and a serious account of the scheme has to price those differences honestly.

Some part of non-application is a judgement about the school on offer. The unaided sector is a market with a steep quality gradient, and the schools with empty quota seats are disproportionately the schools whose fee paying demand is also weak; a parent who declines to apply to a struggling budget school at the end of the lane, preferring the government school with its free textbooks, midday meal and no incidental charges, is exercising an educational judgement and not failing to understand the entitlement. The midday meal alone is a real economic factor: the quota waives fees in a private school, it does not feed the child at noon, and for the poorest households the government school's meal is part of the schooling offer in a way no admission statistic records. Medium of instruction cuts the other way for other families, for whom the English medium signboard of even a modest unaided school is precisely the asset they are claiming, and both judgements are intelligible on the same street.

Another part is the rational anticipation of the filters ahead. A family that knows its documents are imperfect, that has watched a neighbour's application fail verification, or that expects the allotted school to be beyond its transport means, discounts the entitlement by its probability of completion and may not spend the days of wages the application process consumes. Programme designers describe such families as hard to reach. The family's own description is usually that the scheme is for other people, people with papers, and the description is evidence about the documentation link of the chain rather than about the family's aspirations, which every survey of poor households' educational spending contradicts: these are the households that pay fees they can barely afford when a school seems worth it. A scheme that requires the documentary apparatus of the settled state from the least documented population will under-enrol by construction, and the under-enrolment will be misread as apathy by administrators who have never had to obtain an income certificate against a deadline.

## Entry age and the pre-school doorway

The proviso that extends clause (c) to the pre-school class where a school provides pre-school education makes the youngest class the school runs the point of entry, and with it imports every dispute about entry age into the quota. States prescribe the age for admission to class one and to pre-school, the Act's own frame runs from six to fourteen, and pre-school admission under the quota concerns children below the Act's floor age being admitted into a class from which they will flow into class one with the entitlement attached. The design is generous in intent, since it gives the quota child the full run of the school's schooling from its first classroom, and administratively treacherous, because age proof for the youngest children is the document informal households hold least reliably, and because a child admitted a year early or late against the state's age rule carries the irregularity through every subsequent verification. Litigation over entry age cut-offs has been a recurring feature of Delhi's admission seasons for the general and quota categories alike, and the quota's share of that litigation is the price of attaching an eight year entitlement to a single dated doorway. The durable point for the implementation record is that the entry decision is effectively irreversible: the statute knows no lateral entry into the quota at class three or class six for a child whose family missed the doorway or moved into the neighbourhood later, so the whole value of the scheme is concentrated at one annual moment in one year of a child's life, and every failure at that moment is total.

## The repair agenda the record itself writes

No amendment of the Act is required for any of the repairs that the evidence supports, which is the practical meaning of the claim that the scheme fails at the till rather than at the door. The entitlement, the formula's structure and the delegation to states are all workable; what follows is the set of decisions available to a state government, and in two instances to Parliament, that the record identifies as load bearing.

The rate has to be made honest. The formula caps reimbursement at the state's per-child expenditure, and nothing prevents a state from computing that figure truthfully, including the full recurring cost of its own schools and revising it on a published cycle, instead of allowing it to rest on stale and partial accounting. A state unwilling to raise the rate can still remove the second injury, delay, by paying on the admission data its own portal generated, on a calendar fixed in rules, with interest accruing on arrears as a matter of entitlement rather than of grace; the Madras High Court's insistence on statutory timelines points in exactly this direction. The claims process that re-verifies, child by child and year by year, what the department's own lottery allotted is administrative theatre with a fiscal function, and abolishing the second verification would do more for school participation than any exhortation.

The definitions have to be made usable. An income ceiling notified in the scheme's first years and never revised is a decision to shrink the scheme by inflation, taken annually without a signature. Indexing the ceiling, accepting the documents households hold as proof of the facts they prove, treating a ration card as evidence of economic weakness rather than as a preliminary to a certificate attesting the same fact, and providing assisted application as a standard service instead of a charitable extra are all acts within the rule power that created the obstacles. The neighbourhood radius has to be set by the geography of school supply: the Aurangabad bench's test, that the radius must be wide enough for at least one covered school to be reachable and must widen where it is not, is available to any state as a drafting principle without waiting to be ordered.

Two repairs do need Parliament, or at least a legislature. The class eight cliff is written into the definition of elementary education and the text of clause (c), and only an amendment extends the entitlement into the secondary years or builds the bridge to the class ten examination that the record argues for; the national education policy of 2020 has already supplied the policy case for the wider extension to eighteen, and the narrower bridge is available to any state willing to fund it as a state scheme in the interim. The portability defect likewise yields to design rather than to doctrine: a state can recognise the admission of a child who arrives mid-elementary from another state's quota by rule, and a national framework for mutual recognition is an exercise of the same Concurrent List competence that produced the Act. That neither repair has been taken up, in a decade and a half of operation, is the measure of the distance between the scheme's constitutional prominence and its administrative priority.

## What would count as knowing whether it works

An implementation article owes the reader an account of the evidence that does not exist, because the scheme's defenders and critics both draw confidence from measurements the system never made. A state that operates the quota seriously would be able to say, for each admission cycle, how many seats its recognised unaided schools offered at the entry level, how many applications were received and how many were rejected at verification and on what grounds, how many allottees reported and enrolled, how many of each cohort remain in the school at each subsequent class, what each school was paid and when, and whether the children admitted are taught in the common sections of the school. Of those eight numbers, the portal states can produce the first five for the cycles their portals have run, the payment record exists in treasury systems that are not joined to the admission record, and the last two, retention by cohort and integration in the classroom, are not systematically produced anywhere. The scheme is therefore administered, in the strict sense, without knowledge of its own outcomes beyond the admission counter, and every verdict on it, including the verdict of this article, is built on the early links of the chain because those are the links that generate records.

That limitation is worth holding in mind when the scheme is compared with the statute's other promises, because the same pattern, strong entitlement, delegated machinery, unmeasured outcome, runs across the Act and across the implementation framework of this series generally. The comparative check the framework demands is available in one direction: the government school system the Act also governs reports enrolment, retention and transition through the same District Information System lineage, with known defects but with a cohort logic that permits attrition to be seen. The quota's children vanish from measurement at the moment of their greatest policy interest, the years after admission. A research agenda follows from the gap, and some of it has been taken up by the academic and policy institutions whose work this article cites: cohort tracking of admitted children, classroom level study of integration practice, and audit of the payment chain from claim to credit. Until that agenda is routine inside state administrations rather than occasional outside them, the most that honest analysis can claim is what has been claimed here: the pattern of the filling, the location of the failures, and the identity of the decisions that would change them.

## Verdict: a sound entitlement, an unpriced obligation

The One Test question for this provision is whether the reader can assess the most ambitious social integration measure in Indian education law against its evidence, and the evidence supports a verdict with two halves that must not be separated. The first half is that the entitlement works as law. It is textually clear, constitutionally upheld, judicially defended against dilution by rule and against the plea of empty coffers, and capable, in states that operate it as a system, of filling a substantial share of the seats it creates with the children it names. Nothing in fifteen years of operation suggests that the provision asks the impossible of schools or of administrations; the states that fill their seats are not richer, luckier or more virtuous than the states that do not, and their example is a standing refutation of the plea of unworkability.

The second half is that the scheme as priced and defined does not do what it was for. A formula that pays the lower of two figures, one of which the payer itself controls, guarantees a structural shortfall that the regulated schools are ordered to absorb; definitions delegated to states have been used to narrow the beneficiary class and, in the Maharashtra instance, to attempt the withdrawal of the obligation from most urban schools by notification; payment in arrears converts a reimbursement into a credit the school extends to the state; the classroom integration that was the provision's purpose is unmeasured and in places negated by separation; and the entitlement expires at class eight in the middle of the child's schooling. These are not the growing pains of a young scheme. They are settled features of its administration, reproduced cycle after cycle by decisions that identifiable governments take and could take otherwise. The scheme fails at the till, not at the door, and the repair is fiscal and administrative: an honest rate, paid on time, definitions that include the children named, a radius drawn around real geography, and a bridge across the end of elementary education. None of it requires the argument about the provision to be won again. It was won in 2012. What remains is the less glamorous work of paying for the victory, and the implementation record is, in the end, the account of a state that legislated a quarter of every classroom and then budgeted as though it had legislated a gesture.

## The interregnum: two admission cycles under a cloud

The scheme's first two cycles were run while its existence was sub judice, and the habits formed then outlasted the judgment. The Act commenced on 1 April 2010 with the constitutional challenge already filed, and the admission cycles of 2010-11 and 2011-12 proceeded in most states as a provisional exercise: state rules were framed late or not at all, schools were directed to admit against a provision their associations were asking the Supreme Court to strike down, and parents were invited to claim an entitlement whose survival was uncertain. Admissions made in those cycles were real admissions, and the children admitted held their places when the judgment came, the Court having directed that its ruling operate prospectively from the 2012-13 cycle rather than unwinding the arrangements schools had made in reliance on the challenge. But the interregnum taught each actor a lesson that the subsequent record keeps repeating. Schools learned that delay was a strategy with a respectable face, since two years of litigation had suspended the obligation's practical force. Departments learned that the quota could be administered as an announcement, since for two cycles it largely had been. And the first cohorts of parents learned that the entitlement's value depended on events in a courtroom they could not follow. Path dependence is a real force in administration: a scheme whose opening cycles are treated as provisional by everyone charged with it acquires a provisional culture, and the distance between the states in the later record is in part the distance between the administrations that rebuilt the scheme deliberately after 2012 and those that simply continued the improvisation of the interregnum.

## The school administrator's ledger

The administrator of an unaided school experiences the scheme as a ledger with entries on both sides, and the implementation debate is incomplete until the debit side is itemised in operational rather than polemical terms. The first entry is the seat declaration: the school must state its entry class strength to the department, from which the quota is computed, and the declaration is audited against the recognition file, so the administrator's discretion over the size of the obligation is confined to the honesty of a number the department already holds. The second is the admission itself, which in portal states arrives as an allotment list of named children whose documents the school verifies, a task with a real administrative cost borne by the school office, and which in decentralised states remains the school's own burden to run and to defend. The third entry is the education of the child across eight years, in which the school's costs are the ordinary costs of a pupil, with the addition in many schools of bridge support for children entering with weaker preparation, support the scheme neither funds nor forbids and whose presence or absence is the practical content of the integration question. The fourth is the annual claim: child-wise, documented, verified at the district, and paid at the notified rate on the treasury's timetable, so that the school's finance office carries a receivable from the state whose ageing it cannot control.

Against those entries the administrator sets the credits, and they are not imaginary. A school with unfilled general seats gains enrolment it would not otherwise have, and in districts where unaided supply exceeds fee paying demand the quota child is not displacing a fee but filling a chair, a configuration in which the reimbursement, however discounted, is better than the vacancy. A school in good standing with the department gains a smoother relationship with the recognition and inspection machinery on which its existence depends. And a school that integrates its quota children well gains the thing the Maharashtra appeal observations described, a student body that resembles the country, which some managements value as mission and others as reputation in a market where parents choose. The variation in school behaviour that the participation data record, in which neighbouring schools under identical rules behave oppositely, is partly the variation in which side of this ledger dominates for a given balance sheet: the oversubscribed urban school experiences the quota purely as cost, and the under-enrolled school experiences it partly as demand. Policy that treats the sector as uniformly reluctant misreads its own data; the obligation bites where seats are scarce, which is where the children most need them, and the administrator's ledger explains both halves of that sentence without recourse to anyone's character.

## The quota and the state's own schools

Section 12(1)(c) is a provision about private capacity that exists because of a public shortfall, and Section 6 of the Act states the primary duty it supplements: the appropriate government and the local authority must establish a school within the limits of the neighbourhood prescribed, where one does not exist, within three years of the Act's commencement. The neighbourhood school duty and the quota are two answers to the same absence. Where the state has built and staffed its own school to the Act's norms, the neighbourhood child holds the Section 3 right in the ordinary way. Where it has not, the quota conscripts the private supply in the same neighbourhood as a substitute. The Aurangabad bench's observation in the Maharashtra distance litigation made the connection forensic: a settlement with no school within one kilometre is, before it is anything else, a failure of the state's Section 6 duty, and to use that failure as the reason to deny the child the private school beyond the radius compounds the original default.

The interaction explains a feature of the politics that purely financial accounts miss. A state that improves its own school network shrinks the moral basis of the quota while expanding the child's options, and a state that lets its schools decay makes the quota more necessary and harder to operate, since the per-child expenditure by which reimbursement is capped is generated by the same decaying system. The two failures travel together: the low cap that deters unaided schools from filling seats is the fiscal shadow of the underfunded government school around the corner. Conversely, the quota's existence has been used in budget debates as a reason the public network need not expand into areas the unaided sector covers, an argument that mistakes a purchase of seats for a discharge of the establishment duty; the Act imposes both duties cumulatively, and the quota child in a private classroom is evidence of a Section 6 obligation met by substitution, not extinguished. The neighbourhood, the unit on which both duties are built, is where the Act's design is most honest about Indian urban reality: it concedes that schooling is local, that a six year old cannot commute across a city, and that the state's promises must therefore be kept street by street or not at all.

## A note on the evidence used in this article

The figures relied on in this article share a discipline that should be stated once, plainly. Every number is attached to the institution that produced it and the period it covers: the national fill rates to the 2015 State of the Nation reconstruction of the District Information System returns for 2013-14, in both its raw and its cleaned constructions, which are reported together because the distance between them is evidence; the Delhi and Maharashtra process details to the admission frameworks those administrations operate and publish; the Pune and Sindhudurg application figures to the Maharashtra portal's reporting for the cycle cited; the Tamil Nadu sums to the state's own pleading and the Madras High Court's judgment upon it. Where the record offers only a claimant's aggregate, as with the arrears totals compiled by school associations, the article says so and builds nothing on the figure. Where a number that public argument treats as solid does not survive inspection, as with the implied attrition rate in the early continuation data, the article reports the inspection rather than the number. And where no defensible measurement exists at all, on classroom integration and on cohort retention, the article records the absence as a finding instead of filling it with an estimate, because an implementation record that invents its missing numbers has surrendered the only advantage it holds over the argument it set out to replace.

## Enforcement on paper: what the Act can actually do to a school that refuses

The penalty architecture around the quota is thinner than the entitlement's prominence suggests, and its thinness is part of the implementation story. Section 12 itself prescribes no fine for a school that simply fails to admit its quarter. The sanctions that exist stand around the obligation rather than on it. Section 13 punishes the two gatekeeping practices that make refusal deniable: collecting a capitation fee attracts a fine of up to ten times the fee charged, and subjecting a child or parent to a screening procedure attracts a fine of twenty-five thousand rupees for the first contravention and fifty thousand for each subsequent one. Behind those fines stands the recognition power: a school that contravenes the conditions of its recognition exposes itself to withdrawal of recognition under Section 18 after an opportunity of hearing, and a school that functions without recognition faces a fine extending to one lakh rupees and a further ten thousand rupees for each day the contravention continues under Section 19. De-recognition is the scheme's capital punishment, and like most capital punishments in administrative law it is threatened more readily than it is carried out, because closing the school injures the children inside it, quota children included, and every department knows it.

The result is an enforcement gradient that runs in the wrong direction. The practices the Act fines precisely, demanding a donation or conducting an interview, are the practices of a school engaging with the admission process badly. The complete non-participation that the participation data record, the school that admits nobody and files the returns that say so, meets no bespoke sanction at all, and can be reached only through the general recognition power that departments are reluctant to use for this purpose. States with portal systems solved the problem by architecture rather than by penalty, removing the school's opportunity to refuse by allotting children to it, and the contrast is instructive for the whole implementation framework: where the obligation could be converted into a process the department controls, compliance followed the process; where compliance depended on deterring a refusal the Act had not priced, the refusal flourished unpriced. An entitlement enforced by exhortation and capital punishment, with nothing in between, will predictably be enforced at neither setting, and the measured history of the quota's penalties, sparse proceedings under Section 13 against the scale of the documented gatekeeping, bears that prediction out.

One dynamic in the record deserves to be stated before the questions begin, because it qualifies the pessimism of the aggregate figures. A successful admission changes the household that achieves it. The family that has assembled the documents once holds them for the next child; the family that has been through a lottery understands the calendar; the child inside the school becomes the neighbourhood's proof that the entitlement is real, and application volumes in the portal states show the compounding plainly in the oversubscribed districts, where demand triples the seat stock. The scheme's failures are concentrated where the first admission in a settlement has yet to happen. Its successes propagate. That asymmetry is the final reason the early decisions matter so much: a state that funds the documentation help, the assisted applications and the first cycles of a district is not buying one year's admissions but the information network on which every later year runs, and a state that lets the first cycles fail has taught its poorest settlements a lesson about the entitlement that later reforms must first unteach.

For the researcher, the provision offers a final and unusual gift: a natural experiment repeated across twenty-eight jurisdictions under a single statutory text. Because the obligation is uniform and the definitions, radii, portals, rates and calendars vary, the Indian states have run, in parallel and over fifteen years, the controlled comparison that implementation science usually has to simulate. Almost every proposition in this article is a reading of that experiment's results: hold the entitlement constant, vary the documentation burden, and admissions move; hold the definitions constant, vary the payment discipline, and school participation moves; hold both constant, and the neighbourhood radius decides which settlements the experiment reaches at all. The experiment was not designed, its data were not kept as an experiment's data should be kept, and its subjects are children rather than observations. But its findings are the most reliable knowledge the Indian state possesses about how a social entitlement behaves when it is delivered through private capacity at a public price, and they will be waiting, in the admission portals and treasury records of the states that bothered to generate them, whenever the next such entitlement is drafted and its authors ask what will decide whether it works.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How does the 25 percent quota under the Right to Education Act work?

Section 12(1)(c) of the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 requires every specified category school and every unaided school that receives no government aid to admit children belonging to weaker sections and disadvantaged groups in the neighbourhood to at least twenty-five per cent of the strength of its entry class, class one or the pre-school class where the school runs one, and to educate those children free until they complete elementary education. States run the admissions through notified rules, in the larger states through an online application and a computerised lottery, and the school is reimbursed under Section 12(2) for each child admitted.

### Q: Who qualifies as a disadvantaged group or weaker section?

The Act defines the two categories in Section 2 and leaves the operative lists to notification by each state government. A child belonging to a disadvantaged group is a child belonging to the Scheduled Castes, the Scheduled Tribes, socially and educationally backward classes, or another group disadvantaged by social, cultural, economic, geographical, linguistic, gender or similar factors that the government specifies. A child belonging to a weaker section is a child whose parent or guardian belongs to a weaker section specified by notification, in practice defined by an annual income ceiling that each state fixes for itself. Because the notifications are state made, the same household can qualify in one state and not in another.

### Q: How are private schools reimbursed for quota seats?

Section 12(2) entitles the school to reimbursement of the expenditure it incurs, to the extent of the per-child expenditure incurred by the state or the actual amount charged from the child, whichever is less, in the manner state rules prescribe. A further proviso caps the payment at the per-child expenditure of a government school, and a second proviso denies reimbursement altogether for seats the school was already obliged to fill free because it received land, building, equipment or other facilities free or at a concessional rate. The school ordinarily claims child-wise through the district administration, which verifies the claim against enrolment before the treasury releases the money.

### Q: Are the 25 percent quota seats actually being filled?

Only in part, and the proportion depends on the state and on whose count is used. The most careful national reconstruction, the State of the Nation report on Section 12(1)(c) published in 2015 by the RTE Resource Centre at IIM Ahmedabad with the Central Square Foundation, the Accountability Initiative and the Vidhi Centre for Legal Policy, found for 2013-14 a raw fill rate of 30.53 per cent in the official District Information System returns, which fell to 15.12 per cent after the data were cleaned and capped at the statutory quarter. Chhattisgarh filled 63.1 per cent of its seats in the same year, while Uttar Pradesh filled under one per cent, so the honest answer is a range across states rather than a single national figure.

### Q: Why do many children leave school after class eight under the Right to Education Act?

Because the entitlement itself ends there. The quota obliges the school to provide free and compulsory elementary education till its completion, and elementary education under the Act means classes one to eight, so the child admitted in class one holds a right that expires at about the age of fourteen, usually in a school that continues to classes ten and twelve and will charge fees from class nine. The household then faces secondary school fees at the point where the child's potential earnings also rise, and many families cannot carry the cost. The national education policy of 2020 recommended extending the entitlement to the age of eighteen, but that requires amending the Constitution and the Act and has not been enacted.

### Q: Must minority schools admit quota students?

No. The Supreme Court in 2012 held the Act inapplicable to unaided minority schools, and in Pramati Educational and Cultural Trust v Union of India in 2014 a five judge Constitution Bench held that the Act does not apply to minority institutions at all, whether aided or unaided, because compelling them to admit under its scheme would abridge the right of minorities under Article 30(1) to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice. A two judge bench in Anjuman Ishaat-E-Taleem Trust v State of Maharashtra in 2025 questioned the breadth of that exemption and referred the matter to a larger bench, but until a larger bench decides otherwise, minority institutions stand outside the quota.

### Q: What is the neighbourhood rule for Right to Education admission?

The quota is limited to children in the neighbourhood of the school, and the Act leaves the measurement of the neighbourhood to state rules, so the operative rule is a notified distance. Delhi allots first to children within one kilometre of the school, then to those within three kilometres, then within six, and only then to children beyond six kilometres. Maharashtra considers schools within one kilometre first and schools up to three kilometres away for the seats that remain. The Bombay High Court has struck down both a Maharashtra exemption built on proximity to a government school and a rigid one kilometre limit, holding that a radius which leaves a child with no reachable unaided school defeats the purpose of the Act.

### Q: What are the main criticisms of the 25 percent quota scheme?

The criticisms run in opposite directions. Unaided schools argue that reimbursement is fixed at the lower of the state's per-child expenditure and the school's fee, so each quota seat is filled at a recurring loss which fee paying parents cross-subsidise, and that payment arrives years late. Child rights advocates argue that state definitions and documentation demands exclude the children the provision was written for, that minority institutions were removed from the obligation in 2014, and that admitted children are in places taught apart from the rest of the school. A third criticism holds that the scheme subsidises private schooling instead of funding government schools. The common ground is that the failures are fiscal and administrative rather than legal.

### Q: How does a parent apply for a seat under the RTE 25 percent quota?

In states with a centralised system, the parent registers on the education department's admission portal during the notified window, enters the child's details and category, lists preferred schools within the permitted neighbourhood distance, and submits the application, receiving an acknowledgment that should be preserved. Allotment is made by computerised lottery where applications exceed seats, the result is published on the portal, and the parent then reports to the allotted school within the stated period with the original documents for verification. In states without a portal, applications are made under the state rules through the school or the district office. The admission calendar is fixed each year, and an application outside the window cannot be entertained for that cycle.

### Q: What documents are needed to apply for an RTE quota seat?

The exact list is set by each state's rules, but the standard file has four elements: proof of the child's age, proof of residence within the neighbourhood, proof of category, and, for a weaker section claim, proof of income. Category proof ordinarily means a caste or tribe certificate or the document the state notifies for the group claimed. Income proof means an income certificate from the revenue authority the state specifies, though some states accept a below poverty line or Antyodaya ration card instead. Delhi's notified lists go furthest in specifying acceptable documents and the rank of officer who may issue each. Section 14 of the Act separately provides that no child shall be denied admission for want of a birth certificate as age proof.

### Q: What happens when more children apply for a school than it has quota seats?

The school may not choose among them. Section 13 forbids subjecting a child or a parent to any screening procedure, so states resolve excess demand by lot: a computerised draw in the portal systems, conducted after the neighbourhood bands are applied, so that children nearer the school are considered before those further away. Children not allotted in the first round remain in the pool for later rounds against seats freed by reporting failures and rejections, and the rounds continue until the state's calendar closes. An applicant who is never allotted has no claim to a particular school; the entitlement is to be admitted through the process, and the process is a rationing device for a stock of seats smaller than the demand in the places parents most want.

### Q: Can a school screen or interview a child before admitting them under the quota?

No. Section 13(1) of the Act provides that no school or person shall, while admitting a child, collect any capitation fee or subject the child or the parents or guardian to any screening procedure. A school that receives a capitation fee is punishable with a fine extending to ten times the fee charged, and a school that screens is punishable with a fine of twenty-five thousand rupees for the first contravention and fifty thousand rupees for each subsequent contravention. The prohibition covers tests of the child and interviews designed to assess the family, and it is the reason allotment among eligible applicants is made by lottery rather than by any judgement of merit or suitability.

### Q: Are books and uniforms free for a child admitted under the RTE quota?

The courts have read the entitlement that way. In Social Jurist v Government of NCT of Delhi in 2013, the Delhi High Court proceeded on the position that the free education promised by the Act is not exhausted by waiving tuition and extends to the textbooks, writing material and uniforms without which a child cannot in practice attend, and the Gauhati High Court later applied the same understanding when schools required quota parents to pay for books and uniforms and the state undertook to meet the cost through reimbursement under Section 12(2). The position is therefore that a school may not recover the cost of the standard books and uniform from a quota parent, though charges for items outside the standard provision remain a recurring point of dispute in practice.

### Q: Do government-aided schools also have to give free seats under the RTE Act?

Yes, under a different clause and a different measure. Section 12(1)(b) requires an aided school to provide free and compulsory elementary education to such proportion of children admitted as its annual aid bears to its annual recurring expenses, subject to a minimum of twenty-five per cent. Because an aided school's costs are already substantially met from public funds, the obligation operates with far less friction than the unaided quota and generates little litigation. Government and local authority schools, under Section 12(1)(a), must provide free education to every child admitted. The aided limb is administered within ordinary admissions in many states rather than through the quota portal, which is one reason it is less visible in the published figures.

### Q: Why do different studies report different RTE quota fill rates?

Because the rate is a ratio and each of its terms can be built differently. The denominator may be the seats the official data system believes exist, which in the early years included schools reporting impossible numbers, or the seats computed from recognised intake. The numerator may count admissions reported by schools, admissions approved by officers, or allotments made by a portal, and these are three different populations. The 2015 State of the Nation review demonstrated the sensitivity by producing 30.53 per cent from the raw District Information System returns for 2013-14 and 15.12 per cent from the same returns after capping and cleaning. A fill rate quoted without its source, year and construction is not a measurement; it is a slogan with a percentage sign.

### Q: Can a state exempt private schools from the quota if a government school is nearby?

Maharashtra tried, and the courts struck the attempt down. By a notification of 9 February 2024 the state amended its rules to exempt a private unaided school from the quota where a government or aided school stood within one kilometre. In Aswini Kamble v State of Maharashtra, the Bombay High Court quashed the notification on 19 July 2024 as ultra vires the Act and Article 21A, holding that subordinate legislation cannot exceed or contradict the parent Act, which attaches no such condition to the obligation. The Supreme Court dismissed the schools' appeal in Association of Indian Schools v State of Maharashtra, so the quota applies irrespective of how near a government school stands.

### Q: Has the exemption of minority schools from the RTE Act been challenged again?

The exemption has been questioned from within the Supreme Court itself. In Anjuman Ishaat-E-Taleem Trust v State of Maharashtra, decided on 1 September 2025, a bench of Justice Dipankar Datta and Justice Manmohan expressed doubt about the correctness of the 2014 Constitution Bench decision in Pramati, observing that a case concerned substantially with Section 12(1)(c) had resulted in minority institutions being exempted from the entire Act, and that Article 30(1) had never been read as a blanket immunity from all regulation. The bench framed questions, including whether Pramati requires reconsideration and whether the provision could be read down instead, and referred them for consideration by a larger bench. The exemption remains the law unless and until a larger bench holds otherwise.

### Q: What is a specified category school under the RTE Act?

Section 2(n)(iii) of the Act covers schools belonging to a specified category, the standard instances being the Kendriya Vidyalayas, the Navodaya Vidyalayas and the Sainik Schools, institutions established or controlled by government for a defined class of entrants. Section 12(1)(c) names specified category schools in the same breath as unaided schools, so both owe the twenty-five per cent neighbourhood admission at the entry level. The inclusion matters because these are among the most sought after publicly run schools in the country, and a quarter of their entry seats is therefore part of what the provision distributes, reconciled in practice with the transfer and category priorities written into those institutions' own admission schemes.

### Q: In which class does a child enter under the RTE 25 percent quota?

Only at the entry level of the school. Section 12(1)(c) attaches the twenty-five per cent to the strength of class one, and a proviso extends the same regime to the pre-school class where the school imparts pre-school education, so the point of entry is the youngest class the school runs. There is no lateral entry into the quota in class three or class six: a child whose family misses the entry cycle, or moves into the neighbourhood later, cannot claim a quota seat partway through elementary education under this provision. Once admitted at the entry level, however, the child carries the entitlement forward through every succeeding class until elementary education is complete.

### Q: What should a parent do if a school refuses to admit a child allotted a quota seat?

The Act gives a defined route. Section 32 allows any person with a grievance relating to the right of a child to education to make a written complaint to the local authority, which must decide the matter within three months after giving the parties a reasonable opportunity of being heard, and an appeal lies from that decision to the State Commission for Protection of Child Rights, the body charged under Section 31 with monitoring the right's implementation. The parent should keep the allotment record, the application acknowledgment and any written refusal together, since the complaint is decided on the paper, and admissions for a later cycle do not wait for the outcome.
