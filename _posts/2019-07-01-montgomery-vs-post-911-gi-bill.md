The Montgomery vs Post-9/11 GI Bill comparison is asked most sharply by a separating servicemember holding an election form, because for that reader the comparison is a signature rather than a topic. One program was purchased during the first year of service and pays a flat monthly stipend to the veteran. The other was earned by service alone and pays tuition to the school, a housing allowance to the veteran, and a smaller amount for books. Choosing between them requires pricing a course of study that has not begun, at a school that may not be chosen, in a town whose housing costs may not be known, and the election, once made, generally cannot be taken back.

![Montgomery vs Post-9/11 GI Bill payment structures compared side by side - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-09.webp)

The two programs compared here are the Montgomery GI Bill, established by the Montgomery GI Bill Act of 1984 as Title VII of the Department of Defense Authorization Act, 1985, Public Law 98-525, and codified at Chapter 30 of title 38, United States Code, and the Post-9/11 GI Bill, established by the Post-9/11 Veterans Educational Assistance Act of 2008 as Title V of the Supplemental Appropriations Act, 2008, Public Law 110-252, and codified at Chapter 33 of the same title. Each promises 36 months of educational assistance. Almost every other feature differs, and the differences run along five axes: how the money is paid, what entry costs, whether entitlement transfers to a spouse or child, how long entitlement lasts after separation, and whether a veteran must choose between the programs at all.

This article is general information about two statutory programs, presented as a comparison and a calculation method rather than as advice to any individual. Individual circumstances and the rates in force for the academic year and locality in question determine any individual answer, and no figure quoted for one academic year answers a question about another. The source for figures is official and is named here in prose: the Department of Veterans Affairs published rate tables for each program and the GI Bill Comparison Tool maintained by the Department for institution level figures. A reader who rebuilds the calculation from those tables, using the net charges of a named institution and the enrollment pattern of a named student, holds an answer that belongs to that case. A reader who carries a headline figure from one case into another holds a number without a case.

The comparison proceeds in the order a decision requires. Payment structure comes first, because the location of each dollar decides what the other axes can change. Entry cost and the election come second, because the election fixes the terms on which either program can be claimed. Transferability and time come third, because months that cannot reach the intended student, or cannot survive until the intended start date, have no value in any payment calculation. The settings in which the older program pays more come fourth, stated as arithmetic rather than as paradox. The question whether both programs can be used comes fifth, because for one class of veteran the binary framing of the choice was itself contested. A side by side table then gathers the axes in one place, a verdict names the deciding factor, and the final section matches the comparison to the position of the reader asking it.

## Two Payment Structures: A Stipend Paid to the Veteran, a Payment Made to the School

The comparison between the Montgomery GI Bill and the Post-9/11 GI Bill begins with a difference in plumbing. The Montgomery GI Bill, codified at Chapter 30 of title 38, United States Code, was established by the Montgomery GI Bill Act of 1984, enacted as Title VII of the Department of Defense Authorization Act, 1985, Public Law 98-525, and made permanent by Public Law 100-48 in 1987. The Post-9/11 GI Bill, codified at Chapter 33 of the same title, was established by the Post-9/11 Veterans Educational Assistance Act of 2008, enacted as Title V of the Supplemental Appropriations Act, 2008, Public Law 110-252, with benefits payable for training pursued on or after August 1, 2009. Each program promises 36 months of educational assistance, and section 3695 of title 38 caps combined use of two or more federal education benefit programs at 48 months in the aggregate. Beneath that shared entitlement frame, the two programs move money in opposite directions. Chapter 30 concentrates every dollar in one monthly payment to one person. Chapter 33 divides the benefit into separate streams, sends the largest stream to a different payee, and prices each stream by a different measure. Almost every practical difference between the programs, including the cases in which the older program pays more, grows out of that structural choice.

### Who gets paid under each GI Bill?

Under Chapter 30, the Department of Veterans Affairs pays the veteran directly, in a single monthly stipend, and the veteran settles tuition and every other cost from that payment. Under Chapter 33, the Department pays tuition and fees to the institution and pays housing and books amounts to the veteran.

The routing of the money determines who deals with whom. Under Chapter 30, the Department of Veterans Affairs has one financial relationship in each case, with the beneficiary, and the institution has one financial relationship, with the student standing at the bursar window. The government never sees a tuition bill. The institution never waits on a federal payment. Under Chapter 33, the Department stands in a direct payment relationship with thousands of institutions, each of which certifies charges term by term, while the veteran receives only the components meant for living costs and books. A dispute about a charge under Chapter 30 is a private matter between student and school. A dispute about a charge under Chapter 33 runs through the certifying process, because the charge itself is the basis of a federal payment. The full statutory anatomy of Chapter 33, including the eligibility and transfer rules that sit outside the payment question, is set out in [the Post-9/11 GI Bill pillar treatment](/2019/03/01/post-911-gi-bill-2008-guide/).

### The Montgomery Stipend: One National Rate for Every School and Every Town

Chapter 30 pays a flat monthly rate of basic educational assistance under section 3015 of title 38. The rate structure has two principal categories, one for a veteran whose qualifying service rested on an initial enlistment of three years or more, and a lower one for a veteran whose qualifying service rested on an enlistment of two years, with separate treatment for certain veterans who combined active duty with service in the Selected Reserve. Within a category, the full time rate is a single figure for the entire country. Section 3015 raised that figure by legislation at intervals during the program's first decades, and subsection 3015(h) supplied a standing adjustment mechanism, increasing the rates for each fiscal year by the percentage increase in the Consumer Price Index. The rate therefore changes over time, but it changes for every beneficiary at once, by the same percentage, on a schedule set in Washington. It does not change between one institution and the next, between one state and the next, or between a low rent district and an expensive one.

Training time is the one variable that moves the individual payment. A veteran enrolled full time draws the full monthly rate for the category. A veteran enrolled at three quarter time draws 75 percent of that rate, a veteran enrolled at half time draws 50 percent, and a veteran enrolled at quarter time draws 25 percent, with entitlement consumed at a correspondingly reduced pace. The institution attended has no effect on any of these calculations. A full time student at a community college charging modest tuition receives the identical monthly payment as a full time student at a private university charging several times as much, provided both fall in the same service category. The statute prices the veteran's service and the veteran's enrollment intensity. It does not price the education.

Two additions can raise the Chapter 30 payment, and both are fixed in advance rather than calculated from charges. The first is the buy up. Participation in Chapter 30 itself rested on a pay reduction of 1,200 dollars, collected at 100 dollars a month across the first twelve months of service. A service member could then contribute an additional amount, capped at 600 dollars, under the increased benefit contribution program, and each four dollars so contributed added one dollar to the full time monthly rate, so that a maximum contribution added a fixed supplement to every full time month and a proportionately reduced supplement to part time months. The second addition is the service department supplement, often called a kicker, under which the Department of Defense funded an increased monthly amount for members who entered specialties with critical personnel shortages. Neither addition responds to tuition. A kicker was promised at enlistment, in a contract, years before the veteran chose a school, and the buy up supplement was locked in before separation. Every element of the Chapter 30 payment is knowable before enrollment, and none of it is renegotiated when a registrar sets a price.

The consequence of a price blind payment is that the veteran carries both sides of the variance. Where tuition and fees fall below the monthly stipend, the difference remains with the veteran, available for rent, food, transport, and books without any accounting to the Department. Where tuition and fees exceed the stipend, the shortfall is a personal debt problem, met from wages, savings, loans, or other aid, and the federal payment neither rises to meet the bill nor generates any institutional claim. Aid from other sources interacts with the stipend in the same indifferent way. A scholarship reduces the bill the veteran faces, but it does not reduce the Chapter 30 payment by one cent, because the payment was never calculated from the bill. The program behaves as deferred compensation with an education condition attached: service earned a sum, enrollment released it month by month, and the education market had no voice in fixing its size.

### Chapter 33: Three Streams, Three Measures, Two Payees

Chapter 33 rejects the single payment entirely. Section 3313 of title 38 builds the benefit from three components, each with its own payee, its own measure, and its own administrative logic, and the percentage tier described below then scales all three.

The first component is tuition and fees, and it is paid to the institution, not to the veteran. The measure is net cost. The institution reports the tuition and fees charged for the term after subtracting waivers, reductions, scholarships, and other assistance provided directly to the institution for the sole purpose of defraying tuition and fees. Federal student loans and Pell Grants are excluded from that subtraction, because those forms of aid are not confined to tuition. Chapter 33 is therefore a last payer on the tuition line: a tuition specific scholarship does not put money in the veteran's pocket, it reduces the federal payment by the same amount, and the veteran's position on that line is unchanged. For a public institution, the payment covers the net cost of in state tuition and fees, at undergraduate and graduate level alike. That formulation dates from the Post-9/11 Veterans Educational Assistance Improvements Act of 2010, Public Law 111-377, whose central payment changes took effect on August 1, 2011. During the program's first two academic years, from August 1, 2009 to July 31, 2011, the tuition payment had instead been capped by reference to the highest in state undergraduate tuition and fees charged by a public institution in the state where the veteran studied, a state by state ceiling that the 2010 act replaced with the institution's own net in state charges.

For a private or foreign institution, the same 2011 settlement substituted a single national maximum for tuition and fees per academic year. The payment is the lesser of the net cost and that maximum, and the maximum is adjusted for each academic year. For the academic year running from August 1, 2018 to July 31, 2019, the national maximum stood at 23,671.94 dollars. Charges above the maximum remain the veteran's responsibility unless another source covers them. The one mechanism built for that gap is the Yellow Ribbon Program under section 3318, under which an institution may volunteer to contribute toward unmet charges for students at the 100 percent tier and the Department matches the institutional contribution, up to half of the unmet amount. Yellow Ribbon has its own agreements, its own institutional participation decisions, and its own limits, and a separate treatment in this series owns that subject; for the payment comparison, the operative point is narrow, namely that Yellow Ribbon money also flows to the institution as part of the tuition stream and never becomes cash in the veteran's hands.

The second component is the monthly housing allowance, and it reverses every feature of the Chapter 30 rate. The allowance is paid to the veteran, monthly, but its amount is borrowed from an entirely separate federal pay system: the Department of Defense basic allowance for housing for a service member at pay grade E-5 with dependents, calculated for the ZIP code of the institution. Two veterans with identical service, enrolled full time in identical programs, receive different housing allowances if one institution stands in a high cost metropolitan ZIP code and the other in a low cost rural one, because military housing rates are surveyed locality by locality. The allowance carries two gates. Enrollment at half time or less produces no housing payment at all, and a person serving on active duty, along with a spouse using transferred entitlement from a sponsor on active duty, receives no housing allowance, on the ground that the household already draws military housing support. Above the half time threshold, the allowance is prorated by rate of pursuit, rounded to the nearest multiple of ten percent, so that a veteran pursuing training at 80 percent of a full time load draws 80 percent of the locality rate before the service tier is applied. Special cases follow the same locality logic by substitution: enrollment at a foreign institution drew the national average of the E-5 with dependents rate, and enrollment conducted exclusively through distance learning drew, from October 1, 2011, half of that national average rather than the rate for any campus ZIP code.

The third component is the books and supplies stipend, a small payment to the veteran that follows the credit hour rather than the calendar. The stipend accrues at 41.67 dollars per credit hour, up to 24 credit hours in an academic year, which produces the statutory ceiling of 1,000 dollars per academic year. Payment is made at the start of each term in proportion to the enrollment for that term, so a full time academic year ordinarily delivers the ceiling in two term installments. Unlike the housing allowance, the stipend reaches students on active duty as well. Unlike tuition, it is not a reimbursement of documented expense; no receipts are required, and the amount turns on credits attempted rather than on prices charged by any bookseller. The stipend resembles the Chapter 30 philosophy in miniature, a fixed sum released by enrollment, embedded inside a program whose two larger components abandoned that philosophy.

### The Percentage Tier: One Multiplier Applied to Every Stream

Section 3313 scales the entire Chapter 33 benefit by a percentage fixed by the length of aggregate active duty service performed after September 10, 2001. Service of at least 90 days but less than six months places the veteran at the 40 percent tier. Service of at least six months but less than twelve months places the veteran at 50 percent. Service of at least twelve months but less than eighteen months places the veteran at 60 percent, and service of at least eighteen months but less than twenty four months places the veteran at 70 percent. Service of at least twenty four months but less than thirty months places the veteran at 80 percent, service of at least thirty months but less than thirty six months places the veteran at 90 percent, and service of at least thirty six months places the veteran at the full 100 percent tier. One further route leads directly to the top: a veteran discharged for a service connected disability after at least 30 continuous days of active duty takes the 100 percent tier regardless of the aggregate total. The Harry W. Colmery Veterans Educational Assistance Act of 2017, Public Law 115-48, added recipients of the Purple Heart to the full tier as well. A measurement rule qualifies the lower steps: for aggregate service falling short of twenty four months, entry level and skill training time does not count toward the total, so a short period of service spent largely in initial training produces a lower tier than the raw calendar count suggests.

The tier is best understood as a multiplier laid across the whole apparatus rather than as a discount on one component. It multiplies the tuition and fees payment, so that a veteran at the 70 percent tier at a public institution has 70 percent of net in state charges paid to the school, and a veteran at the 70 percent tier at a private institution has 70 percent of the lesser of net cost or the national maximum paid, with the annual maximum itself effectively reduced to 70 percent of its stated figure. It multiplies the housing allowance, so that the locality rate for the institution's ZIP code, already prorated for rate of pursuit, is paid at the tier percentage. It multiplies the books and supplies stipend, reducing the 1,000 dollar academic year ceiling to 700 dollars at the 70 percent tier and to 400 dollars at the 40 percent tier. Entitlement, by contrast, is not multiplied: a month of full time enrollment consumes a month of the 36 months of entitlement at every tier, which means that a veteran at a lower tier spends the same entitlement currency for a fraction of the payment value. Chapter 30 contains no corresponding device. Its two service categories produce two different rates, but a qualifying veteran receives the full rate for the category from the first month of enrollment; nothing in Chapter 30 pays a veteran a stated fraction of the benefit because aggregate service fell a few months short of a higher band.

One further feature of the Chapter 33 design deserves emphasis, because it governs how far the money stretches. For degree study at an institution of higher learning, entitlement under Chapter 33 is consumed by time and rate of pursuit rather than by dollars paid. A month of full time enrollment consumes a month of entitlement whether the tuition payment for that month is large or small, and a month at half time consumes half a month. The price of the institution therefore changes the value extracted from each month of entitlement without changing the speed at which entitlement runs out. Chapter 30 reaches the same time based result by a simpler route, since a fixed stipend has no price variable to separate from the clock. In both programs the scarce resource is months. The difference lies in how much money a month can be made to carry, and under Chapter 33 that amount floats with charges, sector, and locality, while under Chapter 30 it is set in advance by statute.

### Why Identical Service Produces Unequal Payments

Set the two structures side by side and the deciding asymmetry becomes plain. Under Chapter 30, the federal payment is a function of two variables, the service category and the training time, and it is invariant across every institution, every price, and every housing market in the country. Under Chapter 33, the federal payment is a function of the service tier, the institution's net charges, the institution's sector, the institution's ZIP code, and the rate of pursuit, so the payment is partially a function of decisions the veteran makes after service ends. A veteran who chooses an expensive private institution in an expensive city extracts a far larger federal payment from the same 36 months of entitlement than a veteran who chooses a low cost public institution in an inexpensive town, even where both veterans performed identical service and study at identical intensity. Under Chapter 30 those two veterans receive the same money to the dollar, and the veteran at the inexpensive institution keeps a surplus that the veteran at the expensive institution must replace from private resources. Neither result is an accident of administration. Chapter 30 was designed in 1984 as a recruiting and retention incentive whose value a prospective recruit could be quoted in advance, and a payment that can be quoted in advance must be a fixed number. Chapter 33 was designed in 2008 to approximate the actual cost of attendance for a generation that had served in two wars, and a payment that tracks actual cost must surrender the fixed number and accept whatever the institution charges and the locality demands, subject to the public institution rule, the private institution maximum, and the tier. The payment structure axis therefore frames the entire comparison. Every other axis, the cost of entering each program, the transfer rules, and the time limits, modifies a choice whose financial core is set here, by the difference between a stipend that ignores prices and a benefit built out of prices themselves.

## The Price of Entry and the Election That Cannot Be Taken Back

The comparison between the Montgomery GI Bill and the Post-9/11 GI Bill begins at entry into active duty, where the two programs rest on opposite assumptions about who pays. Chapter 30 of title 38, the Montgomery program, treats educational assistance as a contributory benefit: the member pays a defined sum out of basic pay, and entitlement follows only if the contribution and the qualifying service are both completed. Chapter 33, the Post-9/11 program, treats educational assistance as a benefit earned by service alone, with no contribution at all. That difference drives the whole election machinery, because an election moving a member from one chapter to the other must decide what happens to money paid in and to unused months. The full statutory history of the older program appears in [the guide to the Montgomery GI Bill enacted in 1984](/2019/03/15/montgomery-gi-bill-1984-guide/).

Section 3011(b) of title 38 sets the entry price and the collection method. The basic pay of a covered individual who does not elect out of the program is reduced by 100 dollars for each of the first 12 months in which the individual is entitled to basic pay, producing the 1,200 dollar contribution. The reduction operates automatically during the first year of service, when a new member has the least basis for valuing a benefit that might be used years after separation. For a member of the Selected Reserve who establishes entitlement through active duty service, section 3011 supplies a parallel route under which the Secretary of Defense collects the same total within the period the statute prescribes after the qualifying service. The total is fixed in either route and does not vary with rank or pay grade. Section 3011(b) also directs that any amount by which basic pay is reduced reverts to the Treasury and is not, for purposes of any federal law, considered to have been received by the individual or to be within the control of the individual. The 1,200 dollars is not a deposit in an individual account and not a savings balance the member owns. The reduction purchases eligibility if the remaining statutory conditions are met, and nothing more.

### The decision recorded at entry into service

Section 3011(c)(1) permits an individual in the principal active duty category to elect not to receive educational assistance under Chapter 30, and any such election must be made at the time the individual initially enters on active duty as a member of the Armed Forces. The administrative record is DD Form 2366, the Montgomery GI Bill Act of 1984 enrollment form, on which the member acknowledges the terms of participation or signs the disenrollment statement recording a decision to stay out. The structure is an opt out structure. A covered member who signs nothing declining the program is enrolled, and the 100 dollar monthly reduction follows from remaining enrolled, while a member who signs the disenrollment portion has made the section 3011(c) election and the reduction never begins. The consequence is stated in the same subsection without qualification: an individual who makes the election is not entitled to educational assistance under Chapter 30, and the service departments treat disenrollment as a one time and irrevocable decision.

Declining keeps 100 dollars a month in basic pay during the first year and gives up, in exchange, the entire Chapter 30 entitlement the contribution would have supported, including the additional contribution option described below, which exists only for a participant in the base program. Declining is not a deferral, and no general reenrollment window restores the entitlement. Congress did cut a small number of narrow doors back into Chapter 30, each for a defined class and none amounting to a general right of reversal. Section 3018 addresses participants in the Post-Vietnam Era Veterans Educational Assistance Program under Chapter 32 who elect to convert to Chapter 30, with the 1,200 dollar contribution collected as part of the conversion. Section 3018A gives certain members facing involuntary separation an opportunity to enroll before separation, again on payment of the contribution. Section 3018B extends a comparable opportunity to certain members separated voluntarily under the separation incentive programs that section names, and section 3018C provides a further conversion opportunity for a defined group of Chapter 32 participants meeting continuous service and related conditions. A member who signed the disenrollment statement at entry and separated in ordinary circumstances fits none of those classes by virtue of regret alone.

### The additional contribution and what it buys

Section 3011(e) authorizes an eligible individual who has not elected out of the program to contribute amounts for the purpose of receiving an increased amount of basic educational assistance, as provided in section 3015(g). The additional contribution, known in service guidance as the buy up, is voluntary in a way the base contribution is not. Contributions may be made at any time while the member is on active duty, but not more frequently than monthly, in multiples of 20 dollars, up to a total of 600 dollars, paid to the Secretary of the military department concerned and deposited into the Treasury as miscellaneous receipts. The buy up is recorded on DD Form 2366-1, separate from the base contribution, a distinction that matters because the two sums receive different treatment if the member elects Chapter 33. Section 3015(g) supplies the exchange rate as a fixed statutory ratio: effective from the first day of the enrollment period following receipt of the contribution, the monthly amount otherwise payable is increased by 5 dollars for each 20 dollars contributed. The full 600 dollars raises the monthly payment by 150 dollars, totaling 5,400 dollars across 36 months of entitlement if every month is used at the rate to which the increase applies. The increase attaches only to months actually paid, so partial use collects a partial increase with no settlement for the unused portion, and the increase belongs entirely to Chapter 30, which is why its treatment on an election into Chapter 33 is a forfeiture rather than a transfer.

### When the contribution is gone for good, and the one payment Chapter 30 makes to a family

The default rule for the 1,200 dollars is nonrefundability, following directly from the Treasury reversion language in section 3011(b). Chapter 30 contains no provision refunding the base contribution because a member disenrolls, separates without using benefits, or allows the period for using benefits to expire with entitlement unused, and no hardship provision converts an unused entitlement back into cash. An additional contribution under section 3011(e) is likewise deposited as miscellaneous receipts with no provision paying it back. Both sums are sunk at the moment of payment, subject only to the two payment routes Congress wrote expressly, the death benefit in section 3017 and the proportional additional assistance in section 3327(f), each with conditions narrow enough that most departing members satisfy neither.

Section 3017 is the only route operating entirely inside Chapter 30. In the event of the service connected death of an individual within the classes the section describes, including an individual on active duty or an individual whose death occurs within one year after discharge or release from active duty, the Secretary pays a death benefit tied to the contributions made. The amount reflects the reductions from pay under sections 3011(b), 3012(c), or 3018(c), together with any contributions made under sections 3018A, 3018B, or 3018C and any additional contributions made under section 3011(e), reduced by the educational assistance already paid, so a family receives the unrecovered balance rather than a second payment of sums already consumed as benefits. The statute fixes the order of payment: first to the beneficiary or beneficiaries designated under the individual's Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance policy, then to a surviving spouse, then to surviving children in equal shares, and then to surviving parents in equal shares, taking the first class in that sequence that has a survivor on the date of death. Outside section 3017, death does not unlock the contribution.

### Can the election between the two programs be undone?

No. An election to receive Chapter 33 benefits in lieu of Chapter 30 benefits is irrevocable under section 3327, and an election at entry to decline Chapter 30 likewise closes that program, subject only to narrow statutory exceptions and to correction of an election the Secretary made on a member's behalf.

The second election, from Chapter 30 into Chapter 33, begins with the absence of any price. Chapter 33 imposes no contribution. Entitlement under section 3311 rests on qualifying active duty service after September 10, 2001 and on the character of the discharge or release following the service, and no provision of chapter 33 reduces basic pay or conditions entitlement on amounts paid during service. A member whose service qualifies under both chapters faces an asymmetric choice: one entitlement was purchased with 1,200 dollars of forgone pay, the other was earned by the same service without payment, and choosing the second requires surrendering the first in the manner sections 3322 and 3327 prescribe. Section 3322(a) states the coordination rule: an individual entitled under Chapter 33 who is also eligible under Chapter 30 or another listed program may not receive assistance under two or more such programs at the same time, and must elect, in the form and manner the Secretary prescribes, under which chapter or provisions to receive educational assistance.

Section 3327 governs that election. Subsection (a) defines the classes eligible to elect by reference to the position held on August 1, 2009, and to qualification for Chapter 33 entitlement on the date of the election. The classes include an individual entitled to Chapter 30 assistance who had used part of that entitlement and retained an unused balance, an individual entitled to Chapter 30 assistance who had used none of it, and a member eligible for Chapter 30 assistance who was making contributions under section 3011(b) or section 3012(c). For the contributing member, subsection (b) supplies an immediate consequence: effective from the first month beginning on or after the date of the election, the obligation to make Chapter 30 contributions ceases. A member who elects Chapter 33 partway through the first year stops the 100 dollar reductions from that point, loses Chapter 30 eligibility by the same act, and carries into Chapter 33 only the partial contribution already made, valued under section 3327(f) if its conditions are ever met.

The conversion of entitlement is month for month. Under section 3327(d), an electing individual receives educational assistance under Chapter 33 instead of basic educational assistance under Chapter 30. For an individual who had used part of the Chapter 30 entitlement, subsection (d)(2) limits the Chapter 33 entitlement to the months of unused Chapter 30 entitlement held on the date of the election, plus any months of transferred entitlement revoked under subsection (c)(1) so that those months return to the individual rather than remaining with a dependent. A member with 20 months remaining at election receives 20 months under Chapter 33, not a renewed 36 months. A member who had used no Chapter 30 entitlement receives Chapter 33 entitlement in accordance with chapter 33's own terms, subject to the aggregate limits on combined use, including the 48 month aggregate cap in section 3695. Section 3327(e) preserves one seam: where assistance available under Chapter 30 is not authorized under Chapter 33, the electing individual remains entitled to that assistance under Chapter 30, and each month used on that basis charges one month against Chapter 33 entitlement.

Irrevocability is stated in the section itself. Section 3327(i) provides that an election under subsection (a), and an election to revoke transferred entitlement under subsection (c)(1), is irrevocable. The implementing regulation at 38 C.F.R. section 21.9520 builds the same requirement into the act of electing: the individual acknowledges that the election is irrevocable and may not be changed, whether the election is made on the application form the Department of Veterans Affairs prescribes, through a transfer of entitlement designation, or by a written statement identifying the program relinquished and the effective date chosen. Administrative decisions add a safeguard narrower than an exception: where no valid election was completed, because the acknowledgment of irrevocability was never made, the election can fail for want of the statutory act itself, a ruling that no election occurred rather than permission to undo one that did. One qualification, added by amendment in 2016 and operative from January 1, 2017, concerns elections the Secretary makes. Under section 3327(h), if an individual submits an election the Secretary determines to be clearly against the individual's interests, or fails to make an election, the Secretary may make an alternative election on the individual's behalf, must notify the individual within seven days, and must allow a 30 day period from receipt of the notice to modify or revoke that election. The individual's own election receives no comparable window.

The election must also name what is given up. The electing individual relinquishes one program as the price of receiving Chapter 33 in lieu of that program, because a single period of service cannot generate entitlements under two chapters at the same time, and service credited to the relinquished program cannot be credited a second time elsewhere. The regulation at 38 C.F.R. section 21.9520 resolves a case that surprises many entrants: a member who made the section 3011(c) election at entry and declined Chapter 30 altogether may make an irrevocable election to receive Chapter 33 benefits, because Chapter 33 entitlement never depended on the contribution. The reverse passage does not exist. Electing Chapter 33 in lieu of Chapter 30 extinguishes the entitlement relinquished, and no provision permits restoration of Chapter 30 in exchange for unused Chapter 33 months.

### The proportional refund, and why the buy up never comes back

The single route by which the 1,200 dollars can return to the person who paid it runs through section 3327(f), framed as additional Post-9/11 assistance for members who made contributions toward the older program. For an electing individual in the classes the subsection covers, the monthly stipend payable under section 3313 is increased, at the end of the entitlement, by an amount equal to the total contributions made under section 3011(b) or section 3012(c) as of the date of the election, multiplied by a fraction. The numerator is the number of months of Chapter 30 entitlement remaining at the time of the election, plus any months revoked from a transfer under subsection (c)(1), and the denominator is 36 months. A member who elects with the whole entitlement unused qualifies for the full contribution back, and a member who used 18 months of Chapter 30 benefits before electing qualifies for half. For a member who was contributing at the date of election, subsection (f)(2) fixes the remaining entitlement figure at 36 months, so the amount actually contributed by that date is the base. Section 3327(f)(3) requires payment together with the last monthly stipend payment before exhaustion of the Chapter 33 entitlement. The individual must exhaust that entitlement, must be the person who made the contribution, and must be receiving the housing allowance payment stream to which the additional amount is attached at exhaustion. Exhaustion by a spouse or child using transferred entitlement does not generate the payment, because the payment travels with the contributor's own final stipend, and a member who allows part of the entitlement to lapse receives nothing.

The buy up receives no parallel treatment. Section 3327(f) measures its amount exclusively by contributions toward basic educational assistance under section 3011(b) or section 3012(c). Contributions under section 3011(e) are not in the base, the section 3015(g) increase has no Chapter 33 counterpart, and departmental guidance states the result plainly: a member who elects the Post-9/11 program does not receive the buy up increase and is not refunded the additional contribution. A member who paid the full 600 dollars and elects Chapter 33 before using Chapter 30 benefits forfeits the additional contribution and the 5,400 dollars of potential increase it would have purchased, while preserving a claim, on full exhaustion, to the 1,200 dollar base contribution alone. Section 3327(g) sharpens the distinction by carrying other enhancements across the election, including increased assistance for a critical skill or specialty under section 3015(d), in an equivalent amount during Chapter 33 use. Congress carried the service based enhancements across and left the purchased enhancement behind, which makes the buy up the most exposed money in the election.

### Why the statute locks the door behind the election

Irrevocability in both elections follows from the design of the two chapters rather than from administrative habit. The bar on duplicated receipt in section 3322 means the government must know, at any given moment, which chapter a period of service is credited under, and an election open to revision at will would leave that crediting permanently provisional. The month for month conversion in section 3327(d) depends on a count of unused entitlement taken on a single date, the date of the election, and every payment, refund calculation, and transfer revocation downstream inherits that count, as does the numerator in section 3327(f). A revocable election would invite sequential use of whichever program paid better for a given term, the duplication the coordination sections exist to prevent. Finality also protects the fiscal character of the contribution: amounts reduced from pay revert to the Treasury when collected and are treated as never received by the member, so the government's books close on the contribution in the year of collection while the member's entitlement remains the open asset.

A second reason follows from the way entitlement attaches to service. Each chapter grants entitlement on the basis of a period of service, and the aggregate structure of federal education assistance, capped at 48 months across programs under section 3695, assumes that one period of qualifying service supports one stream of entitlement up to the cap rather than parallel streams between which a member could alternate as prices change. Once service has been assigned to a chapter and benefits have been certified, paid, and charged against that entitlement, reassignment would misstate the record of what the service earned and what the government paid against it. Congress expressed the same preference at entry: a disenrollment decision reversible after the contribution year would let members wait for certainty about education plans before deciding whether the government could collect, defeating the contributory structure enacted in 1984. Because the decisions cannot be undone, the law front loads the warning, in the form signed at entry and in the acknowledgment of irrevocability on the Chapter 33 application. The calculation between a flat purchased benefit and a service earned benefit that scales with tuition and housing must therefore be performed while both options remain open, because the act of choosing one closes the other and fixes the month count and the refund fraction on that date.

## Transferability and Time in the Montgomery and Post-9/11 Comparison

Two of the five axes in the Montgomery versus Post-9/11 comparison concern control over the benefit rather than the size of any single payment. The first axis asks who may use the entitlement. The second axis asks how long the entitlement survives after the service that earned it has ended. The two programs answer the first question in opposite ways and answer the second question in ways that diverged further after 2017, when Congress rewrote the time rule for the newer program and left the older program untouched. A veteran weighing the programs on payment structure alone, without settling the transfer question and the time question first, risks assigning value to months of entitlement that the intended student could never use, or to months that would expire before a planned course of study could begin. Transferability determines whether the benefit belongs to one person or to a household. The delimiting period determines whether education can be deferred for a few years, for a working lifetime, or not at all. Both axes therefore shape the election between the programs before any tuition figure or housing figure enters the calculation, and both axes turn on statutory text rather than on administrative practice, which means the answers were fixed by Congress and changed only when Congress acted.

### Which GI Bill can be transferred to a spouse or child?

Only the Post-9/11 GI Bill can be transferred. Chapter 33 permits an eligible servicemember, while serving, to assign unused entitlement to a spouse, to one or more children, or to a combination of both. Chapter 30 contains no transfer authority, so Montgomery entitlement remains personal to the servicemember who earned it.

The absence of transfer authority in Chapter 30 is a structural feature of the Montgomery program, not an administrative gap that a waiver could cure. The program enacted in 1984 was built as a contributory arrangement between the individual servicemember and the government. The servicemember contributed through reductions in pay during the first year of service, and the entitlement that resulted was an individual readjustment benefit, payable to the veteran for education the veteran personally pursued. Nothing in Chapter 30 authorizes the veteran to designate another person to draw on the entitlement, and the Department of Veterans Affairs has no mechanism for paying Chapter 30 assistance to a spouse or child as a transferee. The practical consequences follow directly from the text. A servicemember who completed a degree before separation, or whose career after service never required further schooling, held Montgomery months with no lawful recipient other than the servicemember personally. A family that planned around a child's college costs a decade after separation gained nothing from unused Chapter 30 entitlement, however many months remained unused. Separate programs existed for dependents of veterans who died or were permanently and totally disabled from service connected causes, but those programs rested on the veteran's death or disability rating, not on any transfer of Chapter 30 entitlement, and confusing the two routes produced a recurring error in family planning. Under Chapter 30, entitlement unused at the end of the veteran's delimiting period simply expired, and entitlement the veteran never used during life passed to no one.

Chapter 33 reversed that design choice deliberately. Transferability entered the Post-9/11 program as a retention instrument rather than as a family benefit in the abstract, and the statutory conditions reflect that origin. Section 3319 of title 38 authorizes the Secretary concerned to permit an eligible individual to transfer unused entitlement to a spouse, to one or more children, or to any combination of spouse and children. Because the authority is framed as permission administered through the armed services, approval rests with the servicemember's own service department under Department of Defense policy, and the Department of Veterans Affairs pays transferred benefits only after the service has approved the transfer. The entitlement transferred is measured in months drawn from the transferor's own 36 months of Chapter 33 entitlement, and use by a dependent is charged against the transferor's entitlement at the rate of one month for each month used. A transfer therefore divides a fixed pool of months among family members rather than creating any additional entitlement for the household, and a servicemember with a spouse and two children faced an allocation decision, expressed in the election itself, about how many months each designated dependent would receive.

The conditions attached to a transfer election form a sequence, and each condition has a distinct function. The election had to be made while the servicemember was serving, as a member of the armed forces on active duty or as a member of the Selected Reserve. A veteran who had already separated could not make a new transfer, however compelling the family's circumstances had become after separation. The servicemember had to have completed at least six years of service in the armed forces on the date the transfer request was approved. The servicemember also had to agree to serve four additional years from the date of election. For a servicemember who had already completed at least ten years of service but was precluded by standard service policy or by statute from committing to four additional years, section 3319 substituted an agreement to serve the maximum amount of time allowed under that policy or statute. The dependent designated to receive the transfer had to be enrolled in the Defense Enrollment Eligibility Reporting System, known as DEERS, at the time of transfer, because DEERS registration supplied the government's record of the family relationship on which eligibility rested. Each element screened for a different thing. The service requirement identified a career force member rather than a short term entrant. The additional commitment purchased retention, which was the stated purpose of the authority. The DEERS requirement tied payment to a verifiable dependent status recorded before any benefit moved.

Spouse and child transferees stood under different rules for when transferred entitlement could be used, and the difference reflected the different positions the two classes occupied. A spouse could begin using transferred entitlement as soon as the transfer was approved, and could use it while the servicemember remained in the armed forces or after separation. One restriction qualified that immediacy. A spouse was not eligible for the monthly housing allowance while the servicemember was serving on active duty, because the household already received housing support through the servicemember's own allowances, and paying a second housing benefit to the same household would have duplicated that support. Tuition and fee payments and the books and supplies stipend carried no equivalent restriction for a spouse during the member's active service. A child faced a later starting point on two independent grounds. Transferred entitlement could be used by a child only after the servicemember had completed at least ten years of service, a threshold reached either through the original six years plus the four year additional commitment or through longer service on another path. The child also had to have completed the requirements of a secondary school diploma or an equivalency certificate, or to have reached 18 years of age. A child, unlike a spouse, could receive the monthly housing allowance even while the servicemember served on active duty, because a child studying away from the household did not duplicate the member's housing support in the same way. Against those later starting points stood an early ending point that applied to children alone. A child could not use transferred entitlement after reaching 26 years of age. Marriage did not defeat a child's eligibility to receive transferred benefits, and divorce did not by itself defeat a former spouse's eligibility, although in each case the transferor's retained power to revoke could end the entitlement by a different route.

That retained power is the second half of the transfer design, and it distinguishes a transfer from a gift executed and placed beyond recall. Under section 3319, the individual who made a transfer retained the right to modify or revoke the transfer at any time. Modification covered the allocation among designated dependents, so months assigned to one child could be reassigned to another child or taken back for the servicemember's own use, and the amounts held by each dependent could be adjusted after separation by application to the Department of Veterans Affairs even though no new dependent could be added once service had ended. Revocation withdrew the transferred entitlement altogether. The dependent's interest was therefore derivative in a strict sense. A spouse who had planned a degree around transferred months held an entitlement that the transferor could reduce or cancel, and a child approaching a course of study held months subject to the same power. The design also carried a sanction for the transferor's side of the bargain. A servicemember who failed to complete the additional service agreed to at the election risked having the transfer treated as invalid, with amounts already paid to dependents exposed to treatment as an overpayment, subject to the exceptions the statute and service regulations recognized for separations the member did not choose. The four year commitment was thus an enforceable condition of the transfer rather than a statement of intent, and families evaluating a transfer had to weigh the certainty of the dependent's schooling plans against the contingency of the member's remaining service.

### How long does each benefit remain available after separation?

Chapter 30 entitlement expires ten years after the last discharge or release from active duty, subject to narrow statutory extensions. Chapter 33 entitlement originally expired after fifteen years. Under the Colmery Act, Chapter 33 entitlement does not expire for a servicemember whose last separation occurred on or after January 1, 2013.

The Montgomery delimiting rule sits in section 3031 of title 38. The period during which an individual entitled to Chapter 30 assistance may use that entitlement expires at the end of the ten year period beginning on the date of the individual's last discharge or release from active duty. The clock is personal and mechanical. It runs from the last separation, so a later return to active duty of 90 days or more could set a new period running from the later separation, but ordinary post service events, such as employment, family obligations, or the absence of a suitable local program, did not stop it. Congress wrote specific extensions into the same section for specific hardships, and the list defines the outer boundary of administrative generosity. The period did not run while an individual was prevented from pursuing a program of education because the character of discharge had not yet been corrected by competent military authority. It could be extended where the individual had been captured and held as a prisoner of war by a foreign government or power after the last discharge or release. It could be extended by the amount of time the individual was prevented from initiating or completing a chosen program of education because of a physical or mental disability not resulting from the individual's own willful misconduct, a ground that had to be established by medical evidence showing that the program was medically infeasible during the period claimed. Separate provisions protected a student whose entitlement expired in the middle of an academic term, allowing completion of a quarter or semester in defined circumstances rather than cutting off payment mid course. Outside those channels, unused Chapter 30 months at the tenth anniversary of separation were lost, and the loss was total rather than partial.

Chapter 33 as enacted in 2008 adopted the same architecture with a longer fuse. Section 3321 of title 38 provided that the period for using Post-9/11 entitlement expired at the end of the fifteen year period beginning on the date of the individual's last discharge or release from active duty. The longer period acknowledged a different service pattern, in which reserve component members cycled between civilian employment and activation and in which education was frequently deferred well beyond the transition out of uniform, but the structure matched the Montgomery model of a fixed window anchored to separation. The tolling provisions of section 3031 for discharge correction, captivity, and disability were carried into the Chapter 33 period by cross reference, so the same hardship logic governed both clocks. For dependents, the transferred entitlement inherited a version of the same time discipline. A spouse transferee's period of use tracked the transferor's delimiting framework, while a child transferee's use was governed by the age boundary in section 3319 rather than by a period measured from the member's separation, which meant a young child could hold transferred months for many years before the age limit, rather than the separation clock, closed the window.

The Harry W. Colmery Veterans Educational Assistance Act of 2017, Public Law 115-48, enacted on August 16, 2017, broke the parallel structure. Section 112 of the Colmery Act amended section 3321 to divide Chapter 33 users into two cohorts by separation date. For an individual whose last discharge or release from active duty occurred before January 1, 2013, entitlement expired at the end of the fifteen year period beginning on that discharge or release, exactly as the 2008 act had provided. For an individual whose last discharge or release from active duty occurred on or after January 1, 2013, entitlement did not expire at all. The amendment removed the delimiting date entirely for the later cohort rather than extending it to a longer term of years, which converted the Post-9/11 benefit for that cohort into an entitlement limited by its 36 months of content but not by any anniversary of separation. The same cohort line governed the parallel rule for spouses using transferred entitlement, whose period of use followed the transferor's separation date. Chapter 30 received no corresponding amendment. The Montgomery ten year clock continued to govern every Chapter 30 user, including servicemembers whose service overlapped entirely with the service that qualified a colleague for a limit free Chapter 33 entitlement. The Colmery Act also carried a scheduled change to the Chapter 33 percentage tiers that belonged to the payment axis rather than the time axis, but its timing illustrates how the act staged its reforms. The act provided for consolidation of the benefit levels effective August 1, 2020, eliminating the 40 percent tier and raising the lowest qualifying service band to the 50 percent level, a change enacted in 2017 and scheduled for a future effective date rather than applied on enactment.

Set side by side, the two axes produce different kinds of asymmetry. Transferability is a one way door in the comparison. One program has the feature and the other lacks it altogether, with no degree of partial credit available to Chapter 30 and no set of circumstances in which Montgomery entitlement could be redirected to a dependent. A servicemember for whom financing a spouse's credential or a child's degree stood at the center of the education plan had the transfer question decide the comparison before payment rates were consulted, because only Chapter 33 offered anything to decide about. Time operates differently after the Colmery amendments. The relevant division runs through Chapter 33 itself, separating members whose last separation fell before January 1, 2013 and who carried a fifteen year deadline from members whose last separation fell on or after that date and who carried no deadline at all, while Chapter 30 imposed its shorter ten year deadline on every user without a cohort distinction. Program choice and separation cohort therefore interact. A pre 2013 separator comparing the programs weighed a ten year Montgomery clock against a fifteen year Post-9/11 clock. A servicemember whose last separation fell on or after January 1, 2013 weighed a ten year Montgomery clock against a Post-9/11 entitlement with no expiration, a difference that altered the value of deferral itself, since education postponed for a decade of civilian work consumed the whole of one benefit and none of the other's time. The comparison on these two axes accordingly rewards precision about dates and designations. The date of last separation determines which time rule applies. The date of a transfer election, made during service rather than after it, determines whether any transfer exists at all.

## Where the Cheaper-Looking Benefit Pays More

The comparison between Chapter 30, the Montgomery GI Bill, and Chapter 33, the Post-9/11 GI Bill, misleads whenever it begins with the size of the benefit rather than with the shape of the bill a particular course of study creates. Chapter 33 was designed to scale. Its tuition payment rises with the charges an institution imposes, subject to the applicable limits, and its housing allowance rises with housing costs in the locality where classes are attended, because that allowance rests on the military Basic Allowance for Housing for a member in pay grade E-5 with dependents in that locality. Chapter 30 was designed to stay flat. Its monthly stipend arrives at a rate set by training time and length-of-service category, the same in a high-rent coastal city and in a small town, the same at an expensive private institution and at a community college charging very little, subject to the special limits for active duty use and for training below half time. A scaling benefit defeats a flat benefit wherever the thing it scales with is large, and loses ground, sometimes all of its ground, wherever the thing it scales with is small, absent, or artificially halved. That inversion is the namable claim of this article, and this section works through the arithmetic and the four settings in which the inversion occurs.

### When does the Montgomery GI Bill pay more than the Post-9/11 GI Bill?

The Montgomery GI Bill pays more when net tuition is low or waived, local housing costs are modest, study is entirely online, or enrollment sits at half time, because its flat stipend keeps arriving in full while the Post-9/11 tuition payment shrinks toward zero and its housing allowance contracts or disappears entirely.

Each element of that answer corresponds to a component that Chapter 33 pays and Chapter 30 does not separate. Chapter 33 divides its value into distinct streams, each with its own trigger and ceiling. The tuition stream is paid to the institution, and it can only be as large as the net charge remaining after specified tuition-directed aid has been applied. The housing stream is paid to the student, and it can only be as large as the locality rate, the national average, or half the national average, depending on where and how enrollment occurs, and it stops altogether at or below a stated pursuit threshold. The books stream is paid by credit hour up to an annual maximum, and it is modest beside either of the other two. Chapter 30 consolidates its value into a single stipend paid to the student, with no tuition trigger and no locality trigger at all. Generosity in Chapter 33 is conditional on charges and on place, and conditionality is an advantage in expensive settings and a liability in inexpensive ones.

### How should the two totals be built before any scenario is tested?

The method totals what a defined course of study actually draws under each program, over the same period, for the same person, at the same institution, and on the same schedule. A monthly figure quoted in isolation cannot carry the comparison, because the two programs distribute value differently across a term, and because one program pays part of its value to an institution rather than to the student. The first entry on the Chapter 33 side is the tuition and fees payment to the institution, counted at the amount the institution actually receives: the net charge after waivers, reductions, scholarships, and other assistance designated solely for tuition and fees and provided directly to the institution have been applied. Loans do not reduce that net charge, and neither do funds provided under section 401(b) of the Higher Education Act of 1965, the provision covering the Pell Grant, a distinction the Congressional Research Service account of the program states expressly. Money paid to an institution counts as value because it discharges a bill the student would otherwise owe, but only up to the bill itself, so a large theoretical entitlement produces no additional value at an institution whose net charge is small.

The second entry is the housing allowance the enrollment actually generates, not the allowance attached to full-time resident study in an expensive locality. That entry depends on the locality rate where the student physically attends the majority of classes, on whether enrollment is exclusively through distance learning, on the eligibility percentage earned through qualifying service, and on the rate of pursuit, rounded to the nearest multiple of ten percent under the statutory formula. The third entry is the books stipend, calculated by credit hour and capped across the academic year. A complete total also records how many months of the 36 months of entitlement a term consumes on each side, because slower consumption changes the total value of a course of study. Against the Chapter 33 total stands a simpler Chapter 30 figure, the monthly stipend for the applicable training-time category multiplied across the months of enrollment, because the stipend is the whole payment and the student directs it toward tuition, rent, books, or any other cost. The entry contribution belongs in the calculation only in the circumstances addressed near the end of this section.

The method therefore refuses to credit Chapter 33 with tuition value that other aid has already extinguished, or with a locality rate where enrollment assigns a smaller rate, and the patterns visible in [the evidence on GI Bill education outcomes](/2019/06/01/gi-bill-education-outcomes/) sit behind that discipline, because outcomes follow money actually received rather than the most favorable column of a benefit table. The scenarios follow one variable at a time.

### What happens when tuition has already been covered by other aid?

The low-tuition and waived-tuition scenario removes the largest Chapter 33 component before the comparison begins. A public institution may waive tuition for a category of students under state law, an institution may reduce charges through a scholarship applied directly to tuition, or a state or employer program may pay tuition charges at the institution on behalf of the student. In each case the charge that reaches the Department of Veterans Affairs is the net charge, and a net charge of zero produces a tuition payment of zero, through no failure of eligibility and no reduction in the percentage of benefit earned. The student has the full benefit, and the full benefit attaches to a bill that does not exist. Housing and books payments continue on their own terms, so the Chapter 33 total consists of those payments alone, and the comparison reduces to a contest between a housing allowance plus a small books stipend on one side and a complete flat stipend on the other.

Chapter 30 has no corresponding reduction. The stipend is not calculated from charges and does not shrink because a scholarship office has paid the tuition bill first. A student whose tuition is covered by other aid receives the stipend in full for the training-time category and may apply the whole amount toward living costs, books, transportation, and any charges that tuition-directed aid leaves behind. Outside aid therefore interacts with the two programs in opposite directions. Under Chapter 33, tuition-directed aid often displaces federal payment rather than adding to it, dollar for dollar, because the federal payment was defined as the amount left after that aid. Under Chapter 30, the same aid stacks, because the federal payment was defined without reference to charges at all.

Low tuition produces a weaker version of the same result without any waiver at all. Community colleges and some public institutions charge net tuition and fees that are small beside a monthly stipend, and paying one hundred percent of a small bill is worth less than a flat sum calculated without reference to the bill. The scenario has limits. Fees may survive a waiver and generate a residual Chapter 33 payment, aid not designated solely for tuition and fees does not reduce the net charge, and a private institution balance above the annual cap is a charge the Chapter 30 stipend must absorb first. None of those qualifications restores the displaced tuition value where the waiver or scholarship is complete, and completeness is the condition on which the scenario turns.

### What happens where local housing costs are low?

The low-housing-cost scenario attacks the second Chapter 33 component rather than the first. The housing allowance is the locality-scaled element of the newer program, tied to the Basic Allowance for Housing for pay grade E-5 with dependents in the locality of attendance, and locality has an enormous range across the country. In an expensive metropolitan area the allowance can exceed the entire Chapter 30 stipend by itself, because Chapter 33 pays a large housing sum on top of tuition while Chapter 30 pays one sum for everything. In a locality where rents are modest, the same formula yields an allowance that may sit well below the Chapter 30 stipend, and the scaling that produced an advantage in the expensive setting produces parity or a deficit in the inexpensive one. The flat stipend is indifferent to postal code, and indifference is worth money at the bottom of the housing distribution and costs money at the top.

The two variables frequently travel together. Inexpensive public institutions are disproportionately located outside the most expensive housing markets, so a student at such an institution often presents Chapter 33 with a small tuition charge and a small locality rate in the same term, and the combined total can fall below the flat stipend even though every component has been paid at the full eligibility percentage. A high tuition charge in a low housing locality splits the variables and produces a closer comparison. Locality must therefore be entered from the published tables for the specific place of attendance, and the combination of charges and locality is the unit of analysis.

### What changes when study is entirely online?

Distance enrollment rewrites the housing component by statute. Before amendment, a student pursuing a program exclusively through distance learning received no housing allowance under Chapter 33 at all. The Post-9/11 Veterans Educational Assistance Improvements Act of 2010, Public Law 111-377, changed that position by adding a distance-learning housing stipend to section 3313 of title 38, set at fifty percent of the amount payable under the national-average formula. A precision on dates belongs here because the point is often stated loosely. Most of section 102 of that act took effect on August 1, 2011, but the paragraph governing the distance-learning stipend stated its own effective date, and that stipend, for pursuit solely through distance learning on a more than half-time basis, took effect on October 1, 2011. The operative rule from that date forward is that exclusively distance enrollment draws half the national average housing amount, prorated by rate of pursuit and by eligibility percentage, regardless of housing costs where the student lives.

That rule removes locality from the calculation and caps the housing component at a national figure that expensive and inexpensive locations share. Neither the student studying online from a high-rent apartment nor the student studying online from a low-rent house receives a figure that responds to rent actually paid. Chapter 30 makes no such distinction. Its stipend has no distance reduction and no separate housing component to be halved, so an online student in a full-time training category receives the same stipend as a resident student in the same category. The comparison for exclusively online study therefore places a halved national housing allowance, plus whatever net tuition payment the charges generate, plus the books stipend, against an unreduced flat stipend, and the flat stipend prevails across a wide range of online tuition charges. Online tuition also tends to sit below the charges of the most expensive resident programs, which brings the low-tuition scenario into play at the same time.

A worked illustration shows the size of the gap, using a complete figure set for the 2018 to 2019 academic year only, stated here to demonstrate the method rather than to supply a rate for any other year. In that academic year, the Chapter 30 full-time monthly rate for a student in the category covering an enlistment of three years or more was 1,994 dollars, effective October 1, 2018. In the same academic year, the Chapter 33 housing allowance for a student attending solely online was 825 dollars per month, being half of a national average of 1,650 dollars, effective August 1, 2018, and the books and supplies stipend was paid at 41.67 dollars per credit hour up to 1,000 dollars across the academic year. Consider a student at the full eligibility percentage, enrolled entirely online for a term carrying twelve credit hours across four payment months, whose tuition and fees have been waived in full by tuition-directed aid, so that the net Chapter 33 tuition charge is zero. Chapter 33 pays nothing to the institution, pays 3,300 dollars in housing across the four months, and pays 500.04 dollars in books stipend for the twelve credit hours of the term, a combined total of 3,800.04 dollars. Chapter 30 pays 7,976 dollars across the same four months. The difference is 4,175.96 dollars in favor of the older program for that term, on those stated assumptions. Change the waiver, and tuition value enters the Chapter 33 side. Change the enrollment to resident study in an expensive locality, and a larger housing figure enters. The illustration is an application of the method to one dated figure set, not a standing answer, and its figures must be replaced with the published figures for whatever academic year a real comparison concerns.

Exclusivity is the condition that triggers the distance rule. Enrollment that mixes distance courses with resident attendance is not enrollment solely through distance learning, and the housing treatment of a mixed schedule follows the resident rules rather than the halved national figure, subject to certification of the courses by the institution. The method therefore begins the online scenario with a classification question, how the enrollment is certified, and treats the answer as a fact about the specific term, since the same student can move between categories from one term to the next.

### What changes at half time and below?

Rate of pursuit draws the sharpest line in the entire comparison, because the two programs treat reduced enrollment in structurally different ways. Chapter 33 conditions its housing allowance on pursuit at more than half time. A student whose rate of pursuit is exactly half time receives no housing allowance for that period, and a student below half time receives none either, even though the tuition and fees component is a separate payment governed by separate rules and does not vanish for the same reason. Above the threshold, the allowance is prorated, so a student pursuing at eighty percent of full time receives eighty percent of the applicable locality or distance figure, after the rounding the statute prescribes, rather than the full amount. The threshold and the proration operate together, one switching the component off at the boundary and the other tapering it above the boundary, and both effects press in the same direction as enrollment falls.

Chapter 30 has categories where Chapter 33 has a gate. Its rates step down by training time, from full time to three-quarter time to half time, and payment continues below half time, where the amount payable is capped at the cost of tuition and fees rather than paid as an unrestricted stipend. That cap is a genuine limitation, because a student below half time at an institution with negligible charges draws very little under either program. The decisive case sits at the boundary itself. At exactly half time, in the 2018 to 2019 figure set used in this section, Chapter 30 paid 997 dollars per month in the three-years-or-more category, while the Chapter 33 housing allowance was zero by operation of the more-than-half-time requirement, leaving the Chapter 33 total dependent entirely on the net tuition payment. A student with waived or minimal tuition at exactly half time therefore draws a substantial monthly payment under the older program and very little under the newer one, while a student carrying a large net tuition charge may draw more under the newer program through the tuition payment alone, which is why the scenario is stated as a housing result rather than as an unconditional verdict.

Reduced enrollment is not an edge case in this population. Students combining study with employment, caregiving, a service-connected disability that limits course load, or a final term with few credits remaining all encounter terms at or below half time, and a choice made on full-time arithmetic governs those terms as well. A complete comparison therefore tests the schedule expected across a degree, including the light terms, where the difference can be decided even when heavy terms favor Chapter 33, because the housing gate operates term by term while the Chapter 30 categories continue to pay, subject to the below-half-time cap described above.

### How does the entry contribution enter the arithmetic?

The 1,200 dollar contribution under Chapter 30, collected through a reduction of 100 dollars per month in basic pay across the first twelve months of service, enters the comparison in two different positions depending on when the comparison is made. For a servicemember who has already paid it in full, the money is spent under either outcome. Electing Chapter 33 does not retrieve it in the ordinary course, and remaining with Chapter 30 does not spend it a second time, so the contribution is a sunk cost that drops out of a comparison between future payment streams. Including it penalizes Chapter 30 for an outlay the student cannot avoid by choosing Chapter 33. For a new entrant who has not paid it, the position reverses. The 1,200 dollars is a real future cost of acquiring the Chapter 30 entitlement, Chapter 33 requires no corresponding contribution, and a comparison at the point of entry must subtract the contribution from the Chapter 30 side or state expressly that the comparison begins after entry. The same dollars are decisive in one comparison and irrelevant in the other, and timing determines which treatment is correct.

Two qualifications keep that treatment honest. The contribution interacts with refund rules in defined circumstances, including circumstances in which a person who elects Chapter 33 and exhausts that entitlement may have the contribution returned, and a specific comparison should test whether any such circumstance applies. The optional additional contribution under the buy-up arrangement is a separate outlay with separate effects on the Chapter 30 rate and no effect on Chapter 33. Neither qualification disturbs the central point: a cost already incurred and not recoverable through the choice under examination cannot rationally decide that choice.

### Why does an answer calculated in one year fail in another?

The limits of the method are set by the instability of its inputs. Tuition charges change by institution and by year. Housing figures change when the underlying military housing rates change, and Chapter 33 payments are reset at the start of the academic year on August 1, while Chapter 30 rates are adjusted at the start of the federal fiscal year on October 1, so the two sides do not even renew on the same date and a comparison assembled across that seam mixes two vintages of figures. Eligibility percentages, rules on distance learning and pursuit thresholds, and the other aid a student receives can each change between one term and the next. A numerical answer assembled from one set of inputs describes that set and no other, which is why this article teaches the arithmetic and gives no standing dollar answer to the question of which program pays more.

The sources for the inputs are official and specific, named here in prose and never linked, so that a comparison can be rebuilt rather than repeated. The Department of Veterans Affairs published rate tables give the Chapter 30 rates by training-time category and the Chapter 33 figures for tuition caps, housing, and the books stipend for a stated academic year. The GI Bill Comparison Tool gives institution-level figures combining charges and locality for a named school. Neither source answers the comparison by itself, because neither knows the other aid a student holds, the schedule a student expects to carry, or whether a contribution has already been paid. Those facts come from the individual case, and the method exists to combine them with the published figures in the right order. Where the inputs place a student in one of the four settings examined in this section, low or waived net tuition, a low housing locality, exclusively distance enrollment, or pursuit at or below the housing threshold, the flat stipend of the older program can exceed the combined and conditional payments of the newer one, and a result that appears paradoxical in a headline comparison resolves into ordinary arithmetic once each component is entered at the value the enrollment actually generates.

## Using Both Programs: When a Choice Is Not Always a Choice

The comparison between the Montgomery GI Bill and the Post-9/11 GI Bill presents itself, in most accounts, as a fork in a road: a veteran qualifies for one program or for the other, signs a form, and travels in a single direction. That picture fails for a veteran whose service and study do not fit inside a single entitlement. Federal law built both programs with separate entitlement accounts, measured in months, and placed a combined ceiling over the whole structure. A veteran could draw on one account and then on the other, provided the combined total stayed under the ceiling and no single month of training was paid for twice. Whether that sequential use was a right the statute conferred or a possibility the Department of Veterans Affairs could close off through the election turned on a question that, at the vantage of July 1, 2019, no court had resolved, a question whose answer was measured in months of paid education.

### Can a veteran use both GI Bills for the same course of study?

Sequential use was permitted within a single course of study, but concurrent payment was barred. A veteran could finish one program and continue under the other, subject to the combined ceiling, yet no month of training could be paid under both programs at once.

Section 3322(a) of title 38, the bar to duplication provision for the Post-9/11 program, addressed the simultaneous case in direct terms. An individual entitled to educational assistance under chapter 33 who was also eligible for assistance under chapter 30, chapter 31, chapter 32, or chapter 35 of the same title, or under the reserve and related programs in title 10, could not receive assistance under two or more such programs concurrently, and was required instead to elect under which chapter or provision assistance would be received. The operative word was concurrently: the provision regulated which account paid for a given period of training, and did not, on its face, state that entitlement under a second account ceased to exist because the first had been elected for an earlier period. Section 3322(c) required Selected Reserve service to be credited under only one of the programs that could claim it. Section 3322(h), added by section 111 of Public Law 111-377 in 2011, barred duplication of eligibility based on a single event or period of service for service beginning on or after August 1, 2011. None of those provisions, read alone, answered the sequential question of what happened when two separate foundations existed and the training periods did not overlap.

### The Combined Ceiling on Assistance Under Two or More Programs

The provision that made sequential use arithmetically possible was section 3695(a) of title 38, captioned as a limitation on the period of assistance under two or more programs. The text provided that the aggregate period for which any person could receive assistance under two or more of the listed provisions of law could not exceed 48 months, or the part-time equivalent of 48 months. The listed provisions swept broadly, including the educational assistance chapters numbered 30, 32, 33, 34, and 36 of title 38 and the reserve education chapters of title 10. The implementing regulation at section 21.4020 of title 38 of the Code of Federal Regulations carried the same combined limit into adjudication. Congress did not write that a person could receive assistance under only one program. Congress wrote a ceiling for assistance received under two or more programs, at a figure higher than any single program supplied, and a ceiling of that kind presupposes accumulation: its function is to stop accumulation at a defined point rather than to forbid accumulation altogether.

The arithmetic was simple, and its simplicity explains why the dispute concentrated on entitlement rather than on calculation. Each program supplied a maximum of 36 months of educational assistance: the Montgomery program under chapter 30, a limit reflected in section 21.7072 of the regulations, and the Post-9/11 program under chapter 33, stated in section 21.9550 subject to the combined limit in section 21.4020. Two full entitlements produced a theoretical total of 72 months, and section 3695(a) cut that total to 48 months, so the second program, in the most favorable case, added 12 months beyond a fully used first entitlement. Months already paid counted toward the combined ceiling, because the ceiling measured assistance received rather than programs elected. A veteran who exhausted 36 months under the Montgomery program and then qualified under the Post-9/11 program stood to receive 12 additional months, because 48 minus 36 leaves 12. The 48 month figure formed part of [the longer sequence of veterans education legislation](/2019/07/15/us-veterans-legislation-history/) within which each new program had been layered over its predecessors.

### How the Department Sequenced the Two Programs

The Department administered combined use as a sequence governed by the state of the first entitlement at the moment the second program was claimed, and two paths predominated. Under the first path, a veteran continued under the original program until that entitlement was exhausted, and only then applied for the newer program. Adjudication practice, reflected in Board of Veterans' Appeals decisions and in guidance reproduced for claimants over many years, gave exhaustion a defined consequence: a veteran who had used the whole of a Montgomery entitlement before electing Post-9/11 assistance could receive an additional period under the second program, bounded by the 48 month aggregate ceiling, which in the standard case meant 12 additional months. Under the second path, a veteran elected the newer program while entitlement under the older program remained unused, and the Department treated the resulting entitlement as limited to the months that remained under the older program at the moment of election. A veteran who converted with 20 months unused received 20 months under the new program, not a fresh 36 months.

Board decisions illustrated the conversion path in unforgiving detail, and the governing sources stated the same sequencing rule in general terms. The election regulation at section 21.9520(c) of title 38 of the Code of Federal Regulations, adopted in 2009, provided that eligibility under chapter 33 could be established through an irrevocable election relinquishing eligibility under chapter 30, and section 21.4020 carried the 48 month ceiling. The Congressional Research Service primer on the Post-9/11 GI Bill described the consequence in matching terms: conversion yielded only the time remaining under the first program, whereas continued use until exhaustion, followed by an application under the second program, appeared to yield an additional 12 months. The mechanics of making the election itself are treated in the section of this article devoted to the election; what matters here is the consequence the Department attached to electing, namely relinquishment of the source entitlement, conversion measured by its residue, and foreclosure of a return to the relinquished program.

### One Period of Service, One Entitlement Stream

For a veteran whose eligibility under both programs rested on a single period of service, the election requirement stood on firm statutory ground. The coordination provision incorporated by section 3322(d) of title 38 explained why. Section 3322(d) provided that, for an individual entitled to assistance under chapter 30 or one of the other listed programs as of August 1, 2009, coordination of entitlement between the Post-9/11 program and those programs was governed by section 5003(c) of the Post-9/11 Veterans Educational Assistance Act of 2008, Public Law 110-252. Section 5003(c) was the transition provision Congress wrote for the population that held, or was contributing toward, an older entitlement on the day the newer program took effect. It measured the entitlement that followed an election by reference to the entitlement being given up, and, in subsection (c)(8), as the Department explained in the rulemaking published in the Federal Register on March 31, 2009, made such elections irrevocable. The regulation at section 21.9520(c) implemented that architecture by specifying how an irrevocable election could be made.

The single-period bar added in 2011 pointed in the same direction for a later cohort. Section 3322(h) rested on the premise that one period of service could not be made to do the work of two eligibility foundations. Where a single period generated both entitlements, the two entitlements were two measures of value attached to the same service, and an election between them was an election between alternative valuations of one qualifying act. The Congressional Research Service primer on the Post-9/11 program described the resulting rule in matching terms: an individual eligible for both programs based on a single qualifying period was required to make an irrevocable election giving up one program to receive the other, and where the election followed partial use of the first program, the entitlement under the newer program equaled the months that remained under the first. That account, the regulatory text, and repeated Board decisions applying the rule to single-period veterans formed a settled administrative picture, settled only within the boundary the premise described.

### The Two-Periods Argument

The open question began where the single-period premise ended. A substantial class of veterans held eligibility that rested on two separate and distinct periods of qualifying service. A service member enlisted, completed a period of active service that qualified for the Montgomery program, contributed from pay toward that entitlement, separated, and used part of the entitlement for undergraduate study. The same person later returned to service, through a commission earned after graduation or a further period of service of another kind, and completed service that qualified for the Post-9/11 program on its own terms. Under a period based reading of the statutes, the second entitlement was not a second valuation of the first period but the product of new service, performed after the first entitlement had been earned and after an interval in civilian life. The question was whether the election machinery written for transition and duplication reached across that interval.

The argument that it did not rested on three mutually reinforcing readings of the text. The first returned to section 3695(a). Congress had imposed a single express ceiling on combined use, set at 48 months, in a provision whose very subject was assistance under two or more programs. If Congress had intended a veteran with two qualifying periods to receive no more than 36 months in total, a 36 month combined ceiling for that class would have made the 48 month figure redundant for the veterans most likely to hold two full entitlements. On this reading, the 48 month cap was the only statutory ceiling Congress imposed, and administrative practice that produced a 36 month total added a second ceiling the text did not contain. The second reading concerned section 3322(a): the election that subsection required was an election of which program would pay, framed for a person who might not receive assistance under two or more programs concurrently. A choice of payer for a period of training differed, in ordinary statutory language, from a relinquishment of an independent entitlement for all future training, and proponents maintained that the Department had expanded a concurrency rule into a forfeiture rule. The third reading concerned section 3322(d). Section 5003(c) had been written, in this account, for duplicated eligibility held as of August 1, 2009, for the transition problem of a person who held an older entitlement, or was contributing toward one, when the newer program opened. The election provisions thus formed, in the claimant's account, anti-duplication rules aimed at preventing one unit of service or one period of training from being counted twice, not at preventing two units of service from each being counted once.

### The Department's Position

The Department's position, administered through its regulations, its adjudication guidance, and Board decisions applying both, was that an election was required whenever a veteran eligible under an older program sought benefits under the Post-9/11 program, and that the election was irrevocable regardless of the number of periods of service on which eligibility rested. Section 21.9520 of title 38 of the Code of Federal Regulations conditioned establishment of eligibility under chapter 33, for an individual who held eligibility under chapter 30 or one of the listed reserve programs, on an irrevocable election to receive benefits under chapter 33 in lieu of the other program. The phrase in lieu of did substantial work in that reading, because it described substitution rather than supplementation: the new entitlement replaced the old one, measured by what remained of the old one, instead of standing beside it. The Department also read section 3695(a) as a ceiling and nothing more, a provision that limited combined use where combined use was otherwise authorized without itself authorizing combined use, and a ceiling, in that construction, could not be converted into a grant.

### An Army Claim Carrying the Question

The dispute acquired a concrete vehicle in the claim of James Rudisill, an Army veteran whose service fell into two such periods, an enlisted period that supported Montgomery eligibility and a later commissioned period that supported Post-9/11 eligibility, and who had used part of the Montgomery entitlement before seeking Post-9/11 assistance for further study. The Department applied its administered rule, measuring the Post-9/11 entitlement by the Montgomery entitlement that remained rather than by the combined allowance that section 3695(a) described. Rudisill pressed the two-periods argument: that each period had earned its own entitlement, that the election requirement addressed duplicated eligibility rather than distinct periods, and that the aggregate cap fixed the outer limit of what the two entitlements together could deliver. The claim moved into the veterans appeals system, the channel through which benefits denials passed from the agency of original jurisdiction to the Board of Veterans' Appeals and onward to the United States Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims, and at the vantage of July 1, 2019, the question the claim presented had received no resolution from a court.

### What Turns on the Answer

The stakes can be stated in months, and months of entitlement translate directly into degrees completed or abandoned. Under the Department's reading, a two-period veteran who elected the newer program with part of the older entitlement spent received a combined total capped in effect at 36 months, because the new entitlement merely continued the old one under a new payment structure. Under the two-periods reading, the same veteran received the entitlement earned under each program, delivered sequentially and bounded by the 48 month aggregate ceiling, which in the standard case added 12 months beyond a single program's allowance. Twelve months represented a full academic year of tuition payments, housing allowance, and books support under the newer program, and for a graduate or professional program whose length exceeded 36 months the additional year frequently determined whether the final year was funded by the benefit the veteran had earned or by borrowing and savings.

The second stake concerned finality. If the Department's reading was correct, an election signed years earlier, at the start of an undergraduate program or on the advice that the newer program was simply better, foreclosed the second entitlement stream permanently, and no later recognition that the veteran had served two periods could revive what the election had relinquished. Elections made without knowledge of the conversion consequence, including elections that reduced a claimant to a residue measured in days, stood on the same footing as elections made with full information, because the regulations treated a properly submitted election as effective according to its terms. If the two-periods reading was correct, an election that the statute never required for that class of veteran would arguably have relinquished nothing, and claimants who had already elected would hold a claim to recalculation rather than a request for an exception. Intermediate readings remained possible as well, distinguishing veterans by whether the older entitlement had been exhausted or by whether one period alone could support both entitlements without any month of service being counted twice.

Under the law as the Department administered it, a veteran used both programs sequentially, never concurrently, with the second entitlement measured by the residue of the first where an election intervened, with exhaustion of the first program offering the route to an additional 12 months, and with the whole bounded by the 48 month ceiling of section 3695(a). Under the law as two-period claimants contended Congress wrote it, a veteran whose entitlements rested on separate periods of service held two streams rather than one, deliverable in sequence to the same ceiling, and no election was required that would collapse the two streams into the residue of whichever was elected first. For the majority of veterans with a single qualifying period, the comparison remained a choice, and an irrevocable one. For the veteran with two periods, the description of the comparison as a choice was precisely what stood in dispute.

## Montgomery vs Post-9/11 GI Bill: The Verdict and the Deciding Factor

The verdict of the Montgomery vs Post-9/11 GI Bill comparison can be stated in one sentence, and the remainder of this section defends it. The Post-9/11 program pays more in the majority of degree cases that carry substantial tuition charges or substantial housing costs, the Montgomery program pays more in a defined and recurring minority of cases built on low charges, modest housing costs, distance delivery, or reduced enrollment, and neither statement can be converted into a ranking until two quantities in the individual case are known. The deciding factor is the relationship between the net tuition the study actually generates and the housing allowance the enrollment pattern actually generates. Every other axis modifies that relationship or limits the time and the persons to which it can apply. No other single factor decides the comparison, because no other factor moves both sides of the arithmetic at once.

Net tuition means the charge that survives the last payer rule. A catalogue price, a published rate for a different residency status, and a charge that a waiver or a tuition designated scholarship has already extinguished each describe a bill the Department of Veterans Affairs will never be asked to pay. Where the surviving charge is large, the Post-9/11 tuition payment is large in direct proportion, subject to the public institution rule, the private and foreign institution maximum, and the eligibility percentage. A private institution charge near the annual maximum delivers tuition value across an academic year that the flat Montgomery stipend cannot match from its single stream, because the stipend must also carry rent, food, transport, and books out of the same dollars. A public institution charge at the resident rate delivers less tuition value, and a waived charge delivers none. The tuition term therefore ranges from the largest component in the entire comparison to zero, and its position in that range is a fact about the institution and the aid package rather than a fact about the veteran.

The housing allowance means the amount the enrollment pattern generates after every gate has been applied. The locality rate for the ZIP code of the institution enters first, and that rate enters at its full value only for a student at the full eligibility percentage, enrolled in person, and pursuing training above half time at a full rate of pursuit. Each qualification subtracts. A lower eligibility percentage multiplies the rate down. A rate of pursuit below full time prorates the rate down. Enrollment at half time or below switches the component off. Enrollment conducted exclusively through distance learning replaces the locality rate with half of the national average. Service on active duty removes the component for the servicemember and for a spouse using transferred entitlement. A high rent metropolitan locality at full time in person enrollment produces a housing figure that can exceed the whole Montgomery stipend before tuition enters the calculation at all. A modest rent locality, a distance schedule, or a light course load produces a housing figure that contributes a fraction of that amount or nothing. The housing term, like the tuition term, ranges from decisive to absent on facts that have nothing to do with the length or character of the service performed.

The verdict follows from adding the two terms honestly. Where net tuition is high, the tuition payment alone can carry the Post-9/11 total past the Montgomery total, and a substantial housing allowance widens the margin further. Where locality housing rates are high, the housing allowance alone can carry the total past the stipend even when tuition is moderate, because the newer program pays that allowance on top of the tuition payment while the older program pays one sum against every cost. Where both terms are high, the comparison is not close, and defending the older program in such a case requires ignoring one of the two largest payments in federal education assistance. The newer program dominates that territory completely, and the dominance grows with charges and with rents rather than shrinking, because scaling is the design.

Where net tuition is low or waived and housing rates are modest, the arithmetic reverses through the same mechanism. A tuition payment calculated from a small surviving charge is a small payment, however generous the percentage applied to it sounds. A housing allowance calculated from a modest locality rate is a modest payment. Added together, with the books stipend, the two conditional payments can fall below a flat stipend that was calculated without reference to either figure and that arrives in full for the training time category. Study conducted entirely online strengthens the reversal, because the housing term is capped at half of the national average while the stipend carries no distance reduction of any kind. Enrollment at exactly half time strengthens the reversal further, because the housing term disappears while the Montgomery category for half time training pays half of the full time rate. The older program wins that territory not through a hidden generosity but through indifference: a payment that ignores prices cannot be reduced by low prices.

Between the two territories lies a band in which the answer turns on figures that must be taken from published tables for the specific academic year, institution, and locality. Graduate study at a public institution with moderate resident charges and moderate rents can fall inside that band. Part time study at a private institution can fall inside that band, because a large tuition payment and an absent housing payment pull in opposite directions. Mixed distance and resident enrollment can fall inside that band in one term and outside it in the next. The existence of the band is the reason the comparison teaches a method rather than a slogan. A verdict that named a winner without naming the tuition figure and the housing figure would be a verdict about an average student who attends no institution, lives in no locality, and follows no schedule.

Two qualifications complete the verdict and neither weakens it. The first concerns the frame itself. For the majority of veterans, whose eligibility rests on a single period of service, the comparison is a choice and the election is generally irrevocable, so the calculation must be done before electing, with the figures for the intended school and the intended schedule assembled in advance. For veterans with two separate qualifying periods of service, the binary framing itself was contested and unresolved at the date of this article, because the claim that each period earned an independent entitlement, deliverable in sequence up to the 48 month aggregate cap, had been pressed in the veterans appeals system and no court had decided it. Presenting the verdict as a universal fork in a road would misstate the law for that class, and the verdict therefore governs the single period case in which an election is required, while the two period case turns on the unresolved question treated in the preceding section.

The second qualification concerns the status of the verdict. The verdict states the logic of the comparison: given a net tuition figure and a housing figure, the structure of the two programs determines which total is larger, and that determination is arithmetic rather than opinion. The verdict does not advise any individual to elect either program, because an individual answer also depends on transfer plans, on the delimiting period attached to a separation date, on entitlement already used, on a contribution already paid or a buy up already forfeited, and on facts about future enrollment that a general comparison cannot supply. A reader whose case falls in the high tuition or high housing territory holds a strong structural answer and a short calculation. A reader whose case falls in the low tuition, modest housing, distance, or half time territory holds an equally strong structural answer in the opposite direction. A reader in the band between them holds an instruction rather than an answer: obtain the net charge, obtain the locality or distance housing figure, apply the eligibility percentage and the rate of pursuit, and compare the resulting total against the stipend for the applicable category across the whole course of study, including the light terms. The comparison rewards that work because the signature that follows it cannot be recalled.

## Matching the Montgomery and Post-9/11 Programs to the Reader

The comparison developed in the preceding sections reduces to a small set of variables: the amount a school charges, the place a student studies, the manner of attendance, the length of the window for use, and the presence or absence of a dependent in the plan. No single ranking survives every combination of those variables, because the Montgomery program pays a flat monthly amount to the veteran regardless of tuition or locality, while the Post-9/11 program pays tuition and fees to the school, adds a locality based housing allowance, and scales both payments with the eligibility percentage earned through qualifying service after September 10, 2001. A recommendation therefore has to begin with the position of the person asking. A servicemember approaching separation controls the election itself. A veteran whose election lies years behind controls the school, the course load, and the timing of enrollment, but not the program. A spouse or child controls almost nothing about eligibility, because eligibility for a dependent exists only when a servicemember created that eligibility during service. A student or researcher controls only the order in which the texts and the evidence get read.

### The Separating Servicemember at the Point of Election

The separating servicemember faces the only version of the decision that includes the election itself, and the election between the two programs is generally irrevocable. Under title 38, an individual with a single qualifying period of service who elects Post-9/11 benefits in place of Montgomery benefits gives up the Montgomery entitlement through that election, and the Department of Veterans Affairs treats a processed election as final. A signature given before the arithmetic has been assembled therefore closes the alternative permanently, apart from narrow situations in which an election was never validly made at all. Gathering the inputs belongs before any application form, because the form converts an estimate into a settled legal position.

The first input is the net tuition and fees the intended school will actually charge after other aid has been applied. A catalogue price answers the wrong question. The Post-9/11 program pays tuition and fees to the institution, and aid designated for tuition and fees, such as a waiver or a restricted scholarship, reduces the charge the program is asked to meet, while the Montgomery program pays the same flat monthly amount to the veteran irrespective of the charge. At a public institution charging a modest resident rate, or at an institution waiving much of the charge for another reason, the tuition payment under the Post-9/11 program may be small, and the flat Montgomery payment, which the veteran directs toward any expense, may deliver more usable money across a term. At an institution charging a high rate, the direct tuition payment absorbs a cost the flat Montgomery amount was never sized to cover, and the balance shifts decisively toward the Post-9/11 program. The eligibility percentage belongs inside the same calculation, since a veteran entitled to less than the full percentage receives that percentage of the tuition payment and of the housing allowance, narrowing the advantage the newer program holds at an expensive school.

The second input is the housing allowance the intended locality and enrollment pattern will actually generate. For in person study, the Post-9/11 housing allowance follows the military Basic Allowance for Housing for a service member in pay grade E-5 with dependents in the locality of the school, prorated by the eligibility percentage and by the rate of pursuit. Enrollment at half time or below produces no housing allowance at all, and enrollment modestly above half time produces a prorated allowance rather than the full locality figure. The Montgomery program contains no housing component in any locality, so the entire housing figure counts as a differential in favor of the Post-9/11 program whenever the figure is real. In a high cost locality with full time in person enrollment, the housing allowance alone can exceed the whole Montgomery payment. In a low cost locality, or under a light course load that suppresses the allowance, the differential contracts and the flat payment regains ground.

The third input is whether study will be in person or at a distance, because the delivery mode rewrites the housing term. A student enrolled exclusively in distance courses receives a housing allowance set at half the national average allowance rather than the locality rate, under a rule dating from the Post-9/11 Veterans Education Assistance Improvements Act of 2010. The reduction removes the geographic premium entirely, and wholly online study from a low cost residence produces the weakest version of the Post-9/11 package. Against that package, the Montgomery flat amount, which does not depend on locality and does not fall because courses arrive through a network connection, wins in a recognizable set of cases. Mixed enrollment changes the result again, since in person attendance anchors the housing calculation to the locality rate. A separating servicemember with a settled school, a settled locality, and a settled delivery mode holds all three inputs and can price both programs term by term. A separating servicemember guessing at any one of the three is pricing a program for a student who may never exist.

### The Veteran Whose Election Lies in the Past

For the veteran who elected years earlier, the election settled the program, the conversion of entitlement, and the refund contingency, and none of those items reopens with a change of school or a change of mind. An election of Post-9/11 benefits made after partial use of Montgomery benefits converted the remainder rather than creating a fresh entitlement: the Post-9/11 entitlement began as the months of Montgomery entitlement remaining at the election, subject to the aggregate ceiling on combined educational assistance in section 3695 of title 38. A veteran who exhausted Montgomery entitlement before electing could receive additional Post-9/11 entitlement within that aggregate ceiling, while a veteran who elected early locked in the remaining months as the measure of the new entitlement. The distinction was fixed at the moment of election and explains why two veterans with identical service can hold different balances years later.

The contribution and refund treatment settled at that same moment follows the proportional refund rule stated in full in the election section of this article, including its exhaustion condition and its exclusion of the buy up contribution, and that cross-reference governs here without a second statement of the mechanics.

What remains open for such a veteran is the use of the settled entitlement rather than the identity of the entitlement. The delimiting dates continue to govern: Montgomery entitlement generally runs for ten years after separation, Post-9/11 entitlement generally ran for fifteen years after separation, and the Harry W. Colmery Veterans Educational Assistance Act of 2017 removed the time limit for individuals whose last separation occurred on or after January 1, 2013. School selection, delivery mode, and rate of pursuit remain adjustable term by term, and each adjustment reprices the housing term under the rules described for the separating servicemember. Transfer decisions under the Post-9/11 program also remain partially adjustable, in a limited sense: a veteran who designated dependents during service may modify or revoke the number of months assigned among those designated dependents after separation, but designation of an additional dependent after separation is not available. The open questions are therefore questions of allocation and timing inside a fixed frame.

### The Spouse or Child Whose Benefit Depends on a Transfer

The dependent begins from a different statute in practical effect, because only one of the two programs reaches a spouse or child at all. The Montgomery program is personal to the veteran and permits no transfer to any family member under any service history. The Post-9/11 program permits transfer of unused entitlement to a spouse, to one or more children, or to a combination of spouse and children, but the transfer is a retention instrument administered by the Department of Defense, and every condition attaches to the servicemember rather than to the dependent. The conditions for creating a transfer, including the service thresholds, the additional service commitment, the requirement that the election be made during service, and enrollment of the dependent in the Defense Enrollment Eligibility Reporting System, are stated in full in the transferability section of this article and govern here without restatement. Separation ends the opportunity to create a transfer, so the dependent question is answered inside the member service window or not answered at all.

The conditions governing use differ between a spouse and a child in ways that shape family planning. A spouse may begin using transferred entitlement immediately, during the member service or after separation, but a spouse receives no monthly housing allowance while the member serves on active duty, since the housing allowance would duplicate the housing support attached to the member service. The spouse eligibility period tracks the member separation date, running fifteen years after separation when separation occurred before January 1, 2013, and carrying no time limit when separation occurred on or after that date. A child faces a later starting gate and an age based closing gate: transferred entitlement becomes usable only after the member has completed at least ten years of service, only after the child has earned a high school diploma or an equivalent credential or has reached age eighteen, and only before the child reaches age twenty six. A child may receive the monthly housing allowance even while the member serves on active duty, a contrast explained by the absence of a duplicate housing payment for a child studying away from the household. The member retains authority over the allocation throughout, with power to modify or revoke months assigned to a dependent, so transferred entitlement remains a family resource held under a single signature rather than a vested interest of the dependent.

Importing the full record into a decision article would blur the distinction between a population finding and an individual calculation. The third stage is the decision structure itself, the comparison of payment mechanics, transferability, time limits, and delivery mode effects set out in the preceding sections. Read in that order, the statutes explain the machinery, the evidence disciplines expectations about results, and the comparison converts machinery and evidence into a method for resolving an individual case. Read in reverse order, a verdict arrives before the terms of the verdict have acquired meaning.

The four reader positions converge on the same closing observation. The programs reward different study patterns: the Montgomery program rewards low charges, low housing costs, and distance delivery, because a flat payment retains full value under exactly those conditions, while the Post-9/11 program rewards high charges, high housing costs, and in person attendance, because every component of the newer package scales with price and place. The reader types differ mainly in which inputs each position controls. The separating servicemember controls the election and therefore bears the obligation to price the inputs before signing. The veteran controls enrollment decisions inside a settled entitlement. The dependent controls enrollment decisions inside an allocation created by another person during a closed service window. The researcher controls only the sequence of reading. In each position the decision returns to that arithmetic, assembled honestly from net charges, locality, delivery mode, and time.

## Closing Assessment

The comparison between the two programs is, in the end, a comparison between two theories of what service earns. The Montgomery program treats education assistance as a sum fixed at enlistment, purchased in part by the servicemember, quoted in advance, and paid without regard to the market in which the education is later bought. The Post-9/11 program treats education assistance as a share of the cost of attendance, calculated from charges an institution sets and rents a locality commands, and paid largely to the institution that sets the charges. Neither theory is a defective version of the other. Each theory produces a characteristic strength and a characteristic failure, and the failures are mirror images: the fixed sum fails where costs are high, and the cost based benefit fails where costs are low, waived, or disconnected from place by distance delivery.

That symmetry explains why the comparison has survived a decade of general statements that the newer program is simply the better program. The general statement describes the territory in which most expensive degree study occurs, and within that territory the statement holds. The statement fails as a rule of decision because the population governed by the election includes community college students with waived charges, online students in low cost housing, apprentices, part time students combining study with employment, and veterans whose intended beneficiary is a spouse or a child. For those readers the operative questions concern the size of a surviving charge, the size of a generated housing allowance, the existence of a transfer, and the date on a separation document. The statute answers each question precisely, and the precision is available before the election rather than after it.

The durable guidance of the comparison is therefore procedural. Assemble the inputs in the order the statute applies them. Establish eligibility and the eligibility percentage. Establish the months of entitlement held and the months an election would convert. Price the net tuition at the intended institution after tuition designated aid. Price the housing allowance under the intended delivery mode, locality, and rate of pursuit. Test the transfer question and the delimiting question before valuing months that may reach no student or survive to no start date. Only after that sequence does a total exist that can be compared against the flat stipend, and only a total built in that sequence deserves to govern an irrevocable signature. A comparison performed in any other order produces confidence without a figure, and confidence without a figure is the one outcome the design of the election punishes most severely.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the difference between the Montgomery GI Bill and Post-9/11 benefits?

The Montgomery GI Bill pays the veteran a single flat monthly stipend, set by service category and training time, and the veteran pays tuition and every other cost from that payment. The Post-9/11 GI Bill divides the benefit into separate streams. Tuition and fees are paid directly to the institution at the net charge after tuition designated aid, a housing allowance based on military housing rates for the locality is paid to the veteran, and a books and supplies stipend is paid by credit hour. The Montgomery program required a contribution of 1,200 dollars during the first year of service. The Post-9/11 program requires no contribution and scales every payment by an eligibility percentage based on length of qualifying service.

### Q: Which pays more, the Montgomery GI Bill or Post-9/11?

Neither program pays more in every case, because the payments are built from different quantities. The Post-9/11 total combines a tuition payment calculated from net charges, a housing allowance calculated from locality rates, and a books stipend. The Montgomery total is a flat stipend that does not vary with charges or locality. Where net tuition is high or locality housing rates are high, the Post-9/11 total is larger, often by a wide margin. Where net tuition is low or waived, housing rates are modest, study is entirely online, or enrollment is at half time, the Montgomery stipend can be larger. The deciding factor is the relationship between the net tuition the study generates and the housing allowance the enrollment pattern generates.

### Q: Can the Montgomery GI Bill ever beat Post-9/11?

The Montgomery GI Bill pays more in four recurring settings. At an institution where tuition is waived or covered by tuition designated aid, the Post-9/11 tuition payment falls toward zero while the flat stipend arrives in full. In a locality with modest housing costs, the Post-9/11 housing allowance can sit well below the stipend. In study conducted entirely online, the housing allowance is capped at half of the national average while the stipend carries no distance reduction. At exactly half time enrollment, the housing allowance is not paid at all while the Montgomery half time rate continues. Each setting removes or shrinks a conditional Post-9/11 component that the flat payment never depended on.

### Q: Is switching from the Montgomery GI Bill to Post-9/11 irrevocable?

An election to receive Post-9/11 benefits in place of Montgomery benefits is irrevocable under section 3327 of title 38, and the regulation governing the election requires the individual to acknowledge that irrevocability as part of the act of electing. The entitlement received is measured by the Montgomery entitlement remaining on the date of election where part of that entitlement was used. Narrow qualifications exist at the margins. An election the Secretary of Veterans Affairs makes on behalf of an individual can be modified or revoked within a 30 day period after notice, and an election that was never validly completed can fail for want of the required acknowledgment. A completed election by the individual cannot be undone for regret or for a change of school.

### Q: Can you use both the Montgomery GI Bill and Post-9/11?

The two programs cannot pay for the same period of training at the same time, because section 3322 of title 38 bars concurrent receipt and requires an election of which program pays. Sequential use has a different structure. Section 3695 caps assistance under two or more programs at 48 months in the aggregate, so a veteran who exhausted 36 months under one program could receive up to 12 additional months under another where entitlement under the second program existed. A veteran who elected Post-9/11 benefits before exhausting Montgomery entitlement received a conversion measured by the months remaining rather than a fresh entitlement. For veterans with two separate qualifying periods of service, whether an election could collapse two entitlements into one residue was contested and unresolved at the date of this article.

### Q: What did Rudisill decide about the Montgomery GI Bill?

At the date of this article, no court had decided the claim associated with James Rudisill, and the question the claim presented had received no judicial resolution. Rudisill, an Army veteran, held Montgomery eligibility from one period of service and Post-9/11 eligibility from a separate later period, and had used part of the Montgomery entitlement before seeking Post-9/11 assistance. The Department of Veterans Affairs administered an election rule under which the Post-9/11 entitlement was measured by the Montgomery entitlement that remained. Rudisill pressed the argument that each period of service earned an independent entitlement, deliverable in sequence up to the 48 month aggregate cap in section 3695 of title 38. The claim was moving through the veterans appeals system, and the answer determined whether the combined total reached 48 months or remained confined in effect to 36 months.

### Q: Does online study change the Montgomery GI Bill comparison?

Online study changes the comparison by rewriting one Post-9/11 component and leaving the Montgomery payment untouched. A student pursuing a program exclusively through distance learning receives a housing allowance set at half of the national average of the military housing rate, prorated by rate of pursuit and eligibility percentage, under a rule that took effect on October 1, 2011. Locality disappears from the calculation, so a student in expensive housing and a student in inexpensive housing receive the same housing figure. The Montgomery stipend has no distance reduction and no separate housing component, so an online student in a full time category receives the same stipend as a resident student in that category. Entirely online study therefore narrows or reverses the Post-9/11 advantage across a wide range of tuition charges.

### Q: Which should a student pick, the Montgomery GI Bill or Post-9/11?

The comparison supplies a method rather than a recommendation for any individual student, because the correct total depends on facts only the individual case contains. The calculation begins with net tuition and fees at the intended institution after tuition designated aid, adds the housing allowance the intended locality, delivery mode, and rate of pursuit will generate, applies the eligibility percentage, and compares the result against the flat Montgomery stipend for the applicable training time category across the whole course of study. Transfer plans, the delimiting period attached to the separation date, entitlement already used, and the treatment of the 1,200 dollar contribution and any buy up contribution belong in the same calculation. Because the election is generally irrevocable, the calculation belongs before the signature rather than after it.

### Q: How does the Post-9/11 housing allowance change the comparison in a high rent city?

In a high rent city, the housing allowance is calculated from the military Basic Allowance for Housing for a service member at pay grade E-5 with dependents in the ZIP code of the institution, and that locality figure can be large enough to exceed the entire Montgomery monthly stipend by itself. The allowance is paid on top of the tuition payment to the institution and the books stipend, while the Montgomery stipend is a single sum from which tuition, rent, and every other cost must be met. The allowance is prorated by rate of pursuit, multiplied by the eligibility percentage, and paid only when enrollment is above half time. A full time student in person in an expensive locality therefore holds the strongest version of the Post-9/11 package, and the margin over the flat stipend grows with the locality rate.

### Q: Does the Montgomery GI Bill pay more at a school that charges no tuition?

At a school that charges no tuition, or where a waiver or tuition designated scholarship has reduced the net charge to zero, the Post-9/11 tuition payment is zero because that payment is calculated from the net charge the institution presents. The Post-9/11 total then consists of the housing allowance and the books stipend alone. The Montgomery stipend is not calculated from charges and does not shrink when another source pays the tuition bill, so the veteran receives the full amount for the training time category and can apply the whole payment toward living costs, books, transport, and any charges the waiver leaves behind. Whether the stipend exceeds the housing and books total depends on locality, delivery mode, and rate of pursuit, and in modest housing settings or online study the stipend holds the advantage in that configuration.

### Q: How does the 48 month cap limit a veteran who qualifies for both programs?

Section 3695 of title 38 provides that the aggregate period for which a person can receive assistance under two or more federal education programs cannot exceed 48 months, or the part time equivalent. Each of the two GI Bill programs supplies a maximum of 36 months, so two full entitlements would total 72 months without the cap. The cap cuts the combined total to 48 months, which means the second program adds at most 12 months beyond a fully used first entitlement. Months already paid count toward the ceiling because the ceiling measures assistance received. A veteran who used 20 months under one program and qualified under the other therefore held combined room of 28 further months at most, subject to the entitlement rules of the second program and to the election and conversion rules that govern a change of program.

### Q: How is unused Montgomery entitlement converted when a veteran elects Post-9/11 benefits?

Conversion under section 3327 of title 38 is month for month. A veteran who had used part of the Montgomery entitlement before electing receives Post-9/11 entitlement limited to the months of Montgomery entitlement that remained unused on the date of the election, plus any months of transferred entitlement revoked so that those months return to the individual. A veteran with 20 months remaining receives 20 months under the Post-9/11 program rather than a renewed 36 months. A veteran who had used no Montgomery entitlement receives Post-9/11 entitlement under the terms of Chapter 33 itself, subject to the aggregate limits on combined use including the 48 month cap. Months of transferred entitlement and the proportional refund calculation inherit the same count taken on the election date, which is one reason the election is irrevocable.

### Q: Which program is better for a veteran attending school less than full time?

Less than full time enrollment treats the two programs in structurally different ways. The Montgomery rates step down by training time, paying 75 percent of the full time rate at three quarter time, 50 percent at half time, and 25 percent at quarter time, with payment below half time capped at the cost of tuition and fees. The Post-9/11 housing allowance has a gate rather than a step. Enrollment above half time draws the allowance prorated by rate of pursuit, rounded to the nearest multiple of ten percent, while enrollment at half time or below draws no housing allowance at all. The tuition payment continues under separate rules. A part time student with substantial net tuition can draw more through the tuition payment, while a part time student with low or waived tuition can draw more from the Montgomery category payment.

### Q: Which program is better for apprenticeship or on the job training?

Both programs cover approved apprenticeship and on the job training, and both reduce the federal payment as training progresses and wages rise, but the payment being reduced differs. Under the Post-9/11 program, the trainee receives a housing allowance based on the military housing rate for the locality of the employer, paid at a declining percentage across successive six month periods of training, together with the books and supplies stipend. Under the Montgomery program, the trainee receives the flat stipend itself at a declining percentage of the full time rate across the same progression of training periods. Locality therefore matters under the newer program and does not matter under the older one, and a training place in a high cost locality strengthens the Post-9/11 total while a low charge training place with modest housing costs strengthens the Montgomery comparison.

### Q: How do the two programs differ on books and supplies money?

The Post-9/11 program carries a dedicated books and supplies stipend that the Montgomery program never created. The stipend accrues at 41.67 dollars per credit hour, up to 24 credit hours in an academic year, producing a ceiling of 1,000 dollars per academic year at the full eligibility percentage. Payment is made at the start of each term in proportion to enrollment for that term, no receipts are required, and the amount turns on credits attempted rather than on prices charged by a bookseller. The eligibility percentage scales the stipend, so the ceiling falls to 700 dollars at the 70 percent tier and to 400 dollars at the 40 percent tier. Under the Montgomery program, books and supplies are paid from the flat stipend along with tuition, rent, and every other cost, with no separate amount, no credit hour calculation, and no term opening installment.

### Q: What changes in the comparison for a veteran with less than three years of service?

Shorter service changes the two programs through different devices. The Post-9/11 program applies an eligibility percentage fixed by aggregate active duty service after September 10, 2001, beginning at 40 percent for service of at least 90 days but less than six months and rising through stated bands to 100 percent at 36 months, with a service connected disability discharge after at least 30 continuous days reaching the full tier directly. That percentage multiplies tuition, housing, and books payments while entitlement is consumed at the full rate, so each month delivers a fraction of the payment value. The Montgomery program has no percentage multiplier. Its rates rest on service categories, with a lower rate for a qualifying enlistment of two years than for an enlistment of three years or more, and a qualifying veteran receives the full rate for the category from the first month.

### Q: How does studying abroad change which program pays more?

Study at a foreign institution changes the Post-9/11 calculation in two specific ways. Tuition and fees at a private or foreign institution are paid at the lesser of net cost and a single national maximum per academic year, which stood at 23,671.94 dollars for the academic year from August 1, 2018 to July 31, 2019. The housing allowance for enrollment at a foreign institution is based on the national average of the military housing rate for a service member at pay grade E-5 with dependents, rather than on housing costs in the foreign locality, and the usual gates for rate of pursuit and eligibility percentage apply. The Montgomery stipend does not vary with the country of study. Expensive foreign tuition up to the national maximum strengthens the Post-9/11 total, while low foreign charges combined with the averaged housing figure can leave the flat stipend ahead.

### Q: Which program is better for graduate school?

Graduate study follows the same payment structures as undergraduate study, with one feature that widens the Post-9/11 advantage at public institutions. The tuition payment covers net in state tuition and fees at a public institution at graduate level as at undergraduate level, so a graduate program with substantial resident charges generates a substantial tuition payment. At a private institution, the annual national maximum limits the tuition payment and charges above the maximum remain with the veteran unless another source covers them. The Montgomery stipend does not rise for graduate study and must carry tuition, housing, and books from one sum. A low cost graduate program, a program with tuition waived through an assistantship or scholarship designated for tuition, or a graduate program pursued entirely online can reverse the comparison through the same low charge and distance mechanisms that operate at undergraduate level.

### Q: What should a veteran compare before signing the election at separation?

A veteran approaching the election holds a defined set of figures to assemble before any form is signed. The first figure is net tuition and fees at the intended institution after waivers, reductions, and tuition designated scholarships. The second figure is the housing allowance under the intended locality, delivery mode, and rate of pursuit, after the eligibility percentage has been applied and after the half time gate and the distance rule have been tested. The remaining inputs are the books stipend, the Montgomery stipend for the applicable category including any buy up increase that an election would forfeit, the months of entitlement held and the months an election would convert, any transfer plan for a spouse or child, and the delimiting period attached to the separation date. Assembled in that order, the inputs produce two totals for the same course of study, and the signature follows the totals.

### Q: Can transferred Post-9/11 benefits be taken back by the servicemember?

A transfer of Post-9/11 entitlement is not a completed gift placed beyond recall. Under section 3319 of title 38, the servicemember who made the transfer retains the right to modify or revoke the transfer at any time. Modification covers the allocation among designated dependents, so months assigned to one child can be reassigned to another designated child or taken back for the servicemember's own use, and amounts held by each dependent can be adjusted after separation even though no new dependent can be added once service has ended. Revocation withdraws the transferred entitlement altogether. The dependent interest is therefore derivative, held subject to the transferor decision, and family planning built on transferred months carries that contingency. Failure by the servicemember to complete the additional service agreed to at the transfer election carries separate consequences for the validity of the transfer itself.
