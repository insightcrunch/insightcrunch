The runaway's eyes crux is a single possessive word in the sixth line of Juliet's wedding-night soliloquy, and it has consumed more editorial print than any other word in Romeo and Juliet. Juliet, alone on her wedding day, orders the sun's horses home and begs Night to draw a curtain across the sky: "Spread thy close curtain, love-performing night, / That runaway's eyes may wink, and Romeo / Leap to these arms, untalk'd of and unseen" (3.2.5 to 7, in the pointing of the Arden tradition). Every early edition that prints the line prints the same substantive, spelled in the second quarto of 1599 as "runawayes", without an apostrophe, without a gloss, and without the smallest sign that its first readers found it strange. Posterity found it strange on their behalf. W. J. Rolfe, editing the play in the nineteenth century, called the phrase "the great crux of the play" and reported that more had been written about it than would fill a volume the size of his own edition; the condensed summary of the comment in H. H. Furness's New Variorum edition alone ran to twenty-eight octavo pages of fine print. The candidates proposed for the runaway's identity fill a small dramatis personae of their own: Phoebus, Phaethon, Cupid, Night, the sun, the moon, Romeo, Juliet, and, under the rival pointing, an anonymous crowd of wanderers abroad in the Verona streets. The conjectures offered in the word's place fill another: rumour's, rumourers', unawares, rude day's, Nature's eye. A word of eight letters has been defended, prosecuted, replaced in print, and restored, and the argument it provoked remains the cleanest available demonstration of how editing a classic actually works when the evidence runs out before the ingenuity does.

The verdict argued through this study can be given at the start, with the humility the brief for the crux demands and the evidence permits. The quarto's word is sound and should stand in the text: no conjecture in three centuries has satisfied the double test an emendation must pass, conviction of the transmitted reading and a credible account of how the author's word became the printer's. The word should be pointed as a singular possessive, runaway's, and the runaway is the sun, Phoebus in flight toward his lodging in the west, whose one great eye winks shut in sleep as his chariot goes down, so that the lovers' meeting can happen untalked of and unseen. That identification, Warburton's in the eighteenth century and Dowden's at the close of the nineteenth, is the only candidate that the speech itself supplies, in its own first four lines, before any commentary is consulted. The humility belongs to the margin that remains after the verdict: the plural pointing, runaways', defended by Joseph Hunter, adopted by the Folger Shakespeare, and satisfied in the judgment of Rolfe himself, cannot be refuted by any document, because the early books print no apostrophe and a parsing is an act of judgment rather than a recovery of fact. An honest edition keeps the word, chooses its sleeper, and records the road not taken. The pages that follow earn that verdict slowly, candidate by candidate and conjecture by conjecture, because the crux's real subject is not a word but a method.

## The Sentence First: What Juliet Asks the Curtain to Do

Every reading of the crux must survive contact with its sentence, and the sentence is more disciplined than its reputation suggests. The soliloquy opens as a command addressed to absent horses: "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds, / Towards Phoebus' lodging: such a wagoner / As Phaethon would whip you to the west, / And bring in cloudy night immediately" (3.2.1 to 4). Juliet is not describing the sunset; she is hurrying it, appointing in imagination a reckless deputy driver because the appointed one, Phoebus the sun god, is too slow for a bride on her wedding day. The second movement turns from the dismissed sun to the summoned accomplice: "Spread thy close curtain, love-performing night" (3.2.5). Night is asked to perform an act of upholstery, to draw the close curtain that her name promises, and the purpose clause that follows states what the curtain is for. It is for a pair of eyes to wink, and for Romeo to leap to Juliet's arms untalked of and unseen. Grammar is the first critic of the crux, and grammar reports that the eyes in question are an obstacle: they belong to a watcher whose openness threatens the meeting, and the curtain exists to defeat that watcher. The paired adverbs at the line's end, untalked of and unseen, divide the threat into its two channels, reputation and sight, gossip and the gaze. Whichever candidate the commentary proposes must be somebody, or some body of somebodies, whose watching could plausibly talk and see, and whose closure the falling dark can plausibly accomplish.

The verb carries half the answer, and the other half of the crux's longevity is a failure to hear it in its period register. Wink, in the English of the 1590s, commonly means to shut the eyes in sleep, to close them entirely, and only by later narrowing does it settle into the modern sense of a brief, knowing blink. Dowden's gloss on the clause, offered after a century of argument, was the plain paraphrase "yonder sun may sleep", and the paraphrase exposes how much of the puzzle evaporates when the verb is restored to its first sense. Juliet is not asking any watcher to flutter an eyelid in complicity, as a modern reader, hearing conspiracy in the wink, might suppose. She is asking for the watcher to be asleep, extinguished, off duty for the night, and the curtain is the instrument of that extinction: Night spreads her close curtain across the sky the way a screen is drawn across a window, and behind it the day's surveillance ends. A crux that dissolves this far under a dictionary is a crux whose remaining difficulty is concentrated in a single question of casting. The sentence needs a sleeper. The whole literature of the passage is a casting call for that sleeper, and the candidates can be judged, with unusual exactness, by asking of each one a short series of questions. Is the candidate present in the speech, or must the commentary import it? Can the candidate be said, without strain, to have eyes that the sunset closes? Does the candidate's closure actually purchase the privacy the next words demand, the untalked of and the unseen? And does the word runaway, in the English of its decade, fit the candidate's manner of moving through the world? The speech, read as its own first commentary, answers those questions with a severity that the centuries of ingenuity have mostly been employed in evading.

### Whose watching does the purpose clause need to end?

The clause needs the watching of the day itself to end. Its grammar makes the eyes an obstacle whose closure is the curtain's purpose and Romeo's condition of entry, and the speech has introduced exactly one watcher whose business is seeing everything: the sun on his rounds. The eyes are the day's eyes, however an editor assigns their owner.

The choreography of the speech reinforces the grammar. Juliet addresses powers in sequence, and the sequence is a chain of custody over the sky. First the steeds and their driver are dismissed westward, out of the scene. Then Night is summoned into the vacancy and handed her instrument, the curtain. Then, and only then, the human action the whole invocation exists to enable is named: Romeo leaps to her arms. The eyes of the crux stand at the hinge between the divine machinery and the human act, and they belong to the machinery's outgoing shift. A reading that assigns them to any figure outside that chain, to a god of love who has not been summoned, to a moon that has not risen in the speech, to street traffic that has not been assembled, breaks the chain at its hinge and must then rebuild the sentence out of imported parts. The candidates are heard in their order below, and the order of their hearing follows the order of their failure or survival under the speech's own tests.

## What the Early Books Print: One Word in Four Dresses

The documentary ground of the crux can be walked in a paragraph, and its shortness is the first surprise the subject offers. The second quarto of 1599, the "newly corrected, augmented, and amended" volume that stands as copy-text for the play in modern editions, prints the line with the word spelled "runawayes" and the verb spelled "wincke": "That runawayes eyes may wincke". The third quarto of 1609 and the undated fourth quarto of 1622, reprints in the same line of descent, carry the same substantive in the period's free spelling, and the First Folio of 1623, set from the fourth quarto, carries it again. Rolfe's summary of the early forms lists "runnawayes", "run-awayes", "run-awaies" and "run-aways" across the early editions, a cloud of spellings around a single stable word, the variation being the ordinary weather of the printing house rather than any division of testimony. No early witness prints any other substantive at this place. No early witness prints an apostrophe, because the possessive apostrophe was not yet the settled instrument of English pointing that a modern page assumes; the early books deliver the letters and leave the parsing of the letters to whoever reads them. The crux, in its documentary form, is therefore not a disagreement among witnesses at all. It is a single agreed word wearing an accidental, its grammatical dress, that the witnesses decline to specify, and everything that has been written about the passage since is commentary on a silence in the pointing.

The one witness that might have broken the silence does not print the line. The first quarto of 1597, the short early text whose relation to the second quarto has generated a literature of its own, gives Juliet only the opening of the soliloquy, four lines of invocation, and then brings the Nurse to the door; the appeal to Night's curtain and the runaway with it are among the twenty-nine lines the short text never reaches. Collation between the two quartos, so powerful elsewhere in this play, is powerless here by simple absence, and the comparison of [Q1 vs Q2: What the Differences Reveal](/2026/04/10/q1-vs-q2-romeo-and-juliet-differences/) shows the shape of that powerlessness across the whole text: where the short quarto speaks, an editor arbitrates between voices; where it is silent, the second quarto's page is the only evidence in the world, and its single word must be construed or replaced on internal grounds alone. The Folio, for its part, is a dependent witness at this line, a reprint of a reprint, and its agreement adds the weight of descent but no independent testimony: the chain from 1599 to 1623 is a chain of copies, and a copy cannot corroborate its exemplar. What the documentary record establishes, then, is narrower and stronger than the crux's fame suggests. One word, transmitted without rival, in a sentence whose grammar is intact, in a speech whose text is otherwise among the most stable in the play. Between the second quarto and the Folio, across the soliloquy's thirty-three lines, exactly one substantive divides the books, a participle nine lines below the crux, where the quarto's "grow" meets the Folio's "grown" and editors divide by theory. The crux is an island of disputed meaning in a sea of settled letters, and that disproportion, one word against a serene transmission, is the engine of everything that follows in this study.

### Why does the spelling cloud around the word prove nothing?

The spelling cloud proves nothing, because spelling in the period's printing houses was compositorial weather, set by workmen whose habits varied by stint and hour. Four dresses for one word across four editions record the habits of four printing shops, not four opinions about the text. The substantive's identity survives every change of coat: stability, not suspicion.

The hyphen deserves its sentence of suspicion, because it has done quiet work in the crux's history. A hyphenated "run-awayes" invites a reader to hear a compound adjective, the eyes of a runaway thing, an adjectival haze around the word, where the second quarto's unbroken run of letters leaves the word a substantive whose grammar the sentence must settle. Editors who modernise spelling, as every reading edition does, strip the hyphen and the long s and the stray doubled consonants away together, and the stripping is usually free of cost, a translation between coats of the same word. At this line the translation is not free. To print "runaway's" is to have decided that one fugitive owns the eyes; to print "runaways'" is to have decided that a company of them does; and the decision, taken in the course of dressing the text for a modern page, is the single most consequential editorial act the line undergoes, more consequential than any conjecture ever offered for it, because the conjectures were refused and the apostrophe is printed in every copy sold. The crux is the place where the two halves of an editor's trade, the words and their dress, collapse into one another: a substantive nobody disputes, wearing an accidental that decides what the substantive means.

## The Apostrophe That Is Not There: Singular Against Plural

Strip the problem to its fork and it is a fork in parsing, not in reading. The letters r-u-n-a-w-a-y-e-s can be construed as the possessive of a singular noun, one runaway, whose eyes are to close; or as the possessive of a plural noun, runaways, a company whose eyes are to close together. English spelling of the 1590s leaves the fork unmarked, and the commentary divides at exactly this point into two civilisations of interpretation. On the singular side stands a procession of named gods and persons, each proposed as the one fugitive: the sun, Phoebus, Phaethon, Cupid, Night, the moon, Romeo, Juliet. On the plural side stands an anonymous crowd, the persons running about the streets at night in Joseph Hunter's classic formulation, adopted by the German editor Nicolaus Delius and by Schmidt and Daniel after him, and printed with satisfaction by Rolfe. The fork matters because the two parsings do not merely assign the eyes to different owners; they assign the line to different kinds of poetry. The singular parsing keeps the line inside the speech's cosmology, among the gods and powers Juliet has been addressing since its first word. The plural parsing drops the line out of the cosmology into the street, among the human traffic of a city at nightfall, and the drop is either the line's realism or its bathos, depending on the taste of the judge.

Lewis Theobald, the eighteenth century's great corrector of the text, made the smallest recorded move at the fork, and its smallness is its lesson. Theobald printed the line with an inserted article, "That th' runaway's eyes may wink", supplying in print the "the" that the quarto's compressed possessive leaves to be understood. The change alters no letter of the substantive, no word of the sentence; it makes explicit, for a reader's eye, the parsing the editor had chosen, the singular, and it does so at the cost of one elided syllable. A defence of the insertion was refined by the commentator Allen, cited in Dowden's appendix, on the ground that a spoken "the" standing after "that" could be absorbed in performance and in the copy behind the print, the final letter of one word swallowing the next word whole. Whether or not the mechanism is credited, Theobald's pointing survives as the type specimen of the lightest editorial touch: the text construed in public, the construal visible on the page, the letters untouched. Dowden printed Theobald's pointing in his Arden text at the century's end, and editions in the Arden line have kept the singular, with or without the article, ever since.

### What does the plural parsing ask the word "runaway" to mean?

The plural parsing asks the word to mean a person abroad in the streets for the night's business or pleasure: a roisterer, a night-walker, one of the company whose eyes a secret meeting must avoid. Hunter's explanation requires that meaning, and the singular's defenders attack it, because the word's attested field names flight from custody, not circulation.

The lexical ground beneath the fork deserves a hearing of its own, since half the crux's literature assumes the word is a freak and the other half assumes it is ordinary, and the truth is that it is ordinary in one direction and unattested in the other. The substantive "runaway", in the usage of the decade, names one who runs away: a fugitive from justice, a servant or apprentice fled from a master, a soldier deserted from his colours, a horse bolted with its rider helpless. Every attested sense is a sense of custody escaped, of a charge breaking bounds and making for the horizon, and the word's energy lives in the escape rather than in the escaper's station. Juliet's speech is a machinery of exactly such bounds and breakings: the sun confined to his lodging, the blood hooded like a hawk, the match played within rules, the mansion bought and not yet possessed. A runaway, a being that has broken its custody and is fleeing across the sky at the head of the speech, belongs to that machinery in a way no abstraction can. The singular parsing asks the word to perform, for the sun, the office it performs everywhere else in the language, and asks the verb to perform the office the period allowed it, closing in sleep. Its lexical bill is nil. The plural parsing's bill was presented with uncommon candour by one of its own side. Professor J. W. Hales, writing in Longman's Magazine in February 1892, conceded that "runaway" in the sense the plural requires, a night-wanderer abroad after dark, is a hapax legomenon, a usage attested nowhere else in Shakespeare and nowhere else in English literature. An admission of that quality, from within the camp it wounds, remains the single most quoted sentence in the crux's later literature, and it prices the plural exactly: the parsing is not impossible, but it must invent the sense it needs, and invention is the one currency an editor is not supposed to mint.

The fork's long life in the editions is itself a datum about the fork. A parsing that only cranks defended would have died with its defenders; the plural has instead been carried, in unbroken succession, by editors working at the centre of the profession, Hunter to Delius to Schmidt and Daniel, Rolfe declaring his satisfaction in a teaching edition used on two continents, and the Folger's editors renewing the choice for the twenty-first century's classrooms. Succession of that quality does not make a reading right, since the singular can show a succession of equal quality through the Arden line, but it fixes the terms on which disagreement proceeds: the plural is not an error to be corrected but a judgment to be answered, and the answering must be done with evidence, the word's field and the speech's architecture, rather than with the mere citation of the majority. The crux's maturity as a scholarly object is visible in exactly this courtesy, each camp's editions recording the other camp's existence in their notes as a matter of professional honour. Where the record shows discourtesy, Dyce's fury at Knight and Collier, the offence is never the holding of a wrong parsing; it is the concealment of a weak conjecture inside the text, a sin against disclosure rather than against taste. The fork may be argued forever, provided the arguing stays where the reader can see it. That proviso, learned by the profession across this word's three centuries, is the crux's most exportable product, and an edition of any classic can be audited against it in a minute: find the place where the evidence runs out, and see whether the editor has confessed the spot or papered it over.

## The Sun in Flight: The Case for Phoebus

William Warburton, editing in the generation after Pope, reached the identification that the sober end of the debate has never left: the runaway is Phoebus, the sun himself, fleeing westward to his lodging. The case, assembled across the following century and a half by defenders who included R. G. White after long resistance and Dowden at the debate's close, rests on the speech's own architecture before it rests on any parallel from outside. The soliloquy spends its first four lines dismissing one watcher. The steeds are ordered to gallop; the lodging is named; the reckless substitute driver is conjured to whip the team faster; the immediate arrival of cloudy night is demanded. The appeal to Night's curtain is the second movement of the same operation, and its purpose clause names, with an economy the passage shows everywhere, the casualty of the first movement: the dismissed watcher's eyes, closing as the chariot reaches the lodging and the day's surveillance ends. The sun is the cosmos's one great eye, the eye that sees everything by day, and a sunset is the cosmos's daily act of going to sleep. To ask that the runaway's eyes may wink, on this reading, is to ask that the fleeing god's eye shut early for once, hurried down by Phaethon's whip, so that the leap to Juliet's arms can happen in the unwitnessed dark. Nothing is imported. The candidate stands in the sentence's own first clause-cluster, driving the horses of lines 1 to 4, and the curtain of line 5 is drawn precisely across his line of sight.

The reading's second support is a parallel from the playwright's own hand, produced in the crux's defence at its moment of greatest danger. In October 1853, with the correspondence columns of Notes and Queries full of conjectures against the word, Thomas Keightley pointed to Lorenzo's line in The Merchant of Venice: "For the close night doth play the runaway" (2.6.40). There the substantive names a fugitive who will not be stayed, and the fugitive is a cosmic power, Night herself cast as the runaway who slips her custody. The parallel does not identify Juliet's runaway; it performs the more useful office of proving that "runaway" as a figure for a fleeing power of the sky is the playwright's own coin and not a misprint's accident. A poet who could set Night running away in one play could set the sun running away in another with at least equal right, and the word's figurative range, cosmic flight from custody, is exactly the range the singular parsing requires. Set beside the speech's companion study of desire, [Juliet's 'Gallop Apace' Speech: Desire in Verse](/2022/07/15/juliet-gallop-apace-soliloquy-desire/), the textual case and the erotic case converge: the bride who hurries the cosmos is hurrying one particular eye shut, and the eye is the one her speech began by dismissing.

The third support is the verb, already weighed in the sentence's hearing and worth its verdict here. Dowden's paraphrase, "yonder sun may sleep", is not a flourish but a diagnosis: once wink is allowed its period sense of closing the eyes in sleep, the sun becomes the one candidate whose winking is a daily, visible, cosmic event rather than a metaphor stretched to fit. Gods in this speech are handled as bodies with schedules; Phoebus has a lodging, a team, a route, a deputy. His eye closing at the route's end is of a piece with that handling, the last appointment in the day's diary. The reading also preserves the farewell economy of the lines that follow, in which the lovers are promised a light of their own, "Lovers can see to do their amorous rites / By their own beauties" (3.2.8 to 9): the great eye shuts so that small lights may work unobserved, and the exchange of luminaries is the speech's governing transaction. A reading that explains the verb, the architecture, the parallel, and the sequel, while importing nothing, has the shape that correct identifications have in compressed poetic texts, and the roll of its rivals, heard next, has the opposite shape with a regularity that is itself evidence.

### How does the sun's lodging in the first line govern the sixth?

The lodging governs the sixth line by giving the runaway a destination before the crux names him. The steeds gallop towards Phoebus' lodging from the speech's first breath, so the fugitive of line 6 is a traveller whose journey has been watched since line 1, and his eyes close at the journey's end, by the logic of arrival.

## The Rival Identifications: Night, Cupid, Juliet, Phaethon

George Steevens, the most learned annotator of the eighteenth century, argued at length that the runaway is Night herself, and the argument fails on the sentence's grammar before it reaches its learning. Night is the addressee of the appeal, the power asked to spread her curtain so that the eyes may wink; a power is not asked to veil her own eyes as a favour to herself, and the eyes must belong to another watcher than the curtain's owner. The identification fails again on sense. Night in the speech is the lovers' accomplice, the "love-performing" and "sober-suited" matron whose darkness is their element and whose offices fill the lines that follow; her eyes winking shut would be the accomplice asleep on duty at the very moment her vigilance is being enlisted. Steevens's Night survives in the literature as the monument of a great commentator defeated by a purpose clause, and its long life in the notes owes more to the annotator's authority than to the reading's merits, a pattern the crux exhibits repeatedly: identifications, like conjectures, borrow the credit of their sponsors, and part of an editor's work is to call the loans in.

The Reverend John Halpin's Cupid, in an essay Dowden's edition calls learned and ingenious, is the most beautiful failure in the roll. Cupid is mythology's runaway by profession: the child god whom his mother cannot keep home, the fugitive archer whose absences and returns govern desire's weather, and a case built on the word's fitness for such a god has genuine perfume. It fails on the purpose clause. Cupid's eyes closed in sleep would not unwatch the lovers, because Cupid is not a watcher set over lovers from outside; he is the power that brings lovers into being, the inside of the event rather than its surveillance, and his slumber is neither needed nor sufficient for Romeo to come unseen. Nothing in the speech summons him: no bow, no torch, no wound, no name. The case rests on the elegance of the candidate's biography rather than on the sentence's need for a sleeper, and elegance of biography is the weakest currency in the crux's economy, since the speech's compressed method is to work with the personnel it has already put on its stage. Francis Douce's Juliet, the runaway from her father's house in spirit, fails on the speech's plain choreography: Juliet is the speaker at her window, willing the night, not a fugitive crossing it, and her own eyes are the ones that must stay open for the scene she is imagining. Phaethon fails on grammar and myth together. The boy is present in the speech in one role only, the imagined whip-hand of line 3, a counterfactual driver conjured to hurry the real one; to promote him to the watcher of line 6 is to double his employment within three lines, and to make his eyes, of all eyes, the ones whose closing admits Romeo, when the myth Juliet invokes ends with those eyes closing in a fall that kills him, an omen her sentence is not courting. Romeo fails because his eyes are the instruments of the sequel, the lover's light by which the rites are done. The moon fails in the purest form of the importation fault: the moon is nowhere in the speech, whose night is pointedly moonless, a darkness of curtain and mantle, and a crux is not solved by stocking a passage with a body its author kept out of it.

The roll of failures has a shape, and the shape is the adjudication. Every rejected candidate is fetched from the speech's outskirts, its mythology, its personnel, its unmentioned sky, while the accepted candidate stands at the speech's centre, dismissed in its first line and due at his lodging by its logic. That distribution is not a coincidence of advocacy. It reflects the passage's compositional economy, in which Juliet addresses two powers in turn and stitches them together as cause and condition: the sun's flight is the cause, Night's curtain is the condition, Romeo's leap is the consequence. An identification that respects the stitching needs no defence beyond the pointing of it. An identification that cuts the stitching must spend its ingenuity on repairs, and the crux's three centuries are, in large part, the record of those repairs being attempted by abler hands than the reading they opposed ever required.

### What happens to the picture if the eyes belong to Night?

The picture inverts. Night stops being the lovers' accomplice drawing a screen across the sky and becomes a second watcher whose gaze must be put out, so the curtain turns from an instrument of welcome into a blindfold on the power asked to help. The chain of favours breaks at its middle link.

## The Plural's Best Case: Hunter's Runaways in the Streets

Justice to the crux requires the plural parsing to be heard at its strongest, because its strongest form persuaded editors of the first rank and holds one of the two pointings in the modern settlement. Hunter's explanation is disarmingly ordinary: Juliet wants no witnesses, and the witnesses a wedding night has to fear are people, the persons running about the streets at night whose eyes, once the curtain of darkness is general, may wink in the sense of closing upon a city gone to rest. Rolfe, whose edition's note on the line remains the most readable short account of the whole controversy, declared himself "quite well satisfied" with the plural and with Hunter's explanation as adopted by Delius, Schmidt and Daniel, and he supported the satisfaction with an argument from the line's own tail: the reading is "favoured by the untalk'd of that follows", since a crowd can talk as an eye of heaven cannot, and the fear of being talked of is a fear of tongues in streets and houses. F. J. Furnivall, writing in the Academy in November 1878, added the plural's best documentary card. Cotgrave's dictionary treats runaway and runagate as near equivalents, and in the second edition of Holinshed's Chronicle, a book the playwright used, a company of "traitors, thieves, outlaws, and runagates" appears in a passage that the First Folio's Richard III renders with the very word in dispute: "A sort of vagabonds, rascals, and run-awayes". The plural substantive, on that evidence, is the playwright's own usage for a company of fugitive persons, and the parsing that needs it needs no invented morphology, only an extension of the company's habits from flight to circulation.

The adjudication turns on whether that extension is small or fatal, and the honest answer divides the plural's evidence into what it proves and what it borrows. The Richard III parallel proves that run-awayes could stand in the period's print as a plural substantive for fugitive vagabonds; it does not prove that the word could name persons merely abroad in the streets, since the vagabonds of the Chronicle and the play are fugitives still, men fled from custody and country, not neighbours on an evening's errand. Hales's hapax concession prices exactly that gap: the sense "night-wanderer" is the unattested step, and it is the step the plural cannot do without. White's objection, recorded by Rolfe, presses the same point from the word's other side: runaway seems to have been used only for one who ran away, and runagate, the word for the wandering vagabond, would have suited the verse quite as well had circulation been the meaning. Herford's judgment that the explanation is "a prosaic idea" is the aesthetic form of the same resistance: the plural trades the speech's cosmology for its sociology, and the trade feels, to a line of critics from Herford onward, like a descent from the speech's register at the very moment the register is highest. Against these counts the plural keeps two genuine strengths. It gives the paired adverbs a single human source, talkers who are also seers, and it needs no mythology at all, which recommends it to editors whose first loyalty is to the probable over the poetic. The Folger Shakespeare prints the plural, and an edition of that authority is not refuted by being disagreed with. The verdict of this study stands with the singular, on the lexical bill and the speech's architecture together; the plural stands as the respectable alternative, wrong by a narrower margin than any other rival, and preserved in the apparatus of every honest edition as the road a great line of editors chose.

### Why does the plural reading need Verona's streets to be busy?

The plural reading needs the streets to be busy because its watchers are not given by the speech and must be supplied by the city. A crowd of night-walkers has to exist at the chamber's approaches, awake and observant, for their eyes to be worth wishing shut; the speech assembles no such crowd.

## Rumour's Eyes: Heath, Singer and the Season of 1853

S. W. Singer, reopening the whole question in Notes and Queries in 1853, began his celebrated paper with an observation that frames the conjectural history: the older commentators did not attempt to change the word; they sought to explain it. Emendation, the replacement of the transmitted word by an editor's invention, is the later and more desperate chapter of the crux, and it opens with a conjecture of real beauty. Benjamin Heath, in his Revisal of Shakespeare's text in 1765, read "Rumour's eyes may wink", making the watcher Rumour, the many-eyed monster of the classical tradition, whose gaze the curtain closes so that the lovers may be untalked of as well as unseen. The sense is the neatest fit to the paired adverbs that any conjecture has ever achieved, since Rumour is precisely the power that talks, and a rumour blinded is a wedding night secured. The conjecture founders on the letters. As Singer himself reported in the act of superseding it, Heath's word "differs widely from run-aways in the trace of the letters": no credible journey runs from Rumour's in an author's hand to runawayes on a compositor's page, and an emendation that cannot narrate its own corruption is a gloss wearing the text's clothes. The discipline's double test, conviction of the old reading and mechanism for the new, is failed at the second gate by the most attractive candidate ever offered, and its failure is the crux's standing lesson in the difference between a good reading and a true text.

Singer's own proposal travelled further along Heath's road and fared no better at the gate. He proposed "rumourers' eyes may wink", the eyes of the carriers of gossip, a plural substantive chosen because an unfamiliar word in an indistinct hand might, he argued, have been misread by the printer as the familiar runawayes. The conjecture has the merit of addressing the mechanism its predecessor ignored, and the support of the untalked of that follows; it remains a word the playwright is not known to use, introduced to gloss a word he is known to use, and the correspondence columns of the journal spent the autumn of 1853 demonstrating that the trade was unnecessary. A correspondent signing R. H. C. proposed "rude day's eyes", the day's rude glare extinguished by the curtain, a conjecture praised in the period's courteous terms as one the poet might undoubtedly have written, which is the form admiration takes when adoption is not intended. W. R. Arrowsmith, surrendering to Singer's ingenuity in print, recorded the surrender and kept his allegiance in a single gesture, adhering still to the authentic text, and glossing the darkness as one under cover of which even the fugitive from justice might rest. Keightley's October letter then supplied the defence's documentary anchor, the Merchant of Venice parallel, and posed the question the season had been circling: not what word might improve the line, but whether the line as transmitted needed improving at all. The season closed with the conjectures admired, catalogued, and set aside, and the episode stands as the burden of proof working in public, in real time, with an outcome that every later discussion of the crux has repeated.

### Who was Benjamin Heath, and what did he want the line to say?

Benjamin Heath was an eighteenth-century scholar whose Revisal of Shakespeare's text, published in 1765, proposed the boldest early replacement for the quarto's word. He wanted the line to say that Rumour's eyes may wink: the many-eyed power of gossip blinded by Night's curtain, so that the lovers would be untalked of in exact answer to the words that follow.

## Unawares: The Word That Got Into the Text

One conjecture in the crux's history crossed the line that separates proposal from print, and its crossing is the episode every account of editing the play must answer for. Zachary Jackson proposed "unawares" in place of the quarto's word, giving the clause the limping sense that surprise's eyes may wink, its claim resting on a resemblance of letters that Singer, no friend to the transmitted reading's rivals, conceded was slight and nothing more. Charles Knight nevertheless admitted the word into the text of his edition, and John Payne Collier admitted it into his, so that for a generation of mid-century readers the line in their Shakespeare was a line no early book contains, printed without the ceremony of a conviction against the word it displaced. Alexander Dyce's judgment on the admission is the period's own verdict and has never been improved upon: he pronounced the emendation "villainous". The severity repays attention because Dyce was no enemy of conjecture in principle. His own contribution to the crux was the despairing suggestion that the last syllable of the quarto's word ought to be "days", coupled with the open admission that he could not determine what word originally preceded it: a syllable and a shrug from the most surgical editor of the age, offered in a note and kept out of the text. The contrast is the whole ethics of the trade in one tableau. Dyce's fragment confesses its ignorance and stays in the margin; Jackson's word conceals its weakness and sits in the line, and the difference between the margin and the line is the difference between scholarship and forgery's first cousin.

The wider casebook of the play's editing, surveyed in [Famous Emendations in Romeo and Juliet](/2026/08/14/famous-emendations-romeo-and-juliet/), shows the same ethics at work across the text: a conjecture earns the text only by convicting the transmitted reading and narrating its own corruption, and it keeps the notes, honourably, when it cannot. The crux contributed the casebook's most instructive failure precisely because its temptation was strongest. The most literary of the replacement attempts came from outside the editorial succession altogether, in the essay "Juliet's Runaway, Once More", whose author builds on Marlowe: the soliloquy of Doctor Faustus, with its cry to the hours to run slowly, stands behind Juliet's invocation as its converse, a gallop answering a linger, and in Faustus the magician apostrophises "Fair Nature's eye", the sun, and bids it rise again. The essayist concluded that Juliet's line as written carried the same phrase, and that the quarto's word conceals it: "That Nature's eye may wink". The conjecture has genuine criticism inside it, since the Faustus parallel is real and the converse structure of the two speeches is among the surest things known about the soliloquy's making, and its proposed sense, the sun's eye closing, is exactly the sense the defence of the transmitted word reaches by the grammatical road. It fails as emendation for the usual reason, the absence of any path from Nature's in a manuscript to runawayes on the printed page, and its value is diagnostic rather than textual: a critic arguing from the speech's genesis and a bibliographer arguing from its grammar arrive at the same sleeper by different roads, and where the roads converge, the identification may be treated as settled even while the word that carries it stays the quarto's own.

### What separates a repunctuation from an emendation at this line?

A repunctuation keeps every letter the witnesses transmit and changes only the dress those letters wear, the pointing by which a reader parses them; Theobald's inserted article is the type of the act. An emendation changes the letters themselves, evicting a transmitted word for an invented one, and carries the heavier burden of proof.

## Why One Word Filled a Volume

John Payne Collier remarked that more suggestions had been made for the word than there are letters in it, and the remark, made in passing by a man whose own editorial career would end in scandal, remains the most efficient summary of the crux's sociology. The question the brief for the crux insists on, why so small a phrase generated so much scholarship, has a compound answer, and its ingredients explain as much about the profession as about the line. The first ingredient is the disproportion between stake and evidence. The word stands in one of the most famous speeches in the language, at the hinge of its most famous scene of anticipation, so the reward for solving it is large; the evidence is one agreed substantive and a silent pointing, so the cost of proposing a solution is small. Where reward is large and evidence is thin, ingenuity accumulates, and the crux became a tournament ground on which reputations could be made with a paragraph. The second ingredient is the fork's perfect symmetry. Singular against plural, sun against street, gloss against conjecture: the problem divides into camps with champions, and camps generate literature the way single answers do not. The third ingredient is institutional: the nineteenth century built the machinery that such tournaments require, the correspondence columns of Notes and Queries, the Academy's letters page, the variorum edition itself, and the crux was among the first passages to be processed by all of it, its 1853 season a demonstration of learned culture thinking in public at the speed of weekly print.

The variorum deserves its own sentence in the explanation, because the crux is unintelligible as a phenomenon without it. Furness's New Variorum edition of the play, compiling the condensed summary of comment on this word across twenty-eight octavo pages, performed a service opposite to Knight's and Collier's: it kept the guesses visible precisely so that the text need not carry them, preserving Heath and Singer and Jackson and the whole correspondence of 1853 in a permanent exhibition where their ingenuity could be admired and their claims assessed without any reader's line being displaced by them. Dowden's appendix raised the tally to thirty closely printed pages. A modern edition's footnote, by contrast, compresses the same centuries into a choice and a sentence, and the compression is itself an editorial act with losers: the student who meets the line in a classroom text meets a settled word and perhaps a rival parsing, and the tournament that produced the settlement is invisible. Something is gained in the invisibility, a readable page; something is lost, the knowledge that the settlement was earned. The crux's literature, at its best, is the profession's memory of its own earning, and the reason the word still draws scholars is that the memory is unfinished: the fork in the pointing cannot be closed by any evidence the world still holds, so each generation's editors must walk the old ground and choose again, and the choosing, done in the open, is the closest thing textual criticism has to a rite of passage on this play.

### How does the crux sound in a theatre, where no apostrophe can be heard?

The crux falls silent in a theatre, because an apostrophe is seen rather than heard, and the spoken line delivers the same sounds under either parsing. An audience receives the watcher generically, as surveillance dissolving into dark, and an actor's emphasis can lean the phrase toward a god or a crowd without the words changing.

That theatrical silence is worth dwelling on, because it explains the crux's strange double life as a problem of the study rather than of the stage. No performance has ever been damaged by the ambiguity, no audience has ever rioted over a parsing, and directors, who must decide everything, are spared deciding this: the line is cast-proof, since whichever sleeper an auditor imagines, the dramatic event is identical, the curtain falls and Romeo comes. The crux is therefore a pure specimen of a reader's problem, generated by print and sustained by print, and its long history measures the distance between the play as performed and the play as edited. Shakespeare's first audiences heard a line whose sense, whatever its precise assignment, landed whole; Shakespeare's later readers inherit the line as a document, and documents invite the scrutiny that performances waive. The editor stands between those inheritances, obliged to print a parsing the author never had to choose in any surviving form, and the obligation, honestly discharged with the alternatives on record, is not a failure of the edition but its definition.

## Dowden's Repointing: The Lightest Touch, Weighed

Edward Dowden's Arden edition of the play, published at the nineteenth century's close, is the crux's settlement document, and its most original contribution is a proposal that changes no word and one comma's worth of pointing. Dowden suggested, in his appendix rather than in his text, that the sentence at 3.2.5 to 7 might be pointed so that Juliet's thought runs from the curtain straight through to its twin consequences, the runaway's sleep and Romeo's leap standing as the paired objects of Night's favour, the syntax loosened from a strict purpose clause into a flowing wish. The proposal matters less for its punctuation than for its philosophy. Every other intervention in the crux's history reaches for the word, to gloss it, to assign it, to replace it; Dowden's reaches for the joints between the words, and finds the sentence more capacious than its parsers had allowed. On his pointing, the clause does not demand that the eyes' owner be identified with legal precision before the sentence can proceed, because the sentence is not a warrant but an invocation, and invocations in this speech pile their effects rather than itemising them. The sun may sleep and Romeo may leap, and the curtain is the condition of both, and the grammar of longing is satisfied. It is the editorial equivalent of a judge declining to construe a deed more narrowly than its purpose requires, and it models the virtue the whole controversy keeps needing: intervention proportioned to the fault actually found.

Dowden's second contribution is quieter and has proved more durable, his insistence on beginning the crux's study with the verb. The appendix's treatment of wink, restoring the sense of shutting the eyes in sleep against the modern reader's complicating flicker, dissolves the one element of the phrase that is genuinely strange to a modern ear and leaves the possessive standing in ordinary daylight. The sequence of Dowden's moves deserves to be taught as method, because it inverts the order the tournament usually follows. The tournament begins with the puzzle, the unidentified runaway, and shops for a sleeper; Dowden begins with the sentence, its verb, its addressee, its first line's destination, and lets the sleeper be identified by the sentence's own staff list at the end of the inquiry rather than recruited from mythology at its beginning. His paraphrase, yonder sun may sleep, is the method's souvenir: five words that a reader can test against the speech in a single hearing, and that no rival candidate's paraphrase has ever matched for economy. Halpin's Cupid requires a mythology to say; Hunter's crowd requires a city to be assembled; Heath's Rumour requires a printing-house accident to have occurred. Dowden's sun requires a sunset, which the speech has already ordered. The history of the crux after Dowden is largely the history of editors deciding whether to accept that economy, and the modern settlement's centre of gravity, singular and solar, is Dowden's centre of gravity, inherited through the Arden line he founded for the play.

A caution belongs to the record, and Dowden's example supplies it against himself. The repunctuation proposal, for all its lightness, is still an intervention, and its lightness can seduce an editor into supposing that pointing is a free good, a layer of the text where taste may operate without the burden the substantives carry. The crux refutes the supposition at its own centre: the apostrophe is pointing, and the apostrophe is the most consequential decision the line undergoes. Dowden understood the proportion, printing Theobald's article and the singular in his text while keeping his own repunctuation in the appendix as a proposal, a distribution of confidence across the page's territories that exactly matches the evidence for each item. Text for what the tradition and the grammar jointly support, appendix for what one editor's sense of the sentence suggests: the page's geography is the epistemology, and Dowden's geography at this line has never been bettered.

## A Century of Champions: How the Identifications Were Fought

The identifications were not proposed in a vacuum; they were fought for, in the specific arenas the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries built for learned combat, and the arenas shaped the fighting. The eighteenth century's arena was the edition's footnote and the annotator's appendix, where Warburton could plant the sun in a note and Steevens could answer with Night at a length no modern note would tolerate, each man writing for readers who bought editions as much for the commentary as for the play. Authority in that arena was personal and cumulative: a reading lived because a name carried it, and the crux's early history is a succession of names, the sun descending from Warburton through the century on the credit of its first sponsor, Night surviving on Steevens's learning long after its grammar had been called in question. Francis Douce, the antiquary, proposed Juliet from the same arena, a connoisseur's suggestion offered among collections and correspondences, and the period's tolerance for such suggestions was wide because the period's editions were, in effect, conversation among gentlemen conducted in print at the foot of a classic page.

The nineteenth century changed the arena twice, and the crux bears both changes' marks. Its first arena was the independent essay and the journal paper, in which Halpin could build Cupid's case at a length no footnote allows, with the mythology marshalled and the parallels drawn, and White could conduct a serial advocacy for and finally against his own resistance across years of occasional pieces. The essay arena rewarded construction: a candidate with a biography, a mythology, a system, could be made to look inevitable across thirty pages, and the crux's most beautiful failures are essay-shaped, arguments whose persuasiveness is a function of their room. Its second arena was the correspondence column, Singer's season of 1853 being the type specimen, and the column rewarded the opposite virtues: speed, documentary surprise, the single parallel produced at the decisive moment, Keightley's Merchant of Venice line arriving in October like a witness who had been waiting in the corridor. A conjecture that needed a system withered in the column; a defence that needed one fact flourished. The crux was processed by every arena the century possessed, edition, essay, column, variorum, and its literature's variety of form is the direct product of that processing. To read the variorum's thirty pages in sequence is to watch the arenas succeed one another, footnotes swelling into essays, essays dissolving into letters, letters hardening into an archive, until the archive itself becomes the monument and the word at its centre sits quieter than any of its champions.

The sorting that followed, in the twentieth century's quieter scholarship, is the least dramatic and most decisive part of the story. No new arena produced a new candidate; the roll closed. What the later century produced instead was a change in the question, from who the runaway is to what kind of act an editor performs in saying, and the crux migrated from the commentary tradition into the theory of editing, where it serves as a limiting case in discussions of accidentals and of the burden of proof. That migration is the controversy's true ending, if it has one: the word stopped being a riddle to be solved and became an instrument for measuring editorial conduct, a standard weight against which methods are calibrated. Warburton's sun and Hunter's crowd both survive the migration, transposed from answers into positions, and the student who meets them in a modern note is meeting, whether the note says so or not, the whole history of how the profession learned to argue about a word it could not settle. The identification industry, having failed to identify beyond dispute, succeeded at something larger: it built, around eight letters, the most complete public record of a reading being made.

## The Word at Work Elsewhere: Runaway in the Playwright's Vocabulary

A word's defence is strongest when the word can be shown at work elsewhere in its author's hand, and runaway can be, in scenes whose circumstances rhyme with Juliet's in ways the crux's defenders were quick to hear. The Merchant of Venice parallel, Keightley's anchor of October 1853, deserves a fuller hearing than a citation gives it, because its context is the crux's context transposed. Lorenzo speaks the line in the elopement plot, waiting in the street at night for Jessica to flee her father's house with a casket of ducats, disguised as a boy, while the masquers' torches make the street a theatre of watchers. "For the close night doth play the runaway" (2.6.40): night itself is cast as the fugitive who will not be stayed, slipping the custody of the hour, and the casting happens in a scene where an actual runaway, the daughter, is descending from an actual window to an actual lover. The playwright's imagination, in that scene, binds the word runaway to precisely the elements of Juliet's sentence: darkness as an escaping power, a house whose eyes must not open, a lover waiting below. The parallel cannot identify the sleeper of 3.2.6, and the defence has never claimed that it could. What it establishes is the word's register in the author's vocabulary, a cosmic and erotic register at once, in which celestial powers run away as readily as servants do, and that register is the ground on which the singular parsing of Juliet's possessive stands most comfortably. A compositor misreading rumourers would have had to stumble, by accident, into a word its author used in exactly the crux's imaginative neighbourhood. Coincidences of that quality are the small change of textual criticism, and they accumulate on the defence's side of the ledger.

The Richard III evidence, Furnivall's contribution, cuts across the same vocabulary from the plural side, and the two parallels together map the word's whole field with unusual completeness. In Holinshed's Chronicle, in the passage describing the invading force that the history play dramatises, the company is a gathering of traitors, thieves, outlaws and runagates; the First Folio's text of Richard III renders the last term as run-awayes, in Richmond's contemptuous inventory of his enemy's army, "a sort of vagabonds, rascals, and run-awayes". The substantive there is plural, human, and fugitive to its core: the run-awayes are men who have fled their country and their allegiance, gathered under a pretender's banner, and the word's contempt depends on the custody they escaped. Furnivall cited the passage, with Cotgrave's dictionary at his back, to prove that the plural form was available to the playwright in a human sense; the passage proves that and proves, in the same breath, the sense's content, which is flight once more. Between the Merchant's cosmic fugitive and the history play's fugitive company, the word's field is mapped at both numbers and in both registers, and circulation, the roisterer's stroll, appears in neither. That is the lexical landscape on which Hales's concession was later surveyed: the plural parsing does not lack a plural, it lacks a plural of the kind it needs, and the distinction between those lacks is the difference between a parsing that is merely unproven and one that must extend its word beyond every attested horizon to stand.

One further inhabitant of the word's world belongs in the record, because the crux's first audiences would have brought the association to the theatre unbidden. The runaway of the period's streets was a figure of the household and the law before it was a figure of poetry: the apprentice fled from a master, the servant from a service, the soldier from his colours, each liable to pursuit and punishment, each defined by a bond broken in the direction of freedom. Juliet's speech is steeped in bonds and their keeping, a marriage contracted in secret, a maidenhood staked in a match, a blood to be hooded like a hawk not yet manned to its keeper's voice, and the word runaway, heard with the street's associations alive in it, imports the whole jurisprudence of custody into the cosmology. The sun fleeing his lodging is, among his other dignities, the great apprentice of the sky absconding from his day's service, and the wit of the image is the wit of the whole speech, in which the largest powers are handled with the familiarity of household management. No rival candidate gathers so many of the speech's own preoccupations into one word, and none of the conjectures, rumour's or rumourers' or unawares, gathers any of them. The transmitted word is not merely defensible; it is loaded, and the load is the speech's own freight.

## The Settlement on the Page: What a Reader Meets

Open the editions a reader is likeliest to hold and the crux stands in two forms, both of them the quarto's word, divided only by the apostrophe and by the sleeper the note beneath it names. The Arden line, Brian Gibbons's second-series edition and René Weis's third-series edition after it, prints the singular and defends the sun in the note, so that the university classroom's default Juliet wishes the day's eye shut. Jill Levenson's Oxford edition prints the singular as well, and the Norton text follows, and on that side of the fork the settlement looks complete, a professional consensus rendered in pointing. The Folger Shakespeare, edited by Barbara Mowat and Paul Werstine and carried into more classrooms than any rival, prints the plural, runaways', and keeps faith with Hunter and Delius through Rolfe: the eyes are the eyes of the night streets, and the edition's note performs the confession the crux always extracts, recording the alternatives it has declined. Rolfe's own edition, a century and more old and still in print in libraries' memory, prints the plural with the editor's satisfaction declared in terms, a reminder that the fork's less favoured side was once the side of a great teaching edition's considered contentment. Between the singular and the plural there is no difference of document anywhere in the transaction, only of judgment about which watcher a bride fears, and the profession's centre of gravity sits with the singular because the speech's first movement supplies its watcher from within and the plural must import its crowd from without.

The presentation of the fork is as instructive as the fork itself, because each edition must decide how much of the tournament to show, and the decisions map the editions' theories of their readers. A full scholarly note can carry the roll of candidates and the season of 1853 in miniature, Warburton to Dowden, Heath to Jackson, with the variorum cited as the archive of the rest; a classroom footnote compresses the same centuries into a gloss and a cross-reference, and the compression silently promotes one parsing from a choice into a fact for every reader who does not look further. The crux is the standing refutation of the idea that a note is a neutral apparatus. At this line the note is the verdict's public form, and two students quoting the same sentence from different editions are quoting different speeches, one in which a god sleeps and one in which a city does. Editors know this, and the care with which the major editions write this particular note, its unusual length, its roll of honourable names on the losing side, is the profession acknowledging that here, of all places, the reader is entitled to see the working. The variorum tradition performs the same acknowledgement at full scale, and the modern note at pocket scale, and between the two scales the whole ethics of annotation is contained: show enough of the road not taken for the road taken to be a choice rather than a fate.

There is a final, quieter witness to the settlement, and it is the anthology. The speech is among the most excerpted in the play, printed in quotation books and audition collections and school readers beyond counting, and the anthologies print a pointing, usually the singular of the edition they follow, without a syllable of the tournament attached. The glide that the series thesis for this crux names, the cliché passing over the phrase, is manufactured there, in the excerpt's clean page, where a word that cost the nineteenth century thirty pages of appendix appears as the most natural thing in the world. Nothing about that manufacture is dishonest; an anthology's contract is with beauty, not with evidence. But the crux's student learns to hear, behind every anthology's serene line, the correspondence columns and the variorum pages and Dyce's single furious adjective, and to understand that serenity in a classic text is not the absence of argument but its sediment. The line reads smoothly because three centuries stood in its way and were, one by one, answered or archived. That is the condition of every famous line in the play, and the crux is merely the place where the sediment is thick enough to be seen.

## What the Crux Teaches About Editing a Hard Text

The first lesson is the burden of proof, and the crux teaches it with unusual purity because its evidence is so nearly exhausted. A transmitted reading that can be construed cannot be convicted: difficulty is not corruption, and the feeling that a line is strange is evidence about the reader's distance from the line's century as readily as about the line. The crux's conjectures all begin from the feeling, and they divide into those that respected the burden and those that evaded it. Heath and Singer offered their words to the margin, argued them in papers and correspondence, and accepted the profession's refusal; the text was never in danger from men who understood where proposals belong. Knight and Collier evaded the burden by the simple expedient of printing, and Dyce's fury at the evasion is the sound of a profession defending its constitution: the line between text and note is the only line an editor may never cross in silence. The second lesson concerns the apostrophe's lesson in humility about accidentals. Editors speak of following a copy-text for the dress of the words, and at most lines the dress is given; at this line the copy-text is bare, and the dress is the meaning. The honest apparatus says so, recording that the parsing is judgment all the way down, and the editions that print the rival parsing are not erring against a document but disagreeing within a licence the document itself grants. The wider mechanics of such pointing decisions across the second quarto are the province of the companion study of [Punctuation and Lineation Cruxes in Q2](/2031/07/11/punctuation-lineation-cruxes-q2/); the crux contributes that study's limiting case, a pointing choice with a cosmology on one side of it and a street crowd on the other.

A further lesson concerns proportion, the editor's sense of how much apparatus a difficulty deserves. The crux tempts disproportion in both directions: the variorum's thirty pages can look like mania until their function is understood, and the classroom footnote's two lines can look like negligence until their reader is considered. Proportion in editing is not a ratio between the word and its commentary; it is a ratio between the commentary and the decision the commentary must enable. Where a decision changes what every reader reads, as the apostrophe does here, the fullest display is proportionate, since the display is the decision's only public justification. Where a decision changes nothing beyond itself, a conjecture refused, a spelling normalised, a sentence in the note is proportionate, and the variorum's archive exists precisely so that the note's brevity does not become amnesia. The crux teaches the ratio by exhibiting both of its ends within a single line: the living fork displayed at length because the fork is printed in every copy, the dead conjectures archived at length because the archive is where the profession keeps its conscience. An editor who can hold both proportions at once, generous in display where the text is at stake, economical where only the record is, has learned the trade's central budgeting skill from its most extravagant teacher.

The third lesson is about the uses of a variorum, and of scholarly memory generally. A conjecture refuted is not a conjecture wasted: Heath's Rumour remains the sharpest account ever given of what untalked of wants, Singer's rumourers remain the standing measure of ingenuity's price, and Jackson's unawares remains the warning that keeps editors honest, each preserved at full length in the record the variorum keeps. The crux teaches that a discipline advances by archiving its failures at the same fidelity as its successes, since the next generation's judgment is only as good as the evidence it inherits, and a settlement whose losers have been deleted cannot be re-examined when tastes or theories change. The fourth lesson is the one the brief for the crux names humility, and it can be stated as a rule of evidence: where no document can decide, the editor's duty is to decide anyway, in the text, and to confess the undecidability in the note. Proof is not available for the runaway's identity, and no discovery realistically expected will supply it, since no authorial manuscript of the play exists and no annotated early copy has surfaced in four centuries of looking. What would change the verdict is easy to specify and unlikely to arrive: a contemporary document using the phrase, a marked copy, a parallel in a source the playwright is known to have open. In its absence, the profession's centre of gravity, the speech's architecture, and the word's attested field must do the work that proof cannot, and an editor who says exactly that, in exactly those terms, has done the whole of the job.

### What would count as proof strong enough to end the crux?

Proof would have to be documentary: a contemporary use of the phrase fixing its referent, an early copy annotated by a hand with authority, or a source passage the playwright can be shown to have followed. Arguments from fitness grade probabilities among parsings; only a document could convert the winning probability into fact, and none is known.

## The Crux in the Classroom: Teaching a Word That Will Not Settle

The crux earns its keep in teaching as no settled line can, because it makes the ordinarily invisible labour of editing visible at the scale of a single word. A class that meets the line in a modern edition meets, usually without knowing it, three separable gifts from three centuries: the letters from 1599, the apostrophe from an editor's judgment, and the sleeper's name from a note's adjudication among Warburton and Hunter and the rest. Unpacking the three gifts takes one lesson and changes every later lesson, since students who have seen an apostrophe chosen cannot again suppose that the page in front of them descended from the author's hand unmediated. The pedagogical sequence writes itself from the crux's own structure. Begin with the early spelling, runawayes, and let the class parse it both ways before any authority is consulted; the fork, discovered rather than announced, teaches what an accidental is more efficiently than any definition. Continue with the sentence, its purpose clause and its paired adverbs, and let the candidates be proposed by the room before the record is opened; the room will propose the sun and the street crowd within minutes, will flirt with Night and Cupid, and will thereby reenact, in miniature and in order, the documentary history it is about to read. Finish with the record itself, Heath's beauty and Jackson's disgrace and Dowden's economy, and the class has learned the burden of proof by watching it operate on men with names.

The crux also teaches the difference between a problem and a defect, a distinction students habitually blur and examinations habitually reward them for blurring. A defect is a place where the text fails and must be repaired; a problem is a place where the evidence runs out and judgment must be exercised in the open. The line at 3.2.6 is the type specimen of the second kind masquerading as the first, and the student who learns to ask, of any difficult passage, which kind it is, has acquired the single most transferable skill the study of an edited classic offers. The transfer reaches beyond the play. Every edited text the student will ever meet, every historical document, every statute with a contested comma, presents the same fork between repair and judgment, and the same temptation to call judgment repair in order to borrow certainty. Eight letters in a wedding-night soliloquy are the cheapest tuition in that discrimination the curriculum affords, and the teachers who linger on the word, against every pressure of coverage, are buying the discrimination at the right price.

## The Verdict: A Word Kept, a Sleeper Named, a Doubt Preserved

The crux resolves, to the extent that honest editing can resolve it, into three separable decisions, and the verdict is strongest when they are kept separate. The first decision concerns the letters, and it is not close: the word is runaway, transmitted by every witness that prints the line, construed without strain once its verb is heard in its period sense, and challenged only by conjectures that cannot narrate their own arrival on the page. The letters stand. The second decision concerns the parsing, and it is closer: between a singular fugitive and a plural company, the singular wins on the word's attested field, on Hales's admission from within the plural's own camp, and on the speech's architecture, which supplies a singular watcher in its first line and no crowd anywhere. The third decision concerns the sleeper's name, and it follows from the second with the speech as its only necessary witness: the runaway is the sun, Phoebus fleeing to his lodging, whose eye is the day's eye and whose setting is the event the whole invocation exists to hasten. Warburton reached the name first among the commentators; White reached it last among the resisters, which is its own kind of testimony; Dowden closed the century with it, glossed by the paraphrase that still says the clause's sense in five words, yonder sun may sleep. Against the verdict stand two honest weights, and the verdict is incomplete without naming them. The Folger's plural is held by editors whose judgment the profession trusts, on a reading whose human sense no one has called impossible; and the whole identification, however well the speech supports it, remains an inference from architecture rather than a recovery of fact, the kind of conclusion that a single document could overturn and no accumulation of agreement can quite seal.

One test of the verdict remains unrun, and running it closes the adjudication. Read the sentence aloud with each candidate installed and listen for the strain. With the sun installed, the sentence moves as the speech moves, from cosmos to chamber, the great eye closing as the small room darkens, and the voice passes over the phrase without a hitch of scale. With the crowd installed, the sentence drops a register at the crux and climbs back for the leap, and the drop is either a welcome touch of the real, Verona's streets admitted to the invocation, or a small bathos at the speech's most exalted hinge, depending on the ear that judges. With Cupid installed, the sentence perfumes itself and stalls, the machinery of dismissal and summons replaced by an allegorical nod. With Night installed, the sentence trips on its own address, asking its helper to blind herself as the price of helping. The ear is not a court of final appeal in textual matters, and the history of the crux is full of fine ears on the losing side; but the reading that survives being spoken, in the theatre's own medium, without strain, has passed the test the passage was built for, since the passage was built for speaking. The sun passes it. The crowd passes it at a lower altitude. The others, in the speaking, announce themselves as commentary rather than as text, which is where the settlement has in fact assigned them, and the convergence of the ear's verdict with the grammarian's and the lexicographer's is the closest approach to proof that a crux of this kind admits.

That residue of doubt is not the crux's failure but its gift to the reader willing to receive it. The cliché of the speech, the impatient bride and her galloping horses, glides past the phrase as anthologies have always glided, printing a pointing and moving on; the crux restores the labour underneath the glide, the four centuries of readers who stopped at the sixth line and refused to pretend it was plain. To read the line with its history is to read it more, not less, confidently: the word is the playwright's, the sleeper is the sun, the curtain is drawn across the one eye that could have watched, and behind the certainty the apparatus keeps the street crowd and the blinded Rumour and the villainous unawares in their permanent exhibition, as the price and the proof of the certainty alike. Juliet asked for a watcher to sleep. The editors, in the end, could not let the word sleep at all, and the play is richer for their insomnia.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does Juliet want to happen when she says the runaway's eyes may wink?

Juliet wants the watcher of the day put to sleep so that Romeo can reach her arms unobserved. The clause is a purpose clause hanging on Night's curtain: the curtain is to be spread so that the eyes may wink and the leap may follow, in that causal order. Wink carries its period sense of closing the eyes in sleep rather than the modern flicker, so the wish is for an extinguishing, a surveillance ended for the night, and the paired close of the sentence, untalk'd of and unseen, spells out the two dangers the sleep is meant to cancel, gossip and sight. The wish is granted by the scene's own machinery, since the sun whose dismissal opens the speech is already galloping toward his lodging when the words are spoken.

### Q: How many letters long is the word at the centre of the crux, and what did Collier say about its suggestions?

The word has eight letters, and John Payne Collier remarked that more suggestions had been made for it than there are letters in it. The remark has lasted because it measures the crux's disproportion exactly: a single substantive, spelled runawayes in the second quarto, attracted identifications ranging across Phoebus, Phaethon, Cupid, Night, the sun, the moon, Romeo and Juliet, together with a plural crowd of street wanderers, and attracted replacement words from rumour's to unawares besides. Rolfe's report that more had been written about the phrase than would fill a volume the size of his own edition, and the twenty-eight pages of condensed comment in Furness's New Variorum, give Collier's joke its documentary backing. Few words in the language have been so productive per letter.

### Q: What evidence did Furnivall draw from Holinshed for the plural reading?

Furnivall drew a parallel of usage: in the second edition of Holinshed's Chronicle, a book the playwright demonstrably used, a company of traitors, thieves, outlaws and runagates is rendered in the First Folio's Richard III as "a sort of vagabonds, rascals, and run-awayes". Writing in the Academy in November 1878, and citing Cotgrave's dictionary for the near equivalence of runaway and runagate, Furnivall argued that the plural substantive was the playwright's own instrument for a company of fugitive persons. The evidence is the plural's strongest documentary card, since it proves the morphology from the playwright's practice. Its limit is equally clear: the vagabonds of the Chronicle and the history play are fugitives still, fled from custody and country, and the parallel does not by itself supply the sense of neighbours merely abroad in the streets that Hunter's explanation requires.

### Q: Why does Herford's description of the plural explanation as prosaic matter to the debate?

Herford's word matters because it names the aesthetic count in an argument that is otherwise lexical, and the crux has always been decided by both counts together. To call Hunter's street crowd a prosaic idea is to say that the plural reading, whatever its merits as sense, descends from the speech's register at the moment the register is highest, exchanging a cosmic dismissal for a municipal precaution. Defenders of the singular treat the descent as a symptom of error, since the soliloquy's method throughout is to enlarge its occasions into cosmology, horses into constellations, a bride into a priestess of night. Defenders of the plural reply, in effect, that prosaic is not a synonym for false, and that a bride's practical fear of witnesses may be exactly the human note the passage wants. The quarrel over one adjective is the quarrel over the speech's altitude, conducted by other means.

### Q: How does Cupid's mythology make him a tempting runaway?

Cupid's mythology makes him tempting because flight is his biography: the child god of the old stories is the runaway of Olympus, the son his mother cannot keep at home, whose absences leave desire masterless and whose returns set it alight again. The Reverend John Halpin built the case for Cupid on that fitness, in an essay Dowden's edition describes as learned and ingenious, arguing that the fugitive god's eyes, closed in sleep, would leave the lovers unwatched by desire's own sentry. The temptation is real because the word and the god genuinely suit each other. The case fails at the sentence rather than at the mythology: Cupid is the power that brings lovers into being, not a watcher set over them from outside, so his slumber neither threatens nor protects the meeting, and the speech never summons him by bow, torch, wound or name.

### Q: Why did the argument over the word flare in correspondence columns in 1853 rather than inside editions?

The argument flared in correspondence columns because the nineteenth century had built a public machinery for learned dispute, and Notes and Queries was its fastest instrument. Singer opened the season in the issue of 2 July 1853 with his conjecture rumourers', in a paper whose first move was to quote the early text entire and to concede that the older commentators had explained rather than altered the word. The replies arrived weekly and in the open: R. H. C. with rude day's, Arrowsmith surrendering to the ingenuity while adhering to the authentic text, Keightley closing the autumn with the Merchant of Venice parallel in the defence's hand. An edition appears once and argues in footnotes; a correspondence column argues in real time, with names, dates and reversals preserved, and the crux's 1853 season remains the fullest record of a learned culture processing a doubtful word before an audience. The conjectures were admired and set aside in public, which is the burden of proof working as theatre.

### Q: Why does the variorum keep dead conjectures such as unawares on permanent display?

The variorum keeps dead conjectures on display because a discipline's memory of its failures is the instrument by which its settlements can be re-examined. Furness's New Variorum edition of the play gave the condensed comment on this one word twenty-eight octavo pages, preserving Heath's Rumour, Singer's rumourers, Jackson's unawares and the whole correspondence of 1853 at full argumentative length. The service is the opposite of the one Knight and Collier performed when they printed unawares in the text: the variorum exhibits the guesses precisely so that no reader's line need carry them, and a later age, whose theories may differ, inherits the evidence entire rather than a victor's summary. A settlement whose losers have been deleted cannot be appealed. The crux's archive is therefore not antiquarian clutter but the profession's appellate record, kept open on purpose.

### Q: Why did Dowden gloss the clause as "yonder sun may sleep"?

Dowden glossed the clause that way because the paraphrase performs his whole reading in five words: the runaway identified as the sun, the verb restored to its period sense, and the purpose clause rendered as a wish for the day's end. Edward Dowden, whose Arden edition closed the nineteenth century's work on the line, accepted Theobald's pointing into his text and defended the sun against the century's alternatives in his appendix. The gloss's economy is its argument. Where the plural reading needs a paragraph to assemble its crowd, and the conjectures need a printing-house narrative to arrive on the page, yonder sun may sleep needs only the speech's first line, where the steeds are already galloping toward the lodging the gloss presupposes. A paraphrase that short is a claim about the sentence's own sufficiency: the speech, Dowden is saying, glosses itself, and the editor's task is to hear the gloss rather than to replace the text.

### Q: Who was R. G. White, and why does his change of mind carry weight?

R. G. White was the American Shakespearean who signed himself Shakespeare's scholar and titled an early paper on the passage "Who was Juliet's Runaway?", and his change of mind carries weight because it was a change against his own investment. White resisted Warburton's identification of the runaway as Phoebus for years, arguing the alternatives in print, and came round to the sun in the end. A conversion of that kind is evidence of a different order from a first impression: the reading that survives a strong critic's sustained opposition, and converts the opponent, has been tested against the best case that could be made against it. The crux's history is full of such testimonies by ordeal, defenders and conjecturers alike submitting their cases to correspondence and print, and White's surrender is remembered because surrenders are the rarest documents in a literature of advocacy.

### Q: What does the crux reveal about Juliet's imagination that a one-line gloss misses?

The crux reveals the reach of Juliet's imagination, which a gloss that merely names a sleeper conceals. In six lines the bride has dismissed a god, appointed a reckless deputy to his team, summoned a second power as her accomplice, drawn a curtain across the sky, and arranged for the cosmos's surveillance to be asleep before her husband arrives. The imagination at work is administrative as much as erotic: it manages schedules, personnel and sightlines, and it treats the structure of the universe as staff to be rostered around a wedding night. A gloss that supplies a name for the runaway and stops leaves the impression of a decorative difficulty in a love speech. The crux's history, by forcing attention onto the sentence's machinery, shows the speech for what it is, the play's supreme instance of a mind remaking the world's arrangements in the image of its desire, several scenes before the world declines to be remade.

### Q: Why has no manuscript or marked copy ever surfaced to end the argument?

No such document has surfaced because the play's transmission is print-only at every point that survives. No authorial manuscript of Romeo and Juliet exists, no prompt copy, no annotated early edition with authority, and no contemporary allusion quotes the line; the earliest evidence for the passage is the printed page of 1599 itself, with the short quarto of 1597 silent at the place. Four centuries of collecting, collating and cataloguing have enlarged the documentary base for many passages of the play and have added nothing at this one. The absence is therefore structural rather than accidental, and it fixes the crux's permanent condition: the argument must be conducted with the evidence of 1599, the word's attested field, and the speech's internal architecture, since those are all the evidence the world holds. An editor waiting for a document to settle the parsing is waiting for a witness who died unrecorded.

### Q: How did the word runagate bear on the argument over the plural?

The word runagate bore on the argument as the plural's missing synonym and its accusers' Exhibit A. Cotgrave's dictionary treats runaway and runagate as near equivalents, which Furnivall cited for the plural, since a runagate is precisely a wandering vagabond, a person abroad without a settled place. White turned the same equivalence against the reading: runaway seems to have been used only for one who ran away, and runagate, the word for the circulation sense, would have suited the verse quite as well had circulation been the meaning intended. The playwright, on that objection, had the exact word for Hunter's crowd in his vocabulary and did not use it. The exchange shows the crux's characteristic geometry, a single piece of lexical evidence serving both camps, its force depending on whether equivalence is read as licence or as contrast.

### Q: What did Singer notice about the older commentators before he proposed his own conjecture?

Singer noticed that the older commentators did not attempt to change the word; they sought to explain it. The observation opens his celebrated paper of 1853 in Notes and Queries, the paper that proposed rumourers', and it frames the whole conjectural chapter as a change of professional manners. Warburton, Steevens and their successors had treated difficulty as a summons to commentary, however extravagant the identifications became; the nineteenth century's innovators treated difficulty as a summons to the printing house, and Singer, to his credit, marked the transition even while joining it. His paper quotes the early text entire before arguing against it, concedes the defence's strongest points, and offers the conjecture as a proposal to the profession rather than a substitution in a text. The distinction between Singer's conduct and the conduct of Knight and Collier, who printed Jackson's unawares without ceremony, is the distinction the crux's ethics are built on.

### Q: Which candidate for the runaway did Francis Douce propose, and on what choreography does the proposal fail?

Francis Douce proposed Juliet herself, the runaway from her father's house in spirit, a bride fled in intention before she flees in fact. The proposal fails on the speech's choreography. Juliet is the speaker at her window, willing the night and stage-managing the sky; the eyes of the purpose clause belong to a watcher whose vigilance threatens the meeting from outside, and the speaker cannot be her own surveillance. Her eyes, moreover, are the instruments the sequel requires to stay open, since the speech continues with the lovers seeing by their own beauties and with Juliet watching for the Nurse's return. A candidate who must be simultaneously the invoker, the watcher and the watched collapses three roles the sentence keeps apart, and Douce's reading survives in the record as an instance of ingenuity mistaking a theme, flight from the father's house, for a referent.

### Q: What is the difference between the Folio's agreement at this line and genuine corroboration?

The difference is descent. Genuine corroboration requires an independent witness, a testimony that could have disagreed; the Folio's text of the play was set from the fourth quarto, itself a corrected reprint of the third, itself a reprint of the second, so its agreement at the crux is the agreement of a copy with its exemplar's line, three removes deep. The chain certifies that the word was stable in transmission, which is worth having, and it cannot certify that the word is right, since an error at the chain's head would be copied as faithfully as a truth. Editors therefore cite the Folio's runawayes as evidence of the reading's descent, not of its authority, and the crux's documentary case rests where it began, on the single page of 1599 and on the sense that page can be made to bear. The distinction between descent and corroboration is among the most portable lessons the crux teaches.

### Q: Why do the two adverbs "untalk'd of and unseen" pull the rival readings in opposite directions?

The adverbs pull in opposite directions because each camp can claim one of them as its home ground. Unseen belongs to the singular: sight is the sun's office, the day's one great eye is the seer whose closing the curtain accomplishes, and no crowd is needed to explain a fear of being seen by daylight. Untalk'd of belongs, at first hearing, to the plural and to Heath's Rumour alike: talking is done by people, and a street crowd or a gossip power supplies the tongues the adverb fears. Rolfe rested the plural's satisfaction partly on that pull, judging the reading favoured by the untalk'd of that follows. The singular's answer is architectural: the paired adverbs describe one event, the lovers' privacy, under its two aspects, and the speech concentrates both aspects in the single watcher whose beat covers the whole city by day. The adverbs are thus the crux in miniature, a pair of words over which the two parsings divide the spoils.

### Q: What did the commentator Allen argue in defence of Theobald's inserted article?

Allen argued that the article Theobald printed could have been present in the spoken line and lost in transmission, since a "the" standing after "that" is exposed to absorption, the final sound of one word swallowing the short word that follows it in delivery and in the copy behind the print. Dowden's edition cites the argument in its appendix on the crux. The defence matters beyond its syllable because it converts Theobald's pointing from an editor's ornament into a possible recovery, a place where the printed text of 1599 might be one word shorter than the line as spoken in the theatre and written in the author's papers. The argument cannot be proved, and it does not need to be: the inserted article is defensible as a parsing made visible even if no absorption ever happened, and Allen's mechanism simply gives the lightest of editorial touches a documentary alibi to match its grammatical one.

### Q: How should a student quote the line in an essay when editions differ over its pointing?

A student should quote the line from one named edition, consistently, and should flag the crux in a sentence rather than hide it. The practical method is to take the text of the edition the essay otherwise follows, Arden's singular or Folger's plural, to give the act, scene and line reference, and to note that the early books print the word without an apostrophe, so that the pointing is the edition's judgment and the rival parsing exists. Where the essay's argument touches the line's meaning, the note becomes a paragraph: the candidate identifications and the standings of the survey belong in the open, not in a silent choice. Quoting a crux without acknowledging it borrows an authority the text does not possess; quoting it with its history converts a liability into evidence of care, and examiners reward the conversion because it is the scholarly virtue the line exists, in a classroom, to teach.
