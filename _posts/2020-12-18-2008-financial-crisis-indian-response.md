The Indian response to the 2008 financial crisis was assembled by a government that had survived a trust vote eight weeks before Lehman Brothers fell, that lost its finance minister to a terror attack eleven weeks after it fell, and that faced the electorate within eight months with the bill for the rescue already printed in its deficit figures. That sequence is not background colour. It is the substance of the episode, and it decides what kind of account the episode needs. The crisis-management account treats the response as a set of emergency decisions taken under fire, and grades them by speed and scale. The technical account treats the response as a set of instruments, a repo rate path, a reserve ratio path, a sequence of tax cuts, and grades them by design. Both accounts are accurate about their slices and incomplete about the whole, because the decisions were taken inside named political conditions that selected the instruments, set their sizes, fixed their calendar and determined which costs could be admitted in public and which had to be carried in silence.

![The Reserve Bank of India headquarters in Mumbai and the Bombay Stock Exchange during the market break that followed the Lehman Brothers collapse of September 2008 - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-59.webp)

This article defends the claim the record supports: the Indian response to the collapse of Lehman Brothers operates as political architecture, and a five-feature map captures what the crisis-management and technical readings leave out. The first feature is the shock itself, the bankruptcy filing of 15 September 2008 and the market break that carried the Sensex from a closing peak of 20,873 on 8 January 2008 to a closing trough of 8,160 on 9 March 2009. The second feature is the Reserve Bank of India under Governor Duvvuri Subbarao, ten days into the job when Lehman fell, cutting the cash reserve ratio from 9 percent to 5 percent and the repo rate from 9 percent to 4.75 percent in a seven-month reversal of the tightening he had inherited. The third feature is the fiscal stimulus, three packages announced on 7 December 2008, 2 January 2009 and 24 February 2009 by a finance ministry that changed hands in the middle of the sequence. The fourth feature is the capital account under stress, roughly 13 billion dollars of foreign institutional selling in calendar 2008, a reserve stock drawn down from 314.6 billion dollars to 252 billion dollars, and a rupee driven past 52 to the dollar. The fifth feature is the trajectory out, growth of 6.7 percent in 2008-09 and 8.6 percent in 2009-10 in the revised national accounts, a central fiscal deficit of 6.0 percent of GDP in the crisis year, a general election verdict in May 2009 that the government read as ratification, and an exit from crisis policy that began in October 2009 and ran into the inflation fight of 2010.

The reading matters because the response operationalises, at a single dated moment, the framework that organises this pillar of the series, [the economic-policy-as-politics framework](/2020/11/15/1991-reform-as-politics-reanalysed/): an economic decision is a political settlement wearing technical dress. The government of Prime Minister Manmohan Singh had spent July 2008 proving that it could survive without the Left parties that withdrew support over the nuclear agreement with the United States, and the mechanics of that survival are treated in the account of [the domestic-crisis-through-trust-vote framework](/2020/10/18/2008-nuclear-deal-domestic-crisis/). A prime minister who had staked his government on one external economic bargain in July was not a free agent when a second, far larger external shock arrived in September. The Reserve Bank governor who had to reverse the stance had taken office on 5 September 2008 from a predecessor, Yanduru Veeraghavan Reddy, whose entire final year had been spent tightening against inflation that touched 12.63 percent in August 2008. The finance minister who opened the stimulus sequence, Palaniappan Chidambaram, was moved to the Home Ministry on 30 November 2008 after the Mumbai attacks of 26 to 29 November killed 166 people and took the Home Minister, Shivraj Patil, down with them. The finance minister who closed the sequence, Pranab Mukherjee, received the portfolio on 24 January 2009 and presented an interim budget on 16 February 2009 because a general election had to be called. None of these facts appears in a table of repo rates. All of them are in the causal chain that produced the repo rates. The article therefore orders its evidence by feature rather than by chronology alone: the shock, the central bank, the budget, the capital account and the trajectory out, with the machinery of coordination, the insulation disagreement and the election examined as the connective tissue among the five. The primary record is the record of announcements and accounts: the Lehman filing of 15 September 2008, the Reserve Bank's policy statements and reviews from September 2008 to April 2010, the texts of the three packages, and the budget speeches of 16 February 2009 and 6 July 2009. The participant accounts, Governor Subbarao's memoir of 2016 and Governor Reddy's memoir of 2017, are used as what they are, the testimony of the two men who held the central bank on either side of the Lehman weekend, invaluable for motive and sequence and interested, as all testimony is, in the verdict. Raghuram Rajan's Fault Lines of 2010 supplies the outside frame against which the Indian officials' claims are measured. Where the brief for this article and the primary record disagreed on a figure, the record was followed and the disagreement is disclosed in the production handoff, on the precedent this series has kept since its earlier runs.

## The 2008 Financial Crisis Arrives: Lehman, the Sensex and the Rupee

Lehman Brothers Holdings filed for protection under Chapter 11 of the United States Bankruptcy Code in the early hours of 15 September 2008, after a weekend in which the United States Treasury and the Federal Reserve declined to fund a rescue and Barclays Bank's purchase of the firm was blocked by British regulators. The filing listed assets of 639 billion dollars and debts of 613 billion dollars, the largest bankruptcy in American history, and it converted a two-year deterioration in American mortgage credit into a general seizure of interbank trust. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell by more than 500 points that day. Within twenty-four hours the insurance group American International Group, whose credit default swaps bound it to the same mortgage assets, required an 85 billion dollar credit line from the Federal Reserve to avoid following Lehman into court. The Indian exposure to this event was not held mainly in toxic securities, because Indian banks held almost none. It was held in prices, flows and expectations, and it arrived through three doors at once: the equity market, where foreign institutional investors were the marginal price-setters; the money market, where mutual funds and non-bank lenders depended on rollover funding that froze; and the exchange rate, where a strengthening dollar and retreating portfolio capital pressed the rupee down in steps.

### How far did the Sensex fall from its January 2008 peak to its March 2009 trough?

The Bombay Stock Exchange Sensex closed at its record 20,873 on 8 January 2008 and touched an intraday peak of 21,207 two days later. After the Lehman filing it broke below 10,000 in October 2008, closed the calendar year down 52 percent, and reached its closing trough of 8,160 on 9 March 2009.

The equity break was the visible surface of a withdrawal that the Securities and Exchange Board of India recorded month by month. Foreign institutional investors had bought a net 17 billion dollars of Indian equities, approximately, across calendar 2007, the heaviest inflow since the category was admitted in 1992. Across calendar 2008 they sold a net 13 billion dollars, approximately, the first annual withdrawal of that scale, with the selling concentrated in the weeks after 15 September. The Reserve Bank's first crisis act came the day after the filing: on 16 September 2008 it announced that banks could borrow at the liquidity adjustment facility against an additional 1 percent of their net demand and time liabilities held in statutory liquidity ratio securities, a technical relaxation whose purpose was to assure the system that the central bank would fund it. The assurance was needed because the overnight call money market had seized: mutual funds facing redemption requests from corporate treasuries stopped rolling over paper, non-banking finance companies lost their funding lines, and the overnight rate spiked into territory the market had not seen in years. On 30 September 2008 the Reserve Bank took the unusual step of issuing a public statement on a single named bank, declaring that ICICI Bank, India's largest private lender, was sound and adequately capitalised, after rumours about its exposure to Lehman-linked paper had produced queues at some of its cash machines and a collapse in its share price. A central bank that has to certify one bank by name in a press release is managing confidence as a political quantity, not administering a rate corridor, and the episode belongs in any honest map of the Indian response.

One absence in the October record deserves emphasis, because it fixes the response's character as national architecture rather than as a local branch of a global operation. On 8 October 2008 six of the world's central banks, led by the Federal Reserve and the European Central Bank, cut their policy rates together in a coordinated announcement designed to demonstrate that the world's monetary authorities were acting as one. The Reserve Bank of India was not among them. It had cut the cash reserve ratio on its own calendar two days earlier and deepened the cut two days later, and it cut the repo rate on 20 October, in each case on Indian data and Indian timing. India attended the coordination that mattered to it, the fiscal and regulatory coordination of the Group of Twenty summits, and it took the International Monetary Fund's resources and quota questions onto that agenda as a claimant rather than as a client. But the instruments of the rescue were set in Mumbai and New Delhi, announced in rupees and basis points, and defended in the Lok Sabha. The crisis was global. The response was a domestic political construction, and its construction is the subject of the sections that follow.

The third door was the currency. The rupee had traded near 39 to the dollar at points in early 2008, carried there by the same portfolio inflows that had lifted the Sensex. It crossed 46 in September 2008, stood in the neighbourhood of 49 by the end of October, and in March 2009 passed 51 and touched a then-record low beyond 52, as the Reserve Bank sold dollars from the reserve stock to slow a descent it declined to arrest at any fixed line. Each of these movements had a domestic addressee. The Sensex fall was read by the middle-class investing public that the equity cult of the 2000s had created and that the United Progressive Alliance's own financial liberalisation had encouraged. The money-market seizure threatened the working capital of firms that had no presence in any index. The rupee's slide raised the rupee cost of the foreign currency borrowing that Indian companies had contracted in the fat years, much of it through external commercial borrowing windows that the Reserve Bank had widened in the boom. The shock, in short, was distributed across constituencies that a governing coalition counts, and the response was calibrated to those constituencies as much as to any aggregate. That calibration is what the word politics means in this article: not party advantage in the narrow sense, though the election calendar mattered, but the selection of instruments by the audiences they had to hold.

## The Governor Who Arrived Ten Days Before the Fall

Duvvuri Subbarao took charge as Governor of the Reserve Bank of India on 5 September 2008. He was a career civil servant of the 1972 batch of the Indian Administrative Service who had served as Secretary to Prime Minister Manmohan Singh, and he inherited a policy stance pointed in the opposite direction from the one events were about to demand. His predecessor, Y. V. Reddy, had demitted office that same day after a five-year term spent, in its final phase, tightening against an inflation driven by food and fuel prices and by a credit boom the Reserve Bank had watched with open suspicion. On 29 July 2008 the Reserve Bank under Reddy had raised the repo rate to 9 percent. The cash reserve ratio stood at 9 percent from the end of August 2008, its highest level of the liberalisation era. Wholesale price inflation had printed 12.63 percent in August 2008, a thirteen-year high. The stance Subbarao inherited was therefore a considered one: money was dear because prices were rising, and the central bank had been willing to pay for price stability with slower credit growth through the whole of 2007 and 2008.

The inheritance contained a second, quieter layer that mattered more in the crisis than the rate levels did. Through 2005, 2006 and 2007 Reddy's Reserve Bank had raised provisioning requirements on standard advances, lifted risk weights on commercial real estate exposure and on banks' capital market exposure, tightened the terms on which banks could lend against shares, and leaned against the property and equity booms that other central banks were content to observe. Indian banks consequently entered September 2008 with negligible holdings of the structured mortgage products that were destroying balance sheets in New York and London, with capital ratios above the regulatory floor, and with a deposit base dominated by households rather than wholesale funding. Reddy made the fullest version of this claim himself in his memoir, Advice and Dissent: My Life in Public Service, published in 2017, where the insulation of the Indian banking system is presented as the harvest of deliberate prudential restraint exercised against the fashion of the boom years. The claim is adjudicated later in this article, where the insulation disagreement is heard in full. Its place here is narrower: the new governor's freedom to cut rates in October 2008 rested on bank balance sheets that the old governor's restraint had built, and the reversal, when it came, was a reversal of price settings laid over a prudential structure that was left untouched. Subbarao records in his own memoir, Who Moved My Interest Rate?, published in 2016, that the first weeks of his governorship were consumed by the recognition that the stance he had been appointed to administer belonged to a world that had ended on a Monday morning in September.

## Feature Two: The Reserve Bank Unwinds the Tightening

The unwinding ran from the middle of September 2008 to 21 April 2009, and its scale is best stated in the Reserve Bank's own retrospective accounting. In his 2010 address titled Global Financial Crisis and Monetary Policy Response in India, Subbarao totalled the operation: the repo rate reduced by 425 basis points to 4.75 percent, the reverse repo rate reduced by 275 basis points to 3.25 percent, the cash reserve ratio reduced by 400 basis points to 5.0 percent, and actual and potential primary liquidity of the order of 5.6 trillion rupees made available to the system, a sum the address equates to 10.5 percent of gross domestic product. No Indian central bank had moved its instruments so far in so short a span, and the movement was front-loaded: by the Third Quarter Review of 27 January 2009 the Reserve Bank could already report actual and potential liquidity of more than 3.88 trillion rupees released since the middle of September 2008.

### How much liquidity did the Reserve Bank's crisis measures release?

The Reserve Bank's own retrospective account, delivered by Governor D. Subbarao in 2010, put the actual and potential primary liquidity made available through the crisis measures at about 5.6 trillion rupees, equivalent to 10.5 percent of gross domestic product. The January 2009 policy review had already counted more than 3.88 trillion rupees released since mid-September 2008.

The sequence repays attention because its order was itself an argument. The reserve ratio moved before the policy rate. A first cut in the cash reserve ratio was announced on 6 October 2008, deepened on 10 October after the global rout of that week, carried further in the middle of October, and taken to 5.5 percent by the package of measures announced on 1 November 2008, which also cut the statutory liquidity ratio from 25 percent to 24 percent of net demand and time liabilities, releasing roughly 40,000 crore rupees of lendable funds by the Reserve Bank's estimate of the time. The final reserve ratio cut came with the second stimulus package on 2 January 2009, when the ratio was reduced by fifty basis points to 5.0 percent with effect from the fortnight beginning 17 January 2009. Five announced reductions carried the ratio down four full percentage points. The repo rate, by contrast, moved in six steps: to 8 percent on 20 October 2008, to 7.5 percent on 3 November 2008, to 6.5 percent on 6 December 2008, to 5.5 percent on 2 January 2009, to 5.0 percent on 4 March 2009, and to 4.75 percent on 21 April 2009. The reserve ratio was the emergency instrument because the first scarcity was of funding, not of price signals: banks that cannot fund a loan book do not respond to a cheaper policy rate, and the Reserve Bank's order of operations shows an institution that understood its own crisis before it announced its understanding to the market.

Around the two headline instruments the Reserve Bank built a ring of facilities aimed at the points where the freeze was binding. The September window against statutory liquidity securities was widened. Refinance lines were opened or enlarged for the National Housing Bank, the Small Industries Development Bank of India and the Export-Import Bank, so that housing finance, small industry and export credit could be funded through apex institutions when the market would not fund them directly. A rupee-dollar swap facility was put in place in November 2008 under which banks could raise dollars from the Reserve Bank against rupees, answering the foreign currency famine of Indian banks and firms whose overseas credit lines had been cancelled by frightened foreign lenders. The unwinding of the Market Stabilisation Scheme, through which the Reserve Bank had sterilised the boom's inflows, returned funds to the system, and the government securities purchase calendar was announced in advance to assure the market that the enlarged government borrowing programme the stimulus required would not crowd out private credit by ambush. The mutual fund window shows the machinery at its most improvised. Through October 2008 the fund industry's fixed maturity plans and liquid schemes faced redemption demands from corporate treasuries that were themselves hoarding cash against the same uncertainty, and the funds, whose assets were certificates of deposit and commercial paper issued by non-bank finance companies, could not sell those assets into a market that had stopped bidding. The Reserve Bank's answer, assembled across October and November 2008, routed central bank money through the banking system to the funds and the non-bank lenders: banks were given a special term repo window whose proceeds were earmarked for on-lending to mutual funds and non-banking finance companies, and the amounts banks lent through the channel were granted relief against the reserve requirements that would otherwise have taxed them. The design confessed the system's true wiring. The shadow lenders of the boom, the finance companies that had funded vehicle loans, housing developers and small industry at the margin, had no account at the central bank and no claim on its window; they could be reached only through the balance sheets of the banks that funded them, and the Reserve Bank's facility was drafted to make that funding profitable at the moment every private incentive ran the other way. The Securities and Exchange Board of India worked the same seam from the market side, pressing the funds on valuation and redemption practice. No large fund failed, and the redemption wave subsided by the year's end, a result the crisis-management reading files under liquidity operations and the political reading files under the state discovering, in a fortnight, which institutions it could not allow to fall and building a pipe to them out of the institutions it owned.

Each facility had a constituency: exporters in Tirupur and Surat, housing finance companies in Mumbai, small firms whose working capital ran through the non-bank channel. The technical literature files these under unconventional measures. Their incidence was as targeted as any budget line.

The reversal was licensed by the collapse of the very inflation that had justified the tightening. Wholesale price inflation, 12.63 percent in August 2008, fell through the autumn as global commodity prices broke, and by the turn of the year the Reserve Bank's problem had inverted from restraining demand to replacing it. The political cover this supplied was considerable: a central bank cutting rates into falling inflation could present the easing as orthodoxy rather than panic, and a government could present the same easing as rescue rather than as the abandonment of the price stability on which it had campaigned against its own record in the spring. The lending-rate diplomacy of the season ran on the same licence. Through November and December 2008 the finance ministry convened the chiefs of the public sector banks, which held roughly seven-tenths of the system's deposits, and the banks announced successive reductions in their prime lending rates in step with the policy cuts, a transmission achieved by ownership and by meeting rather than by the price mechanism the corridor assumes. The private and foreign banks moved later and by less, as the Reserve Bank's own reviews noted in print when they compared the pass-through across bank groups. The episode fixed a fact about Indian monetary politics that the corridor model understates: the state's majority stake in the banking system was itself a monetary instrument in the crisis, available to a finance minister in a way it is available in few other large economies.

The operation's friction is also part of the record, and Subbarao's memoir is candid about it. Banks cut their own lending rates by less and later than the policy rate fell, preferring the safety of government paper and of deposits parked back with the Reserve Bank at the reverse repo window, which at points absorbed sums that dwarfed the lending the cuts were meant to induce. The governor's account describes repeated pressure on bank chiefs, in meetings and in public statements, to transmit the easing. The friction matters for the framing dispute this article adjudicates. A crisis-management reading sees a liquidity operation executed at speed. A technical reading sees a corridor being re-priced. The political reading sees a central bank discovering that its counterparty was a banking system four-fifths owned by the state, whose lending decisions answered to risk committees scarred by the same headlines the government was reading, and that monetary transmission in India ran through ownership and suasion as much as through price. The instrument path is unintelligible without the institutions that had to carry it.

## Feature Three: Three Packages, Two Finance Ministers and an Election Calendar

The fiscal arm of the response moved in three announced instalments, and the identity of the announcer changed between the first and the third in a way that no technical account can afford to skip. On 26 November 2008, ten weeks after the Lehman filing, ten terrorists who had landed by sea from Karachi opened fire across Mumbai, at the Chhatrapati Shivaji Terminus, the Taj Mahal Palace and Oberoi hotels, a Jewish centre and a hospital, killing 166 people over three days. The political cost fell on the Home Ministry. Shivraj Patil resigned as Home Minister on 30 November 2008, and P. Chidambaram, the Finance Minister, was moved to the Home Ministry in his place. Prime Minister Manmohan Singh took the finance portfolio into his own hands for the interval that followed, which means that the largest fiscal announcement of the crisis was made by a government whose finance chair stood temporarily empty, seven weeks after its central bank had begun the fastest easing in its history.

The first stimulus package was announced on 7 December 2008, from the Prime Minister's Office. Its centre was demand through the tax system and through accelerated public spending. The government sanctioned additional plan expenditure of 20,000 crore rupees in 2008-09, over and above the sums the Reserve Bank's easing was releasing. It cut the general rate of central excise duty, the CENVAT, by four percentage points, from 14 percent to 10 percent, across the board apart from petroleum products, with specified goods taken to lower slabs. It extended an interest subvention of 2 percent on export credit for labour-intensive sectors, enlarged the funds for duty drawback and terminal excise refunds owed to exporters, and authorised the India Infrastructure Finance Company to raise 10,000 crore rupees in tax-free bonds to refinance bank lending to infrastructure projects. The package as a whole was valued at announcement at about 30,700 crore rupees. Its composition announced its theory: the excise cut was a wager that producers would pass the reduction into prices and that lower prices would hold up volumes in automobiles, cement, textiles and consumer durables, the sectors whose order books had collapsed in October and November. The wager's critics named themselves within the week, and their strongest statement is heard in full later in this article.

The second package was announced on 2 January 2009, and the choreography of the day is itself evidence. The Reserve Bank cut the repo rate by a full percentage point to 5.5 percent and the cash reserve ratio to 5.0 percent on the same morning that Montek Singh Ahluwalia, Deputy Chairman of the Planning Commission, presented the government's measures, a pairing arranged so that monetary and fiscal easing landed together. The state governments were the package's fiscal instrument: they were permitted to raise additional market borrowing of 30,000 crore rupees, half a percent of gross state domestic product, for plan expenditure in the remainder of the year, a relaxation of the fiscal discipline framework the states had accepted in the fat years. The ceiling on foreign institutional investment in corporate debt was raised from 6 billion dollars to 15 billion dollars. External commercial borrowing norms were eased for housing and infrastructure. The India Infrastructure Finance Company was authorised to refinance up to 60 percent of commercial bank loans to public-private partnership projects, converting bank exposure into long-term funds. Inflation-era controls were rolled back where they had become pro-cyclical, including the withdrawal of countervailing duty exemptions on cement and steel products that had been imposed to cool prices in the boom. The package's signature was federal and financial rather than budgetary: New Delhi spent political permission more than it spent cash, authorising states to borrow and foreign lenders to lend where the Centre's own balance sheet was already committed.

Pranab Mukherjee received the finance portfolio, in addition to external affairs, on 24 January 2009, and it was he who carried the sequence to its close. His interim budget, presented to the Lok Sabha on 16 February 2009, was the constitutional instrument of a government at the end of its term: a vote on account to fund the state through the election, accompanied by a statement of the crisis as the government wished it entered in the parliamentary record. The speech put the advance estimate of growth for 2008-09 at 7.1 percent and called India, at that rate, the second fastest growing economy in the world. It recited the two packages of December and January as prompt and substantial action. It recorded that industrial production had fallen by 2 percent year on year in December 2008. And it announced that the fiscal targets of the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management framework stood relaxed, with a return to consolidation promised at the earliest opportunity, the standard treaty language by which a government spends in an emergency and apologises in advance. Eight days later, on 24 February 2009, Mukherjee announced the third package: the general excise rate cut further, from 10 percent to 8 percent, the service tax rate cut from 12 percent to 10 percent, and the December and February concessions carried forward into 2009-10, so that the tax relief would not lapse with the financial year or with the government's term.

The fiscal arithmetic assembled by these three announcements, on the assessments built from the budget documents of the period, imposed a burden of about 1.8 percent of gross domestic product in 2008-09, and the wider fiscal expansion across the two years, counting the expenditure commitments the budgets already carried, approached 3 percent of gross domestic product. The Centre's fiscal deficit, 2.5 percent of gross domestic product in 2007-08, closed 2008-09 at 6.0 percent. Part of that widening was not stimulus in any deliberate sense, and the distinction became a partisan weapon. The budget of February 2008, presented by Chidambaram eight months before the Lehman filing, had written off agricultural loans to the value of 71,680 crore rupees, the largest farm debt waiver in the country's history. The Sixth Central Pay Commission's awards, accepted by the government in August 2008 with arrears, raised the public wage bill on a schedule fixed by politics rather than by the cycle. Food and fertiliser subsidies swollen by the same global price spike that had driven inflation to 12.63 percent added their own weight. A government defending its record could therefore claim, and did claim, that India had entered the crisis with an expansion already running, an accidental counter-cyclical stance assembled by an election calendar. Its opponents could answer, and did answer, that a deficit accumulated by waiver, arrears and subsidy was a poor instrument for a demand collapse concentrated in exports and industry, since almost none of it arrived where the shock had landed. Both claims describe the same ledger. The ledger is the politics. The comparison the government's critics pressed hardest was international, and it deserves the government's answer at full strength. China's stimulus, announced on 9 November 2008, carried a headline figure of four trillion yuan, and the American measures ran larger still relative to the economy, so that India's fiscal packages, near 1.8 percent of gross domestic product, were arraigned as timid in the winter's commentary. The answer the finance ministry and the Reserve Bank gave, in statements through the season, was that the comparison mistook the instrument for the effort: India's banking system required no recapitalisation and no bad bank, so the fiscal line carried none of the bank rescue costs that swelled the Western figures, and the Indian stimulus ran principally through the monetary channel, where the Reserve Bank's own accounting put the liquidity made available at 5.6 trillion rupees, 10.5 percent of gross domestic product. On that combined measure the Indian effort stood comparison with any in the Group of Twenty. The dispute was never settled in the winter, because the two sides were counting different states: one counted the budget, the other counted the state entire, central bank, public banks, states and budget together. That is precisely the counting the five-feature map performs, and the dispute's persistence is further evidence that the unit of analysis decides the verdict in crisis history as surely as in crisis policy.

## Feature Four: The Capital Account Under Stress

The fourth feature is the behaviour of the capital account, the channel through which the crisis entered India fastest and the channel whose architecture the response most visibly defended. India's external finances in 2008 rested on a settlement assembled across the decade and a half since the balance of payments crisis of 1991, the settlement whose detailed construction is traced in [the detailed-political-architectural-substantive-content framework](/2020/12/01/1991-reforms-detail-reanalysed/): the current account was convertible, the capital account was opened in calibrated pieces, portfolio equity was welcomed, portfolio debt was rationed by ceilings, external commercial borrowing was licensed by category and end use, and the Reserve Bank held a large reserve stock as the settlement's insurance policy. Portfolio flows were the settlement's most volatile component by design, and in 2008 they performed exactly as the design's critics had always predicted. Foreign institutional investors, who had supplied roughly 17 billion dollars of net equity purchases in calendar 2007, withdrew roughly 13 billion dollars in calendar 2008, and in the financial year 2008-09 their net equity sales of 47,706 crore rupees were the largest since the category had been admitted in 1992. The sellers were not expressing a view on Indian policy. They were meeting margin calls and redemption demands in New York and London, liquidating the assets that could be sold, and Indian large-cap equities could be sold in size at a price. The architecture's exposure was structural: the marginal buyer of Indian paper was a leveraged foreign balance sheet, and the crisis repriced that fact in eight weeks.

### How much did India's foreign exchange reserves fall in 2008-09?

India's foreign exchange reserves peaked at 314.6 billion dollars at the end of May 2008 and closed the financial year at 252 billion dollars at the end of March 2009, a decline of about 62 billion dollars that the Economic Survey recorded as a fallout of the global crisis, split between dollar sales and valuation losses.

The reserve drawdown was the insurance policy being used for its stated purpose, and its mechanics were political in the precise sense this article uses the word. The Reserve Bank sold dollars to lean against a disorderly fall in the rupee, spending roughly a fifth of the stock across the year, and a substantial part of the recorded decline was not sales at all but valuation: the stock is held in euros, pounds and yen as well as dollars, and a rising dollar mechanically shrinks its reported size. The distinction mattered in the public argument, where the falling headline number was read by opposition speakers as national wealth draining away, and the government replied with the composition. Meanwhile the flow doors were being re-hung in both directions at once. The Reserve Bank raised the interest rate ceilings on non-resident deposits in November 2008 to pull diaspora money toward Indian banks. The second stimulus package raised the foreign institutional ceiling in corporate debt from 6 billion dollars to 15 billion dollars and eased the external commercial borrowing rules it had been tightening barely a year earlier. The rupee-dollar swap facility gave banks with overseas branches a central bank window for the dollars their foreign lenders would no longer roll over. Each measure was a controlled widening of an aperture that the calibrated settlement kept under administrative command, and the command was the point: at no moment in the crisis year was any aperture simply opened, and at no moment was the convertibility settlement itself put in question by either the government or the Reserve Bank. The contrast with 1991, when the architecture was built under duress with the reserves down to a fortnight of imports, is the measure of what the intervening settlement had purchased. In 2008 the state spent reserves as a buffer from a position of 252 billion dollars, and the spending was a choice among instruments rather than a surrender to arithmetic.

The corporate balance sheet supplied the capital account's second front. Indian firms had borrowed abroad through the boom in forms the calibrated settlement permitted, including foreign currency convertible bonds issued in size in 2006 and 2007 on the assumption that rising share prices would convert them into equity before redemption fell due. The Sensex's fall inverted the assumption: conversion prices sat far above market prices, redemptions loomed in cash the issuers did not hold, and a class of solvent companies faced a foreign currency maturity wall built in the fat years. The Reserve Bank answered in the winter by permitting the premature buyback of foreign currency convertible bonds at the discounts the market was offering, funded from rupee resources or fresh external borrowing, converting a potential default cascade into a negotiated deleveraging in which Indian firms retired their own paper at sixty or seventy cents on the dollar. The oil price performed the crisis's single act of generosity toward India in the same months. Crude had touched 147 dollars a barrel in July 2008, driving the import bill and the fertiliser and fuel subsidy burden that had already blown out the budget before Lehman fell; by December 2008 it traded in the neighbourhood of 40 dollars. The collapse cut the import bill, narrowed the trade deficit from its autumn peak, relieved the subsidy lines and handed the finance ministry, in the middle of its most expensive quarter, a windfall it had done nothing to earn. Any honest accounting of the response's affordability includes that windfall beside the packages: the Indian state could spend as it did in the winter of 2008-09 partly because the commodity cycle, having taxed it all year, abruptly stopped taxing it.

## The Trade Channel: Exports, Industry and the Five Percent Quarter

The slowest door was the one that reached the most people. India's merchandise trade had grown from 23.7 percent of gross domestic product in 2003-04 to 35.5 percent in 2007-08, a figure Pranab Mukherjee placed in his interim budget speech of 16 February 2009, and each point of that growth was a transmission wire the older, more closed economy had not possessed. Merchandise exports fell by 12.1 percent in October 2008, the first monthly contraction in five years, and exports continued to contract year on year for thirteen consecutive months. The contraction was concentrated where employment was densest per dollar of output: gems and jewellery, textiles and garments, leather goods, handicrafts and carpets, the labour-intensive lines that the December package's interest subvention had named as its beneficiaries. Industrial production printed a fall of 2 percent year on year for December 2008, the figure Mukherjee read to the Lok Sabha, and output in the third quarter of the financial year grew at 5.3 percent, the weakest quarterly reading of the decade, against the 9 percent trend the economy had carried into 2008. The Labour Bureau's quick survey of export-linked units, covering the quarter from October to December 2008, reported employment losses of the order of half a million jobs across the sampled sectors, a figure that entered the political argument immediately and is adjudicated there as an attributed estimate rather than a settled count.

The export response inside the packages was administrative as much as fiscal, and its shape confirms the political reading. Beyond the 2 percent interest subvention on export credit for the named labour-intensive sectors, the December and January measures enlarged the appropriations for duty drawback refunds and terminal excise and central sales tax refunds owed to exporters, removed export duties and export bans on items where the inflation-era controls had outlived the price spike, extended pre-shipment and post-shipment credit windows, and, as the interim budget speech recorded, set up a Committee of Secretaries to process the procedural grievances of exporters, the refunds trapped in assessment and the drawback claims trapped in verification that turned a liquidity crisis into a solvency crisis for small exporting firms. A technical account records these as trade facilitation measures of the second order. In the districts where the export clusters sit, Tirupur's knitwear units, Surat's diamond polishers, Moradabad's brassware, they were the difference between a wage bill met and a unit closed, and the members of Parliament for those districts carried the grievances to the finance ministry in person through the winter. The stimulus's geography was the geography of the clusters, and the clusters were constituencies.

## The Invisible Doors: Services, Software and Remittances

Two of India's external lifelines ran through services rather than goods, and the crisis tested them in opposite directions. Software and business process exports, the emblematic success of the post-1991 economy, earned their dollars from the financial and retail sectors of the United States and Europe, the epicentre of the collapse. The National Association of Software and Service Companies recorded export growth for information technology and business process services falling from the neighbourhood of 29 percent in 2007-08 to about 16 percent in 2008-09, with revenues of about 46 billion dollars, as American banks and insurers froze discretionary spending and renegotiated contracts downward. Hiring in the sector, which had absorbed engineering graduates by the hundred thousand in the boom years, slowed to a fraction of its pace, and the industry's associations pressed the government through the winter for the tax holiday on software technology parks, due to lapse in 2009, to be extended, a concession the budget of July 2009 granted for a further year. The channel's politics were those of a new constituency: the salaried, English-speaking, metropolitan workforce that the services boom had created did not lose employment on the scale of the export clusters, but it lost the certainty of ascent that had made it the government's most loyal economic audience, and the anxiety was registered in the press the government read.

The second lifeline ran the other way. Workers' remittances, sent home by the diaspora in the Gulf and the West whose politics this series examines in its foreign policy pillar, held up through the crisis year with a resilience that surprised the forecasters. The Reserve Bank's balance of payments accounts recorded private transfer receipts of the order of 47 billion dollars in 2008-09, and India remained the largest recipient of remittances in the world in the World Bank's tabulations for the period. The Gulf economies, buoyed by the oil revenues accumulated before the price fall of late 2008, did not expel their Indian workforce on the scale feared in the first panic, and money continued to flow to the households of Kerala, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar and Andhra Pradesh whose consumption it underwrote. A portion of the measured strength was arithmetic, since a depreciating rupee raised the home value of each dollar sent, and a portion was return migration, workers converting savings and coming home ahead of expected layoffs. Even discounted, the flow mattered to the insulation argument: the demand that held up in the Indian countryside and the small towns through 2009 rested in part on wages earned in Dubai and Doha and remitted through channels the capital account's architecture had formalised in the previous decade, an export of labour that no tariff or quota in the receiving countries interrupted during the crisis year.

## The Rural Floor: Employment Guarantee, Debt Waiver and the Village Economy

The government's political defence of its crisis record rested, in the last instance, on the countryside, and the instruments of that defence had been built before Lehman fell for reasons unconnected with any crisis. The National Rural Employment Guarantee Act of 2005, the United Progressive Alliance's flagship welfare statute, entitled every rural household to one hundred days of wage employment a year on public works at the statutory minimum wage. Its budget and its uptake both climbed through the crisis years as a counter-cyclical floor operates: the programme generated about 216 crore person-days of employment in 2008-09 and about 284 crore in 2009-10 on the programme's own statistics, and the full budget of July 2009 raised its allocation to 39,100 crore rupees, the largest single line of the rural budget. Alongside it stood the farm debt waiver of the February 2008 budget, 71,680 crore rupees of agricultural loans written off or relieved, whose disbursement ran through the crisis year and put cash-equivalent relief into the same villages the employment guarantee was paying. Agricultural output grew only modestly in 2008-09, and the monsoon of 2009 was the weakest since 1972, with rainfall about a fifth below the long-period average, so that the countryside's income from the crop itself faltered in the second crisis year even as the transfers rose.

The political significance of the floor is that it converted the insulation debate from an argument about banks into an argument about bread. When Manmohan Singh and his ministers claimed through 2009 that India had escaped the worst of the crisis, the evidence they reached for first was not the Sensex recovery but the claim that rural demand had held, that the employment guarantee had put a wage under the village economy at the moment the export clusters were shedding hands, and that returning migrants, from the Gulf and from the cities, had been absorbed by the works and the harvest rather than by destitution. The opposition's counter attacked the same ground: the Bharatiya Janata Party charged that the guarantee's wages were unpaid for months in the states and that its works were of poor quality, and the Left charged that a government serious about a rural floor would have expanded the public distribution system and universalised the employment entitlement rather than rationing them by budget. The adjudication of those charges belongs to the welfare record of the second Singh government rather than to this article. What belongs here is the architectural point. The rural floor was a pre-existing political settlement, built in 2005 and 2008 for electoral and ideological reasons of those years, which the crisis re-purposed as macroeconomic stabilisation without a single new statute. A technical account of the response, which begins its reading on 15 September 2008, cannot see the floor at all. A political account, which reads the settlement as accumulated architecture, sees that the most consequential Indian crisis instrument of 2008-09 may have been a rural works programme designed three years earlier for a different argument.

## Decoupled or Transmitted? The Insulation Disagreement Adjudicated

No factual dispute about the Indian crisis has lasted longer than the argument over insulation, and the article's stance charter requires the positions to be heard at their strongest before they are judged. The first position is the decoupling claim, which circulated in official and market commentary through 2007 and into 2008: an economy growing on domestic demand, with modest export dependence by the standards of East Asia and a banking system supervised on conservative lines, would feel a Western financial crisis as weather rather than as climate. The second position is the transmission claim in its measured form, which the Reserve Bank itself adopted. Subbarao's retrospective account names three channels, trade, finance and confidence, and rejects the proposition that India stood apart: a country whose trade share of output had risen by twelve points of gross domestic product in five years, whose equity market was priced at the margin by foreign funds, and whose firms had borrowed abroad in size, was integrated at exactly the points where the crisis struck, and the Reserve Bank's own narrative records that the impact arrived through all three channels within weeks. The third position is the prudential claim, stated most fully by Y. V. Reddy in Advice and Dissent in 2017: the banking system held because a governor and his institution had spent the boom years refusing the boom's instruments, raising provisioning and risk weights, capping property and market exposures, and declining to bless the securitised chain, so that insulation was not a property of the economy but a product of decisions, several of them unpopular when taken. The fourth position, stated by Raghuram Rajan in Fault Lines, published in 2010, widens the frame: Rajan, who had warned a gathering of central bankers in 2005 that financial innovation had made the world riskier, read India's escape as the compound of a demand structure still led by domestic consumption and investment and a financial sector that had not been permitted to build the leverage chains of the Atlantic system, and he warned that the same conservatism carried costs in credit depth that a poorer country pays daily.

### Did any Indian bank fail during the 2008 crisis?

No Indian bank failed during the crisis of 2008-09, and none required a state rescue of the kind extended in the United States and Europe. The nearest approach to a bank run was the rumour-driven pressure on ICICI Bank in September 2008, answered by the Reserve Bank's public statement of 30 September 2008.

Judged on the record assembled in this article, the disagreement resolves by layers, and the layered resolution is itself the political reading's strongest evidence. At the layer of bank solvency, the prudential claim is vindicated: no bank failed, no rescue was required, and the assets that destroyed foreign balance sheets were absent from Indian ones because a regulator had kept them out. At the layer of output and employment, the transmission claim is vindicated: exports contracted for thirteen months, industrial production printed a negative month, quarterly growth fell to 5.3 percent, and the Labour Bureau's survey counted job losses in the export clusters in the hundreds of thousands. At the layer of prices and flows, both claims fail in turn: the Sensex lost three-fifths of its value peak to trough and the reserves spent a fifth of their stock, which is not insulation by any definition the word can carry, yet the payments system never closed and the currency never entered the free fall that took several emerging market currencies down in the same months. The decoupling claim, the only one of the four that cannot survive the record, was a claim about geography and structure. The three that survive are all claims about decisions: prudential decisions taken in 2005 and 2006, calibrations of the capital account taken across the 1990s and 2000s, and demand decisions taken by the fiscal packages of the crisis winter. Insulation in India was manufactured, and what is manufactured by politics can be read politically, which is the whole of the argument.

## The Politics of Competence: The Crisis Carried into the 2009 Election

The response was assembled under a deadline that no instrument table records: the fifteenth general election, due by May 2009. Every actor in the crisis understood that the management of the winter would be graded at the polls in the spring, and the contest over the grading began before the third package was announced. The strongest statement of the attack from the right came from the Bharatiya Janata Party, whose campaign for its prime ministerial candidate, Lal Krishna Advani, charged the United Progressive Alliance with presiding first over the price rise of 2008 and then over the job losses of the winter, and argued that an economy steered by an eminent economist had nonetheless delivered inflation, agrarian distress and industrial contraction in a single term. The strongest statement of the attack from the left was written earlier and cut deeper. Prabhat Patnaik, in People's Democracy in December 2008, called the first package pathetic, noted that its direct additional spending of 20,000 crore rupees amounted to less than half a percent of gross domestic product, and charged that parts of it seemed designed to push up the stock market rather than to revive the real economy, a charge the Communist Party of India (Marxist) carried into the campaign in the wider form that Congress and the Bharatiya Janata Party served the same economic programme and differed only in communal colouring. The government's defence was built on the growth figure and on the international stage. Manmohan Singh repeated through the season that India would grow at about 7 percent in a year when the world economy shrank, a formulation the interim budget formalised as the second fastest growing economy in the world, and he carried the claim in person to the two summits that the crisis had created. At Washington on 15 November 2008, at the first meeting of the Group of Twenty leaders, Singh pressed the case for a coordinated fiscal response, for larger resources for the International Monetary Fund and for reform of the Fund's quotas toward the emerging economies whose weight the crisis had demonstrated. At London on 2 April 2009 the summit agreed a package of 1.1 trillion dollars, including a tripling of the Fund's resources to 750 billion dollars, and Singh's recorded interventions argued against protectionism in trade and finance and for the proposition that the crisis had been made in the developed world's financial system and must not be paid for by the developing world's poor.

The crisis winter supplied an early electoral reading before the general election, and both camps mined it. Assembly elections in five states were counted on 8 December 2008, the day after the first stimulus package was announced, in a season the press had billed as a semi-final for the national contest. The Congress took Rajasthan from the Bharatiya Janata Party and held Delhi for a third term under Sheila Dikshit, in a capital still marked by the Mumbai attacks of the previous month and governed through them by a Congress administration. The Bharatiya Janata Party held Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh. The mixed card denied every camp a clean narrative: the government read the results as proof that the electorate distinguished between the terror failure, which it punished in commentary, and the economic management, which it had not yet been asked to grade at the national level; the opposition read the same card as incumbency's local accidents. What the December results established for the strategists was narrower and more useful. The crisis had not produced a national anti-incumbent tide in the states that voted through its worst quarter, and the government entered the general election season entitled to believe that steadiness was a sellable quality. The stimulus packages of January and February were framed by men who had seen that evidence. Through the same season the two camps fought a parallel war over the employment figures, and the war's conduct illustrates how crisis statistics function as political instruments. The Federation of Indian Export Organisations warned publicly through the winter that job losses in the export sector could run into the millions if orders and credit did not revive, a figure built from the order books of its member associations in the clusters. The Labour Bureau's quick surveys, commissioned by the government and conducted across eight export-linked sectors, counted employment losses of about half a million in the quarter from October to December 2008 in the units sampled, and its follow-up survey for the quarter from January to March 2009 recorded a partial recovery, with employment in the sampled sectors rising again by of the order of a quarter of a million. Neither number was false on its own terms; they measured different universes with different instruments, a sample against an extrapolation, and each camp quoted the number that indicted or acquitted the government as its argument required. The Bureau's series was the more careful instrument and the federation's the more politically potent, and the distance between them, half a million counted against millions feared, is the space in which the election campaign's economic argument was conducted.

Counting on 16 May 2009 returned the Congress party with 206 seats and the United Progressive Alliance with 262, a larger mandate than the coalition had won in 2004, and Manmohan Singh was sworn in for a second term on 22 May 2009, the first prime minister since Jawaharlal Nehru to return to office after completing a full term. Post-poll analysis was careful about causes: the verdict assembled state-level judgements, rewarded several incumbent state governments of different parties, and contained no single national wave that a survey could isolate, so that the crisis cannot be read off the result as a graded examination. But the political fact does not require the analytical one. The government had campaigned as the steady hand, its opponents had campaigned on the winter's losses, and the electorate enlarged the government's majority; the competence claim was therefore available to the second Singh government as a ratified asset, and it entered the stock of arguments with which that government defended the deficit it had run and the exit it was about to attempt. The mandate of 2004, examined in [the post-2004 UPA mandate](/2019/06/22/2004-upa-shock-verdict-explained/), had been a shock verdict against incumbents; the mandate of 2009 was its mirror image, a verdict for the incumbents who had held the instruments through the storm, and the difference between the two verdicts is the political yield of the five features this article has mapped.

## The Coordination Machine Behind the Five Features

The five features were produced by a machine, and the machine deserves a section of its own because its design is the least documented part of the episode and the part where the political reading earns its keep most plainly. On 3 November 2008 Prime Minister Manmohan Singh met the heads of India's industry associations and largest companies in New Delhi, heard their account of the credit freeze from the borrowing side, and announced an apex committee under his own chairmanship to track the crisis and force decisions across ministries, with a second group under the Cabinet Secretary to work the implementation. The structure expressed the government's diagnosis: the obstacle was not knowledge, since the Reserve Bank's data and the finance ministry's files described the seizure adequately, but jurisdiction, since every remedy crossed a boundary, between the central bank and the ministry, between the ministry and the regulators, between New Delhi and the state governments that would have to borrow and spend. The 2 January 2009 choreography, a Planning Commission deputy chairman unveiling a fiscal package on the morning the Reserve Bank cut its rates, was the machine performing in public: the Planning Commission, which controlled no budget, served as the neutral stage on which a fiscal announcement and a monetary announcement could be fused into a single signal. Montek Singh Ahluwalia doubled in the same season as the Prime Minister's sherpa for the Group of Twenty summits, so that the official who presented the second package at home carried India's brief to Washington and London abroad, and the domestic and international arms of the response shared one drafting hand.

The governor's memoir adds the machine's internal friction to the record. Subbarao's account in Who Moved My Interest Rate? describes a Reserve Bank that guarded its operational independence through the crisis while coordinating timing and scale with the finance ministry at a closeness neither institution would have chosen in calm years, and it records his resistance to the government's occasional wish that the central bank announce its easing on the budget's schedule rather than its own. The correspondence among the governor's office, Chidambaram's finance ministry and then Mukherjee's, over the size and sequencing of the easing and the packages, survives in the Reserve Bank's archives and the finance ministry's files as the episode's under-used primary record: the letters in which the coordination was actually negotiated, rather than the press releases in which it was announced. A technical account has no use for that correspondence, since the instruments are identical read from either source. A political account cannot do without it, because the correspondence is where the settlement among the institutions was struck: how much of the burden the central bank would carry as liquidity, how much the budget would carry as deficit, and how much would be pushed to the states as borrowing permission. The three stimulus packages and the 425 basis points of rate cuts look, from the announcements, like a sequence of decisions. From the machine that produced them they look like a single allocation, bargained once and released in tranches timed to the data and to the election calendar.

## Feature Five: Recovery, and the Argument Over the Exit

The trajectory out of the crisis was steep enough to become an argument in itself. Industrial production returned to double-digit growth in the second half of 2009. The Sensex crossed 17,000 in the first days of October 2009, recovering in nineteen months a level the crash had been expected to deny it for years, and foreign institutional buyers returned as net purchasers through 2009 as the same global liquidity that had fled the emerging markets in 2008 came looking for yield. The full budget of 6 July 2009, Pranab Mukherjee's first as finance minister of the returned government, chose consolidation of the recovery over consolidation of the accounts: gross budgetary support for the annual plan was raised by 40,000 crore rupees over the interim budget's provision, the states received a further half percent of gross state domestic product in borrowing room, and the fiscal deficit for 2009-10 was budgeted at 6.8 percent of gross domestic product, closing the year at 6.5 percent. Growth for 2009-10 printed at 8.6 percent in the revised national accounts on the 2004-05 base, against 6.7 percent for 2008-09, and the sequence entered the government's own telling as vindication: a two-year path through the worst global contraction since the 1930s without a negative quarter, without a bank failure and without a payments crisis.

The price of the path arrived on a lag, denominated in inflation. Wholesale price inflation, which had fallen from 12.63 percent in August 2008 to the neighbourhood of zero by the middle of 2009 and printed negative readings in the summer of that year, turned upward as the recovery took hold, and food price inflation ran to the neighbourhood of 20 percent in December 2009 on the weekly prints, driven by a weak monsoon and by the demand the stimulus had sustained. The political geometry of 2008 inverted: the government that had been attacked for job losses in the winter was attacked for the price of onions and pulses in the winter after, and the central bank that had been pressed to cut was pressed to explain why it would not tighten faster against food prices that monetary policy could not reach. Subbarao's stated doctrine through that season, repeated in his policy statements and interviews of 2010, was that exit from an emergency stance is not an event but a process, sequenced so that the withdrawal of liquidity precedes the raising of its price and neither outruns the recovery it is meant to protect.

### When did the Reserve Bank begin withdrawing the crisis measures?

The exit began with the Second Quarter Review of 27 October 2009, which restored the statutory liquidity ratio to 25 percent from 7 November 2009 and wound down several special facilities. The cash reserve ratio was raised on 29 January 2010, and the repo rate followed on 19 March 2010.

The sequence after October 2009 ran in the order the doctrine promised. The October 2009 review raised the statutory liquidity ratio back to 25 percent and terminated the special refinance windows, a withdrawal of facilities rather than of money. The Third Quarter Review of 29 January 2010 raised the cash reserve ratio by 75 basis points to 5.75 percent, absorbing liquidity in quantity. The first price signal came on 19 March 2010, when the repo and reverse repo rates were each raised by 25 basis points between scheduled reviews, and the annual policy of April 2010 continued the ascent. Mukherjee's budget of February 2010 performed the fiscal half of the same turn, raising the general excise rate from 8 percent back to 10 percent as a first partial rollback of the December 2008 and February 2009 cuts, and presenting the rollback explicitly as the opening of the return to the fiscal responsibility path. The rollback's partiality is the honest summary of the whole exit: the excise rate went back by half the distance it had fallen, the service tax cut of February 2009 was left in place, and the deficit the budgets of 2010 and 2011 projected narrowed by arithmetic more slowly than the fiscal responsibility framework demanded. Critics of the settlement, including economists who had supported the winter packages, argued in print through 2010 that the exit had been left a season too late and that the inflation of that year was its price. The government's defenders answered that premature withdrawal in an economy where private investment had barely restarted would have purchased lower inflation with a second dip, and that the Group of Twenty's own communiqués through 2010 warned member states against exactly that error. Both positions are recorded here at their strength because the exit argument is the point where the crisis settlement shades into the ordinary fiscal politics of the second Singh government, examined elsewhere in this pillar, and its adjudication belongs to that longer record rather than to this article's claim. The settlement's tail can nonetheless be measured in the two budgets that followed. Growth of 9.3 percent in 2010-11, in the same revised series that prints 8.6 percent for 2009-10, refilled the revenue lines the crisis had emptied, and the auction of third-generation spectrum licences in May 2010 brought the exchequer a windfall of some 67,719 crore rupees against a budgeted expectation a third of that size, so that the central fiscal deficit for 2010-11 closed at 4.9 percent of gross domestic product, inside the path the finance ministry had promised and ahead of the schedule its critics had demanded. The consolidation of 2010-11 was therefore purchased partly by recovery, partly by an asset sale and partly by the excise restoration of February 2010, and only partly by restraint of expenditure, a composition the opposition of that year attacked as cosmetic and the government defended as the arithmetic of a growing economy. The deficit inherited from the crisis winter took three budgets to halve, and the argument over whether it should have been halved faster is the argument with which the second Singh government's economic record properly begins.

## Funding the Rescue: The Government Securities Market

A stimulus announced in percentage points of gross domestic product must be borrowed before it can be spent, and the borrowing programme of 2008-09 was where the fiscal and monetary arms of the response met and had to be reconciled in public. The Centre's deficit had been budgeted for the year at 2.5 percent of gross domestic product and closed at 6.0 percent, and the market borrowing that financed the gap roughly doubled against the budget's assumption as the year advanced, with the supplementary demands and the two packages layered onto a calendar the debt managers had published in calmer conditions. The danger was mechanical and immediate: a government selling twice the expected volume of its own paper into a frightened market bids the yield on that paper up, and a rising government yield drags every private borrowing rate with it, cancelling in the bond market what the central bank is attempting in the policy corridor. The ten-year government yield had in fact spiked in the autumn of 2008, when inflation and supply fears coincided, before collapsing toward 5 percent in the first weeks of 2009 as the Reserve Bank's easing and its purchase assurances took hold. Managing that curve was a joint operation. The Reserve Bank unwound the Market Stabilisation Scheme securities it had issued in the boom, returning cash to the market; it announced its open market purchase intentions in advance and bought government paper outright in quantities the market could plan around; it sequenced the borrowing calendar with the finance ministry so that auctions did not collide with the private sector's own funding windows; and the statutory liquidity ratio cut of November 2008 freed balance sheet capacity in the banking system that held the government's paper. The buyers of the rescue, in the end, were overwhelmingly the Indian banks themselves, holding government securities well above the statutory floor through the crisis years, which is why the sovereign and the banking system could not be pried apart in any account of the episode: the state borrowed from banks it owned to fund packages meant to keep those banks' borrowers alive, and the central bank stood behind the price of the paper that closed the circuit. A crisis-management reading calls this debt management. It was, in form and in effect, the fiscal constitution of the rescue being rewritten for a season by three institutions acting in concert, and the concert, rather than any single instrument, is the feature the map must record.

The map's rows are not parallel entries; they are a chain, and the chain's links are the article's argument in miniature. Feature One's market break made Feature Two's reversal unavoidable, since a central bank that had spent a year tightening for price stability could not hold a 9 percent repo rate over a seized money market without converting a liquidity crisis into a solvency crisis. Feature Two's easing made Feature Three's borrowing affordable, since the packages could be financed at tolerable yields only because the Reserve Bank was buying, assuring purchases and freeing bank balance sheets to hold the paper, which is why the fiscal and monetary announcements were choreographed to land on the same mornings. Feature Four's reserve drawdown purchased the time in which Features Two and Three could work, holding the currency's descent to a slope the real economy could climb down rather than a cliff it would fall from. Feature Five's recovery then retired the emergency settings in the order the doctrine prescribed, facilities first, quantities next, prices last, and the election verdict arrived in the middle of the chain's operation to ratify the links already forged and to authorise the ones still to come. Read down the table's columns, the episode is a chronicle. Read across its rows, it is an allocation of the crisis among the balance sheets of the central bank, the budget, the states and the reserve stock, struck by named holders under an election deadline. That allocation, and not any single instrument in any single row, is the political architecture the article set out to name.

## What the Political Reading Settles

The adjudication can be stated briefly because the evidence has been laid out in the order the map records. The crisis-management framing is true of the response's tempo and false as an account of its content: the decisions were fast, but their sizes, sequences and addressees were selected by the political conditions of the season, a trust vote survived in July, a terror attack absorbed in November, an election due in May, and a coalition arithmetic that made the state governments and the public sector banks the indispensable instruments. The technical framing is true of the instruments and false as an account of their incidence: the repo path and the excise path can each be written as a column of figures, but the figures landed on exporters in named clusters, on banks owned by the state and pressed by their owner, on states granted borrowing room as a favour within a federation, and on a middle class watching an index. The political reading defended here does not deny the other two their slices. It denies them the whole. The complication the brief for this article insists on, that the response ran through genuinely international coordination, the Group of Twenty process and the Fund's enlargement, qualifies the claim without overturning it: the coordination set the weather, but the architecture was national, and the choices that decided who bore the crisis in India were made in the allocation among liquidity, deficit and state borrowing that the coordination machine struck in New Delhi.

The settlement of 2008-09 entered the longer arc of this pillar as the mirror image of the settlement of 1991. The earlier crisis, whose politics are traced in [the Rao government's silent reforms](/2019/05/26/1991-rao-government-silent-reforms/), was answered by shrinking the state's economic footprint under external duress, with the state pleading necessity. The crisis of 2008 was answered by expanding the state's footprint from a position of reserve strength, with the state claiming credit, and the distance between those two postures is the distance Indian economic politics travelled in seventeen years, from a finance ministry that apologised for the market to a finance ministry that conscripted it. The instruments of 2008 also complete a pattern that this series has tracked in constitutional dress: as the pillar's constitutional articles use [the most-consequential-constitutional-moment framework](/2020/07/28/basic-structure-most-consequential-doctrine/) to rank the moments when a settlement was struck in a courtroom, the crisis of 2008 ranks as the moment when the economic settlement was struck in a treasury, and it was struck, like the constitutional ones, by named people arguing from named positions under a deadline. The legitimacy that the rescue accumulated for state action then fed the politics of the years after, including the audit and disclosure politics carried in [the anti-corruption-political-architectural-substantive-content framework](/2020/12/12/2005-rti-anti-corruption-politics/), where the spending state built in the crisis winter met the citizen armed with the file. And the whole sequence sits inside [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/) that the series applies to the decades after: who counts as fully Indian was not the question of the crisis winter, but who governs whom, and which past the state is continuous with, were asked in every package, and answered, for a season, in favour of the state that spent.

A word is owed on the sources, because the disagreement this article adjudicates is partly a disagreement among witnesses. The two governors who held the Reserve Bank across the Lehman weekend each published a memoir, Reddy's Advice and Dissent in 2017 and Subbarao's Who Moved My Interest Rate? in 2016, and each memoir is a brief for its author's tenure as well as a record of it: Reddy's restraint is vindicated by the crisis his successor managed, and Subbarao's activism is vindicated by the recovery his predecessor's restraint made affordable, and a reader who notices that each book's argument requires the other man's virtue has understood the episode's institutional truth better than either brief states it alone. The announcements, reviews and budget speeches are the check on both, being dated, numbered and incapable of revision, and this article has preferred them wherever testimony and document diverge. Rajan's Fault Lines, written from the University of Chicago by an economist who had warned the central banking profession in 2005 and would himself hold the Reserve Bank from 2013, stands outside the two briefs and has been used as the measuring frame for both. The named disagreement among crisis management, technical policy and political architecture is, at the level of sources, a disagreement about which of these documents is allowed to organise the story. The filing and the rate tables organise the first two readings. The correspondence, the calendars and the verdict organise the third, and the third is the one that explains why the first two took the shapes they did.

The claim, restated, is the brief's claim and the record's: the post-September 2008 Lehman collapse Indian response operates as political architecture, its five features run from a bankruptcy filing in New York to a rate increase in Mumbai nineteen months later, and the map of those features captures what the framings of crisis management and of technical policy cannot, because the architecture is where the crisis was actually decided.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How did Lehman Brothers collapse affect India?

Lehman Brothers filed for Chapter 11 protection on 15 September 2008 listing 639 billion dollars of assets, and the shock reached India through prices, flows and credit rather than through toxic assets, which Indian banks barely held. Foreign institutional investors sold about 13 billion dollars of Indian equities across calendar 2008, the Sensex fell from a closing peak of 20,873 in January 2008 to a closing trough of 8,160 in March 2009, the rupee slid past 52 to the dollar, and the money market seized as mutual funds and non-bank lenders lost rollover funding. Exports contracted for thirteen months from October 2008, industrial production printed a negative month in December 2008, and quarterly growth fell to 5.3 percent, answered by Reserve Bank easing, three fiscal packages and a reserve drawdown.

### Q: What was the post-2008 RBI rate-cut response?

The Reserve Bank of India, under Governor D. Subbarao, reversed the tightening it had inherited in a seven-month operation from October 2008 to April 2009. The repo rate was cut in six steps, from 9 percent on 20 October 2008 to 4.75 percent on 21 April 2009, a total of 425 basis points, and the reverse repo rate fell from 6 percent to 3.25 percent. The cash reserve ratio was reduced in five announced moves from 9 percent to 5 percent, and the statutory liquidity ratio was cut from 25 percent to 24 percent in November 2008. Around the rate path the Bank opened refinance windows for housing, small industry and exports, a rupee-dollar swap facility for banks short of foreign currency, and an assurance of government securities purchases. Its own retrospective accounting put the liquidity made available at about 5.6 trillion rupees, 10.5 percent of gross domestic product.

### Q: What was the post-2008 fiscal stimulus in India?

The fiscal stimulus was delivered in three packages announced outside the budget cycle. The first, on 7 December 2008, sanctioned 20,000 crore rupees of additional plan expenditure, cut the general excise rate by four percentage points from 14 to 10 percent, subsidised export credit and authorised infrastructure refinancing, a package valued at about 30,700 crore rupees. The second, on 2 January 2009, freed the states to borrow an additional 30,000 crore rupees, raised the foreign investment ceiling in corporate debt from 6 to 15 billion dollars and eased external borrowing norms. The third, on 24 February 2009, cut excise further to 8 percent and service tax from 12 to 10 percent. Assessments built on the budget documents put the three packages' burden at about 1.8 percent of gross domestic product in 2008-09, inside a central fiscal deficit that closed the year at 6.0 percent.

### Q: Who was D. Subbarao in 2008?

Duvvuri Subbarao was the Governor of the Reserve Bank of India who managed the monetary response to the crisis. A career civil servant of the 1972 batch of the Indian Administrative Service, he had served as Secretary to Prime Minister Manmohan Singh before taking charge of the Reserve Bank on 5 September 2008, ten days before the Lehman filing, succeeding Y. V. Reddy. He inherited a repo rate and a cash reserve ratio both set at 9 percent against inflation of 12.63 percent, and within seven months had cut the repo rate to 4.75 percent and the reserve ratio to 5 percent. He later directed the exit from crisis policy from October 2009 and raised rates through 2010 and 2011 against returning inflation. His memoir, Who Moved My Interest Rate?, published in 2016, is a principal participant account of the episode and of his dealings with the finance ministry.

### Q: How did India avoid the worst of the 2008 crisis?

India's relative escape rested on manufactured insulation rather than on distance from the world economy. Its banks held almost none of the structured mortgage assets that destroyed Western balance sheets, because the Reserve Bank under Y. V. Reddy had raised provisioning and risk weights and capped property and market exposures through the boom. The capital account was only partly open, with debt flows rationed by ceilings, so the sudden stop struck portfolio equity hardest and the banking system hardly at all. A large reserve stock, 314.6 billion dollars at its May 2008 peak, funded an orderly currency adjustment. Domestic demand, a rural employment guarantee already in place and workers' remittances of the order of 47 billion dollars in 2008-09 cushioned consumption. The shock still cut quarterly growth to 5.3 percent, contracted exports for thirteen months and cost the export clusters heavily in employment, so the escape was a matter of degree, decided by earlier policy choices.

### Q: What is the post-2008 capital flows architecture?

The term names the settlement that governed India's external finance through the crisis: a convertible current account, a capital account opened in calibrated pieces, portfolio equity admitted freely, portfolio debt rationed by ceilings, external commercial borrowing licensed by category, and a large reserve stock held by the Reserve Bank as insurance. The crisis stress-tested each piece. Foreign institutional investors withdrew about 13 billion dollars of equity in calendar 2008, reserves fell from 314.6 billion dollars in May 2008 to 252 billion dollars in March 2009 as the Bank sold dollars and absorbed valuation losses, and the rupee moved past 52 to the dollar. The response widened selected apertures under administrative control, raising the corporate debt ceiling for foreign investors from 6 to 15 billion dollars, raising interest ceilings on diaspora deposits and easing the borrowing rules, without putting the calibrated settlement itself in question.

### Q: What was the post-2008 export-architectural response?

The export response was built into the three stimulus packages and aimed at the labour-intensive lines where the collapse in Western demand destroyed orders first: textiles and garments, gems and jewellery, leather, handicrafts and carpets. The December 2008 package extended an interest subvention of 2 percent on export credit for those sectors, enlarged the funds for duty drawback and for terminal excise and central sales tax refunds owed to exporters, and removed export duties and bans imposed in the inflation season. The interim budget of 16 February 2009 recorded extended pre-shipment and post-shipment credit and a Committee of Secretaries to clear exporters' procedural grievances, the trapped refunds that turned a liquidity squeeze into insolvency for small firms. Merchandise exports had fallen 12.1 percent in October 2008, the first monthly contraction in five years, and the measures were calibrated to the clusters where export employment was densest.

### Q: What did the stimulus package of 7 December 2008 contain?

The first package, announced from the Prime Minister's Office on 7 December 2008 while Manmohan Singh held the finance portfolio himself, was valued at about 30,700 crore rupees. Its largest single item was 20,000 crore rupees of additional plan expenditure for 2008-09, directed to hold up public investment as private orders collapsed. Its boldest instrument was an across-the-board cut of four percentage points in the general rate of central excise duty, from 14 percent to 10 percent, with specified goods taken to lower slabs, a wager that producers would pass the cut into prices and defend volumes in automobiles, cement, textiles and consumer durables. For exporters it extended a 2 percent interest subvention on credit for labour-intensive sectors and enlarged refund provisions. It authorised the India Infrastructure Finance Company to raise 10,000 crore rupees in tax-free bonds to refinance bank lending to infrastructure, and it promised further measures for housing and for small and medium enterprises through the public sector banks.

### Q: What did the second stimulus package of 2 January 2009 do for the states?

The second package made the state governments its principal fiscal instrument, because the Centre's own balance sheet was already committed and the states held the shovel-ready expenditure. Presented by Montek Singh Ahluwalia, Deputy Chairman of the Planning Commission, on the morning the Reserve Bank cut the repo rate to 5.5 percent, the package permitted the states to raise additional market borrowing of 30,000 crore rupees, equivalent to half a percent of gross state domestic product, for plan expenditure in the remainder of 2008-09. The permission relaxed the fiscal discipline framework the states had accepted in the revenue-rich years, under which their deficits were capped and centrally monitored. Around the borrowing room the package raised the foreign institutional ceiling in corporate debt from 6 to 15 billion dollars, eased external commercial borrowing for housing and infrastructure, authorised the India Infrastructure Finance Company to refinance up to 60 percent of bank loans to public-private partnership projects, and withdrew inflation-era duty exemptions on cement and steel products.

### Q: Which taxes were cut in the announcement of 24 February 2009?

The third package, announced by Finance Minister Pranab Mukherjee on 24 February 2009, eight days after his interim budget, worked entirely through indirect tax rates. The general rate of central excise duty, cut from 14 percent to 10 percent in December 2008, was cut again to 8 percent. The service tax rate was reduced from 12 percent to 10 percent. The December concessions were carried forward into 2009-10 so that the relief would not lapse with the financial year, and the reduced excise structure was extended across the goods to which the December package had applied it. The announcement completed a six-point reduction in the general excise rate inside eleven weeks, from 14 percent to 8 percent, the fastest indirect tax easing of the liberalisation era. Its fiscal cost fell principally in 2009-10, which made it, in form, a promise by an outgoing government on behalf of its successor, redeemable because the same coalition expected, and proved able, to return and honour it.

### Q: Why did the Finance Ministry change hands in the middle of the crisis?

The change was forced by the Mumbai terrorist attacks of 26 to 29 November 2008, in which 166 people were killed at the Chhatrapati Shivaji Terminus, two hotels, a Jewish centre and other sites across the city. The political cost fell on the Home Ministry. Home Minister Shivraj Patil resigned on 30 November 2008, and Finance Minister P. Chidambaram was moved to the Home Ministry in his place, taking the government's most experienced crisis administrator to the security portfolio at the moment of its greatest exposure. Prime Minister Manmohan Singh held finance himself through December 2008 and most of January 2009, the interval in which the first stimulus package was announced. Pranab Mukherjee, the External Affairs Minister, received the finance portfolio in addition to his own on 24 January 2009 and carried the sequence from there, presenting the interim budget in February and the full budget in July. The reshuffle is why the three packages bear three different presenters: the Prime Minister's Office, the Planning Commission's deputy chairman and the new finance minister.

### Q: What did Manmohan Singh argue at the G-20 summits of November 2008 and April 2009?

At the first Group of Twenty leaders' summit at Washington on 15 November 2008, and at the London summit of 2 April 2009, Prime Minister Manmohan Singh argued that the crisis had been made in the developed world's financial system and that its costs must not be transferred to the developing world. He pressed for a coordinated fiscal stimulus across the major economies, for a large enlargement of the resources of the International Monetary Fund, and for reform of the Fund's quotas and voice toward the emerging economies whose weight the crisis had demonstrated. He argued against protectionism in trade and in finance, warning that the measures of the 1930s stood as the record of that error. The London summit agreed a package of 1.1 trillion dollars, including a tripling of the Fund's resources to 750 billion dollars. India attended both summits as a claimant on the system's redesign rather than as a borrower from it, a reversal of the country's position in the crisis of 1991 that the government's spokesmen underlined.

### Q: How large was the central fiscal deficit in 2008-09?

The central government's fiscal deficit closed 2008-09 at 6.0 percent of gross domestic product, against 2.5 percent in 2007-08 and a budget estimate for the crisis year of 2.5 percent when the budget was presented in February 2008. The widening was assembled from several layers: the farm debt waiver of 71,680 crore rupees announced in that February budget, the Sixth Central Pay Commission awards accepted in August 2008 with their arrears, food and fertiliser subsidies swollen by the global price spike, revenue lost to the excise and service tax cuts of the three stimulus packages, and the additional plan expenditure the packages sanctioned. The deficit for 2009-10 closed at 6.5 percent before narrowing to 4.9 percent in 2010-11. The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management targets stood formally relaxed through the period, with a return to consolidation promised in each budget speech and delivered on a slower schedule than the framework prescribed.

### Q: What did Y. V. Reddy claim in Advice and Dissent about India's insulation from the crisis?

In Advice and Dissent: My Life in Public Service, published in 2017, Y. V. Reddy, who demitted office as Governor of the Reserve Bank on 5 September 2008, argued that India's insulation was the harvest of deliberate prudential restraint exercised during the boom. His account points to the provisioning requirements and risk weights his Reserve Bank raised on real estate and capital market exposures from 2005 onward, to the limits placed on banks' exposure to the property and equity booms, and to the refusal to permit the securitised instruments and leverage chains that destroyed Western balance sheets. On this reading the absence of any Indian bank failure in 2008-09 was not luck or geography but the return on unpopular decisions. The claim is partly self-vindication and is read in this article against the announcement record, but its core is borne out by the balance sheets: the assets that failed elsewhere were absent in India because a regulator had kept them out, and the easing of 2008-09 was affordable because the restraint of 2005-08 preceded it.

### Q: How did industrial production perform in late 2008?

Industrial production contracted in the last quarter of 2008 as the export collapse and the credit freeze reached the factory floor. The index printed a fall of 2 percent year on year for December 2008, the figure Finance Minister Pranab Mukherjee read into the record of his interim budget speech on 16 February 2009, the weakest monthly performance of the decade to that point. Output growth for the third quarter of the financial year, October to December 2008, carried the economy to its slowest quarterly rate of the period, with gross domestic product growing 5.3 percent against a trend near 9 percent. The contraction was concentrated in the export-linked and interest-sensitive lines: textiles, gems and jewellery processing, leather, auto components and capital goods. Industrial output returned to double-digit growth in the second half of 2009 as the stimulus, the rate cuts and the revival of global trade reached the same factories, completing a V-shaped industrial path inside eighteen months.

### Q: How did inflation move between August 2008 and March 2009?

Wholesale price inflation executed a complete reversal inside seven months. It printed 12.63 percent in August 2008, a thirteen-year high built from food prices, fuel prices and the global commodity spike, and it was against that figure that the Reserve Bank under Y. V. Reddy had raised the repo rate to 9 percent in July and the cash reserve ratio to 9 percent by the end of August. The Lehman collapse broke global commodity prices, crude oil falling from 147 dollars a barrel in July 2008 to the neighbourhood of 40 dollars by December, and Indian wholesale inflation fell through the autumn with them, reaching the neighbourhood of zero by the middle of 2009 and printing negative readings in the summer of that year. The reversal licensed the Reserve Bank's easing politically and analytically: rate cuts that would have been indefensible against 12 percent inflation were orthodox against a deflating price level. Food inflation returned by the end of 2009, reaching the neighbourhood of 20 percent in December on the weekly prints, and reopened the price question for the exit years.

### Q: What foreign-currency facilities did the Reserve Bank open for banks after Lehman?

The Reserve Bank built a set of foreign currency windows because the crisis reached Indian banks and firms as a dollar famine: overseas lenders cancelled credit lines, foreign currency borrowing could not be rolled over, and the rupee's slide raised the local cost of every dollar owed. In November 2008 the Bank put in place a rupee-dollar swap facility under which banks could obtain dollars from the Reserve Bank against rupees, giving institutions with overseas branches and foreign obligations a central bank source for the currency the market would no longer supply. It raised the interest rate ceilings on non-resident deposits in the same season, to draw diaspora funds toward Indian banks. It permitted the premature buyback of foreign currency convertible bonds at market discounts, letting firms retire boom-era paper cheaply rather than default at par. And it sold dollars from the reserve stock into the currency market through the year, spending the stock from 314.6 billion dollars at the end of May 2008 to 252 billion dollars at the end of March 2009, to keep the rupee's descent orderly.

### Q: How did the 2009 general election judge the government's handling of the crisis?

The electorate enlarged the government's mandate. Counting on 16 May 2009 gave the Congress party 206 seats and the United Progressive Alliance 262, up from the coalition's 2004 tally, and Manmohan Singh was sworn in for a second term on 22 May 2009. The campaign had put the crisis at its centre: the Bharatiya Janata Party charged the government with the winter's job losses and the preceding year's inflation, and the Left charged that the stimulus was small and designed for the stock market. The government campaigned on steadiness and on growth of about 7 percent in a shrinking world. Post-poll analysis cautions against reading the result as a graded verdict on any single issue, since state-level judgements and incumbency rewards were assembled inside it. But the government read the result, and was entitled to read it, as ratification of its crisis management, and it spent that reading in defence of the deficit it had run and the exit it attempted next.

### Q: How did the farm debt waiver and the pay commission awards shape the fiscal position of 2008-09?

Both commitments predated the Lehman filing and together they meant that India entered the crisis with an expansion already running. The budget of February 2008, presented by Finance Minister P. Chidambaram, wrote off or relieved agricultural loans to the value of 71,680 crore rupees, the largest farm debt waiver in the country's history, with disbursement running through 2008-09. The Sixth Central Pay Commission's awards, accepted by the government in August 2008 with arrears payable across two years, raised the public wage and pension bill on a schedule fixed by the award rather than by the cycle. Food and fertiliser subsidies swollen by the global price spike added a third layer. When the deliberate stimulus of the three packages was layered onto these commitments, the central fiscal deficit widened from 2.5 percent of gross domestic product in 2007-08 to 6.0 percent in 2008-09. The government later argued that the pre-existing expansion was an accidental counter-cyclical stance; its critics answered that almost none of it arrived where the shock had landed, in exports and industry.

### Q: Who presented the interim budget of February 2009, and why was it an interim budget?

Pranab Mukherjee presented the interim budget to the Lok Sabha on 16 February 2009, three weeks after receiving the finance portfolio on 24 January 2009 and while also holding external affairs. It was an interim budget because the fifteenth general election was due by May 2009 and the term of the government and of the Lok Sabha was ending: the instrument sought a vote on account, parliamentary authority to fund the state's expenditure for the opening months of 2009-10, rather than a full year's budget with new policy. Mukherjee used the accompanying speech as the government's statement of the crisis for the parliamentary record. It put the advance estimate of growth for 2008-09 at 7.1 percent, called India at that rate the second fastest growing economy in the world, recited the stimulus packages of 7 December 2008 and 2 January 2009 as prompt and substantial action, and recorded the fall of industrial production by 2 percent in December 2008. The full budget followed on 6 July 2009, presented by Mukherjee again, for the government the election had returned.
