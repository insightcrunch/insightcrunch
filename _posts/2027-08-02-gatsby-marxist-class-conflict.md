Tom Buchanan does not lose his wife in the parlor of the Plaza Hotel. He wins a jurisdictional dispute. Reading class conflict through a Marxist lens changes what that afternoon is: not a husband defeating a rival for a woman's affection, but an established fortune putting down a challenge from an unestablished one, using the only weapon that settles such disputes, which is the question of where the money came from. Tom does not argue that he loves Daisy more. He produces evidence about drugstores. That is not jealousy. That is a credit check.

![Class conflict through a Marxist lens in The Great Gatsby explained - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The novel invites a personal reading because its surface is personal. Two men want the same woman, a mechanic's wife dies under a car, a mechanic kills the wrong man. Every event has a name attached to it and a motive that sounds like feeling. What a Marxist reading does is refuse to stop at the name. It asks what economic position each character occupies, what that position gives them the power to do, and whose labor holds the whole arrangement up. When those questions are put to the summer of 1922 on Long Island, the love triangle reorganizes itself into something with three sides rather than two, and the third side, the one that does the work and gets buried in the second-to-last chapter, turns out to be the side the other two have in common.

This article takes the class-conflict facet of the Marxist reading and works it all the way down. The full theoretical apparatus, the concepts of base and superstructure, commodification, false consciousness, and ideology, belongs to [the complete Marxist reading of the novel](/2027/07/26/marxist-reading-great-gatsby/), which is the hub for this method. What follows here is the conflict dimension specifically: who is fighting whom, over what, with which weapons, and why the novel keeps disguising the fight as a romance.

## What Class Conflict Through a Marxist Lens Actually Means

Start with a distinction that decides whether the reading will be any good. Analyzing class conflict through a Marxist lens is not the same as noticing that the novel contains rich people and poor people and that the rich ones behave badly. That observation is available without any theory at all, and a reader who stops there has produced a moral verdict, not a structural analysis. The moral verdict says the Buchanans are careless. The structural analysis asks what social position makes carelessness affordable, who absorbs the cost of it, and why the arrangement reproduces itself rather than collapsing.

Conflict in the Marxist sense is not personal hostility. It is the antagonism built into a relationship where one group's position depends on another group's labor. Two men can loathe each other without their loathing being structural, and two men can be perfectly polite while occupying positions that are structurally opposed. Tom is jovial with George Wilson. He slaps him on the shoulder, calls him old man, and asks after business. Nothing in the exchange looks like conflict. But Tom owns a car Wilson needs to sell in order to leave, Tom controls when the sale happens, and Tom is sleeping with Wilson's wife in an apartment Wilson does not know exists. The friendliness is not evidence against antagonism. It is the form the antagonism takes when one party holds every card.

That is the first thing the lens supplies: a way of reading pleasantness as a symptom rather than as a fact about character. Tom's geniality toward Wilson is not a redeeming feature. It is what dominance sounds like when it is unchallenged. The moment a challenge appears, from Gatsby rather than from Wilson, the geniality vanishes and the vocabulary changes to swindler, bootlegger, and Mr. Nobody from Nowhere.

### What is class conflict in The Great Gatsby?

Class conflict in the novel is the structural antagonism among three economic positions: inherited wealth represented by the Buchanans, newly acquired wealth represented by Gatsby, and the working poor of the valley of ashes represented by the Wilsons. These groups contend over legitimacy, mobility, and survival, and the novel stages that contention as personal rivalry.

The second thing the lens supplies is a rule about evidence. A Marxist reading is not permitted to assert antagonism where the text does not show it. It has to point at scenes. This matters because the reading has a bad reputation among students who have seen it done lazily, as a set of assertions about capitalism draped over a plot summary. Done properly it is one of the most evidence-hungry methods available, because every claim about a structural relationship has to be cashed out in a specific transaction, a specific piece of property, or a specific exercise of power that the novel actually depicts. Fitzgerald gives an unusual amount of this material. He tells you what Nick pays in rent. He tells you what Wilson charges Tom for gas. He tells you how long the Wilsons have lived above the garage. Those numbers are not decoration. They are the substrate a class analysis works on.

The third thing the lens supplies is a question about disguise. If the novel's real conflicts are economic, why does it present them as emotional? A weak answer says Fitzgerald was hiding something or did not understand his own material. A stronger answer says the disguise is itself the subject. Characters in this book experience structural antagonism as personal feeling because that is how such antagonism is actually experienced by the people inside it. Wilson does not think he is a proletarian whose surplus labor has been appropriated. He thinks his wife is cheating and he wants to move West. Gatsby does not think he is a fraction of upstart capital seeking legitimation. He thinks he is in love. The novel is accurate about how economic positions feel from the inside, and the Marxist reading recovers the shape those feelings have from the outside.

## Marxist Criticism in Plain Terms: The Concepts a Conflict Reading Needs

The full theoretical vocabulary lives in the hub article. A conflict reading needs four ideas, and they can be stated without jargon.

The first is that economic position is not the same as income. A person's position is defined by their relationship to productive property, which means whether they own the things other people work with or whether they work with things other people own. This distinction explains something a simple rich-and-poor split cannot. Nick earns a salary in the bond business and rents an eyesore for eighty dollars a month, so by income he is closer to Wilson than to Tom. By position and by origin he is closer to Tom, since he is a Yale man from a comfortable Midwestern family with money behind him. His income and his position point in different directions, which is exactly why he can move through every setting in the novel and be tolerated in all of them.

The second is that classes are not blocks but coalitions with internal seams. The rich in this novel are not one group. They are at least two, and the antagonism between them drives the plot more visibly than the antagonism between rich and poor. Marxist criticism has a standard term for this, the fraction, meaning a subdivision of a class with its own interests, its own sources of wealth, and its own claim to legitimacy. Old money and new money are fractions of the same class. They share an interest in the arrangement that produces their wealth and they fight bitterly over who gets to be respectable inside it. The novel devotes most of its dramatic energy to the fight between fractions and almost none to the fight between the class as a whole and the people beneath it, and that imbalance is itself something a conflict reading has to explain rather than ignore.

The third is that ideology is not lying. Ideology in this sense is the set of ideas that make an arrangement feel natural to the people inside it, including the people it disadvantages. Myrtle's aspiration is ideological in this precise sense. She does not want the arrangement changed. She wants a better position inside it, and she believes the difference between her position and Daisy's is a matter of quality rather than of structure. Her famous complaint about her husband, that she married him because she thought he was a gentleman and that he was not fit to lick her shoe, is a class judgment delivered in the vocabulary of personal worth. She has taken a structural fact, that Wilson owns a failing garage in an industrial dumping ground, and converted it into an insult about breeding.

The fourth is that the invisible is evidence. A conflict reading pays attention to who is not shown. Gatsby's parties are staffed. Somebody carries the crates of oranges and lemons in and the pulpless halves out. Somebody repairs the house, mows the lawn, and cleans up after several hundred guests. Fitzgerald mentions the caterers and a corps of servants in passing and then leaves them out of every scene that matters. The Buchanan household runs on a butler whose most memorable action is answering a telephone. This absence is not an oversight and it is not a failure of the novel. It is a precise reproduction of how such labor appears to the people who benefit from it, which is to say not at all, and it becomes readable the moment the lens asks who is missing.

### How is a Marxist reading different from just saying the rich are bad?

The moral reading judges individuals and stops. A Marxist reading traces the system that produces those individuals: who owns what, whose labor supports whom, and which conflicts follow from those positions rather than from personality. It replaces the verdict that Tom is cruel with the analysis of what social position makes his cruelty consequence-free.

## The Three Contending Classes and What They Fight Over

Here is the artifact this article contributes to the series, a map of the three-front conflict that the personal plot conceals. Call it the three-front map. Its usefulness is that it separates fights a summary treats as one fight, and it makes visible a front the summary does not treat at all.

| Contending position | Source of its power | Who it fights, and over what | The scene that shows it | What the lens exposes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Established wealth: Tom and Daisy Buchanan, East Egg | Inherited capital, family name, institutional standing at Yale and in the sporting world | Fights new money over legitimacy and over the right to define who counts as respectable | The Plaza Hotel confrontation, chapter seven | That legitimacy is a weapon, not a quality, and that it is deployed only when threatened |
| New wealth: Jay Gatsby, West Egg | Illicit accumulation through bootlegging and securities fraud, spent on display | Fights old money for admission; fights nobody beneath him because he has forgotten that position exists | Tom's investigation into the drugstores, chapter seven | That the challenger wants entry, not change, and so cannot win a fight whose rules the incumbent writes |
| The working poor: George and Myrtle Wilson, the ash-grey men, the servants | Labor power alone, sold in a market with no leverage | Fights to leave rather than to win; has no organized antagonist and so turns its conflict inward | Wilson's request to buy Tom's car, chapter seven | That the base holding both wealthy fractions up is dramatized only when it dies |
| The mediator: Nick Carraway | Salaried professional with inherited social access | Fights nobody; converts structural antagonism into moral commentary | The final judgment on careless people, chapter nine | That the observer's neutrality is itself a position with material conditions |

The map earns its keep by showing that the novel's three fronts have wildly unequal amounts of narrative attention. The old-money-against-new-money front gets the Plaza scene, the party sequences, the investigation, and the entire back half of the book. The capital-against-labor front gets the opening of chapter two and Myrtle's death. The third front, the one inside the working poor, gets the apartment party and Wilson's locked upstairs room. A conflict reading has to account for that distribution rather than pretending the novel weighted the fronts evenly.

The namable claim this article defends follows from the map, and it can be stated in one sentence. Personal drama in The Great Gatsby is class war conducted by other means: the rivalry between Gatsby and Tom is a fight between fractions of capital over legitimacy, and the deaths of Myrtle and Gatsby are the cost that fight imposes on the people standing closest to it. The love triangle is not a cover story invented by the novel to hide the economics. It is the form the economics takes when the participants have no vocabulary for what is happening to them.

## The First Front: Established Money Against New Money

The fight the novel actually dramatizes is a fight within the owning class. Tom and Gatsby are both rich. Neither works in any sense that would be recognized as labor. Both live on the same body of water, both have houses larger than they need, both command the time and attention of other people without paying for it in a way the novel records. On any measure of consumption they belong to the same group. What separates them is provenance.

Fitzgerald establishes Tom's provenance in the first chapter and does it through inheritance rather than achievement. Tom's family were enormously wealthy, and Nick notes that Tom had been one of the most powerful ends that ever played football at New Haven. Money and institutional standing arrive together and arrive before Tom does anything. The polo ponies make the point sharpest. Nick reports that Tom had brought down a string of polo ponies from Lake Forest and adds that it was hard to realize that a man in his own generation was wealthy enough to do that. The detail is not about horses. It is about a form of wealth so settled that it can be moved around the country for amusement, and about the fact that Nick, who is himself from a comfortable family, finds the scale of it disorienting.

Then Fitzgerald establishes Gatsby's provenance and does it in reverse, by withholding it. For four chapters nobody knows where the money came from, and the not knowing is the plot. Jordan tells Nick that Gatsby told her once he was an Oxford man and adds immediately that she does not believe it. The rumors at the parties are that he killed a man, that he was a German spy. What the guests are actually doing when they trade these stories is running an informal audit. They are trying to determine the provenance of the money they are drinking, because provenance is what decides whether the host is a member of their world or a curiosity in it.

### Why does Tom attack Gatsby's money instead of his character?

Because provenance is the only ground on which Tom can win. Gatsby is younger, better looking, more attentive to Daisy, and unencumbered by a mistress in the valley. Tom's single decisive advantage is that his fortune arrived legitimately by the rules his own group wrote, and he presses that advantage the moment the confrontation opens.

Notice the sequence in the Plaza suite. Tom opens with a challenge to Gatsby's social identity rather than his conduct: "Who are you, anyhow?" He follows with the affiliation, that Gatsby is one of that bunch that hangs around with Meyer Wolfshiem. Then he produces the investigation, saying that he had made a little investigation into Gatsby's affairs and would carry it further tomorrow. Only then does he deliver the finding, that he had found out what the drugstores were, and that Gatsby and Wolfshiem bought up a lot of side-street drugstores in New York and Chicago and sold grain alcohol over the counter, adding that he had picked Gatsby for a bootlegger the first time he saw him and had not been far wrong.

Read that as an emotional outburst and it is a jealous husband throwing whatever he can find. Read it structurally and it is a member of the owning class performing an act of exclusion using the class's own machinery. The word he chooses at the peak of the scene is not adulterer or liar. It is swindler, and he pairs it with a claim about property, saying that Daisy is not leaving him, certainly not for a common swindler who would have to steal the ring he put on her finger. The insult is that Gatsby's money is not his, which in this world is the only insult that disqualifies.

Gatsby's counterattack proves the fronts are not what they appear. He does not deny the drugstores. He says of Tom's friend Walter Chase that he guessed Chase was not too proud to come in on it, and that Chase came to them dead broke and was very glad to pick up some money. That is a devastating reply on the merits, because it demonstrates that the boundary Tom is defending is porous, that respectable people cross it whenever they need cash, and that the difference between the fractions is a difference of visibility rather than of conduct. It is also a reply that concedes the entire framework. Gatsby is not arguing that the boundary should not exist. He is arguing that he should be on the right side of it. He wants Tom's approval structurally even while refusing it personally, which is why the fight between the two men reads as a struggle over admission rather than a struggle over the system that decides admissions.

That is the pattern worth carrying into an essay. The relationship between [old money and new money in the novel](/2024/05/20/old-money-new-money-gatsby/) is antagonistic at the level of legitimacy and identical at the level of interest. Neither man proposes any change to an arrangement in which enormous fortunes buy enormous houses on a shore where the people who built them cannot afford to live.

## The Second Front: Capital Against Labor in the Valley of Ashes

The front the novel does not dramatize is the one that holds up both sides of the front it does. Fitzgerald opens chapter two with the valley of ashes, and the passage is the single most concentrated piece of economic writing in the book. He describes a fantastic farm where ashes grow like wheat into ridges and hills and grotesque gardens. The metaphor is agricultural, which is the first signal: this is a place of production, and what it produces is waste. Then he populates it. The ashes take the forms of houses and chimneys and rising smoke and, with a transcendent effort, of ash-grey men, who move dimly and already crumbling through the powdery air.

The men are made of the same material as the landscape. They are not workers in a place; they are an outgrowth of it, and the verb Fitzgerald attaches to them is crumbling, in the present participle, ongoing. Then the crucial sentence for a conflict reading. The ash-grey men swarm up with leaden spades and stir up an impenetrable cloud, which screens their obscure operations from your sight. Labor here is doubly obscured: obscured by the cloud its own work raises, and obscured by the pronoun, since Fitzgerald says the cloud screens the operations from your sight, addressing the reader as someone who is passing by and being prevented from seeing.

That sentence is the thesis of the front. The work that sustains this economy is performed in a cloud of its own making, in a place the motor road and the railroad hurry past, and the novel's narrator sees it exactly twice in nine chapters, both times because he is being taken there by Tom. The valley as [a wasteland of moral and economic exhaustion](/2025/05/12/valley-of-ashes-wasteland/) has been read many ways; the class-conflict reading insists on the least metaphorical one, which is that it is a workplace.

### How does the valley of ashes work as the exploited base?

It supplies the labor and absorbs the waste that keep East Egg and West Egg habitable. Its residents sell fuel, repair cars, and dispose of refuse for the wealthy who drive through it, and they receive in return neither wealth nor mobility. When the wealthy need something removed, whether ashes or a body, the valley takes it.

Now look at how Fitzgerald renders the one member of that front he individuates. George Wilson appears as a blond, spiritless man, anaemic, and faintly handsome. When Tom's car pulls in, a damp gleam of hope sprang into his light blue eyes. Hope, in Wilson's case, is a transactional reflex: a customer has arrived. His garage interior is unprosperous and bare, and Nick's first thought is that this shadow of a garage must be a blind concealing sumptuous and romantic apartments overhead. That guess is wrong and its wrongness is the point. Nick, who has just come from the Buchanan lawn, cannot initially believe that a business could simply be failing. He assumes a hidden richness because visible poverty is outside his range.

The exchange that follows is a masterpiece of asymmetry rendered as small talk. Tom slaps Wilson on the shoulder and says, "Hello, Wilson, old man," and asks, "How's business?" Wilson answers unconvincingly, "I can't complain," and then immediately asks when Tom is going to sell him the car. Tom says next week, that he has got his man working on it now. This is repeated in chapter seven, months later, with the car still unsold. The transaction Wilson needs to change his life is one Tom can defer indefinitely at no cost, and Tom defers it. That is not cruelty in the psychological sense. It is the ordinary operation of a relationship in which one party's urgent need is the other party's minor errand.

By chapter seven the deferral has consequences. Wilson tells Tom he has been there too long, that he wants to get away, that he and his wife want to go West. When Tom asks what he wants money for all of a sudden, Wilson says he just got wised up to something funny the last two days. Tom, alarmed for entirely personal reasons, asks twice what he owes. Wilson answers, "Dollar twenty." Set that figure beside the polo ponies and the map draws itself. The same afternoon on which a man is discussing a dollar and twenty cents of gasoline, another man is renting a suite at the Plaza to have an argument.

The final detail on this front is the one most often missed. Michaelis is the only other named worker in the valley: the young Greek who ran the coffee joint beside the ash-heaps and was the principal witness at the inquest. He is the person who sits with Wilson through the night, who tries to get him to eat, who notices what Wilson is staring at. The novel's only sustained act of care by one character for another in extremity is performed by a small proprietor for a smaller one, in the district everyone else drives through. Neither Egg supplies anybody to that vigil.

## The Third Front: Conflict Inside the Working Poor

The front the lens exposes that a theme summary usually skips is the one that runs inside the subordinate group rather than between groups. Myrtle Wilson is not fighting Tom Buchanan. She is fighting her husband, her position, and by extension the other women in her building. Her antagonism runs sideways and downward, never upward, and the novel is exact about why.

Consider what Myrtle actually wants. In the apartment on 158th Street she announces that she is going to make a list of all the things she has got to get, and the list is a massage and a wave, a collar for the dog, one of those cute little ashtrays where you touch a spring, and a wreath with a black silk bow for her mother's grave. The items are a portrait of aspiration in miniature: services, ornaments, a small luxury with a mechanism, and an act of filial display. Not one of them changes her position. Every one of them performs a position she does not have. She is buying the visible surface of a life she cannot enter, and she is buying it with money that arrives from the man whose wife already has the life.

Her grievance against Wilson is delivered in the same currency. She married him because she thought he was a gentleman, she says, and thought he knew something about breeding, but he was not fit to lick her shoe. Breeding is the operative word. It is the vocabulary of inherited standing borrowed by a woman living above a garage, and she uses it against the only person in her world with less leverage than she has. Catherine supplies the material fact that anchors the whole scene: they have been living over that garage for eleven years. Eleven years of the same two rooms, and the conflict that eleven years generates is aimed at the man in the next room rather than at the arrangement that put them both there.

Tom's participation in this front is instructive because it costs him nothing. He rings for the janitor and sends him for some celebrated sandwiches. A janitor is on call in a building Tom does not live in, and Tom's command of that man's time is so unremarkable that Nick reports it in a subordinate clause. The apartment party is often read as a study in vulgarity. Read as a scene of conflict, it is a demonstration that the party's host has no power over anyone present except the people already below her, while the guest who says almost nothing controls the room, the lease, the sandwiches, and, when Myrtle says Daisy's name too many times, her face.

### Why does Myrtle attack her husband rather than the system?

Because the system is not visible to her as a system. She experiences her position as a personal misfortune, a bad marriage to a man without breeding, and she pursues a personal remedy, an affair with a man who has it. Her conflict runs sideways because that is the only direction where she has any leverage at all.

The reading extends to Wilson, whose response to catastrophe is theological rather than economic. He tells Michaelis what he said to his wife, that God knows what she has been doing, that she may fool him but she cannot fool God, and then stares at the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg emerging from the dissolving night and repeats that God sees everything. Michaelis, who knows the neighborhood, tells him flatly that it is an advertisement. Wilson does not hear it. A man crushed by an economic arrangement he cannot name locates his grievance in a billboard for an eye doctor, and the last act of his life is to shoot the wrong rich man. It is difficult to construct a more precise image of misdirected class rage.

## Gatsby's Own Position: The Challenger Who Forgot the Bottom

The most revealing fact about Jay Gatsby, for a conflict reading, is where he started. Fitzgerald puts it in chapter six with no softening. His parents were shiftless and unsuccessful farm people, and Nick adds that Gatsby's imagination had never accepted them as his parents at all. Before Dan Cody's yacht he had spent over a year beating his way along the south shore of Lake Superior as a clam-digger and a salmon-fisher, or in any other capacity that brought him food and bed. That is not poverty as a background detail. That is a young man selling his labor by the day in a market with no leverage, which places him, at seventeen, in exactly the position George Wilson occupies at forty.

The invention of Jay Gatsby is therefore a change of position accomplished by the only route available, which is a change of identity. He does not organize, agitate, or improve his terms. He replaces himself. Fitzgerald describes the new self as sprung from a Platonic conception, dedicated to the service of a vast, vulgar, and meretricious beauty. The adjectives are doing double work: vulgar and meretricious are class words, applied by a narrator whose own family had money, to the aesthetic of a man who acquired his late. Even the sentence that grants Gatsby his mythic dimension marks him as new.

### Does Gatsby's origin change how the conflict reads?

It changes everything. A challenger who began as a day laborer and ends by buying a mansion has crossed the entire structure, and his refusal to acknowledge the bottom he came from is the reading's sharpest irony. He has the experience that would let him see the valley clearly, and he never once looks at it.

That is the finding worth pressing. Gatsby drives through the valley of ashes constantly. His car is the one Myrtle runs toward. He knows what the road between West Egg and Manhattan looks like because he has traveled it for years. And in nine chapters he never remarks on it, never recognizes anything of himself in Wilson, never registers that the men crumbling through the powdery air are doing the work he did on Lake Superior. His entire attention runs the other way, toward a dock light on the far side of the bay. A man who has climbed out of the base and now stands with his back to it is not a revolutionary figure; he is a defector, and the class-conflict reading has to say so even while granting him the sympathy Fitzgerald so plainly extends.

This explains why his money buys him nothing. Gatsby has the material means of the owning class and none of its social credit. He can produce the house, the shirts, the hydroplane, the parties, and he cannot produce a family, a school, or a provenance, which are the assets that actually confer standing. His five-year accumulation was directed at a target that money does not reach, and Tom's investigation demonstrates this in a single afternoon. The wider [theme of wealth and class in the novel](/2024/05/13/wealth-class-great-gatsby/) covers what the book argues about money generally; what the conflict reading adds is the specific mechanism by which a fortune can be large and still be worthless in the only market its owner cares about.

## The Conflict Nobody Fights: Servants, Caterers, and the Party Machine

There is a fourth group in this novel with no representative, no scene of its own, and no name beyond a job title, and its treatment is the strongest single piece of evidence that the book's economy is real rather than decorative.

Chapter three inventories the machinery of a Gatsby party with an accountant's precision. On Mondays eight servants, including an extra gardener, toiled all day with mops and scrubbing-brushes and hammers and garden-shears, repairing the ravages of the night before. Five crates of oranges and lemons arrived every Friday from a fruiterer in New York and left every Monday as a pyramid of pulpless halves. A machine in the kitchen could extract the juice of two hundred oranges in half an hour if a little button was pressed two hundred times by a butler's thumb. At least once a fortnight a corps of caterers came down with several hundred feet of canvas.

That last figure is the one to sit with. Two hundred presses of a button by a single thumb, every half hour, so that guests who have not been invited can drink something cold. Fitzgerald gives the number and the thumb and then moves on to the guest list, which runs for pages. The proportions of the paragraph reproduce the proportions of the world it describes.

The Buchanan household supplies the sharpest instance, and it arrives as a joke. Daisy offers Nick a family secret about the butler's nose, and the story is that he was not always a butler; he used to be the silver polisher for some people in New York that had a silver service for two hundred people, and he had to polish it from morning till night until it began to affect his nose. A man's body was damaged by his work. His employers' response was to reassign him. Daisy's response, years later, is to serve the episode as an anecdote at dinner. It is the only biography of a working person the novel supplies in full, it is told for amusement by the woman whose class it served, and it is the single best miniature of how labor registers in this world.

The pattern holds to the end. When Gatsby goes to the pool he leaves word with the butler that if anyone phoned word was to be brought to him. No message arrives, but the butler went without his sleep and waited for it until four o'clock. That is a man staying awake all night for an employer who is already dead, and the novel gives it one sentence. When the body is found, Nick reports that four of them, the chauffeur, butler, gardener, and he, hurried down to the pool. Three job titles and a name.

The critical move here is not to accuse Fitzgerald of ignoring these people. It is to notice that the invisibility is consistent, total, and matched exactly to the perceptions of the characters through whom the novel is focalized. Nick sees what a man in his position sees. Service appears to him the way weather appears, as a condition rather than as labor performed by individuals with histories of their own, which is why the butler's nose can be funny to Daisy and merely odd to Nick, and why neither of them notices that they have just described an industrial injury. The one time the novel breaks the pattern is when it individuates Michaelis, a proprietor rather than an employee, and even then it does so because he is needed as a witness.

For an essay, this is the section where a class-conflict reading proves it is doing more than restating the wealth theme. Anyone can observe that the novel contrasts rich and poor. Only a structural reading treats a systematic silence as evidence, asks what the silence is consistent with, and answers that it is consistent with an economy so thoroughly naturalized that its beneficiaries cannot see it operating even when it is carrying their oranges.

## The Plaza Hotel: Where the Conflict Surfaces

Chapter seven is where the buried structure comes up into the air, and it is the passage to work on if an essay needs a single scene. Fitzgerald arranges the geography deliberately: the party drives from East Egg through the valley, stops at Wilson's garage where the gasoline transaction and the request to go West take place, and continues into Manhattan to a rented suite. The route runs from inherited wealth, through the labor that sustains it, to a neutral space rented by the hour for the confrontation. Every position on the map is crossed in one afternoon.

Tom's opening move in the suite is not about Daisy. He complains that people begin by sneering at family life and family institutions and that next they will throw everything overboard. Nick observes that Tom, flushed with his impassioned gibberish, saw himself standing alone on the last barrier of civilization. That is the ideological register: Tom experiences a challenge to his marriage as a challenge to civilization, which is precisely how a threatened dominant position understands itself. Jordan's dry response, that they are all white there, punctures the register by naming what Tom means by civilization.

This is not the first time Tom has spoken this way. In chapter one he is agitated about a book, saying that civilization is going to pieces, that it is up to those who are the dominant race to watch out or these other races will have control of things. The continuity matters for a class analysis. Tom's racial theory and his contempt for Gatsby are the same instinct in two idioms. Both are defenses of an inherited position by a man who did nothing to earn it and who therefore has to explain his standing as a natural fact rather than a historical accident. When his position is challenged from below in the racial register he reaches for pseudoscience; when it is challenged from beside him in the economic register he reaches for the provenance of money. The article on [Tom's power, race, and brutality](/2023/07/24/tom-buchanan-power-and-brutality/) works this connection out at the level of character; the point here is that both idioms perform the same structural function.

The most quoted moment of the fight belongs to Gatsby rather than Tom, and it belongs to the class analysis more than to the romance. Nick remarks that Daisy has an indiscreet voice, hesitates, and Gatsby supplies the completion: her voice is full of money. Nick then says he had never understood before, and that it was full of money, the inexhaustible charm that rose and fell in it, the jingle of it. What Gatsby has done is name the object of his desire correctly for the first time, and the naming ruins nothing about his feeling. He is not disillusioned. He loves her more accurately. A conflict reading takes that as the clearest evidence available that the romance and the economics are not two subjects in this novel; the romance is how the economics is experienced by a man who has spent five years assembling capital in order to purchase a position, and who does not distinguish between the position and the woman who occupies it.

Tom wins the suite, and the way he wins is worth stating precisely. He does not out-argue Gatsby. He waits until Daisy is forced to make a claim about her own history and then reveals that Gatsby's demand, that she say she never loved Tom, cannot be met. But he only reaches that moment because he has already destroyed Gatsby's standing with the drugstore evidence. Daisy's retreat follows the disqualification. The order is not incidental. She does not choose Tom over Gatsby; she declines to leave a legitimate position for an illegitimate one after the illegitimacy has been established in front of witnesses.

## How the Personal Masks the Structural

The counter-reading this article has to answer is the obvious one, and it deserves a fair statement rather than a straw version. On that reading, the conflicts of The Great Gatsby are personal, and treating them as class antagonism replaces what is specific about the characters with a scheme that would fit any novel about money. Gatsby loves Daisy; Tom is possessive; Myrtle is unhappy; Wilson is broken by grief. These are motives with names and histories. Fitzgerald wrote a novel about longing, not a case study in economic relations, and a reading that converts every feeling into a position loses the thing the book is best at.

Take the objection seriously and it improves the analysis rather than defeating it. Three responses do the work.

First, the class reading does not deny the feelings; it explains why they take the shapes they take. Gatsby's longing is not generic longing. It is longing organized around a house across a bay, a dock light, a specific woman's voice, and five years of accumulation. Strip out the economics and the longing has no content. The novel supplies his origin precisely because it wants the reader to measure the distance: his parents were shiftless and unsuccessful farm people, and he spent over a year beating his way along the south shore of Lake Superior as a clam-digger and a salmon-fisher. A man who has done that work and then invents Jay Gatsby is not experiencing an emotion detached from his position. He is experiencing the emotion his position makes available.

Second, the novel itself performs the conversion of structure into feeling, repeatedly and visibly, which suggests the disguise is the subject. Myrtle's structural grievance becomes an insult about breeding. Wilson's structural grievance becomes a search for his wife's lover. Tom's structural anxiety becomes a lecture about civilization. Gatsby's structural exclusion becomes a five-year courtship. If four characters independently translate an economic position into a personal emotion, the translation is a pattern the novel is exhibiting rather than a confusion the critic is imposing.

Third, the personal reading cannot account for the outcomes. If the conflicts were personal, the results would track personal qualities, and they do not. Gatsby is more devoted than Tom, more generous, and considerably more attentive, and he ends face down in a pool. Myrtle is more alive than Daisy by any measure the novel offers, and she ends on a workshop table. Wilson is guilty of nothing, and he ends in the ashes. The two people who die are the two who attempted to move between positions, and the two who survive without inconvenience are the two who were born where they intended to stay. Outcomes that sort by position rather than by conduct are the signature of a structural cause.

### Are the novel's conflicts personal or structural?

Both, and the relationship between them is the point. The characters experience structural antagonism as personal feeling because they have no other vocabulary for it. A reading that takes only the feelings misses why the outcomes sort by position; a reading that takes only the structure misses why the book is painful to read.

The deepest evidence that the two levels are one comes from Nick's father, in the third paragraph of the novel. The advice is to remember, whenever you feel like criticizing anyone, that all the people in this world have not had the advantages that you have had. Nick offers it as a lesson in tolerance and it is usually read that way. It is also a class statement, delivered by a wealthy man to his son, that classifies the world into those with advantages and those without and recommends a posture of restraint toward the second group. The novel's ethical frame is installed by a father with money, and the reserve of judgment it recommends is a luxury of the same kind as a string of polo ponies.

## Nick Carraway and the Position That Calls Itself Neutral

A class-conflict reading has to place the narrator, because the narrator is the instrument through which every scene reaches the reader. Nick is not outside the map. He is a bond salesman from a family whose money came from a hardware business, educated at Yale, related to Daisy, acquainted with Tom from college, and living in a rented eyesore next to a mansion. His summary of his own situation is exact: his own house was an eyesore, but it was a small eyesore, and it had been overlooked, so he had a view of the water, a partial view of his neighbor's lawn, and the consoling proximity of millionaires, all for eighty dollars a month.

That sentence is the best single description of Nick's position in the novel, and it is economic from beginning to end. He is close enough to the wealth to enjoy its view and far enough from it to pay rent. Across the courtesy bay, as he puts it, the white palaces of fashionable East Egg glittered along the water. The word courtesy is doing quiet work. The bay is a courtesy; the proximity is consoling; the arrangement is comfortable and provisional and entirely dependent on the tolerance of people richer than he is.

What follows from that position is a specific kind of narration. Nick can enter every setting because he is legible to everyone: acceptable at the Buchanans' table, welcome at Gatsby's parties, tolerated in Myrtle's apartment, unremarkable in the valley. Universal access looks like objectivity and is not. It is the mobility of a man whose position is ambiguous enough to be read as friendly by everyone, and it comes with an obligation he honors throughout, which is to convert every conflict he witnesses into a moral observation rather than a structural one.

The conversion is most visible at the end. Nick's verdict is that they were careless people, Tom and Daisy, that they smashed up things and creatures and then retreated back into their money or their vast carelessness, and let other people clean up the mess they had made. It is a devastating sentence and it is also, examined closely, a psychological indictment where a structural one is available. Carelessness is a character flaw. The sentence's own evidence points elsewhere: the retreat is into their money, and the mess is cleaned up by other people. Nick names the mechanism in the act of blaming the temperament. A conflict reading takes that as the novel's last and most eloquent instance of the personal masking the structural, performed by the narrator himself, one page from the end.

## The Ending as a Verdict on the Conflict

Count the outcomes. Myrtle, who tried to move up, is killed by a car driven by the woman she wanted to replace. Gatsby, who tried to move up, is shot by the husband of the woman who was killed. Wilson, who wanted only to leave, dies in the ashes. Tom and Daisy, who tried nothing, go abroad. That is not a distribution of poetic justice. It is a distribution that tracks one variable, which is whether a character attempted to change position.

The funeral makes the verdict explicit. Nobody comes. The guests who drank Gatsby's liquor for a whole summer do not appear, Wolfshiem sends regrets, and the mourners are Nick, Henry Gatz, a few servants, the postman, and the man with owl-eyed glasses who says, of the host, that he was a poor son-of-a-bitch. The word poor is doing double duty and the novel knows it. A man who died with a mansion and a pool is called poor by the only guest who bothered to attend, and the description is accurate in the sense that matters: he never acquired the thing money was supposed to buy him, which was a position.

Compare the treatment of Myrtle's death and Gatsby's. Myrtle dies in the road in front of a garage and the inquest turns on the testimony of the man who runs the coffee joint. Gatsby dies in a pool and the newspapers run for pages. Both deaths are caused by the same afternoon and the same two wealthy people, and the difference in how the world processes them is a difference of position, recorded by the novel without comment.

Then the last page reaches for something larger, and a conflict reading should be honest about the reach. Nick imagines the island as it appeared to Dutch sailors, a fresh, green breast of the new world, and turns Gatsby's private failure into the national one. The move is beautiful and it is also a final act of translation, converting a specific economic defeat into a general condition of American desire. Whether that translation is an evasion or an insight is the genuine interpretive question the ending leaves, and an essay that names it as a question rather than resolving it too quickly is doing better work than one that does not notice the move at all.

## The Limits of the Conflict Reading and the Strongest Objection

Any lens should be able to state where it stops. Three limits are worth conceding in an essay, because conceding them makes the rest of the argument sturdier.

The first limit is that the novel is not a work of economic analysis and does not supply the data such an analysis would want. We never learn what the ash-grey men are paid, who employs them, whether the dumping ground is municipal or private, or what Gatsby's operation actually nets. The novel gives impressions of an economy, not an account of one, and a reading that pretends otherwise is inventing evidence. What the text supports is a claim about relationships and outcomes, not a claim about the mode of production on Long Island.

The second limit is that the conflict the novel dramatizes most fully is intra-class rather than inter-class. If the book were primarily about the antagonism between capital and labor, the valley would occupy more than two set pieces. It does not. An honest reading says the novel is centrally about a fight between fractions of the owning class and that the labor beneath them is present as condition and cost rather than as agent. That is a real limitation of the book, not of the lens, and naming it is more useful than overclaiming.

The third limit is that the lens can flatten Fitzgerald's prose if applied mechanically. The sentences about the green light, the shirts, the last page, do work that is not exhausted by their economic content. A reading that converts every image into a token of class position produces a thinner book than the one Fitzgerald wrote. The discipline is to let the analysis run where the text supports it and to stop where it does not.

The strongest objection, though, is none of these. It is that a class reading of this novel risks importing a politics the book does not have. Fitzgerald was not writing a critique of capitalism from outside it. He was fascinated by wealth, wanted it, wrote about it with palpable longing, and his narrator ends by grieving for a bootlegger rather than for the mechanic who died in the road. The answer is that a Marxist reading does not require the author's endorsement. It asks what the text shows, not what the author believed, and a novel can be more radical or more conservative than its writer intended. What Fitzgerald shows, whatever he meant, is a world where two people die trying to change position and two people who inherited theirs go abroad. That is the finding, and it does not depend on his politics or on ours.

## How to Build an Essay Around Class Conflict Through a Marxist Lens

A method is only as good as the essay it produces, so here is the procedure, stated as a sequence a student can actually follow.

Begin with a thesis that names a mechanism rather than a moral. A thesis that says the novel criticizes the rich will produce a paper of examples with no argument. A thesis that says the novel presents intra-class conflict as romance while rendering inter-class conflict as scenery gives the paper something to prove and something to prove it with. The strongest thesis available from this article's map is that the novel's central rivalry is a dispute over legitimacy between fractions of the owning class, and that its two deaths fall on the characters who attempted to cross positions.

Select passages that show a relationship, not a mood. The gasoline transaction, the drugstore revelation, the janitor and the sandwiches, the dollar twenty, the eleven years above the garage. Each of these is a fact about who owes what to whom, and each can be quoted in a sentence. Passages that describe atmosphere are harder to argue from because atmosphere can be read many ways; passages that record a transaction cannot.

Handle the counter-reading early rather than in the last paragraph. The strongest structure puts the personal reading in the second section, grants it what it deserves, and then shows that outcomes sort by position rather than by conduct. An essay that saves the objection for a concluding concession looks defensive; an essay that answers it in the middle looks confident.

Keep the lens distinct from the theme. This is where marks are most often lost. An essay on the theme of wealth and class describes what the novel says about money. An essay using the Marxist lens applies a method and reports what the method reveals. If a paragraph could survive with the word Marxist deleted, it is a theme paragraph wearing a costume, and it should either be rewritten to use the method or cut. The neighboring analysis of [class anxiety in the novel](/2025/02/03/class-anxiety-great-gatsby/) is a useful contrast case: anxiety is a psychological condition produced by position, while conflict is a relationship between positions, and an essay that conflates them will muddle both.

Gather evidence in one pass rather than hunting for quotations as you write. Read chapters two and seven with a single question in mind, which is who is asking whom for something, and mark every instance. That pass alone will produce more usable material than a general reread. It is worth doing with an annotated text so the marks survive; you can [read and annotate The Great Gatsby free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.org/tools/great-gatsby-annotated-text.html), where the quotation search and the annotation tools make a transaction-by-transaction pass much faster than paging through a paperback, and where the character and theme trackers let you follow a single figure like Wilson across every appearance.

Finally, resist the temptation to end on a verdict about capitalism. The essay's job is to demonstrate what the lens reveals about this novel. A conclusion that reports a finding about the book, that its personal drama is class antagonism in disguise and that the disguise is the novel's real subject, is stronger than one that reports a finding about the world.

## The Verdict

Class conflict through a Marxist lens does not turn The Great Gatsby into a different book. It turns the same book right side up. The love triangle stays where it is; what changes is the recognition that a triangle with Gatsby and Tom at two corners has a third corner nobody drew, occupied by the people who pump the gasoline, carry the sandwiches, and clear the ashes, and that the two figures at the visible corners are fighting over which of them gets to stand on that third one with full title.

The reading's central finding is that the novel is honest about the disguise. Fitzgerald does not hide the economics behind the romance; he shows character after character performing the concealment themselves, translating position into feeling because feeling is the only language they have. Myrtle calls a structural fact bad breeding. Wilson calls it God. Tom calls it civilization. Gatsby calls it Daisy. Nick, who sees the most, calls it carelessness, and gets closest without arriving.

What the lens finally exposes is the sorting. Two characters attempt to move between positions and both die within a week of each other, one on a road and one in a pool. Two characters attempt nothing and leave the country. The novel does not editorialize about this. It simply arranges it, and a reader who has learned to look at position rather than personality cannot unsee the arrangement afterward. That is the value of the method: not a verdict on the rich, which any reader can supply, but a map of who was standing where when the summer ended.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How does a Marxist lens read class conflict in Gatsby?

It reads the novel's antagonisms as products of economic position rather than of personality. Instead of asking why Tom dislikes Gatsby, it asks what each man's relationship to property is, what that relationship entitles him to do, and whose labor the arrangement rests on. Applied to the summer of 1922, the method separates three fronts: established wealth against newly acquired wealth, both of those against the working poor of the valley of ashes, and a third front running inside the working poor themselves. The lens then explains why the fronts receive such unequal attention, with the fight between wealthy fractions occupying most of the book and the labor beneath it appearing mainly as scenery and as casualties. The reading is evidence-hungry rather than assertive: every structural claim has to be cashed out in a transaction the novel actually shows.

### Q: What classes contend in the novel?

Four positions appear, though only three genuinely contend. The Buchanans hold inherited capital, institutional standing, and the power to define respectability. Gatsby holds a large fortune acquired illegally and spent on display, without the provenance that would convert it into standing. The Wilsons and the ash-grey men hold nothing but their labor, sold in a market where they have no leverage at all. Nick occupies a fourth position, salaried and mobile, with inherited social access but a rented house, which is precisely why he can move through every setting. The contention runs between the first two positions loudly, between the wealthy and the workers silently and one-sidedly, and inside the third position sideways, as Myrtle turns her grievance on her husband rather than on the arrangement that produced them both.

### Q: How is personal drama also class conflict?

Because the characters have no vocabulary for structural antagonism and translate it into feeling. Myrtle's grievance about her economic position becomes an insult about her husband's breeding. Wilson's becomes jealousy and then theology. Tom's anxiety about a challenge to his standing becomes a lecture about civilization going to pieces. Gatsby's exclusion becomes a five-year courtship. The novel does not hide the economics behind the romance; it shows character after character performing the concealment, which is what makes the disguise a subject rather than a flaw. The strongest evidence that the levels are one is the pattern of outcomes: the two characters who attempt to change position die within days of each other, and the two who inherited theirs leave the country undisturbed.

### Q: How does Tom show class contempt?

Through vocabulary, through deferral, and through the assumption that other people's time is his. He calls Gatsby a common swindler who would have to steal the ring he put on Daisy's finger, and Mr. Nobody from Nowhere, both of which attack provenance rather than conduct. He tells Wilson that the car will be sold next week, then repeats the deferral months later, keeping a man's escape route open as an unfulfilled promise at no cost to himself. He rings for the janitor in a building he does not live in and sends him for sandwiches. He slaps Wilson on the shoulder and asks how business is while sleeping with his wife. The geniality and the contempt are the same behavior: what dominance looks like when it faces no challenge.

### Q: How does the valley function as the exploited base?

It supplies the labor and absorbs the waste that make the two Eggs livable. Fitzgerald describes it as a farm where ashes grow like wheat into ridges and hills, which frames it as a site of production whose product is refuse. Its workers appear as ash-grey men who move dimly and already crumbling, and who raise an impenetrable cloud that screens their obscure operations from the reader's sight. Wilson sells gasoline to the men who drive through. Michaelis runs a coffee joint beside the ash-heaps. The valley receives no wealth from the traffic it services and offers no exit from itself. When something has to be disposed of, whether ashes or a body, the valley takes it, and the novel visits it only when a wealthy character has business there.

### Q: Are the novel's conflicts personal or structural?

Both, and the relationship between them is the whole argument. Treating the conflicts as purely personal cannot explain why the outcomes sort by position rather than by conduct: Gatsby is the more devoted man and dies, Myrtle is the more vivid woman and dies, Wilson is guilty of nothing and dies, while the two characters who attempted nothing sail for Europe. Treating them as purely structural loses what the novel is best at, which is rendering how a position feels from inside it. The productive reading holds both, arguing that the characters experience structural antagonism as personal feeling because feeling is the only register available to them, and that this translation is the novel's actual subject.

### Q: Is the Gatsby and Tom rivalry a fight between fractions of capital?

Yes, and naming it that way explains why it is so bitter and so unproductive. A fraction is a subdivision of a class with its own source of wealth and its own claim to legitimacy. Tom's fortune is inherited and institutionally backed; Gatsby's is accumulated through bootlegging and securities work and spent on display. They share every interest that matters, since neither proposes any change to a world where enormous fortunes buy shorefront houses. What they fight over is admission. Gatsby's reply about Walter Chase, that Chase came to them dead broke and was glad to pick up some money, demolishes Tom's moral claim while conceding the framework entirely: Gatsby wants to be on the respectable side of a boundary he never questions.

### Q: Why does Tom call Gatsby a common swindler?

Because provenance is the only ground where Tom can win, and swindler is the word that disqualifies. Gatsby is younger, more attentive to Daisy, and unencumbered by a mistress in the valley, so any contest on personal merit is lost before it starts. What Tom has is a fortune that arrived by the rules his own group wrote. The insult is precisely calibrated: he pairs swindler with a claim about property, saying Daisy will not leave him for a man who would have to steal the ring he put on her finger. The charge is not that Gatsby is immoral but that his money is not his, which in this world is the only accusation that removes a rival from consideration.

### Q: What does Wilson's garage reveal about the working poor?

Almost everything the novel is willing to show. The interior is unprosperous and bare, with a dust-covered wreck of a Ford in a corner as the only visible car. Nick's first assumption is that the place must be a blind concealing something richer overhead, which reveals more about Nick than about Wilson: visible failure is outside his experience. Wilson himself is blond, spiritless, anaemic, and a damp gleam of hope enters his eyes when a customer arrives. He and Myrtle have lived above the shop for eleven years. His entire plan for escape depends on reselling a used car that a wealthy man keeps promising and not delivering. The garage is a business with no capital, no leverage, and one customer who matters.

### Q: Does Myrtle belong to the working class or is she trying to leave it?

Both at once, which is what makes her the novel's most instructive figure on this front. She lives above a failing garage in an industrial dumping ground, which fixes her position materially. Her ambitions are entirely about appearing to occupy a different one: the list she recites includes a massage and a wave, a collar for the dog, a small ashtray with a spring, and a wreath for her mother's grave. Not one item alters her circumstances; each performs a status she does not have. Her contempt for Wilson uses the language of breeding, borrowed from the group above her and aimed at the only person below her. She wants a better place inside the arrangement, never a different arrangement.

### Q: How does the Plaza Hotel scene stage a class showdown?

By bringing every position into one rented room after a drive that crosses all of them. The party leaves East Egg, stops at Wilson's garage where a dollar and twenty cents of gasoline changes hands and a man asks to leave, and continues to a suite in Manhattan. Tom opens not with jealousy but with ideology, complaining that people sneer at family life and imagining himself standing on the last barrier of civilization. He then produces his investigation into the drugstores. Gatsby counters by naming Walter Chase, proving the boundary is porous. Daisy retreats only after the disqualification lands. The sequence matters: she does not choose between two men, she declines to abandon a legitimate position for one just publicly exposed as illegitimate.

### Q: Why does bootlegging matter to a class-conflict reading?

Because it identifies exactly how a man without provenance can accumulate capital, and exactly why that capital fails to convert into standing. Tom's revelation is specific: Gatsby and Wolfshiem bought up side-street drugstores in New York and Chicago and sold grain alcohol over the counter. Prohibition created a market where enormous fortunes could be built quickly by people the established order excluded, which is the structural fact behind the novel's entire premise. But money earned outside the rules stays legible as outside money. Gatsby can buy the mansion, the shirts, the hydroplane, and the parties; he cannot buy a family, a school, or a history, and those are what confer position. His fortune is large and, in the only market he cares about, worthless.

### Q: What role does Nick play in the class struggle?

He is the mediator, and his neutrality is itself a position with material conditions. He describes his own house as an eyesore, but a small one, giving him a partial view of his neighbor's lawn and the consoling proximity of millionaires for eighty dollars a month. That is the whole of his situation: close enough to enjoy the view, far enough to pay rent. Because his position is ambiguous he is legible as friendly everywhere, which produces access that resembles objectivity without being it. His characteristic operation is converting structural antagonism into moral commentary. The famous verdict on careless people names the mechanism, retreat into money and other people cleaning up the mess, while blaming a temperament.

### Q: Does the novel take a side in the class conflict it stages?

Not explicitly, and that restraint is worth respecting rather than overriding. Fitzgerald was fascinated by wealth and wrote about it with obvious longing, and his narrator ends by grieving for a bootlegger rather than for the mechanic who died in the road. But a text can show more than its author intends, and what this one shows is a sorting. Everyone who attempts to cross positions is destroyed; everyone who inherited a position keeps it. The novel arranges that outcome without editorializing about it. A reading does not need Fitzgerald's political endorsement to report the arrangement, and an essay that claims the book is a critique of capitalism is claiming more than the evidence supports.

### Q: How does class antagonism explain the ending?

By accounting for who dies and who does not. Myrtle attempted upward movement and is killed by a car driven by the woman whose position she wanted. Gatsby attempted upward movement and is shot by the husband of the woman who was killed. Wilson wanted only to leave and dies in the ashes. Tom and Daisy attempted nothing and go abroad. The funeral completes the pattern: nobody attends, and the only guest who speaks calls the host a poor son-of-a-bitch, which is accurate in the sense that matters, since he never acquired the position his money was meant to purchase. The distribution tracks one variable, which is whether a character tried to change where they stood.

### Q: What evidence should I gather for a class-conflict essay on Gatsby?

Prioritize passages that record a transaction over passages that describe a mood, because a transaction can only be read one way. The strongest set includes the dollar and twenty cents Wilson charges Tom for gasoline, the repeated deferral of the car sale, the eleven years the Wilsons have lived above the garage, the janitor sent for sandwiches, the eighty dollars a month Nick pays, the string of polo ponies brought down from Lake Forest, the two hundred button presses by a butler's thumb, and the drugstore revelation in the Plaza suite. Read chapters two and seven in one pass asking a single question, which is who is asking whom for something, and mark every instance. That pass yields more usable material than a general reread.

### Q: Is class conflict in the novel open warfare or hidden?

Almost entirely hidden, and it becomes visible in exactly one scene. For six chapters the antagonism runs beneath politeness: Tom is jovial with Wilson, guests speculate cheerfully about their host's fortune, Myrtle's parties proceed. The Plaza confrontation is the only place where the structure surfaces into speech, and it surfaces because a challenger has forced the incumbent to defend a boundary he normally never has to mention. The concealment is not authorial evasion. It reproduces how such antagonism actually operates, since a dominant position that is never challenged has no reason to announce itself and every reason not to. Tom's geniality is not the absence of conflict; it is the shape conflict takes when one side holds every advantage.

### Q: How do the servants and workers stay invisible?

By appearing as functions rather than as people, which matches exactly what the narrator's position lets him perceive. The novel names a corps of caterers, eight servants including an extra gardener, an orchestra, a chauffeur, and a butler, and gives none of them a line that matters. The one full biography of a working person arrives as Daisy's dinner-table joke about the butler's nose, damaged by years of polishing silver for a household with a service for two hundred. A body ruined by work is served as amusement. Even at the discovery of Gatsby's body the group is described as the chauffeur, butler, gardener, and Nick: three job titles and a name. The silence is systematic, and a structural reading treats systematic silence as evidence.

### Q: Does class conflict in Gatsby track race as well?

The novel connects them through Tom, though it does not develop the connection far. In chapter one he is agitated about a book arguing that the white race will be submerged, insisting that it is up to those who are the dominant race to watch out. At the Plaza he moves from defending his marriage to warning that people will next throw everything overboard, and Jordan punctures the rhetoric by observing that they are all white in the room. The continuity is structural: both outbursts defend an inherited position by presenting it as natural rather than historical. Tom reaches for pseudoscience when the challenge comes from below and for the provenance of money when it comes from beside him. The instinct is one instinct in two idioms.

### Q: What is the difference between class conflict and class anxiety in the novel?

Anxiety is a psychological state produced by a position; conflict is a relationship between positions. Daisy's discomfort, Nick's ambivalence about the world he moves through, and Tom's need to explain his standing as a natural fact are all anxiety: they are felt privately and require no antagonist. The gasoline transaction, the deferred car sale, and the drugstore revelation are conflict: they involve two parties whose interests are structurally opposed, and they can be described without reference to anyone's feelings. Essays lose marks by treating the two as one topic. A useful test is whether a claim survives with the emotional vocabulary removed. If it does, it is about conflict. If it collapses, it is about anxiety.
