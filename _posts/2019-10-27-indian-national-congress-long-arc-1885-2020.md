The Indian National Congress is usually introduced as the oldest continuous thread in Indian politics: founded at Bombay in December 1885, carrier of the freedom struggle for six decades, governing party of the republic for most of the period after 1947, and diminished electoral force after 2014. That introduction carries a hidden assumption, that one organisation lived through all of it. This article rejects the assumption outright. Read organisationally, the party that met at Bombay in 1885, the party Gandhi rebuilt at Nagpur in 1920, the party Rajni Kothari analysed as a system in 1970 and the party that contested the 2019 general election are four distinct organisational entities that share a name, a registration and a memory, and very little else.

The claim can be tested, which is what separates it from a rhetorical flourish. An organisation is its architecture: who may belong and on what terms, where decisions are taken, how leaders succeed one another, what work ideology performs, and how the electoral machine connects to the membership machine. On each of those five dimensions, the Congress of the founding-Moderate phase from 1885 to 1907 differs categorically from the Gandhi-mass phase from 1920 to 1947; the Gandhi-mass phase differs from the Nehru-Indira phase from 1947 to 1991, which operated as the Congress System of Kothari's *Politics in India* (1970); and that phase differs again from the dynastic-organisational phase whose first sub-period ran from the Congress(I) founding of January 1978 to 1991 and whose second has run from the Sonia Gandhi succession of March 1998 onward. The boundaries are not tidy, because organisations change by rupture and drift rather than by decree, but the discontinuities are substantive enough that treating the four as one party mistakes a label for a structure.

The stakes of the distinction are highest at the end of the arc. The standard account of the post-2014 Congress treats 44 Lok Sabha seats in 2014 and 52 in 2019 as a fall from an inherited height, a rupture demanding explanations pitched at the level of leadership failure or a rival's charisma. The four-phase reading relocates the explanation. The Congress System that Kothari described rested on an absorptive mechanism: factions inside the party bargained, lost, stayed and bargained again, so that discontent was metabolised within the organisation rather than expelled from it. That mechanism belonged to one phase's architecture. The dynastic-organisational phase progressively dismantled it, replacing factional bargaining with arbitration from a leadership household, and the decline after 2014 is that phase's return to a structural fragility the System had managed, and never removed. Decline, on this reading, is not the opposite of the Congress tradition. It is one of its recurring conditions, visible in 1907, in 1977 and after 2014, whenever a phase's architecture stopped performing the work it was built for.

This article is the hub for a pillar of studies that take each phase and each crisis in turn: the 1885 founding and the Moderate method, the Surat split of 1907, the Nagpur reorganisation of 1920, Kothari's System, the organisational reading of the 1969 split, the Congress(I) founding of 1978, the 1998 succession, the dynastic tension and the post-2014 decline each receive a dedicated treatment later in the pillar. What the hub supplies is the frame those treatments share: the phase boundaries, the five dimensions along which they are drawn, and the comparison table that makes the reading citable. The evidence is drawn from the organisation's own record, the Congress archives preserved at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library and by the All India Congress Committee, the Nagpur Congress Constitution of 1920, the founding documents of 1978 and the succession resolutions of 1998, read against Kothari's *Politics in India* (1970), Sumit Sarkar's *Modern India 1885-1947* (1983) and Inder Malhotra's *Indira Gandhi: A Personal and Political Biography* (1989).

A word on the frame's edges before the evidence. The arc runs from the founding session of 1885 to the Congress's position at the end of the second decade of the twenty-first century, and the end date matters less than the test the frame sets itself: any proposed phase must differ from its neighbours on the dimensions of Table 1 by documentary evidence, not by a change of leaders, a famous speech or an election result alone. Leaders change inside phases constantly; Kamaraj and Indira Gandhi led the same System. Elections are won and lost inside phases; the Gandhi-mass organisation lost provincial contests through the 1930s without ceasing to be itself. What cannot change inside a phase is the constitution of belonging, the place where decisions bind, and the way a successor is made. Those are the joints at which this article cuts, and the cuts fall in 1920, across the long hinge of 1969 to 1978, and again in 1998, not because those years are famous but because the documents change there: a new constitution, a lapsed electoral calendar and a new symbol, a succession by resolution.

## The Textbook Story of the Indian National Congress and Its Two Failures

Two framings dominate the standard telling. The first is the continuous-tradition framing, carried by the party's own historiography and by the school and university textbook tradition that leans on it. B. Pattabhi Sitaramayya's *History of the Indian National Congress*, published in two volumes in 1935 and 1947 with the organisation's blessing, narrates the party as a single unfolding national will, and the narrative habit survived him: one founding, one struggle, one inheritance. The second framing is the rise-and-decline arc of popular commentary, in which a heroic ascent to 1947 and a governing plateau under Nehru give way to a long fall through personalisation, Emergency, dynastic succession and the twin defeats of 2014 and 2019. The two framings disagree about tone. They agree about the thing that matters analytically: both assume the organisation underneath the story stayed the same kind of object while events happened to it.

### What is wrong with the continuous-tradition history of the Congress?

It confuses nominal continuity with organisational continuity. The name, the legal registration and the broad ideological self-description persisted across 1885 to 2020, and those continuities are real. The architecture did not persist: membership, decision locus, succession mechanism, the role of ideology and the electoral link were each rebuilt at least twice.

The tradition is therefore a genealogy of distinct organisations, not the biography of one. The failure shows in what each framing cannot explain. Continuous-tradition history cannot explain why the party of 1906, meeting annually to pass resolutions, could not simply continue into mass politics, and had to be refounded on a new constitution at Nagpur in 1920 before it could run a nationwide campaign. Rise-and-decline history cannot explain why the supposedly fallen party of the 1970s, organisationally hollowed by its own leader, could still absorb the shock of the 1977 defeat and return to office in 1980 with 353 seats, nor why the supposedly restored party of 2004 to 2014, governing for a decade, entered decline from a position of incumbency rather than persecution. Both puzzles dissolve once the unit of analysis changes. Parties do not rise and fall as organisms do. Architectures are built for a task, perform it, and then fail at a different task, and the Congress has run through four such architectures, each fitted to its moment: petitioning a liberal empire, mobilising a colonised society, governing a dominant-party democracy, and finally arbitrating a family-anchored coalition-era party.

The distinction also disciplines the evidence. A phase claim is falsifiable in a way a tradition claim is not. If the four-phase reading is right, the documentary record should show formal discontinuities at the boundaries: a new constitution in 1920, a lapse of internal elections and a change of succession practice after 1969, a new party with a new symbol in 1978, a leadership succession effected by committee resolution rather than organisational ballot in 1998. The record shows all four. It should also show the absorptive mechanism working where the reading says it worked, in the factional settlements of the 1950s and 1960s, and failing where the reading says it failed, in the exits of 1997 to 2019, when dissatisfied factions stopped bargaining inside the party and left it. That record, phase by phase, is the substance of the sections that follow.

The continuity framing has a ceremonial history as well as a textual one, and its peak is datable. In December 1985 the party celebrated its centenary at Bombay with the full apparatus of a ruling organisation: a commemorative session, official volumes carrying the narrative from 1885 to the incumbent leadership as one unfolding story, and a presidential address from Rajiv Gandhi that is remembered for its attack on the party's own brokers of power. The juxtaposition is the framing's refutation in a single event. A body secure in organisational continuity does not need its centenary oratory to accuse its own machinery of capture, and a leadership confident of its machinery does not celebrate the anniversary of its founding by announcing that the machine has been stolen. The centenary volumes narrated continuity because narration was the one continuity fully in the leadership's control. The archive underneath them, constitutions, election records, succession files, tells the four-phase story to any reader who consults it instead.

## Phase One: Petition Politics and the Founding-Moderate Architecture, 1885 to 1907

The first session of the Indian National Congress opened on 28 December 1885 in the hall of the Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College at Bombay, with 72 delegates present and Womesh Chunder Bonnerjee, a Calcutta barrister, in the presidential chair. The convening figure was Allan Octavian Hume, a retired member of the Indian Civil Service who had spent the early 1880s assembling an all-India network of correspondents and who served as the organisation's general secretary, its one-man permanent secretariat, for the founding years. Around Hume stood the Indian leadership of the Moderate generation: Dadabhai Naoroji, the Bombay merchant and scholar who had founded the East India Association at London in 1866; Pherozeshah Mehta, the Bombay municipal politician; Dinshaw Edulji Wacha, who managed the organisation's correspondence and finances for decades; Surendranath Banerjee of Bengal, detained that December by his own Indian National Conference at Calcutta; and Gopal Krishna Gokhale, then a twenty-year-old teacher from Poona beginning his apprenticeship under Mahadev Govind Ranade.

What these men built was an architecture for a defined task: pressing constitutional demands on a colonial state that professed liberal principles, in language that state could not dismiss as sedition. The demands of the early sessions were correspondingly precise. Resolutions asked for the simultaneous holding of the Indian Civil Service examination in India and England, for the expansion of the legislative councils with elected Indian members, for the separation of judicial from executive functions, for reduction of military expenditure and of the home charges, and for the repeal of the Arms Act. The method was the resolution, the memorial and the deputation: petition-politics, conducted in English, by professionals, in the confidence that an informed British public and Parliament would correct an erring bureaucracy. The organisational form matched the method exactly. The Congress met for a few days each December, in a different city, heard the presidential address, passed its resolutions through a subjects committee, and dispersed. Between sessions it barely existed as a body. There was no mass membership to enrol, no dues-paying base to service, no provincial machinery with defined powers. Delegates were returned by public meetings and local associations, lawyers and journalists predominated, and the costs of the annual gathering were carried by wealthy patrons, among them the Maharaja of Darbhanga, whose money underwrote the founding years.

The founding body did not assemble from nothing, and its substrate explains both its reach and its limits. Provincial associations had been rehearsing all-India politics for a generation: the Poona Sarvajanik Sabha, founded in 1870 and run by Ranade and G. V. Joshi, which sent memoranda on famine and taxation; Surendranath Banerjee's Indian Association at Calcutta, founded in 1876, which had campaigned across northern India in 1877 against the lowering of the civil service age limit and had convened an Indian National Conference in 1883 and again in the same December week as the first Congress session in 1885; and the Madras Mahajan Sabha of 1884. These bodies supplied the delegates, the draft resolutions and the habit of annual deliberation. They were also, every one of them, associations of the English-educated: lawyers predominated, journalists and teachers followed, and the landholding and mercantile wealth that financed the proceedings attended as patrons rather than as members. The first Congress's 72 delegates were drawn from this stratum and from little else. No peasant could have paid the travel and lodging costs of a December session in a distant presidency city, and the architecture made no provision for one to try. Membership of the nation, in Phase One, was represented rather than enrolled, and the representativeness claimed by the presidential addresses rested on the argument that the educated class spoke for interests it shared with the unrepresented: a defensible argument in 1885, and the exact argument the Extremists and then Gandhi would indict.

The architecture did win concessions, and the manner of the winning taught it the wrong lesson about its own strength. The Indian Councils Act of 1892 enlarged the legislative councils and conceded, in guarded form, the principle of election, allowing bodies such as municipalities and universities to recommend members whom the government then nominated. The Moderates counted the Act a vindication of petition-politics, and in the terms of their design it was one: the demand had been framed constitutionally, pressed through the British Committee's parliamentary allies and granted by a Liberal-inclined administration. Dadabhai Naoroji's election to the House of Commons from Central Finsbury in 1892, the first Indian to sit at Westminster, belonged to the same logic of access. What the victories concealed was their dependence on the disposition of the governing party in London. When that disposition changed, under the Conservative governments of the later 1890s and Curzon's viceroyalty after 1899, the petitions met a state that had stopped listening, and the organisation possessed neither the members nor the local machinery to make itself heard by any other means. The partition of Bengal in 1905 was announced to a Congress that had protested it in advance and could not raise the political price of ignoring the protest. Phase One's tragedy is written into its design: it was an instrument for persuading a persuadable empire, built by men who had every reason, in 1885, to believe the empire persuadable.

The colonial state's own verdict on the phase arrived early and is quoted in every history: Lord Dufferin, the Viceroy who had tolerated the founding, dismissed the Congress in a speech at Calcutta in November 1888 as representing a microscopic minority of the population. The charge stung because the architecture made it half true, and the Congress's answer is equally diagnostic. George Yule, a Scottish merchant of Calcutta, presided over the Allahabad session of 1888 as the first English president, a demonstration of the organisation's respectability staged for British consumption; the delegates' lists of the early sessions read like a directory of the Indian bar and the vernacular and English press. The Moderates did not regard the narrowness as a defect to be hidden. It was the credential on which their method depended: men of property and education, proceeding constitutionally, were precisely the interlocutors a liberal empire was pledged to hear. When the empire ceased to be liberal in its Indian administration, the credential depreciated, and no enlargement of the social base had been prepared from which a different claim could be pressed. The Extremist indictment of the Moderates as mendicants, Tilak's word for the politics of prayer, petition and protest, was unfair to their achievements and exact about their architecture, and the organisation's inability to answer it institutionally, by producing a membership that could outvote the indictment, is the organisational meaning of Surat.

Two extensions gave the petition architecture a permanent skeleton. At London in 1889 the organisation set up a British Committee, chaired by William Wedderburn, the retired Bombay civil servant who presided over the Congress in 1889 and again in 1910, and from 1890 the committee published the journal *India* to carry the Congress case to British readers and members of Parliament. At home, the presidential address became the phase's characteristic intellectual product, and Naoroji its characteristic intellect. His drain theory, set out across decades of papers and gathered in *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India* (1901), argued that Britain's administration transferred India's wealth abroad through salaries, pensions and guaranteed railway returns, and it gave petition-politics an economic doctrine: the empire was not merely illiberal in practice, it was extractive in structure. Naoroji presided over the Congress three times, at Calcutta in 1886, at Lahore in 1893 and again at Calcutta in 1906, an eighty-one-year-old returning to hold the organisation together at the moment its architecture began to fail.

One dispute about the founding must be stated at its strongest on both sides, because later phases inherited it. Critics within the nationalist tradition charged that Hume had founded the Congress as a safety valve, a channel through which educated discontent could discharge itself harmlessly under official tolerance, and Lala Lajpat Rai gave the charge its canonical form in *Young India* (1916), drawing on Wedderburn's biography of Hume, published in 1913, which recorded Hume's talk of a growing volume of discontent that needed an outlet. The party's defenders reply, and historians such as Bipan Chandra have argued the point systematically, that Indian leaders used Hume and the official tolerance he represented as a lightning conductor for their own purposes: the organisation was Indian in its demands from the first session, whatever its convenor's motives. For the organisational argument the dispute matters less than the agreement underneath it. Both readings describe a small, elite, petitioning body whose access to the colonial state was its principal asset and whose design assumed that asset. An architecture built on access has no use for a million members, and no machinery for acquiring them.

The strain that ended the phase came from Bengal. The partition of Bengal, effected by Lord Curzon's government in October 1905, detonated a politics the petition method could not contain: boycott of foreign goods, swadeshi enterprise, national education, and a generation of younger leaders, Bal Gangadhar Tilak in Maharashtra, Bipin Chandra Pal in Bengal and Lajpat Rai in Punjab, the Lal-Bal-Pal of nationalist memory, who held that resolutions addressed to British conscience were a confession of weakness. Tilak had been building an alternative infrastructure for a decade through the Ganapati festival from 1893 and the Shivaji festival from 1895, public festivals that carried political organisation into towns the Congress never reached. At the Calcutta session of December 1906, with Naoroji presiding as a compromise figure, the Congress adopted four resolutions on swaraj, swadeshi, boycott and national education, and Naoroji's address declared self-government as the goal. The words papered over a structural fact. The Moderate architecture had no procedure for deciding between two programmes, because it had never needed one: its conferences ratified a consensus the founders carried in their persons. When the consensus broke, the organisation had no ballot, no membership roll and no executive capable of arbitrating the break.

Surat, in December 1907, is where the deficit became visible. The session, moved from Nagpur to Surat in the expectation that the venue would favour the Moderates, dissolved in disorder on 27 December, with a shoe thrown toward the dais and the police clearing the pandal; the presidential election of Rash Behari Ghosh could not proceed. The Moderates met separately the following day and appointed a committee to draft a constitution, adopted at Allahabad in April 1908, which defined the Congress creed as the attainment of self-government within the empire by strictly constitutional means and made acceptance of that creed a condition of belonging. The Extremists, refusing the condition, stayed out. Formally the Moderates had saved the organisation. In substance they had converted a loose conference of the like-minded into a defined body with an exclusion rule, and the body they saved shrank steadily for a decade. Phase One ends at Surat not because a vote was taken but because the event proved the architecture's limit: a petitioning club could deliberate, but it could not decide, mobilise or survive its own success in creating a politics larger than itself.

## An Architecture in Abeyance: The Congress Between Surat and Nagpur, 1907 to 1920

The thirteen years between the Surat split and the Nagpur refounding are best read as an interregnum in which the name persisted while the organisation thinned. The Moderate Congress continued its annual sessions, but attendance and authority ebbed. The Indian Councils Act of 1909, the Morley-Minto reforms, expanded the legislative councils the Moderates had petitioned for, while conceding separate electorates to Muslims, a concession the Congress protested and could not prevent; the councils absorbed Moderate energy into the very institutions the next phase would vote to boycott. Tilak, convicted of sedition in 1908, spent six years in Mandalay prison, and returned in 1914 to a movement whose centre of gravity was moving elsewhere. His Home Rule League, founded in April 1916, and Annie Besant's rival league, founded at Madras in September 1916, built the branch networks and the vernacular propaganda that the Moderate Congress had never possessed, and both leagues worked outside the Congress frame because the frame could not hold them.

The reunion, when it came at Lucknow in December 1916, was negotiated between organisations rather than accomplished inside one. The Extremists re-entered the Congress, and the Congress and the Muslim League, meeting simultaneously at Lucknow under the presidency of Ambica Charan Mazumdar, adopted the joint constitutional scheme remembered as the Lucknow Pact, accepting separate electorates in exchange for a common programme of demands. The pact demonstrated a capacity for coalition that Phase Two would use repeatedly. It did not solve the organisational question. The Congress of 1916 still had no executive committee in continuous session, no mass roll, no provincial structure mapped to where Indians actually lived and spoke. What it had was prestige, an annual platform, and a vacancy where a machine should be.

The vacancy was filled from outside the old leadership's repertoire. Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi returned to India in January 1915 after two decades in South Africa, observed Gokhale's instruction to travel and listen for a year, and then, in a sequence of local campaigns, assembled the method and the cadre of the next phase: Champaran in 1917, where he organised the indigo ryots of Bihar against the tinkathia system; Ahmedabad in 1918, where he fasted in a mill-hands' dispute; Kheda in 1918, where he led a no-tax campaign among peasants hit by crop failure. Each campaign was run on disciplined, limited, verifiable demands, and each recruited local workers who stayed organised afterward: Vallabhbhai Patel from Kheda, Rajendra Prasad from Champaran, Jawaharlal Nehru from the U.P. kisan movement a little later. Meanwhile the colonial state supplied the provocation. The Rowlatt Act of March 1919, extending wartime detention powers into peacetime, met Gandhi's first all-India satyagraha; on 13 April 1919 troops under Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer killed hundreds of unarmed people at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar; the Hunter Committee's inquiry and the Congress's own Punjab inquiry, whose report Gandhi drafted, divided moderate from mass politics for good.

At the special session at Calcutta in September 1920, presided over by Lajpat Rai, the Congress adopted Gandhi's programme of non-cooperation, boycott of councils, courts, schools and foreign cloth, in alliance with the Khilafat movement of Indian Muslims protesting the treatment of the Ottoman Caliph. The vote was a transfer of sovereignty inside the organisation: the programme passed against the opposition of most of the senior leadership of the old phase, with Chittaranjan Das, the Bengal leader who had opposed it at Calcutta, moving the resolution at Nagpur three months later after converting to it. Jinnah, who warned against mass extra-constitutional politics, left the Congress. The personnel of petition-politics could deliberate about the new method; they could not operate it. Gandhi therefore did at Nagpur what founders do. He rewrote the constitution.

The interregnum also settled, by demonstration, the question of where a successor organisation would find its people. The Home Rule leagues at their height claimed memberships in the tens of thousands, organised by branch and by vernacular pamphlet, and Besant's internment by the Madras government in June 1917 turned a propaganda network into a grievance machine that embarrassed the state into her release and into the Montagu declaration of 20 August 1917, promising the gradual development of self-governing institutions. The Montagu-Chelmsford report of 1918 and the Government of India Act of 1919 then offered dyarchy in the provinces, a half-transfer of power that the Congress of the old design would have debated accepting at length. Gandhi's campaigns had meanwhile built the only all-India cadre with recent experience of disciplined illegal action, and the Khilafat alliance had given him a constituency, Indian Muslims organised through their own committees, that no Congress body had ever reached. When the special session met at Calcutta in September 1920, the choice before the delegates was therefore not between two programmes in the abstract. It was between a deliberative body that had been shrinking for thirteen years and a leader with a method, a cadre and an alliance ready to hand. The delegates chose the leader, and the leader, at Nagpur, chose to build them an organisation.

## Phase Two: The Nagpur Constitution and the Mass-Membership Machine, 1920 to 1947

The Nagpur session of December 1920, presided over by C. Vijayaraghavachariar and attended by 14,583 delegates, adopted the new Congress constitution that Gandhi had drafted, and with it a different species of organisation. The creed was rewritten: the goal became the attainment of swaraj by peaceful and legitimate means, replacing the 1908 formula of self-government within the empire by constitutional methods. A Working Committee of fifteen members was created as a standing executive, the first the Congress had ever possessed, charged with carrying out the programme between sessions. The All India Congress Committee was fixed at 350 members as the deliberative body beneath the annual session. Provincial Congress Committees were reorganised on linguistic lines, twenty-one provinces drawn by language rather than by the boundaries of British Indian administration, a decision whose consequences for the republic's federal map were still working themselves out in 1956. Below the provinces came district, taluka and town committees, and at the base, primary committees in the villages. Membership was opened to any adult who signed the creed and paid four annas, a quarter of a rupee, a sum set deliberately low enough for a peasant to pay.

### How did the Nagpur session of 1920 change who could join the Congress?

It replaced a delegate body of professionals, chosen by associations and public meetings, with an open mass enrolment. Any Indian of eighteen years could become a primary member by accepting the creed and paying the four-anna fee, which placed membership within reach of a peasant household for the first time.

The reorganisation of provincial committees on linguistic lines meant the new member joined an organisation that spoke the language of the member's own district. The architecture was built to run campaigns, and it ran them at a scale Phase One could not have imagined. The Tilak Swaraj Fund, launched in 1921 with a target of one crore of rupees, was subscribed to its target within months, while the companion drive for one crore of members fell well short of its goal and produced rolls whose accuracy the organisation's own officials doubted, a gap between claimed and verifiable membership that Sarkar notes in *Modern India 1885-1947* (1983) and that recurs, as a structural temptation of mass enrolment drives, in every later phase.

Gandhi tried to solve the quality problem by tying membership to work. The constructive programme, khadi and hand-spinning, village sanitation, national schools, the removal of untouchability and Hindu-Muslim unity, was not decoration around the political campaigns; it was the daily occupation that kept a district organisation alive between campaigns and gave the primary member something to be a member of. In September 1924, after his release from prison, Gandhi proposed that the qualification for Congress membership be recast in spun yarn, a monthly quota of self-spun thread in place of the four annas, so that the roll would consist of people who performed a small recurring act of allegiance. The proposal met resistance from leaders who feared it would shrink the rolls and empty the committees, and it was adopted in diluted form and unevenly enforced. The episode matters for the long arc because it states, at the very origin of mass membership, the tension every later phase inherited: a membership cheap enough to be mass is cheap enough to be fictitious, and the organisation would spend a century alternately widening the roll and doubting it, a doubt that surfaces in the AICC reviews of 1972 and 1973 and in the unverified enrolment drives of the dynastic phase.

The non-cooperation campaign of 1920 to 1922 boycotted the November 1920 council elections, emptied government schools and law courts of a portion of their Indian participants, and made bonfires of foreign cloth a district-level organisational routine. When a mob killed twenty-two policemen at Chauri Chaura in the United Provinces on 5 February 1922, Gandhi suspended the movement at Bardoli a week later, to the dismay of colleagues who saw victory approaching; the episode fixed a principle of the phase, that the centre could halt a campaign the base wanted to continue, discipline being the price of mass scale. The Congress that emerged from the suspension split functionally rather than formally: the Swaraj Party of Chittaranjan Das and Motilal Nehru, founded in January 1923, entered the councils to wreck them from inside, while the no-changers under Rajendra Prasad and C. Rajagopalachari ran the constructive programme of khadi, national schools and communal unity. Both wings remained inside one organisation, an early demonstration that the mass architecture could house organised internal difference, provided the difference accepted the creed and the Working Committee's arbitration.

The two decades after 1922 show the machine learning its repertoire. The Hindustani Seva Dal, founded by N. S. Hardikar at the Kakinada session of 1923, drilled volunteers into a uniformed service corps. Bardoli in 1928 made Vallabhbhai Patel's reputation as an organiser of peasants. The boycott of the Simon Commission in 1928 gave the machine an all-India rehearsal; the Lahore session of December 1929, with Jawaharlal Nehru in the chair, raised the creed's stakes to Purna Swaraj, complete independence, and the flag was hoisted on the banks of the Ravi on 31 December. The Dandi march of March and April 1930 and the salt satyagraha that followed turned the district committees into the skeleton of a parallel authority. The Gandhi-Irwin Pact of 5 March 1931 suspended the campaign by negotiation; the Karachi session later that month adopted the resolution on fundamental rights and economic policy that projected the movement's programme into a future constitution. The Congress Socialist Party, founded in 1934 by Jayaprakash Narayan, Acharya Narendra Deva and others, organised an ideological faction inside the Congress with its own programme and discipline, and the Working Committee tolerated it, because the architecture of Phase Two absorbed factions as the empire's pressure made unity worth more than uniformity.

The second civil disobedience movement of 1932 to 1934 subjected the machine to its severest organisational test before 1942. The government, having arrested Gandhi on his return from the Round Table Conference in January 1932, governed the Congress by ordinance: the organisation was declared unlawful, its offices seized, its funds frozen, its newspapers throttled, and tens of thousands of its workers imprisoned in successive waves as the campaign renewed itself. That the committees re-formed after each wave, that funds moved and instructions circulated through a structure the state could raid but not map completely, is the practical meaning of the Nagpur ladder of committees: redundancy as survival. The period also forced the organisation's hardest internal question of belonging. The Communal Award of August 1932 granted separate electorates to the Depressed Classes; Gandhi's fast in Yerwada prison in September against the award produced the Poona Pact with B. R. Ambedkar, joint electorates with reserved seats, and committed the Congress to a constitutional position, representation through a common roll, that shaped the republic's reservation system. A party that could be driven to renegotiate its social contract under the pressure of one man's fast was governed, in the last resort, by charisma audited by conscience; the Working Committee ratified the Poona settlement it had not negotiated. Phase Two's constitution was democratic in form and, at the summit, charismatic in fact, and the tension between those two truths is the seed of everything the succession dimension does later in this history.

Government, when it came within reach, changed the machine's centre of gravity in ways that prefigured Phase Three. Under the Government of India Act of 1935 the Congress contested the provincial elections of early 1937 and, after exacting assurances about the governors' discretionary powers, accepted office in July 1937 in six provinces, rising to eight: Bombay, Madras, the United Provinces, the Central Provinces, Bihar, Orissa, the North-West Frontier Province and Assam. A Parliamentary Board, dominated by Patel, Maulana Azad and Rajendra Prasad, supervised the ministries, and the supervision established a precedent of lasting consequence: the organisational wing, through the Working Committee and its boards, claimed authority over Congressmen who held state power. The ministries resigned in October and November 1939, protesting the Viceroy's declaration of war on India's behalf without consulting them. The Tripuri session of March 1939 exposed the phase's succession mechanism in the same years. Subhas Chandra Bose, standing for a second presidential term against Gandhi's nominee Pattabhi Sitaramayya, won by 1,580 votes to 1,377; Gandhi declared the defeat his own; the Working Committee resigned in a body; Bose, unable to form an executive, resigned in April and founded the Forward Bloc in May. The mass architecture could hold an election. It could not yet hold a result the movement's supreme leader refused to underwrite, and succession in Phase Two therefore ran on Gandhi's arbitration, a personal mechanism housed inside a formally democratic constitution.

The war years tested the architecture's discipline in a different register. When the ministries resigned in 1939, the Working Committee refused both collaboration and premature rebellion, and Gandhi's individual satyagraha of October 1940 onward sent selected satyagrahis, beginning with Vinoba Bhave, to court arrest one by one in a campaign calibrated to assert the demand for a declaration on India's post-war status without embarrassing Britain's war effort at its most vulnerable point: organisation as thermostat, holding a mass movement at a chosen temperature for two years. The Cripps mission of March 1942 offered a post-war dominion with a provincial right of secession; the Working Committee's rejection of the offer, on the ground that the secession clause dissolved the nation the Congress claimed to embody, was the last great programmatic decision of Phase Two taken by its executive in session, and the speed with which the same executive then authorised the Quit India resolution in August showed how completely the machine's parts, provincial committees, volunteer corps, underground couriers in waiting, had been integrated for a single throw. No earlier Congress body could have resolved on a nationwide rising and expected to be obeyed. The expectation was met: arrests emptied the leadership within hours, and the organisation functioned for three years without it.

Two further features of the mass architecture deserve record, because later phases inherited their absence as a problem. The first is reach into communities the old Congress had never organised. The Khilafat alliance gave the machine a Muslim constituency organised through religious leadership; in the North-West Frontier Province, Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan's Khudai Khidmatgars, founded in 1929, affiliated to the Congress and delivered a Muslim-majority province to Congress ministries in 1937 and 1946, an achievement no other phase of the party has repeated in comparable conditions. The Muslim mass contact movement of 1937, run from the AICC office under K. M. Ashraf's influence, attempted to convert that wartime and Khilafat-era reach into permanent enrolment, and its frustration by provincial ministries jealous of their own Muslim allies is an early entry in a long ledger: the Congress repeatedly possessed, and repeatedly failed to institutionalise, a direct organisational line to the country's largest minority. The second feature is the treatment of provincial sentiment as material for organisation rather than as a threat. The linguistic provincial committees of 1920 meant that Telugu, Marathi, Gujarati, Kannada and Malayali political life could be conducted inside Congress bodies, in the language of the province, a generation before the republic conceded linguistic states. Phase Two's Congress was, in this dimension, more federal than the constitution it eventually wrote, and the contrast with the later phases, in which provincial identity increasingly organised itself against the Congress in regional parties, measures what the Nagpur design had made possible and its successors let lapse.

The final years of the phase consumed the organisation in its own success. At Bombay on 8 August 1942 the AICC adopted the Quit India resolution; on 9 August the leadership was arrested, the Congress was declared unlawful, and the machine operated underground and leaderless, with Jayaprakash Narayan, Ram Manohar Lohia and Aruna Asaf Ali among the organisers of a clandestine network, until the releases of 1945. The elections of 1945 and 1946, the Cabinet Mission, the Constituent Assembly and the transfer of power followed in quick succession. By August 1947 the organisation's defining function, the struggle against the empire, had been discharged, and its provincial machines, its parliamentary board and its habits of factional containment were about to be repurposed for a task Nagpur had not designed them for: governing a sovereign republic while remaining a party.

## Phase Three: The Congress System and Government by Absorption, 1947 to 1969

The transition to Phase Three began with a resignation that defined the new phase's central tension. J. B. Kripalani, elected Congress president at Meerut in November 1946, resigned in November 1947, maintaining that the Congress organisation, as the body that had won freedom, should guide and check the government; Nehru and Patel held that ministers were answerable to the legislature, not to the party secretariat. Kripalani's defeat settled the question in the government's favour for a generation, but the organisational wing took its revenge in the party's internal elections. At the Nasik session of September 1950, Purushottam Das Tandon, backed by Patel, defeated Kripalani for the presidency, in a contest Nehru read as a challenge from the party's conservative wing; Patel's death in December 1950 removed the one figure who could mediate the quarrel; Tandon resigned in September 1951; and Nehru himself took the presidency, holding it until 1954 alongside the prime ministership. The fusion of the two offices in Nehru's person became the phase's working settlement. Thereafter the party president was either the prime minister or the prime minister's nominee, and the organisation's autonomy survived principally in the states, where chief ministers ran provincial machines of their own.

It was this arrangement that Rajni Kothari theorised in *Politics in India* (1970) as the Congress System, and the theory fits the architecture precisely because Kothari described a mechanism rather than a mood. The Congress, in his account, was a party of consensus: an umbrella that contained, inside itself, factions corresponding to nearly every organised interest and ideology in the society, so that the real competition of Indian politics occurred within the party's committees and legislature party meetings. Parties outside performed the role of parties of pressure, influencing policy by acting on the Congress factions closest to them, while the factions of the margin, those near defeat inside the party, policed the factions of the centre. Opposition, in short, had been internalised. W. H. Morris-Jones, writing on the same arrangement in 1964, called its idiom dominance and dissent: one party dominated, and the dissent that in other systems organised rival parties ran through its own ranks. The mechanism had a demanding precondition, which the next two decades supplied: the factions had to believe that staying inside and bargaining beat leaving, and the leadership had to keep the belief rational by distributing office and policy concessions widely enough that no major loser preferred the wilderness.

The electoral record of the System's high years is the record of that belief, and it is set out in this pillar's treatment of [the first general election of 1951 and 1952](/2019/01/30/first-general-election-1951-1952-india/): 364 of 489 Lok Sabha seats on about 45 percent of the vote in 1951 and 1952, 371 seats in 1957, 361 in 1962, the Congress forming governments in nearly every state. The architecture beneath the numbers was federal in fact. Provincial bosses of genuine independent standing ran the state units: K. Kamaraj in Madras, B. C. Roy in West Bengal, Partap Singh Kairon in Punjab, S. K. Patil in Bombay, Atulya Ghosh in the Bengal organisation, C. B. Gupta in the United Provinces. These men delivered their states at election time and were left, in return, substantially autonomous in state affairs, including the selection of candidates, which the central Parliamentary Board ratified more often than it dictated. Ideological positioning, the fourth dimension, was handled as internal distribution: the Avadi session of January 1955, under U. N. Dhebar's presidency, committed the party to a socialist pattern of society, satisfying the party's left while the right took comfort from the pace of implementation; the Nagpur resolution of January 1959 on cooperative farming alarmed the party's propertied wing, and the alarm was absorbed, at the cost of the resolution's quiet burial, in the manner the System existed to make possible.

The machinery of selection shows the same federal bargain at work. Candidates for the assemblies and for Parliament were proposed by district and state election committees, contested hard inside the state units, and cleared by a Central Election Committee attached to the Working Committee, which intervened decisively in a minority of seats and relied on the state bosses for the rest. The annual and special sessions of the AICC functioned as the party's parliament, where resolutions on prices, land reform and foreign policy were debated with a frankness the legislature rarely matched, because the delegates understood the session as the one arena where the government's mind could still be changed by argument. Organised ideological factions used the arena openly: the Congress Forum for Socialist Action, formed in the early 1960s, pressed the party leftward from inside; the Young Turks of the later 1960s, Chandra Shekhar, Mohan Dharia and Krishan Kant among them, attacked the party's own establishment on bank nationalisation and privy purses and were answered, promoted and contained within the tent. None of this was tidy. It was, however, continuous, minuted and consequential, and its absence in later phases is measurable in the documentary record: the AICC of the 1950s and 1960s left debates; the AICC of the dynastic phase left attendance registers.

### How did the Congress System handle opposition without a strong opposition party?

It internalised opposition as factional competition inside the party. Organised groups corresponding to interests and ideologies bargained within Congress committees, district organisations and the legislature party; outside parties acted as pressure parties, lobbying the inside faction nearest to their position.

And the factions that lost an internal contest stayed, because the System distributed office widely enough to make the next round worth waiting for.

Absorption was tested severely twice in the 1950s and passed both tests, at a price. The reorganisation of states on linguistic lines, recommended by the States Reorganisation Commission in 1955 and enacted in 1956, required the Congress to disappoint powerful regional claims, Telugu, Marathi, Gujarati and Punjabi among them, and the provincial machines carried the settlement through without a split, though Bombay's bilingual experiment stored up trouble that arrived in 1960. In Kerala in 1957 the Communist Party won a state election and formed a ministry under E. M. S. Namboodiripad, the System's first experience of a rival governing a major state; the Congress organisation in Kerala, joining the agitation known as the Vimochana Samaram, pressed the centre until the ministry was dismissed in July 1959 under Article 356. The episode is recorded here because it marks the mechanism's limit as clearly as its successes mark its strength: where an outside party actually won, absorption gave way to the constitutional override, and the precedent entered the republic's federal practice with consequences later phases inherited.

The Bhubaneswar session of January 1964, the last Nehru attended, catches the System at its zenith and at the edge of its succession problem in a single scene. The session adopted a resolution on democracy and socialism drafted to hold the party's wings in one formula, Nehru defended it in a speech delivered while visibly unwell, and four months later he was dead. That the transition which followed was orderly was not luck. It was the Kamaraj Plan's harvest: a party president of independent provincial standing, a bench of senior leaders owed their eminence to organisation rather than to the departing leader's favour, and a parliamentary party accustomed to being counted. The System's resilience in 1964 and 1966 is the strongest evidence for Kothari's framework, and the strongest contrast with what the same party would do with the vacancies of 1991 and 2019, when the bench, the president's independent standing and the habit of counting had all been dismantled.

The Andhra episode of 1952 and 1953 shows absorption working by concession at the level of the map itself. Potti Sriramulu, a veteran of the salt satyagraha, fasted from October 1952 for a Telugu-speaking state and died on 15 December after fifty-eight days; the disorder that followed in the Telugu districts forced the centre's hand, and the state of Andhra was inaugurated on 1 October 1953. The demand had been Congress policy since Nagpur's linguistic committees, and its concession, however reluctant, kept the Telugu movement inside the national frame and the Congress competitive in the region for three decades. Set beside the later phases the contrast is sharp. A demand of comparable force arising after 1998 would meet a centre whose instinct was arbitration and delay, because the machinery for converting regional pressure into a negotiated settlement, provincial committees with standing, factional spokesmen with followings, sessions where the argument could be had and lost, had been allowed to lapse. Absorption is not a doctrine. It is a set of rooms in which bargains can be struck, and Phase Three kept the rooms furnished.

Succession, the third dimension, is where Phase Three looks most different from what followed it. Nehru's death on 27 May 1964 produced a managed transition: Kamaraj, as Congress president, canvassed the party and announced Lal Bahadur Shastri as the consensus choice within days. Shastri's death at Tashkent on 11 January 1966 produced the System's most formal act of self-government: the Congress Parliamentary Party voted, and Indira Gandhi defeated Morarji Desai by 355 votes to 169, a contested ballot among elected legislators deciding the prime ministership. Whatever else changed after 1966, those two transitions show a party whose succession mechanism existed, functioned and was used. The strain showed first at the periphery. The general election of 1967 cut the Congress to 283 Lok Sabha seats and removed it from power in eight states, where coalition governments of its opponents formed, from Madras, where the DMK won outright, to West Bengal and Kerala, where United Fronts took office; the defection of legislators between parties became a market, and the phrase Aaya Ram Gaya Ram entered the language from a Haryana legislator's serial floor-crossing in 1967. The System's answer, historically, would have been to bargain the dissidents back. The events of 1969 showed that the answer had stopped working at the centre itself.

## The Absorption Mechanism Breaks: 1969 to 1977

The men who broke with Indira Gandhi in 1969 were themselves products of the System's last successful act of organisational management. The Kamaraj Plan of August 1963, adopted by the Working Committee on Nehru's urging, had required senior ministers at the centre and in the states to resign office and return to organisational work; Morarji Desai, Lal Bahadur Shastri, S. K. Patil and Jagjivan Ram left the Union cabinet, and Kamaraj himself, Biju Patnaik and Bakshi Ghulam Mohammad left chief ministerships. The episode, treated in this pillar's study of [the Kamaraj Plan of 1963](/2019/02/27/kamaraj-plan-1963-congress/), refreshed the organisation's senior ranks and incidentally assembled the college of leaders, Kamaraj, S. Nijalingappa, Patil, Atulya Ghosh and their allies, who as the Syndicate would manage the successions of 1964 and 1966 and then attempt to manage the prime minister they had installed. The 1969 crisis was, structurally, a dispute over whether that college's arbitration still governed the party.

The sequence is documented in detail in the decisive-moment treatment of [the 1969 split's structural-cultural rupture](/2019/03/26/1969-congress-split-decisive-moment/); the organisational reading attends to the machinery. At the Bangalore session of the AICC in July 1969, Indira Gandhi circulated an unsigned note of economic proposals, the stray thoughts that her opponents read as a challenge to the party's settled programme. On 19 July 1969 her government nationalised fourteen major banks by ordinance, a policy stroke against the Syndicate's preferences, executed through the state rather than negotiated through the party. The presidential election of August 1969, made necessary by Zakir Husain's death in office in May, then converted a factional contest into a constitutional one: the party's official nominee was N. Sanjiva Reddy, the Syndicate's candidate; Indira Gandhi, having signed his nomination papers, called on Congress legislators to vote by conscience for the independent candidate V. V. Giri, who won. A party of consensus can survive a lost vote. What it cannot survive is its own prime minister instructing its legislators to defeat its own nominee, because the act declares that arbitration inside the organisation no longer binds the strongest faction. On 12 November 1969 the Working Committee's majority expelled Indira Gandhi from the party. She answered by constituting her followers as the Congress (Requisitionist), soon Congress (R), against the Congress (Organisation) of the Syndicate, and the Election Commission, asked to decide which body was the Indian National Congress for the purposes of the reserved election symbol, froze the old symbol of a pair of bullocks under a yoke and allotted the ruling faction a cow and calf.

The mid-term election of March 1971 ratified the new architecture with 352 Lok Sabha seats for Congress (R) against 16 for Congress (O), on a campaign, garibi hatao, abolish poverty, that addressed the electorate over the heads of every intermediate organisation. Inder Malhotra's biography *Indira Gandhi: A Personal and Political Biography* (1989) reads the years after 1971 as the systematic conversion of a federal party into a court: organisational elections, held for the last time in 1972, were thereafter postponed from year to year on grounds of national circumstance; state Congress presidents and chief ministers came to be appointed, transferred and dismissed from Delhi; the Working Committee met to ratify. Kothari's mechanism required the leadership to keep losing factions inside the tent. After 1971 the leadership no longer needed them inside, because the leader's direct electoral connection with the voter, demonstrated in 1971 and again in the state elections of 1972, substituted for the machine that had once made and unmade governments. The substitution is the hinge between Phase Three and Phase Four, and this pillar's treatment of Indira Gandhi through [the personality-as-institution framework](/2019/03/21/indira-gandhi-personality-institution/) examines the personal logic of it. Its organisational cost can be read in the under-cited record this hub draws on: the internal organisational reviews preserved among the AICC Papers at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library for 1972 and 1973, which record complaints from the party's own functionaries about inflated membership registers, bogus enrolments manufactured to capture committee elections that were then not held, and district committees that existed on paper between one election campaign and the next. Researchers working through digitised copies of such organisational files, reports, minutes and statistical returns preserved as office documents, can open them in a browser through the [Office File Viewer for Excel, DOCX and PPTX files](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) without the original software, which is how much of this archive is in practice consulted. The fragility the reviews document is the same fragility the post-2014 party exhibits. It was present, minuted and filed, forty years before the fall it explains.

The other half of the 1969 division supplies the control case. Congress (Organisation), which retained the party's older office-bearers, much of its state machinery in Gujarat, Tamil Nadu and Mysore, and leaders of the eminence of Morarji Desai and Kamaraj, contested the 1971 election in a Grand Alliance with the Jana Sangh, the Swatantra Party and the Samyukta Socialist Party and won sixteen seats. Machinery without the leader proved as insufficient as the leader without machinery had once seemed: the Syndicate's bosses discovered that their district networks had been built to distribute the favours of a governing national party, and, severed from the government and the name's electoral magic, the networks withered within one parliament. Kamaraj died in October 1975; Congress (O) merged into the Janata Party in 1977. The lesson the survivors drew, that in the new architecture the leader was the organisation's only irreplaceable asset, became the founding premise of Phase Four, and the premise was self-fulfilling: every subsequent investment flowed to the anchor, and none to the machinery that might have made the anchor dispensable. The state elections of 1972, won handsomely by Congress (R) across most of India in the afterglow of the Bangladesh war, were the last elections the old machinery could plausibly claim to have delivered, and the organisational elections of the same year were the last it was permitted to hold.

Money traces the same arc as authority, and the finance record of these years belongs in the organisational evidence. The founding-Moderate phase ran on patrons, princes and prosperous lawyers funding an annual conference out of commitment and prestige. The mass phase democratised income as it democratised membership: four-anna subscriptions, the Tilak Swaraj Fund's crore of small and large donations, and the support of Indian business houses, the Birla connection to Gandhi being the documented archetype, gave the movement a treasury answerable, in principle, to its committees. The Congress System regularised corporate donation to a governing party whose factions and state bosses each maintained their own channels to local industry and trade, a plural and competitive finance that matched the party's federal structure. The amendment of the Companies Act in 1969, carried by Indira Gandhi's government, banned company donations to political parties outright, in the name of cleansing public life. Its organisational consequence was the opposite of plural: lawful, auditable finance contracted, unaccounted finance expanded to fill the space, and the custody of unaccounted money concentrated where custody could not be questioned, in the leader's immediate circle. A party financed through many hands can argue with its leader. A party financed through one door must knock on it. The ban's repeal in 1985, restoring corporate donation in regulated form, changed the law without dispersing the custody, and the finance dimension of the dynastic phase, centralised, opaque and personal, was fixed in these years as durably as its succession principle.

The Emergency of June 1975 to March 1977 completed the subordination of organisation to leadership. The Allahabad High Court judgment of 12 June 1975, setting aside Indira Gandhi's 1971 election from Rae Bareli on two proved charges of corrupt practice, put the leader's personal survival at stake, and the proclamation of Emergency on the night of 25 and 26 June converted the party into an instrument of that survival: the organisation that had once arbitrated prime ministers passed resolutions endorsing the suspension of the democracy its members governed under. A parallel centre, Sanjay Gandhi and the Youth Congress, issued its own programme and vetted its own loyalties, an arrangement without precedent in the party's history, in which authority flowed from kinship rather than from any committee. When elections were called in January 1977 and held in March, the machine that in 1952 had read the country district by district could not read it at all: the Congress won 154 seats, lost every seat in several northern states, and Indira Gandhi lost Rae Bareli to Raj Narain, the petitioner of 1971. Phase Three had ended in substance by 1972, when internal elections lapsed. In 1977 the electorate certified the fact.

The first great rehearsal of the exit logic that defines Phase Four came in the defeat itself. On 2 February 1977, with the election campaign under way, Jagjivan Ram, the most senior Dalit leader in the government, resigned from the cabinet and the party together with Hemvati Nandan Bahuguna and Nandini Satpathy, and formed the Congress for Democracy, which allied with the Janata Party and carried a decisive bloc of the Congress's own electorate into the opposition's column. Under the System's rules, a leader of Jagjivan Ram's standing, twice a contender for the succession, would have been bought back with the promise of the next round. By 1977 there was no next round on offer: the organisation could not promise him a ballot, a committee or a succession in which his strength would count, because all three had been suspended in the name of the leader's authority. He therefore took his following out of the party, and the party discovered, in the count of March 1977, what a following is worth when it leaves. The exits of 1997 to 2011 that built the Trinamool Congress, the Nationalist Congress Party and the YSR Congress are this episode generalised into a system, which is why the article dates the dynastic phase's logic from the Emergency years even though its constitution arrives in 1978.

## Phase Four Begins: The Leader as Organisational Anchor, 1978 to 1991

The Congress that lost in 1977 split within the year over the only question the new architecture could not arbitrate: the position of the leader. The official Congress under K. Brahmananda Reddy sought terms on which Indira Gandhi would accept a subordinate place; on 2 January 1978 her supporters constituted the Congress (I), the I for Indira, as a separate party, and the Election Commission allotted it the open hand as its symbol. The founding documents of 1978 are terse beside the Nagpur constitution of 1920, and the terseness is the point. The new party did not need a theory of membership or a ladder of committees, because its organising principle was allegiance to a named person, and its constitution lodged effective authority in the president and the president's nominees at every level. Indira Gandhi's return through the Chikmagalur by-election of November 1978 and the general election of January 1980, which gave Congress (I) 353 seats, proved the design electorally sufficient. This pillar's study of [the post-1980 office-rebuilding completed](/2019/05/04/1980-indira-return-power/) traces the rebuilding in detail; its organisational signature was appointment. Pradesh Congress presidents were named from Delhi and replaced at short notice. Chief ministers were treated as the centre's deputies: Andhra Pradesh, a state the Congress had governed without interruption since 1956, cycled through Tanguturi Anjaiah, Bhavanam Venkatarami Reddy and Kotla Vijayabhaskara Reddy between 1980 and January 1983, when the state's voters ended the experiment by electing N. T. Rama Rao's Telugu Desam, a party founded the previous year, in a demonstration that the anchor model forfeited the provincial autonomy on which Phase Three's state machines had run.

The interlude of 1977 to 1980 also ran the experiment of a Congress deprived of the family and recorded the result. The remnant official party, passing from Brahmananda Reddy to Devaraj Urs and renamed Congress (Urs) in 1979, attempted to operate as an old-style organisation of notables, and dissolved into the Janata experiment's own collapse; the Indira faction, in opposition, did not rebuild a single district committee of consequence in three years and did not need to, since its asset was a person around whom the electorate's memory organised itself. When Charan Singh's government fell in August 1979 after Congress (I) withdrew the support it had lent him, the withdrawal was announced as one leader's decision and executed without a recorded vote of any party body. The contrast with 1966, when a prime ministership changed hands on a counted ballot of 355 to 169, is the whole argument of this article in miniature. A party that can count its legislators can survive its leader. A party organised as a leader's following can only wait for the leader, and between 1978 and 1980 the Congress, in both its fragments, was learning to wait.

The southern verdicts of January 1983 generalised the Andhra lesson before Delhi absorbed it. In Karnataka, governed by the Congress without interruption since independence, the Janata Party under Ramakrishna Hegde took office after an election in which the Congress's state unit, its leadership rotated from the centre through the chief ministerships of R. Gundu Rao's predecessors and successors, Devaraj Urs's expulsion having removed the one provincial figure of independent mass standing, campaigned as an administration rather than as an organisation. Two of the party's four southern strongholds thus fell within one week to parties organised around provincial pride and provincial leadership, the Telugu Desam explicitly so. The centre's response was diagnostic rather than organisational: campaign tours by the prime minister, changes of state leadership by announcement, and no restoration of the provincial elections or provincial discretion through which the lost ground might have been retaken by residents. The anchor model could win a national election, as 1980 had proved and 1984 would prove again on an altogether larger scale. It could not reproduce itself in a province, because reproduction is an organisational act and the model's only organisational act was appointment.

The succession of October 1984 displayed the new mechanism in its purest form. Indira Gandhi was assassinated by her own guards on 31 October 1984, in the aftermath of the army action at the Golden Temple that June; within hours, Rajiv Gandhi, a party general secretary with three years in politics, was sworn in as prime minister by a decision taken among a small circle and ratified by the parliamentary party afterward. The general election of December 1984, held in the shadow of the assassination and the anti-Sikh massacres that followed it in Delhi, returned Congress (I) with 414 seats, the largest tally in the party's history, on about 49 percent of the vote in the seats it contested. A Phase Two or Phase Three reading of that result would look for the machine that produced it. There was scarcely a machine to find: the organisation's contribution was the name, the symbol and the memory of the assassinated leader, and the verdict's scale measured the anchor's pull rather than the architecture's health.

Rajiv Gandhi understood the deficit and said so in the most quoted organisational speech of the phase. Addressing the Congress centenary session at Bombay on 28 December 1985, he charged that the party had been captured by brokers of power and influence, that its rank and file had been displaced by a caucus of the self-seeking, and that the organisation had drifted from the values of the movement that built it. The speech is the dynastic phase's internal audit, delivered by the anchor himself. Its sequel defines the phase more precisely than the speech does: no organisational elections followed, no brokers were displaced by ballot, and the remedy attempted was administrative, a younger cabinet and a rhetoric of modernisation, applied from the same centre the speech had indicted. The Bofors disclosures from 1987 onward consumed the government's credibility; V. P. Singh, expelled from the party in 1987 after raising the corruption question as a minister, built the Jan Morcha and then the Janata Dal out of the Congress's own dissident material. The election of November 1989 reduced the Congress to 197 seats and opposition. The year in opposition exposed the organisational deficit in its purest form, because 1990 demanded of the party the one thing its architecture could no longer produce: a deliberated position under pressure. V. P. Singh's government implemented the Mandal Commission's recommendation of 27 percent reservation for the Other Backward Classes in August 1990, and the BJP answered with the Rath Yatra of September and October 1990 toward Ayodhya. A Phase Three Congress, squeezed between caste mobilisation below and religious mobilisation above, would have fought the question through its committees, its state units reporting the ground, its factions forcing a line that the losers would then defend in public. The Congress of 1990 had no arena in which such a fight could occur. Its Working Committee neither endorsed Mandal nor opposed it with conviction; its state units took contradictory positions according to local caste arithmetic; its leader declined to be counted clearly on either mobilisation; and the party watched both of the decade's defining movements organise themselves in other parties' structures. The seats of 1989 were a defeat. The silence of 1990 was a diagnosis.

In May 1991, campaigning for the election that might have restored him, Rajiv Gandhi was assassinated by an LTTE suicide bomber at Sriperumbudur. The party turned, as its constitution by then required it to turn, to the family, and when Sonia Gandhi declined, the parliamentary leadership settled on P. V. Narasimha Rao, a former general secretary of the old school, who formed a minority government in June 1991. The phase's first sub-period thus closed with its organising question unanswered: the anchor had held the party together, and nothing else had been built that could.

## The Dynastic-Organisational Consolidation: 1991 to 2014

The Rao years are the dynastic phase's exception that proves its rule, a sub-period inside a sub-period in which an organisational man briefly ran the party on older principles. At the Tirupati plenary session of April 1992 the Congress held elections to its organisational bodies, the first full round of internal polls in twenty years, and Rao was elected president by the AICC rather than installed by acclamation among courtiers. His government, a minority in the Lok Sabha, carried the economic liberalisation of 1991, a programme the party's programme documents had not contemplated, and the organisation absorbed the ideological reversal the way Phase Three would have, by factional grumbling contained within the tent. The limits of the restoration showed at the ballot box and in the secretariat together. The demolition of the Babri Masjid on 6 December 1992, under a Congress government at the centre that had assured the courts the structure would be protected, broke the party's claim on the Muslim vote across north India for a generation. The general election of 1996 reduced the party to 140 seats; it declined the first invitation to form a government despite those 140 seats, supported the United Front from outside, and withdrew that support twice, while its own presidency passed from Rao to Sitaram Kesri in the autumn of 1996 through a Working Committee settlement rather than an organisational contest. The party that had theorised absorption could no longer absorb even its own succession.

Sonia Gandhi's assumption of the presidency on 14 March 1998, after the Working Committee asked Kesri to step down and installed her by resolution, is the dynastic-organisational phase's constitutional moment, the point at which succession by family acclamation became the party's settled law. The organisational consequences were double-edged, and both edges belong in the record. On one side, the anchor worked as anchors do: a party drifting toward regional fragments acquired a single centre of arbitration whose decisions, on candidates, alliances and chief ministers, were final because no rival centre could be imagined. The Chintan Shivir at Pachmarhi in September 1998 resolved on ideological self-reliance, the ekla chalo instinct of a party that still thought of itself as the natural party of government; the arithmetic of the states, where regional parties held the ground the Congress had vacated, reversed the resolve within one election cycle, and the phase's defining practice became the management of dependence, coalition arithmetic as an organisational condition rather than a tactic. On the other side, the price of arbitration from a household is that factions cease to bargain and begin to position. The contrast with Kothari's mechanism is exact. A losing faction in the Congress System waited for the next internal round. A losing faction after 1998 weighed the exit price, and some of the party's most consequential figures paid it: Mamata Banerjee, leaving in 1997 to found the Trinamool Congress in January 1998 after her challenges to the Bengal leadership were arbitrated away; Sharad Pawar, P. A. Sangma and Tariq Anwar, expelled in May 1999 for raising the question of Sonia Gandhi's foreign birth, who founded the Nationalist Congress Party within weeks; Y. S. Jagan Mohan Reddy, who left in 2010 after his claim to succeed his father in Andhra Pradesh was refused, and built the YSR Congress into the state's governing party by 2019. Each exit removed a provincial machine the party had once contained. The exits, more than any single election, are the dynastic phase's organisational balance sheet.

One body of evidence complicates any simple story of organisational death in these years, and honesty requires it in the record. Between 1998 and 2003 the Congress won and held a string of major state governments: Delhi under Sheila Dikshit from 1998, Madhya Pradesh under Digvijaya Singh, Rajasthan under Ashok Gehlot, Karnataka under S. M. Krishna from 1999, and by 2003 the party governed, alone or in coalition, in fourteen states. These victories were won by state leaderships operating with a local rootedness the centre's model did not create and could not command: Dikshit's Delhi administration, Gehlot's Rajasthan organisation and Digvijaya Singh's Madhya Pradesh machine were provincial constructions, closer in spirit to Phase Three's bosses than to the nomination culture of the high command. The paradox is instructive. Where a state leader rebuilt a functioning provincial organisation, the party remained electorally alive; where the centre's arbitration substituted for organisation, as in Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, the party sank toward single figures and stayed there. The dynastic phase did not destroy the state machines in a single act. It starved them of the one resource they could not generate locally, a credible prospect that internal work would be rewarded by internal advancement, and the machines that survived did so on the personal credit of their builders, a wasting asset that retired with them.

The decade of power from 2004 to 2014 displayed the architecture in government. The election of 2004, treated in this pillar's study of [the 2004 UPA shock verdict](/2019/06/22/2004-upa-shock-verdict-explained/), gave the Congress 145 seats and the premiership through the United Progressive Alliance, a pre-poll and post-poll coalition held together by a common minimum programme and by outside support from the Left parties, who held the government's survival in their gift until they withdrew over the civil nuclear agreement in 2008. The arrangement institutionalised the phase's two structural features. Externally, the party governed as the largest component of an arithmetic it did not control, the position the hub article on [the coalition era from 1989 to 2014](/2019/05/15/coalition-era-1989-2014-explained/) anatomises. Internally, the National Advisory Council, chaired by Sonia Gandhi from 2004, stood beside the cabinet as a second centre where the party's programme was drafted, a dual structure that reproduced, at the level of the state, the separation between the anchor and the administration. The election of 2009, 206 seats, the party's best tally since 1991, appeared to vindicate the design. It vindicated instead its dependence on favourable arithmetic and a popular welfare programme, for the organisation beneath the result continued to thin: organisational elections remained formalities that returned the leadership unopposed, state units waited on Delhi for candidates and chief ministers until delay itself became a cause of defeat, and when Rahul Gandhi became vice-president at the Jaipur Chintan Shivir of January 2013, the succession was arranged as inheritance announced, not as a contest concluded. A party so arranged has no mechanism for registering alarm. The alarm, in May 2014, arrived from outside.

One experiment of the period deserves record precisely because it tested the diagnosis and was abandoned. In the run-up to the 2014 election, Rahul Gandhi's office introduced American-style primaries for candidate selection in a small set of Lok Sabha constituencies, sixteen in the usual count, in which enrolled local workers and office-bearers voted to choose the party's candidate. The experiment's premise was the four-phase diagnosis in miniature: that selection imposed from Delhi had severed the link between the organisation and the candidate, and that restoring a counted internal vote might rebuild it. The primaries were held, produced some winners and several revolts by passed-over aspirants, and were not repeated for the assembly elections that followed or for 2019. An organisation confident in its internal life would have treated the revolts as the price of a system and persisted. The episode closed instead with the older method restored, screening committees and the centre's final list, and it stands in the record as the dynastic phase's own controlled experiment on itself: the machinery of internal democracy was switched on once, briefly, in sixteen places, and switched off when it behaved like machinery.

Beneath the national leadership, the transmission belts through which every earlier phase had reproduced itself had also worn through. The Indian Youth Congress, a formidable campaign organisation under Sanjay Gandhi's command in the Emergency years and a nursery of later chief ministers, settled into an appointed auxiliary whose brief experiment with internal elections after 2008, conducted under Rahul Gandhi's direction with an external election authority, was not sustained as a general system. The National Students' Union survived as a campus presence in a few universities. The Mahila Congress and the Seva Dal persisted as frontal organisations with ceremonial roles at sessions. State units in the party's former heartland recorded the decay in results that no leadership change disturbed: in Uttar Pradesh the party won 28 assembly seats in 2012 and seven in 2017 as the junior partner in an alliance; in Bihar it contested the 2015 assembly election as the third component of a victorious alliance and held no independent provincial organisation worth the name. A mass party is reproduced in its districts or not at all. The dynastic phase's districts were, by the testimony of the party's own introspection exercises, staffed by nomination, funded by candidates and activated at elections, an apparatus for contesting power that had forgotten how to accumulate it. The party's own review of the 2014 defeat, chaired by A. K. Antony and submitted in August 2014, recorded organisational weakness among the causes placed before the leadership, in the careful language of a body reporting to the authority it was examining.

## After 2014: The Return of Structural Fragility

The general election of May 2014 returned the Congress with 44 seats and 19.3 percent of the national vote, the lowest figures in its history on both measures; it fell short of the 55 seats required for its leader in the Lok Sabha to be recognised as Leader of the Opposition, and it lost, in the months and years that followed, a sequence of state governments that had survived the national defeat, Maharashtra and Haryana in 2014, Assam in 2016, Uttarakhand and Uttar Pradesh in 2017, where it won seven seats as a junior ally. The election of 2019, fought with Rahul Gandhi as party president after his unopposed election to the post in December 2017, returned 52 seats. Rahul Gandhi resigned the presidency on 3 July 2019, publishing a letter that took responsibility for the defeat and asked the party to choose a successor outside the family; the Working Committee, unable to operate a succession mechanism it had not used in decades, turned on 10 August 2019 to Sonia Gandhi as interim president. As the record stands at the end of 2019, the party's central organisational fact is that sentence: a party of 134 years, possessed of committees, constitutions and a membership in the millions on its own registers, could resolve a leadership vacancy only by returning to the household that the vacancy was supposed to transcend.

### What replaced factional bargaining inside the post-1978 Congress?

Arbitration from the leadership household. Under the Congress System, factions settled their contests in committee elections, legislature-party votes and negotiated distributions of office. After 1978 those arenas emptied, and disputes travelled upward as petitions to the family instead of being settled in any committee of the party.

State presidents and chief ministers were appointed and removed by the centre, and a faction that lost the arbitration had no next internal round to wait for, which is why exit replaced voice as the dissatisfied leader's rational move. The reading of these events as a sudden collapse, whether told as a rival's triumph or as a leadership failure, mistakes the timescale of the cause. Pratap Bhanu Mehta's essays after 2014, the most cited statement of the institutional-decline argument, locate the hollowing of the Congress in the long subordination of the organisation to its leadership and in the party's loss of the intermediate institutions, state machines, local notables, ideological cadres, through which it once converted votes into presence. That argument, at its strongest, is a four-phase argument without the name: it dates the damage not to 2014 but to the decades in which the anchor model substituted allegiance for architecture. The events since 2014 keep supplying the corroboration. In Assam, Himanta Biswa Sarma, the state organisation's most effective manager, left for the BJP in August 2015 after his claim on the chief ministership was arbitrated against, and took the state's government with him at the next election. In Arunachal Pradesh in 2016 the party's legislative majority dissolved through defection to a regional vehicle. In Karnataka in July 2019 the coalition government the Congress had sustained fell to a sequence of legislative resignations its organisation could neither prevent nor reverse. A Phase Three Congress, confronted with the same ambitions, would have bought the ambitious with office inside the tent, because the tent was where office was. The post-2014 Congress confronts them with a tent whose only furniture is access to the household, and access, unlike office, cannot be divided among rivals. The state elections of 21 October 2019, counted three days before this article's locked date, supply a further confirmation of the pattern within the evidence window. In Haryana the Congress, written off after a decade of factional litigation between its state factions, doubled its assembly strength to 31 seats under Bhupinder Singh Hooda, a provincial leader of the old school who had consolidated the state unit around himself in defiance of the centre's earlier preferences; in Maharashtra the party survived as the fourth party of a hung assembly on the strength of its remaining district pockets. Where a provincial organisation with a rooted leader existed, the vote held. Where the party presented itself as a national brand with a famous name and no district machinery, in seat after seat of the Hindi belt it had once governed outright, its candidates forfeited their deposits. The electorate of 2019 was not refusing the Congress's history. It was reporting, constituency by constituency, which of the party's architectures still existed on the ground.

The structural fragility was the Congress System's own suppressed premise: Kothari's mechanism worked because the society's conflicts arrived inside a party built to process them. The dynastic phase kept the party's name and reach while dismantling the processing machinery, and the decline after 2014 is the sound of conflicts being processed elsewhere.

The counter-evidence of the state record after 2014 deserves the same honesty, because it refines rather than refutes the structural reading. In Punjab in 2017 the Congress won a clear assembly majority under Amarinder Singh, a state leader who had defied the centre's preferences and campaigned on his own authority; in December 2018 the party won Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan and Chhattisgarh, in each case led by state figures, Kamal Nath, Ashok Gehlot, Bhupesh Baghel, whose organisations had been rebuilt provincially. The pattern is Phase Three's ghost: where a provincial machine exists, the Congress can still win a state. What the centre could no longer do was convert these survivals into national recovery at the general election held five months later, or arbitrate the ambitions inside them: Rajasthan's government spent 2019 managing the public rivalry between its chief minister and his deputy, Madhya Pradesh's survived on a majority of two, and Karnataka's coalition, the third prize of that season, fell in July 2019 to the resignation sequence described above. A party whose remaining strength is archipelagic, islands of provincial organisation in a sea of central vacancy, has the shape of the Congress of 1916 more than of 1956, and the four-phase frame names that condition precisely: the architecture of the whole has reverted to the condition from which a refounding once rescued it.

The boundary dates require one paragraph of defence, since two of them are double. The third phase is dated 1947 to 1991 in the table's lineage because the Congress System's parliamentary dominance, the asset on which absorption rested, survived in residual form until the elections of 1989 and 1991 ended it, yet its organisational substance, internal elections, autonomous provinces, counted successions, was dismantled between 1969 and 1978; the table therefore records the phase by its constitutional span and the text describes the hollowing within it. The fourth phase carries two founding dates for the mirror reason: 1978 supplied its constitution, the leader-anchored party with a new name and symbol, while 1998 supplied its settled succession law, the point after which the anchor and the family were constitutionally the same thing. Readers who prefer single dates may take 1978 for the fourth phase's founding and treat 1998 as its consolidation; the architecture described is identical on either dating, which is the test of whether a boundary dispute is real.

Two cautions belong with the table. The phases are ideal types built from the record, and individual years straddle them: the Congress of 1950, with a party president defying a prime minister, fits the third phase's architecture under strain, not outside it; the Rao interlude of 1991 to 1996 fits the fourth phase with a first-phase memory working inside it. And the table measures architecture, not worth. The founding-Moderate design was exquisitely fitted to its task of extracting concessions from a liberal empire by argument. It failed at Surat because the task had changed, which is precisely the article's point about every phase, including the one whose failure is still unfolding.

## Continuity, Rise and Decline, or Four Organisations? The Adjudication

The strongest case against the four-phase reading deserves to be put at its full strength, because it rests on facts. The Indian National Congress of 2019 is, in law and in self-understanding, the body founded in 1885: the name is continuous, the Election Commission's recognition descends through the splits to the faction that holds the name, the constitution under which the party operates claims descent from the constitution of 1920 as amended, the flag and the memory of the freedom struggle are undivided inheritances, and the broad ideological positioning, secularism, a mixed economy leaning toward welfare, suspicion of majoritarian nationalism, is recognisably the positioning of 1947 and, in embryo, of 1885. The party's official historiography, from Sitaramayya onward, builds its narrative on these continuities, and a reading that dissolves the party into four organisations can appear to dissolve the continuities with it. The rise-and-decline framing adds a second argument: the electoral curve does rise to a mid-century plateau and does fall, steeply, after 1989 and precipitously after 2014, and a framework that refuses the curve's plain shape can appear to be sophistication purchased at the price of the obvious.

### Does the survival of the Congress name prove organisational continuity?

No, and the splits demonstrate why. In 1969 and again in 1978 the Election Commission had to decide which of two bodies was the Indian National Congress, and in both cases the claimant that prevailed was the one holding the parliamentary majority and the leader.

The claimant that lost held the older constitution and the older office-bearers. The name follows power and numbers: it is evidence of legal and symbolic continuity, which is real, and it is no evidence at all about architecture, which is where organisations live.

The adjudication turns on keeping two kinds of continuity separate. Nominal-institutional continuity, of name, registration, symbols and self-description, is genuine and undisputed, and the four-phase reading does not deny it; a family that rebuilds its house three times on the same plot has continuous tenure and three successive houses. Organisational-architectural continuity is the disputed claim, and on the five dimensions of Table 1 it fails at each boundary on documentary evidence: a new constitution and a new membership principle in 1920; the lapse of internal elections and the transfer of candidate selection and state leadership to the centre after 1971; a new party, name-suffix, symbol and succession principle in 1978; succession by household acclamation formalised in 1998. Kothari himself supplies the adjudication's decisive testimony, because the Congress System was never, in its author's presentation, a permanent constitution of Indian politics. *Politics in India* (1970) described a working mechanism resting on a consensus that the freedom movement had deposited and that time would spend; Kothari's framework is a theory of why the System would work while its conditions lasted, and the conditions, an encompassing party facing pressure parties in a polity where every major interest sought a Congress address, are Phase Three conditions. Myron Weiner's *Party Building in a New Nation* (1967), a sympathetic study of the Congress organisation at its height, already worried in print about the decay of the party's local bodies and the substitution of governmental patronage for organisational work. The fragility was diagnosed from inside the System's best years. It did not arrive in 2014.

The scholarly record, as distinct from the party's own, mostly convicts the continuous-tradition framing on the same evidence. Sarkar's *Modern India 1885-1947* (1983) treats the pre-1920 and post-1920 movements as different political worlds, connected by personnel and programme but divided by the social depth of their mobilisation, and his scepticism about claimed membership figures undercuts the myth of an unbroken mass tradition from within the most respected narrative of the period. Bipan Chandra and his collaborators, in *India's Struggle for Independence* (1988), narrate the movement as a succession of phases with distinct strategies, an account whose periodisation quietly concedes the four-phase premise while retaining the single-subject grammar. Judith Brown's studies of Gandhi's rise argue that Gandhi built his all-India position by assembling provincial coalitions of local patrons and brokers, which is to say that Phase Two was a new organisational construction resting on the ruins and networks of the old, not the old body continuing. The four-phase reading of this hub is therefore less an invention than a systematisation: it takes the discontinuities the specialists already record, gives them dimensions, dates and a table, and draws the consequence they leave undrawn, that a party so often refounded cannot be understood, or its decline explained, as one life.

Against the rise-and-decline framing the verdict is complementary. The curve is real and the four-phase reading does not flatten it; it re-describes its cause. What rose between 1885 and 1952 was not one organisation growing but three successive architectures each capturing a larger arena than the last, petition, mobilisation, governance. What declined after 1989 was not a party ageing but an architecture, the dynastic-organisational one, whose design traded the processing of internal conflict for the arbitration of it, and which therefore lost members, provinces and finally elections in the order the design predicts: first the provincial machines to exits and regional rivals, then the parliamentary majority to coalition dependence, then, when the arithmetic failed, the appearance of a floor. The same re-description governs this pillar's reading of the wider field: the post-2014 ruling party's integration of cadre, government and ideology, examined in the hub treatment of [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/), is an organisational fact before it is an electoral one, and it competes against a Congress whose comparable integration belongs to a phase that ended decades ago. Where does that leave the Nehruvian settlement with which this pillar's frame began, [the Nehruvian-era Congress System operationalisation](/2019/01/25/nehruvian-era-settlement-three-tensions/) of the republic's founding tensions? It leaves it where the evidence puts it: as the achievement of one phase's architecture, admirable and unrepeatable on its own terms, because the architecture, not merely the era, has passed.

## What the Long Arc Leaves Standing

Four conclusions survive the evidence. First, the unit of Congress history is the phase, not the party: arguments about the Congress that do not specify which architecture they describe will keep mistaking the properties of one phase for the character of the whole, in praise and in obituary alike. Second, succession is the dimension on which phases die. The founding-Moderate phase had no mechanism for deciding between programmes and died of the lack at Surat; Phase Two ran on one man's arbitration and bequeathed the habit; Phase Three, uniquely, built contested and consensual mechanisms and used both, in 1964 and 1966; the dynastic phase replaced mechanism with inheritance, and the vacancy of 2019, resolved by an interim return to the previous incumbent, is the design stating its own limit. Third, the absorptive mechanism is the variable that connects organisation to electoral fate across the whole arc: where it worked, in the 1950s and in the factional settlements of the mass phase, the Congress converted opponents into participants; where it failed, at Surat in 1907, in the exits of 1997 to 2011, and in the defections of 2015 to 2019, the party's losses were organisational before they were electoral, and the seats followed the structure with a lag. Fourth, the post-2014 decline is therefore neither an inexplicable collapse nor a routine defeat. It is the dynastic-organisational phase arriving at the structural fragility that the Congress System carried beneath its absorptive surface from the beginning, the fragility Kothari's mechanism managed, the 1972 and 1973 reviews minuted, and the anchor model of the years after 1978 deepened by dismantling the machinery that had kept it in check.

The frame also states, for the record, what would falsify its application to the future. A fifth phase would have begun when the party next changes its constitution of belonging or its succession mechanism in fact: a contested election for the presidency carried through against the incumbent leadership's preference, organisational polls whose results the centre accepts in states it cares about, or a succession in which the family certifies a choice it did not make. None of those events appears in the record to the end of 2019, and the frame makes no prediction about their arrival. It asserts only that recovery, if the word is to mean more than a favourable election, will be legible in exactly those documents, a constitution amended, a ballot counted, a state unit authorised to lose an argument with Delhi and remain standing. Parties announce revivals in speeches. Architectures announce them in paperwork.

The pillar that follows this hub tests the frame episode by episode: the founding and the Moderate method on their own terms; Surat as the first architectural failure; Nagpur as the century's most consequential act of party design in India; Kothari's System in its author's full argument; 1969 read organisationally rather than theatrically; 1978 as a founding disguised as a split; 1998 as the moment succession became constitution; the dynastic tension examined without the consolations of either camp; and the post-2014 decline measured against the long arc that predicts its shape. If the frame survives those tests, it earns the claim with which this article began. The Indian National Congress, across 1885 to 2020, is four organisationally distinct phases sharing one name, and its history is the history of what each architecture could do, and of the moment each discovered what it could not.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: When was the Indian National Congress founded?

The Indian National Congress was founded in December 1885. Its first session opened on 28 December 1885 in the hall of the Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College at Bombay and sat until 31 December, with 72 delegates attending and Womesh Chunder Bonnerjee, a Calcutta barrister, as its first president. Allan Octavian Hume, a retired member of the Indian Civil Service, had convened the gathering after years of correspondence with Indian public men across the provinces, and he served as general secretary of the organisation through its founding period. The session passed nine resolutions, on subjects including the expansion of the legislative councils and the holding of the civil service examination in India as well as England, which set the petitioning programme of the founding-Moderate phase.

### Q: Who founded the INC?

The INC was founded by Allan Octavian Hume together with the Indian leaders of the Moderate generation. Hume, a retired civil servant who had served as secretary to the Government of India, supplied the all-India network, the correspondence and the convening initiative, and acted as general secretary. The Indian founders included Womesh Chunder Bonnerjee, who presided over the first session; Dadabhai Naoroji, the scholar of the drain theory; Pherozeshah Mehta and Dinshaw Edulji Wacha of Bombay; and associates of the provincial associations, the Indian Association of Calcutta, the Poona Sarvajanik Sabha and the Madras Mahajan Sabha among them. Whether Hume acted as an official safety valve, as Lala Lajpat Rai charged in 1916, or as a lightning conductor for Indian purposes, as the party's historians reply, remains disputed and is attributed in this article to its holders on both sides.

### Q: How did the INC operate organisationally after 1885?

After 1885 the INC operated as an annual deliberative conference rather than a standing organisation. It met for a few days each December in a rotating city, heard a presidential address, debated resolutions in a subjects committee and passed them to be forwarded as memorials to the government and to Parliament. Delegates were returned by public meetings and local political associations; the composition was overwhelmingly professional, lawyers, journalists, teachers and merchants, and the costs fell on wealthy patrons. Between sessions the organisation subsisted as correspondence among its leaders, sustained after 1889 by a British Committee at London and its journal *India*. There was no mass membership roll, no dues-paying base, no provincial executive and no machinery for enforcing a decision on a reluctant member.

### Q: How has the Congress organisation changed over time?

The Congress organisation has changed by replacement rather than by evolution, in four distinct architectures. The founding-Moderate body of 1885 to 1907 was an elite annual conference conducting petition-politics, without mass membership or a standing executive. The Gandhi-mass organisation built by the Nagpur constitution of 1920 added open four-anna membership, linguistic provincial committees and a Working Committee, and ran nationwide campaigns. The Nehru-Indira organisation of 1947 to 1991 governed as the Congress System, absorbing factions inside a dominant party whose state bosses held real autonomy. The dynastic-organisational phase from 1978, consolidated after 1998, replaced factional bargaining with arbitration by a leadership family, nominated state units and coalition dependence. The name is continuous throughout. The membership principle, the decision locus and the succession mechanism are not.

### Q: Why did the Congress decline after 2014?

The Congress declined after 2014, on the four-phase reading defended in this article, because the dynastic-organisational architecture had dismantled the mechanism that once converted internal discontent into continued membership. Factional bargaining had been replaced by arbitration from the leadership household; state organisations had thinned into nomination and campaign service; and dissatisfied leaders had learned to exit rather than wait, taking provincial machines with them, as the departures that built the Trinamool Congress, the Nationalist Congress Party and the YSR Congress had already shown. The electoral expression was 44 Lok Sabha seats in 2014 and 52 in 2019. Other explanations, pitched at leadership failure or a rival's appeal, are attributed in the article to their holders; the structural reading locates the cause in the architecture inherited by 2014, not in the year itself.

### Q: What is the dynastic-organisational phase of the Congress?

The dynastic-organisational phase is the fourth architecture of the Congress, in which the leader's family functions as the organisation's anchor and final arbiter. It has two sub-periods: from the founding of the Congress (I) in January 1978 to 1991, under Indira Gandhi and then Rajiv Gandhi; and from Sonia Gandhi's assumption of the presidency in March 1998 onward. Its marks are succession settled within the family by committee resolution rather than organisational ballot, state presidents and chief ministers appointed and removed from the centre, the lapse of contested internal elections, and dependence on coalition arithmetic for government. The phase differs from the Congress System it succeeded in that allegiance to the anchor, not bargaining among factions, is what holds the party together.

### Q: How is the post-2014 Congress different from the Nehruvian Congress?

The post-2014 Congress differs from the Nehruvian Congress on every dimension of its architecture. The Nehruvian party was a party of consensus whose internal factions reproduced the society's conflicts inside the organisation, whose provincial bosses selected candidates and governed their states with substantial autonomy, whose presidents were chosen by canvassed consensus in 1964 and by a contested parliamentary ballot in 1966, and which won three successive general elections with more than 360 seats. The post-2014 party settles succession inside one family, appoints its state leadership from the centre, has no functioning internal arena in which a losing faction can wait for the next round, and won 44 seats in 2014 and 52 in 2019. The older party processed opposition. The later party petitions its own leadership.

### Q: What distinguishes one Congress phase from another organisationally?

Five dimensions distinguish the phases. Membership architecture asks who may belong and on what terms, a delegate conference, an open four-anna roll, a factionally managed mass roll or a leader's following. Decision-making locus asks where binding choices are taken, the annual session, the Working Committee, a balance of centre and provincial bosses, or a leadership household. Leadership-succession mechanism asks how one leader replaces another. Ideological-positioning role asks what work doctrine performs, public argument, campaign programme, internal distribution or inherited identity. Electoral-organisational integration asks how the campaign machine connects to the membership machine. A boundary is drawn where several dimensions change together, as they did in 1920, after 1969 and in 1978 and 1998.

### Q: What was the Congress Working Committee and when was it created?

The Congress Working Committee was created by the Nagpur constitution of December 1920 as the organisation's standing executive. It consisted of fifteen members, including the president and the general secretaries, and it was charged with carrying out the Congress programme between sessions, directing the provincial committees and conducting campaigns. Before 1920 the Congress had possessed no executive at all between its annual sessions, which is why the committee's creation marks the birth of the mass-phase architecture. The Working Committee survived every later phase and became, in turn, Gandhi's instrument, the Congress System's cabinet of faction managers, and, in the dynastic phase, the body that ratifies the leadership's decisions and records successions arranged elsewhere.

### Q: What is the All India Congress Committee?

The All India Congress Committee, universally shortened to AICC, is the Congress's central deliberative body, standing between the annual plenary session and the Working Committee. The Nagpur constitution of 1920 fixed its strength at 350 members, returned by the provincial Congress committees. The AICC meets more frequently than the plenary, debates and adopts the party's programme resolutions, elects the Working Committee and, in the phases when internal elections functioned, constituted the electoral college from which the party's direction could be challenged. Sessions such as Bangalore in July 1969, where Indira Gandhi circulated her economic note against the Syndicate's preferences, show the AICC operating as the arena in which factional strength was counted before it was tested elsewhere.

### Q: What is the difference between nominal continuity and organisational continuity in the Congress?

Nominal continuity is the persistence of the name, the legal registration, the symbols and the self-description of an organisation. Organisational continuity is the persistence of its architecture: its membership principle, its decision locus, its succession mechanism, its use of ideology and its electoral machinery. The Congress possesses the first kind uninterruptedly from 1885, and the Election Commission's rulings of 1969 and 1978, awarding the name to the faction holding the parliamentary majority, show that the name is a legal and symbolic asset rather than a structure. The four-phase reading denies the second kind of continuity: the architecture was rebuilt in 1920, hollowed after 1971, refounded in 1978 and re-anchored in 1998. A continuous name over discontinuous architectures is the article's central distinction.

### Q: Why did J. B. Kripalani resign as Congress President in 1947?

J. B. Kripalani resigned the Congress presidency in November 1947 over the constitutional position of the organisation in the new republic. Elected at the Meerut session of November 1946, Kripalani held that the Congress, as the body that had won independence, should guide, supervise and where necessary check the government of India. Jawaharlal Nehru and Vallabhbhai Patel held that ministers were responsible to the legislature and could not take direction from a party secretariat. Kripalani found himself neither consulted on major decisions nor able to convene the organisation's authority behind his view, and resigned within months of independence. His defeat fixed Phase Three's settlement: the government would lead and the organisation would serve, a settlement Nehru sealed by holding the presidency himself from 1951 to 1954.

### Q: What role did provincial Congress bosses play in the Congress System?

Provincial bosses were the load-bearing structure of the Congress System. Leaders such as K. Kamaraj in Madras, B. C. Roy in West Bengal, Partap Singh Kairon in Punjab, S. K. Patil in Bombay, Atulya Ghosh in the Bengal organisation and C. B. Gupta in the United Provinces commanded state machines built on district networks, caste and factional alliances and government patronage. They delivered their states in general elections, controlled the selection of candidates in practice, and supplied the party with its governing personnel. In exchange the centre left them substantially autonomous in state affairs. Their existence made absorption federal: a faction defeated in Delhi could be compensated in a province. When later phases reduced state leaders to appointees removable at the centre's pleasure, that compensation market closed, and with it the System's resilience.

### Q: What happened when the Congress formed provincial governments in 1937?

In the provincial elections of early 1937, held under the Government of India Act of 1935, the Congress won majorities across much of British India and, after receiving assurances that governors would not use their discretionary powers to override ministerial advice, accepted office in July 1937 in six provinces, rising to eight with the North-West Frontier Province and Assam. The ministries, supervised by a central Parliamentary Board in which Patel, Azad and Rajendra Prasad were dominant, gave the movement its first experience of administration: tenancy and civil-liberties legislation, and the discipline problems of governing. They resigned in October and November 1939 when the Viceroy declared war on India's behalf without consulting them. Organisationally, 1937 is the rehearsal for 1947, the moment the parliamentary wing began its ascent over the movement's committees.

### Q: How many Lok Sabha seats did the Congress win in 2014 and 2019?

The Congress won 44 Lok Sabha seats in the general election of 2014, on 19.3 percent of the national vote, its lowest tally and lowest vote share since independence, and it fell short of the one-tenth of the House, 55 seats, required for formal recognition of its parliamentary leader as Leader of the Opposition. In the general election of 2019 it won 52 seats, a marginal recovery that left it again below the recognition threshold and again without a majority in any large state assembly contingent of its own. For scale, the party had won 414 seats in 1984, 353 in 1980, 352 in 1971 and 364 in 1952. The figures are those recorded in the Election Commission's statistical reports for each election.

### Q: What is the Congress high command?

The high command is the informal name for the Congress's central leadership authority, the party president together with the small circle through which the president's decisions reach the state units. The term acquired its settled meaning in the dynastic-organisational phase, where the Working Committee and the AICC ratify rather than originate decisions, and where chief ministers, state Congress presidents and candidates for legislatures are settled by the centre, in practice by the leadership household and its secretariat. The expression is used by the party's own members, in petition and in complaint, to describe an authority that is real but not located in any single clause of the party constitution. Its existence is the everyday evidence for the article's fourth dimension: the decision locus of the later phases.

### Q: How were Congress presidents selected between 1947 and 1969?

Between 1947 and 1969 Congress presidents were formally elected by the delegates of the annual plenary session, and the elections were sometimes genuinely contested. At Jaipur in 1948 Pattabhi Sitaramayya defeated Purushottam Das Tandon; at Nasik in 1950 Tandon defeated J. B. Kripalani in a ballot that registered the organisation's conservative strength against Nehru's preference. After Nehru held the office himself from 1951 to 1954, selection settled into managed consensus: U. N. Dhebar, Indira Gandhi in 1959, N. Sanjiva Reddy and K. Kamaraj from 1964 were chosen without a contest, the name settled beforehand by the leadership and the chief ministers. The mechanism nonetheless remained a mechanism, with a college of electors and the possibility of a count, which is what distinguishes the phase from the successions arranged after 1978.

### Q: What was the Congress Seva Dal?

The Congress Seva Dal was the party's uniformed volunteer corps, founded by N. S. Hardikar and adopted at the Kakinada session of 1923 as the Hindustani Seva Dal. It drilled volunteers, men and women, in discipline, physical training and social service, supplied the marshals and organisers for Congress sessions and campaigns, and embodied the Gandhi-mass phase's belief that a political organisation should form the conduct of its members and not merely enrol them. Volunteers of the Dal carried the movement's presence into districts where committees were thin, and its camps trained a generation of provincial workers. The corps survives within the party as the Congress Seva Dal, a frontal organisation, its reduced place in the party's life itself an index of the distance between the mass phase's service ethic and later architectures.

### Q: What did the 1972 and 1973 AICC organisational reviews document?

The internal organisational reviews of 1972 and 1973, preserved among the AICC Papers at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library, document the Congress System's absorption mechanism decaying from inside during the very years of the party's greatest electoral strength. The functionaries' reports record membership registers inflated by bloc enrolments made to capture committee positions, bogus members who could not be traced to any address, district and town committees that existed in the returns but transacted no business between elections, and state units reporting organisational health in the language the centre wished to hear. The reviews matter because they are the organisation's own testimony, filed before the Emergency and four decades before the post-2014 collapse, that the fragility later phases exhibited was present, observed and recorded at the height of the System's successor regime.

### Q: Why did the Congress remain an umbrella party rather than a cadre party?

The Congress remained an umbrella party because its founding tasks rewarded breadth over discipline. A movement seeking to represent a whole nation against an empire needed to contain landlords and tenants, capitalists and socialists, conservatives and radicals, inside one tent, and after 1947 a governing party in a diverse federation needed the same breadth to assemble majorities in every province. Cadre parties, built on ideological examination, dues discipline and expulsion, purchase coherence at the price of reach; the Congress, as Sarkar's account of the movement and Kothari's account of the System both show, repeatedly chose reach. The choice had a deferred cost. An umbrella has no test of belonging to enforce when the weather turns, and no cadre formed by conviction to hold the structure when patronage fails, which is the organisational root of the fragility the final phase inherited.
