## The Verdict First: Zandonai Restores the Italian Operatic Line

Riccardo Zandonai's *Giulietta e Romeo*, first performed at the Teatro Costanzi in Rome on 14 February 1922, is the last major opera in which the Veronese tragedy returns home. The claim needs its terms fixed before it can be defended. The story of the two lovers was Italian before it was English: Luigi da Porto gave the lovers their names, their city, and their tomb in a novella printed in 1530, and Matteo Bandello retold the tale a generation later in the version that travelled north. Shakespeare received the story through Arthur Brooke's English poem and rebuilt it so completely that the rebuilt version swallowed its sources in the world's memory. Operas followed the same fork. Some settings descend from the play. Others descend from the novelle line that produced the tale before the play existed. Zandonai's opera belongs to the second descent, and its librettist, Arturo Rossato, built the text from the novelle of da Porto and Bandello rather than from the English drama. That single fact of descent explains the opera's shape, its cast list, its ending, and its strange position in the repertoire: a famous story in an unfamiliar form, sung in the idiom of Italian verismo at the moment that idiom was ageing.

![Gilda dalla Rizza and Miguel Fleta, the first Giulietta and Romeo of Zandonai's opera at the Teatro Costanzi - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-78.webp)

The verdict argued through this article is that *Giulietta e Romeo* is a minor opera standing in a major line, and that the line, not the ranking, is the reason the work matters. Judged bar by bar against the composer's own *Francesca da Rimini* of 1914, the later score is the less inspired of the two: its melody comes in shorter breaths, its love music leans on orchestral colour where the earlier opera sang. Judged as an act of restoration, the 1922 score has no rival in its century. Ninety-two years after Bellini's *I Capuleti e i Montecchi* and ninety-seven years after Nicola Vaccai's *Giulietta e Romeo*, an Italian composer of the first rank set the Veronese tragedy again from its Italian sources, with a tenor Romeo in place of the old trouser role, a promised husband who carries da Porto's own name for him, and an ending in which the heroine wakes, speaks, and dies without a dagger. The opera failed to hold the stage after the middle of the twentieth century. The failure was a failure of fashion and of casting economics, not of conception, and the pages that follow defend that distinction section by section.

Three tests govern the argument, and each is applied where the evidence falls. The first test is descent: which features of the libretto can be traced to the novelle and which, if any, require the play as a source. The second test is intention: the composer stated his aim in plain words, a return to melodramma and to popular opera of the kind he called more essentially Italian, and a work conceived as defiance must be measured against its declared purpose before it is measured against purposes it refused. The third test is survival: what the record shows about performance, recording, and revival, from sixty-seven productions in the first fifty years to a centenary staging in New York in 2022. A score can pass the first two tests and fail the third, and the honest verdict keeps the three results separate. *Giulietta e Romeo* passes the test of descent completely. It passes the test of intention on the evidence of its first audiences, who encored the very passage its first critics damned. It fails the test of survival for reasons the final sections itemise, and its one tenor aria, carried by three generations of singers on record, stands as the exception that proves how completely the rest of the work depended on the stage.

## A Composer Groomed for a Succession: Zandonai Before Verona

Riccardo Zandonai was born on 28 May 1883 at Sacco, near Rovereto, and died at Pesaro on 5 June 1944. The geography of the life matters to the music. He was formed at the Liceo Rossini in Pesaro, the conservatory where Pietro Mascagni had directed and taught, and the formation shows in everything he wrote: a verismo schooling in the orchestra pit rather than the library, a belief that an opera lives or dies in the theatre, and a craftsman's command of orchestral effect learned from the generation of Mascagni and Puccini rather than from the symphonic tradition. He was twenty-two when Arrigo Boito brought him to the attention of Tito Ricordi, head of the publishing house that had issued Rossini, Bellini, Donizetti, Verdi, and Puccini. The encounter decided his career. Ricordi took the young composer up, set him to work on an adaptation of Dickens's *The Cricket on the Hearth*, and produced the result, *Il grillo del focolare*, in 1908. The opera achieved a modest success. Its successor achieved notoriety of a more useful kind.

*Conchita*, given in 1911, set Pierre Louÿs's scandalous novel *La femme et le pantin*, a subject Puccini had rejected as too depraved, and the setting carried its composer's name across Europe. The episode fixed the pattern of the career. Zandonai was being groomed, in the plain sense the record supports, as the successor in the Ricordi stable: the composer who would carry the firm's Italian line forward when Puccini's line closed. Tito Ricordi's investment was concrete as well as rhetorical. When the rights to Gabriele d'Annunzio's verse tragedy *Francesca da Rimini* came into negotiation, the price was heavy enough that the young composer agreed to reduce his own advance so the firm could meet the poet's terms. Mascagni, his old teacher, was bargaining with d'Annunzio in the same season for *Parisina*, another tragedy of forbidden love in a medieval Italian court. The pupil reached the stage second and won the larger prize. What that prize was, and what it cost the rest of his work, is the subject of the next section, because no account of the Veronese opera can proceed without the standard against which it has always been judged.

The grooming also explains the confidence of 1922. A composer who has been told for a decade that a national tradition waits on his desk does not approach the most famous Italian love story in the world as a tourist. He approaches it as an heir. The novelle of da Porto and Bandello were, in the strictest sense, family property: Italian prose classics that the Ricordi firm existed to keep in circulation. When Zandonai turned to Verona after the war, the turn had the character of a reclamation, and the quarrel that greeted the premiere, examined in its own section below, was a quarrel about whether the reclamation was legitimate art or commercial nostalgia. The composer knew the quarrel was coming. The score was written, by his own later account, almost as an act of defiance, against younger colleagues who affected not to write for the public. Defiance is a dangerous compositional brief. It produces plain speaking where the age wants sophistication, and it dates quickly when the age wins. Both consequences are audible in *Giulietta e Romeo*, and both must be weighed rather than merely deplored.

## Francesca da Rimini: The Calling Card of 1914

*Francesca da Rimini* was first performed at the Teatro Regio in Turin on 19 February 1914. Tito Ricordi himself drew the libretto from d'Annunzio's tragedy of 1901, which drew in turn on the fifth canto of Dante's *Inferno*, where Francesca tells her story to the pilgrim in a handful of lines that Italian schoolrooms have memorised for centuries. The opera runs in four acts, and its premiere was regarded as a masterpiece. The succession that Ricordi had planned seemed, in that season, secure: the pupil was thirty, his score fused Italian melody with an orchestral palette learned from Debussy and Richard Strauss, and the work travelled at a speed few Italian operas of the decade matched. Covent Garden took it within the year. Milan, New York, and Chicago followed within three years. The Metropolitan Opera gave the American premiere in 1916 with Frances Alda in the title role, lost the opera for almost seven decades, and restored it in 1984 in a production built for Renata Scotto and Plácido Domingo that forced a reconsideration of the whole score. The house returned to it again in 2013. A work with that performance pedigree is not a period piece. It is the standing evidence of what its composer could do.

The *New Grove Dictionary of Opera* gives the considered verdict in the words of Renato Chiesa, who calls the score one of the most original and polished Italian melodramas of the twentieth century, combining a powerful gift for Italian melody with an exceptional command of orchestration. The pairing in that sentence is the whole Zandonai question in miniature. Melody and orchestration are the two currencies of Italian opera, and posterity has usually paid him in the second while discounting the first. Singers remember Francesca herself: Gilda dalla Rizza made the role a signature, Magda Olivero recorded excerpts in 1969, and the part has drawn dramatic sopranos of the first rank in every generation since. Orchestras remember the atmosphere, the archaic colours with which the score ages its medieval world, the sensual refinement that separates its sound from the rougher verismo of Mascagni. Both memories are accurate, and the tension between them reappears, sharpened, in the Veronese opera, where the orchestral gift is intact and the melodic gift is asked to carry a story whose most famous scenes the libretto deliberately withholds.

One further fact about *Francesca* belongs in the record before Verona enters it. When Puccini understood that death would leave *Turandot* unfinished, the composer he favoured to complete the score from the sketches was Zandonai. The preference was reported and is part of the documented reputation: the older master trusted the younger man's hand in his own workshop. Nothing came of the preference, and the completion went elsewhere, but the episode fixes the altitude at which Zandonai stood in the early nineteen twenties. He was not a provincial epigone hoping for a hearing. He was the designated continuator of the Italian line, and *Giulietta e Romeo*, premiered two years before Puccini's death, was the work in which the continuator took possession of the line's most famous subject. The altitude also fixes the scale of the later fall. A minor composer whose opera fades has met the common fate. A designated successor whose Veronese opera fades has lost an inheritance, and the loss demands an explanation more precise than neglect.

## Rome, 14 February 1922: The Costanzi Premiere and Its Stars

The premiere of *Giulietta e Romeo* took place at the Teatro Costanzi in Rome on 14 February 1922, with the composer conducting. The date carried its own publicity: a tragedy of young love unveiled on the feast of the saint of lovers, in the capital, by the most discussed Italian opera composer of the postwar seasons. A fashionable crowd thronged the house. The reaction was far from unanimous, and the record preserves a detail worth more than a column of reviews: there was fighting in the gallery. Rome's opera public divided physically over the score, partisans and detractors coming to blows in the cheap seats while the evening ended, on balance, in the success the composer had hoped for. A premiere that produces a fight has at least produced conviction. The gallery had heard something worth defending with fists, and the sections below argue that the thing defended was exactly the thing the professional critics attacked: an unembarrassed popular melodramma, built for the broad public the composer had named as his target.

The casting announced the same intention. The title roles went to veritable stars of the period, and both were singers of the verismo first rank. Gilda dalla Rizza, born in 1892, was Puccini's late-career muse and the creator of Magda in *La rondine*; she was also the Francesca of her generation, the soprano whose identification with the earlier opera made her the living link between the two scores. Miguel Fleta, the Spanish tenor born in 1897, brought a fame of a different temperature: a tempestuous artist whose impassioned singing divided opinion wherever he appeared, and who four years later would create the role of Calaf in the posthumous premiere of *Turandot*. A house that fields dalla Rizza and Fleta is not hedging. The premiere told Rome that the new opera was to be judged as a star vehicle in the grand Italian manner, and the judgement has followed the work ever since: the roles were written for voices of spinto weight, and every later failure to cast them at that weight has been charged against the score rather than against the casting.

### How did dalla Rizza and Fleta shape the opera's first reception?

The two stars fixed the opera's identity as a vehicle for dramatic voices before a note of it reached print, because dalla Rizza carried the authority of the composer's Francesca and Fleta carried the notoriety of the most discussed tenor instrument of the postwar seasons.

Critics heard the new score through those voices, as a continuation of the earlier triumph sung by its own heroine, and audiences responded to the double event of the singing and the subject. The shaping cut both ways. Singers of that rank made the premiere a success worth fighting over, and their example set a casting standard that ordinary houses struggled to meet, so the work's reputation for heaviness was born with its reputation for splendour.

The date also deserves a colder look. February 1922 stood at a hinge in Italian musical life. The war had broken the old international circuit; the avant-garde was consolidating its claim that popular opera was a exhausted form; Puccini had two years to live and one opera to finish. A successor who chose that moment to write a self-declared popular melodramma on the most familiar subject in Italian literature was making a wager about the future of the art form itself. The wager's terms were clear. If the broad public still wanted sung tragedy in the Italian manner, *Giulietta e Romeo* would hold the stage as *Francesca* had held it. If the public's loyalty had been a wartime survival rather than a living taste, the opera would become a monument to a closed era. The first decade answered in the composer's favour, as the production count examined below demonstrates. The second decade reversed the answer. What no season reversed was the premiere's central transaction: a famous story, restored to its Italian sources, sung by the two most emblematic Italianate voices of the day, in a house that fought over the result. Premieres are usually weather. This one was a plebiscite.

## Rossato's Libretto: A Tragedy in Three Acts From the Novelle

The printed libretto names the work a tragedy in three acts, and the compression begins on the title page. Arturo Rossato, born in 1882 and dead in 1942, was a man of the theatre who understood what a three-act verismo structure demands: few principals, no subplot that cannot be sung, and a catastrophe prepared from the first scene. His dramatis personae strip the story to its skeleton. Giulietta and Romeo stand at the centre. Tebaldo, the Capulet kinsman, carries the feud almost alone, sung by a baritone. Isabella, the heroine's friend and handmaiden, replaces the Nurse as confidante and accomplice. Around them stand functional figures rather than characters: Gregorio and Sansone, fighting men of the two houses; Bernabò; a Montecchio partisan; the street singer whose ballad detonates the third act; the herald whose proclamation pronounces the banishment; masked revellers; servants. The older generation does not exist. There are no Capulet parents to rage or plead, no Montague father, no Prince to open and close the civic frame, no Mercutio to steal the middle act, and no Friar Laurence to engineer the plot. The promised husband, the Count Lodrone, never appears at all: he exists as a name in other mouths, the marriage Giulietta refuses and flees.

The stripping is the libretto's boldest decision and its most Italian one. The novelle line had always kept the cast leaner than the play. Da Porto's tale moves on a handful of figures, and the operas that descend from the novelle, Vaccai's and Bellini's among them, inherit the leanness. Rossato pushed the inheritance further than any predecessor. By removing the parents, he removed the tragedy's social machinery and left its emotional machinery exposed: two households survive only as armed retinues and a single articulate enemy. By removing the friar, he removed the plot's engineer and made the catastrophe a product of messages, songs, and weather rather than of a plan. By keeping Lodrone offstage, he turned the arranged marriage into pure pressure, a fate announced by herald and kinsman rather than a rival embodied in a tenor or baritone of his own. Each removal has a cost, and the article counts the costs where they fall due. The aggregate gain is speed. The score's music runs, in performance, to under two hours, a span nearer to *Cavalleria rusticana* doubled than to the five-act sprawl of the French setting, and the speed is purchased exactly where Rossato spent his cuts.

The lovers, most strikingly, do not meet. The opera opens after the secret marriage: the two are husband and wife before the curtain rises, their attachment an established fact of the Verona night rather than an event the audience witnesses. Reviews of the modern revival state the point with some surprise, since the story's most famous scene is a first meeting, but the libretto's logic is sound on its own terms. A three-act tragedy that begins with a wedding already accomplished can spend its first act on the marriage's exposure rather than its formation, and the exposure is where Rossato locates the drama. Giulietta calls Romeo her husband. The fiction of the libretto treats the bond as sacrament and fact, which raises the stakes of every later scene: Tebaldo's rage is not against a flirtation but against a marriage, the banishment separates spouses rather than sweethearts, and the tomb scene reunites a wife with a husband rather than a girl with a memory. The play's lovers marry in the middle of their story and die within days of the ceremony. Rossato's lovers carry their marriage into the story from before its start, and the opera becomes, in consequence, a tragedy of a marriage destroyed rather than a romance prevented. That reframing, more than any single aria, is the libretto's claim on a reader's attention.

## The Source Question: Da Porto and Bandello Against the Play

Every programme note for the opera answers the source question in one sentence, and the sentence is consistent across the record: the libretto was inspired not by the play but by Italian sources, the novelle of Luigi da Porto and Matteo Bandello. Opera Scotland's note on the composer's work states the descent in its cleanest form: the Veronese opera, like Bellini's before it, was not derived from the dramatist but used the early Italian sources that had also inspired the English playwright. The claim survives inspection. The Count Lodrone is the fingerprint. In da Porto's novella the heroine is promised to a count of Lodrone who is never given a personal name; Bandello, retelling the tale, christened the man Paris. A libretto descended from the play would inherit the name Paris, as the French libretto does. Rossato's text keeps the count of Lodrone, unnamed and unseen, which is da Porto's furniture carried across four centuries without an English intermediary. The waking heroine tells the same story. Da Porto and Bandello both grant the lovers a final meeting in the tomb: the heroine wakes while her husband still lives, they speak, and she dies of grief at his side. The play abolished the meeting, waking its heroine to a corpse and a dagger. Rossato restores the meeting and abolishes the dagger. Two fingerprints of that clarity settle the question of descent for any scene they touch.

Honesty requires the complication to be stated in its full strength, because the brief for this article, like many summaries, describes the libretto as drawing on the Italian tradition and on the play together, and the documented record does not support the second half of that formula as a claim about texts. No scene in Rossato's libretto has been shown to require the play as its source. The confrontations that feel most dramatic in a modern sense, the masked entry into the enemy's feast, the dawn parting after a night together, the death carried by a false report, all stand in the novelle or follow from the novelle by operatic compression. What the play contributed was not text but climate. By 1922 the English tragedy had been famous in Italy for a century, in translation, on the stage, and in the paintings of Francesco Hayez and his successors; no Italian librettist could approach Verona innocently, and Rossato wrote for a public whose imagination of the lovers was coloured by the play's European fame even where his page followed da Porto. The accurate statement, and the one this article adopts, distinguishes the two kinds of debt. The libretto's documented descent runs through the novelle and through the Italian operatic line that the novelle fed. The play's influence runs through the audience, the marketing, and the very decision that the subject was worth a major opera in 1922. Descent and climate are different forces, and confusing them has muddied the opera's reputation for a century, sending listeners to judge a novelle tragedy by a play it never set.

The distinction also corrects the cliché named in the series thesis. The lazy genealogy descends all versions from the English text, as if the story were a play that other arts borrow. The Italian operatic record refutes the genealogy by existing. Zingarelli set the tale at La Scala in 1796, Vaccai in 1825, Bellini in 1830, and each of those scores stands closer to da Porto than to the play in its decisive features. Zandonai's opera closes that line as its last major entry, and the closing was conscious: a composer groomed by the firm that published Bellini cannot have chosen the novelle by accident. The choice was the thesis of the work before a note was written. An Italian house composer, an Italian librettist, an Italian prose source, an Italian stage: the opera argues, by its mere existence, that the story had a native life independent of its English transformation, and that the native life could still fill the Costanzi in 1922. Whether the music sustains the argument at the highest level is a separate question, and it is the question the verdict sections keep separate. The argument itself was made, and made successfully enough to run for three decades in the Italian repertory.

## The InsightCrunch Zandonai Note: The Opera Against the Novelle and the Play

The comparison that settles the descent question deserves a permanent reference form, and the table below supplies it. The InsightCrunch Zandonai note sets the opera's decisive features against the two traditions that claim them: the Italian novelle line of da Porto and Bandello, and the English play. Each row isolates one element, states the form the element takes in each tradition, and closes with the form Rossato and Zandonai chose. Read down the final column and the opera's genealogy stands bare: on the promised husband's identity, on the heroine's death, on the tomb meeting, and on the cast's leanness, the 1922 score follows the novelle at every point where the two traditions divide. The table also marks the one element where the opera follows neither source and invents for itself: the street singer, a figure with no counterpart in novella or play, invented because a libretto without a friar still needs a mechanism to carry false news, and Rossato chose to carry it in a ballad. Reference tables of this kind earn their keep in argument, and the essay guidance in the questions at the end of this article shows the note at work.

The rows reward a second reading for what they reveal about operatic inheritance in general. A librettist adapting a famous story does not choose sources in the abstract. Rossato chose the version of the tale that Italian opera had already naturalised, the version whose tomb scene Vaccai had written so memorably that theatres substituted it into Bellini's opera for sixty-five years, the version whose heroine wakes. The waking heroine is the Italian line's signature, its counter-image to the dagger, and every Italian setting of consequence carries it. Zandonai's fidelity to the signature was therefore not antiquarianism. It was brand loyalty to a national tradition of the story, asserted in a Roman theatre against the international fame of the English version. The table's final row records the counterweight: the voice. Where the Italian line's earlier Romeos were castrati and then women in trousers, Rossato and Zandonai wrote a tenor of verismo weight, a husband rather than a youth, and in that single casting decision the opera declares its century as plainly as its sources declare its descent.

## Act One: A Masked Stranger in the Capuleti Square

The first act sets its scene in a small square in Verona at night, outside the palace of the Capuleti, and opens with motion rather than exposition. The Capuleti, led by Tebaldo, crash a festivity thrown by their enemies the Montecchi. A heated argument flares and is interrupted by a masked stranger whose arrival arrests the quarrel. Guards are heard approaching; the crowd scatters; the square darkens. The masked man remains, hidden while the town crier passes, and emerges when the danger clears. The mask comes off in effect if not in gesture: the stranger is Romeo, and Giulietta comes to greet her husband. The lovers steal their scene, profess an attachment that the libretto treats as established and consecrated, and part with a final kiss as dawn approaches. Rossato's dramaturgy in this act repays attention because it inverts the famous original. The play's first act builds toward a meeting between strangers. The opera's first act builds toward a reunion between spouses, conducted in hiding, under threat of discovery, in a public square patrolled by guards. The suspense is not whether the two will find each other. The suspense is whether the city will find them.

### Why does the opera begin after the secret marriage instead of before it?

The libretto begins after the secret marriage because a three-act verismo tragedy has no room for a courtship: Rossato needs the bond established so that exposure, rather than attraction, can drive the action from the first scene onward. The marriage is the premise of the action, never its prize.

A married heroine defying a kinsman risks more than a girl meeting a stranger, since her refusal of the Count Lodrone reads as fidelity to a sacrament instead of caprice, and a banished husband leaves a wife behind rather than a sweetheart. The compression also matches the novelle line's impatience with preliminaries, where the attachment forms early and the tale spends its length on consequences.

### What does the masked stranger in the first scene conceal?

The masked stranger conceals a husband inside an enemy's festival: Romeo has entered the Montecchi celebration and the Capuleti ambush that follows it under a mask, hiding not a stranger's curiosity but a spouse's presence in the one quarter of Verona where his name alone would start a killing.

The concealment fails in stages across the act, since Tebaldo's suspicions are raised by the intrusion and the feud's machinery begins to close on the marriage before the lovers have finished their parting. Rossato makes the mask do double work, as disguise in the plot and as emblem of a marriage that can exist in Verona only while it stays hidden.

The scoring of the act follows the dramaturgy. Street confrontation in this libretto has an earthy, *Cavalleria rusticana* colour, choruses and fighting men handled in the verismo manner of crowds observed rather than decorated, while the love scene that caps the act opens into the florid poetic manner reserved for the spouses. The contrast is the opera in miniature: public Verona written in prose rhythms and massed voices, private Verona written in long-breathed orchestral song. A director's problems begin here as well. The act must establish, without a word of exposition, that the central pair are married, that the city is armed, and that one Capulet of rank suspects the truth. Rossato trusts the staging to carry the first and third of those facts and the music to carry the second. When the act succeeds, the audience leaves it knowing everything the catastrophe requires. When it fails, the later acts look arbitrary, and the opera's reputation for thin dramaturgy is usually traceable to a first act that played the square scene as decoration. The libretto's economy is a wager on production, and the wager is the first thing a revival must win.

## Act Two: The Torch Dance, the Confrontation, and the Duel

The second act moves to a courtyard of the Capuleti palace in late afternoon, and opens with the Danza del torchio, the Torch Dance, whose music the composer later salvaged for the concert hall in the arrangement examined in its own section below. The dance establishes festivity under guard: the Capuleti are preparing the marriage that will dispose of Giulietta, and the torches light a celebration she experiences as a sentence. Tebaldo enters the act with knowledge. He has learned of the attachment between his kinswoman and Romeo, and he confronts her in a scene that modern reviewers have called hair-raising, the baritone's accusation against the soprano's defiance. Giulietta has been promised to the Count Lodrone. She refuses the match and swears herself to Romeo. The confrontation is the libretto's substitute for the play's great domestic scenes: where the dramatist gives the heroine a father whose rage fills a chamber, Rossato gives her a kinsman whose knowledge fills the same space with a colder threat, the threat of exposure to the house and the city.

Fighting erupts in the street beyond the courtyard, the feud's daily weather, and Romeo is certain to be in the thick of it. Tebaldo leaves to seek his revenge. What follows is the act's hinge. With Isabella's help, Romeo makes his way to Giulietta inside the palace, and the lovers' stolen meeting is discovered by Tebaldo returned. The challenge is immediate. Romeo attempts to reason with his wife's kinsman, a husband pleading with family in the play's own spirit, since the dramatist's hero also refuses the duel on the ground of a new and secret kinship: the plea fails here as there. Forced to defend himself, Romeo kills Tebaldo. The women's screams raise the palace. In the confusion the two accomplices drag Romeo down a secret passage, and the act closes on the fact of exile: flight from Verona is the only course left, and the farewell between the spouses is sung under the pressure of pursuit. Giulietta is left sobbing as the act falls. The dramaturgy deserves a clear verdict. Rossato compresses the play's third act, its duel, banishment, and forced marriage, into a single span, and the compression holds because each element causes the next: knowledge forces confrontation, confrontation forces the duel, the duel forces the flight. Nothing in the act is decoration except the dance, and the dance earns its place by lighting the trap.

The banishment itself is pronounced by proclamation, the herald's voice carrying the sentence of the city. The device is worth a moment's attention because it replaces the play's Prince, the civic authority who opens and closes the English action with judgements. Rossato's Verona has no face of authority, only a voice crying a sentence in the street. The choice fits the libretto's politics, in which institutions exist as sounds and armed men rather than as persons, and it fits the opera's scale, since a Prince with a singing part would require a presence the three-act frame cannot nourish. It also sharpens the tragedy's bleakness. In the play, authority at least attempts judgement and fails by lateness. In the opera, authority is a proclamation nailed to the air, and no appeal against it is imaginable. The lovers are not failed by a person who might have acted otherwise. They are processed by a city that acts through heralds, singers, and messengers, a machinery of voices carrying words that kill. Rossato, perhaps without intending the pattern, built a tragedy whose agents are messages: a kinsman's knowledge, a herald's sentence, a singer's ballad, a messenger's confirmation. The pattern completes itself in Mantua.

## Act Three in Mantua: The Singer, the Storm, and the Cavalcata

The third act divides into two pictures. The first is a square in Mantua in the afternoon, with a storm gathering. Romeo paces the square in exile, waiting for word from Verona, a husband suspended between a marriage he cannot avow and a city he cannot enter. The word arrives in the opera's most original invention. A street singer, il Cantatore, performs a ballad in the square, and the ballad tells of the death of Giulietta, the most beautiful flower of Verona, dead on the threshold of her marriage to the Count Lodrone. Romeo seizes on the singer and violently demands the whole story. The report is false in fact and true in appearance: the heroine lies in a deathlike sleep contrived to escape the wedding, and the city has sung her funeral in good faith. A messenger then appears and confirms the singer's account, the machinery of voices completing its work, and Romeo, with nothing left to hold him in Mantua, rides out into the storm for Verona.

### How does a street song do the work of the play's failed letter?

A street song does the work of the failed letter by turning private mischance into public fact: the play kills its lovers through a message that never arrives, while the opera kills its hero through a message that arrives too well, sung in a public square where a ballad about a famous death is entertainment for strangers.

The inversion is Rossato's sharpest stroke. Shakespeare's mechanism depends on silence, a letter stopped by quarantine in the same scene group where the friar learns the failure at 5.2, while the opera's mechanism depends on noise, a city so eager to narrate the tragedy that the hero hears his wife's funeral performed as street music before any private word can reach him.

### What separates the Cavalcata from the aria that follows it in Act 3?

The Cavalcata is separated from the aria that follows it by the difference between motion and arrival: the intermezzo paints the ride itself, a horseman crossing storm and darkness in orchestral sound, while the aria that follows stops the motion at the tomb gate and turns the same energy inward, into a husband's address to a wife he believes dead.

The ride is the opera's most famous orchestral page and the aria its most famous vocal page, and the pairing is deliberate, since the orchestra spends its violence on the journey so that the voice can enter the chapel with nothing left to express except recognition and grief.

The ride music, the Cavalcata, forms the act's intermezzo and the opera's most debated passage. It describes a nocturnal gallop from Mantua to Verona amid a thunderstorm, a single horseman rendered in an orchestral tumult that the Roman critic Alberto Gasco heard as the uproarious gallop of three thousand uhlans. The public heard something else. By the third performance the passage was being encored, and the encore became standard practice, a fact preserved in the scholarly record and weighed in the quarrel section below. Between the two hearings stands the whole argument about the opera. As scene painting, the Cavalcata is excessive by design: grief at that pitch does not ride quietly, and a composer aiming at popular melodramma writes the storm the audience feels rather than the storm a meteorologist would certify. As concert music, the passage proved detachable, which is why the composer could lift it into the *Episodio sinfonico* of 1927 and why it survived in the concert repertoire long after the opera itself thinned out. The second picture of the act, the chapel at dawn, is treated in the next section, where the ride ends and the opera's argument about the Italian ending is made in full.

## The Tomb at Dawn: Poison, Waking, and a Death Without a Dagger

The second picture of the third act sets the chapel of the Capuleti at dawn. Romeo arrives at a locked gate. Beyond it lies Giulietta in her deathlike sleep, visible to him, unreachable. He begs the still figure to wake and come to him, receives no answer, and concludes what the city has already concluded. From a vial he drinks poison greedily, and the poison works on him in view of the audience, contorted and failing, as the sky clears over the chapel. Then the opera performs its inheritance. Giulietta opens her eyes and stands. She finds her husband barely alive on the ground, opens the gate, and throws herself into his arms. The lovers have their minutes. Kisses are exchanged; Romeo confesses the poison; Giulietta explains the draught, the feigned death contrived to escape the Lodrone wedding, and the messenger she sent with the plan, the one true message in an opera of false ones, sent too late or lost on the road. Romeo dies in the reunion. Giulietta, who cannot and will not live without him, sinks down beside the body and dies entwined with it as the morning bells ring and sunlight greets the day.

Set the ending beside the play's and the descent argument completes itself. The dramatist wakes his heroine to a corpse and gives her the most famous suicide in English: the line "O happy dagger! This is thy sheath; there rust, and let me die" (5.3.169-170, Folger Shakespeare Library edition) closes a death performed alone, at speed, in terror of the watch arriving. Rossato's heroine wakes to a living husband, speaks with him, hears the truth and tells it, and dies without a weapon, of the refusal to continue. Da Porto's heroine dies in the same refusal, holding her breath against a world that has taken her husband; Bandello's dies of grief in the same posture. The Italian ending is not a softened ending. It is a different theory of the catastrophe: not the machinery of accident grinding two people separately, but a bond so complete that its severance is itself the cause of the second death. The opera's final image, the lovers entwined under morning bells, states the theory in a stage picture, and the picture is the libretto's answer to the dagger. A century of Italian settings had kept faith with that answer. Zandonai's fidelity to it, in a Rome still arguing about verismo, was the opera's thesis sung at full voice.

The dramaturgical cost must be admitted in the same breath. A heroine who takes a sleeping draught requires a supplier, a plan, and a confidant, and the libretto, having abolished the friar, leaves the machinery of the draught in shadow: the plan is the heroine's own, confided to a messenger, and the opera hurries past its logistics to reach its consequences. Reviewers have noted the gap, observing that an extended potion scene might have clarified the final act's foundations. The observation is just, and the defence is structural rather than evasive. Rossato's subject is not the plan but the city's narration of the plan's result: the ballad, the proclamation, the confirmation. An opera about messages cannot linger over the one message that matters without breaking its own pattern, since the sent word is defined by its absence from the stage. The defence does not wholly acquit the libretto. It identifies the gap as a chosen risk rather than an oversight, which is the most a fair hearing can grant, and the risk is priced into the verdict at the close of this article.

## Romeo's Page: "Giulietta! Son io!" and the Tenor Tradition

Romeo's address at the tomb, "Giulietta! Son io!", is the opera's most famous vocal page and one of the durable tenor excerpts of the Italian century. The words are a husband's self-announcement at a locked gate: Juliet, it is I. The aria stands in the third act at the moment of arrival, when the ride's orchestral violence has spent itself and the voice must carry recognition, hope, and error in a single span. Its survival is a matter of record rather than of taste. Fleta, the role's creator, left an impassioned recording of the piece, fixing its performance manner at the source: the ardent, declamatory attack of the verismo tenor, pitched between speech and song. Mario Del Monaco, the defining Italian dramatic tenor of the postwar decades, made the aria a signature recording, and through his disc it reached the international audience that the staged opera had stopped reaching. Jonas Kaufmann included the piece on the 2015 Decca album *The Age of Puccini*, conducted by Antonio Pappano, a placement that files the excerpt exactly where the tradition puts it: among the tenor pages of the Puccini succession, sung by the leading German tenor of his generation as a specimen of the Italian manner.

The excerpt's durability explains something about the opera's eclipse, and the explanation cuts against lazy criticism. A work whose single aria remains a staple of the verismo tenor repertory, in the words of the scholarly introduction to the Ricordi score materials, has not failed for want of melody in the absolute sense. It has failed as a whole while succeeding in a part, which is a different diagnosis with a different prescription. The aria survives because it is extractable: a self-contained span of address that needs no staging, no soprano, no chorus, only a tenor, an orchestra, and a gate imagined by the listener. The opera around it is not extractable. Its best effects are relational, the confrontation, the ballad overheard, the ride into the chapel dawn, and relational effects die when the work leaves the stage. Record companies therefore mined the aria and left the opera, and generations of listeners formed their judgement of Zandonai's Verona from four minutes of it. The judgement so formed is not false, but it is partial in the strict sense: it hears the husband's arrival and nothing of the wife, the city, or the storm that delivered him.

A word on the aria's construction serves the larger argument about the score. The page is built as arioso raised to the pitch of aria, declamation that flowers into line at the moments of direct address, which is the post-verismo manner at its most characteristic: the old closed forms dissolved, the voice riding an orchestral continuum, melody emerging where feeling concentrates rather than where the scheme requires a tune. Critics who call the score short of melodic inspiration are usually measuring it against the closed forms it deliberately abandoned, the world of the cavatina and cabaletta that Bellini's Verona still inhabited. The measurement is anachronistic. By 1922 the Italian aria had been dissolving for half a century, through late Verdi and the whole Puccini career, and Zandonai's tomb page represents the dissolution's Italian endpoint: a number that record catalogues list as an aria and that the score presents as the crest of a scene. Fleta's recording proves the crest could be detached and sold. The scene from which it crests remains the property of the stage, and the stage is where the opera must be judged.

## The Orchestra as Protagonist: Post-Verismo Colour and Archaic Instruments

Zandonai's orchestra is the most consistently praised element of the score across a century of divided opinion, and the praise follows a pattern. The 2022 New York reviewers, hearing the work staged for the first time in that city, separated the same elements the premiere's defenders had separated: a score short of melodic inspiration in the vocal line, in one account, but full of post-Wagnerian and post-Debussian atmosphere, with effective orchestral passages and string writing of particular strength. Another account of the same production found the vocal writing mostly declamatory, rising to extended arioso without quite reaching melodic heights, while allowing the composer his mastery of orchestral colour. The convergence matters more than either verdict alone. Listeners separated by a century and by sympathy keep returning the same split decision: the voice argues, the orchestra sings. In *Francesca* the split had been narrower, the soprano's line carrying a melodic gift that Grove's dictionary singles out. In the Veronese opera the balance tips toward the pit, and the tip explains both the opera's concert afterlife and its stage fragility, since a score whose protagonist is the orchestra can survive in excerpts of the *Episodio* kind while starving as drama when the staging cannot match the sound.

### Which archaic colours does the score use to age its Verona?

The score ages its Verona with archaic instruments and antique timbres of the kind the composer had already deployed in Francesca da Rimini, where reviewers of the modern revival record the same device at work: early-music sonorities set inside a post-Wagnerian orchestra, signalling a story older than the drama performing it.

The device serves the descent thesis in sound. An audience that hears archaic colour under verismo voices experiences the opera's double date, a sixteenth-century tale and a twentieth-century score occupying one stage, and the friction between the two dates is the sound of the Italian line itself, old material carried in a modern vessel.

The post-verismo label, used throughout this article, deserves one precise paragraph. Verismo proper, the school of *Cavalleria rusticana* and *Pagliacci*, belonged to the eighteen nineties: contemporary subjects, low life, violence compressed into single acts. Zandonai's generation inherited the school's orchestra and its declamatory vocal manner while abandoning its subjects, turning instead to historical and literary tragedy in the line that runs through *Francesca*, through Giordano's later work, and through the Veronese opera. Post-verismo names that inheritance exactly: the technique of verismo applied to the library rather than the street. The hybrid explains the opera's double reputation. Heard as verismo, the score disappoints, since its lovers are aristocrats in a legendary city and its violence is feudal rather than contemporary. Heard as the Italian line's late flowering, the score coheres: long orchestral paragraphs, chromatic harmony absorbed from Strauss and Debussy, melody that surfaces in arioso crests, and a theatre sense that times every effect to the stage picture. Genre is a verdict disguised as a description, and the opera's critics and defenders have often been describing the same sounds under different genre names. The adjudication offered here is that post-verismo, understood as defined in this paragraph, is the name under which the score's virtues become visible and its faults become explicable, and the name under which it should be filed.

## Voices Written for Spinto Weight: Why the Lovers Cannot Sound Young

The opera's vocal writing imposes a casting condition that has governed its fortunes as strictly as any clause in a contract. The three principals, soprano, tenor, and baritone, are written at spinto-dramatic weight, voices suitable, as one modern review put it, for *Tosca* or *Andrea Chénier*. Giulietta is not a lyric ingenue's part. It asks for a dramatic soprano who can confront a baritone across a courtyard, sustain the love scenes against a full post-Wagnerian orchestra, and still find the thread of tone for a death by grief in the final picture. Romeo asks for the Fleta instrument, a tenor of ardent declamatory power whose tomb aria became a dramatic tenor's calling card in Del Monaco's hands. Tebaldo asks for a baritone who can carry an act's menace almost alone, the feud's single articulate voice. A house that casts the three roles at that weight has spent its budget and its courage; a house that casts them lighter has falsified the score's premise. The opera's stage history, with its long Italian currency and its thin international spread, maps almost exactly onto the availability of such voices in such combinations.

The condition has a dramatic consequence that reviewers of the revival noticed with surprise: these lovers do not sing like teenagers. The attachment in the libretto is a marriage between adults of established passion, and the voices match the fiction. The play's heroine is famously on the edge of fourteen, a fact the English tradition has never stopped worrying at, and the novelle heroines are young brides in a world of arranged matches. Rossato and Zandonai sidestep the entire question by writing a wife. A dramatic soprano's Giulietta is a woman defending a sacrament against her house, and the defence, not the awakening, is the role's centre of gravity. The sidestep costs the opera the story's most famous frisson, the first love of the very young, which the French setting exploits in its waltz and the play in its sonnet. The gain is a heroine of agency: a woman who chooses, refuses, contrives the draught, sends the message, and dies by decision. Whether the exchange profits the drama is a genuine critical question, and the honest answer is double. As Shakespeare, the loss is real. As the novelle line's own drama, the gain is the point, since da Porto's heroine was always read in Italy as a wife in a tragedy of marriage rather than as a girl in a tragedy of awakening.

Casting also explains the shape of the work's recorded legacy. The complete recording discussed below fields serviceable rather than stellar voices, and the aria's separate legacy belongs to stars. The opera thus exists in the record shop as two disconnected objects: a whole performed by the competent, and a fragment performed by the great. No other major setting of the story suffers the split so completely. Gounod's opera is recorded entire by singer after singer of the first rank, and Bellini's has drawn leading mezzos and sopranos to complete sets across the decades. Zandonai's waits in the catalogue as a Cetra document and a tenor's four minutes. A revival movement that wants the opera back must solve the casting equation first, since the score offers no reduced form of itself: the voices are the production. The New York staging of 2022 solved it at chamber scale outdoors, with a spinto-weighted trio, and the experiment's success with critics suggests the equation has more solutions than the mid-century houses believed. The point is developed in the revival section below.

## The Missing Generation: No Parents, No Friar, No Nurse

An inventory of absences defines the libretto as sharply as its contents. Missing from Rossato's Verona are Lord and Lady Capulet, Lord Montague, the Prince, Mercutio, the Nurse, Paris in person, and Friar Laurence: in effect, the entire older generation and the entire comic register of the play. The absences are systematic. The parents embody the feud as domestic tyranny, the Prince embodies it as civic failure, the friar embodies its remedy and its catastrophe, the Nurse embodies the heroine's childhood, and Mercutio embodies the wit that makes the feud's world habitable until it kills him. Remove all five functions and the tragedy changes its physics. The feud becomes weather rather than policy, a condition of the streets that no one on stage has the standing to end. The heroine's isolation becomes total, relieved by one handmaiden. The catastrophe becomes a failure of information rather than of engineering, since no friar exists to design a plan whose failure could be blamed. Each absence is a choice with a price, and the prices are worth listing because the opera's detractors usually list them as accidents.

The price of the missing parents is the loss of the play's most harrowing domestic scene, the chamber confrontation in which a father threatens to drag his daughter to church by force. Rossato pays the price knowingly and buys Tebaldo's confrontation with the proceeds: a kinsman's accusation, colder than a father's rage because it is prosecuted as honour rather than felt as betrayal. The price of the missing friar is the draught's shadowy logistics, admitted in the tomb section above. The price of the missing Nurse is the heroine's past: Isabella is a friend and accomplice of the heroine's own generation, so Giulietta exists in the opera entirely in her present tense, as wife and fugitive-maker, with no voice to recall her childhood or measure her fall. The price of the missing Mercutio is tonal: no wit interrupts the feud's grimness, and the opera's Verona is a city without laughter, which is why its street scenes lean on the crowd writing of verismo rather than on any single character's sparkle. Against the prices stand the gains already counted: speed, concentration, and a heroine whose agency no elder dilutes. The balance sheet is the libretto's real argument, and it balances. Rossato did not fail to include the play's population. He declined it, at stated cost, for a tragedy of two households reduced to two lovers, one enemy, and a city of voices.

## Three Italian Settings: Zingarelli, Vaccai, and Bellini Beside Zandonai

The Italian operatic line of the story runs through four names, and Zandonai's opera is unintelligible except as its fourth term. Niccolò Antonio Zingarelli set da Porto's tale at La Scala in 1796, in a score whose Romeo was written for the castrato Girolamo Crescentini and whose tomb aria became the line's first famous page. Nicola Vaccai followed in 1825 with a *Giulietta e Romeo* to a libretto by Felice Romani, the same poet who would supply Bellini, and Vaccai's final scene achieved the strangest afterlife in operatic history: theatres that staged Bellini's opera substituted Vaccai's tomb scene for Bellini's own ending, a practice that survived for decades because the older composer's waking heroine sang the Italian ending better than the younger man's. The full system of that shelf of settings, and the substitution that governed it, belongs to the companion study of [Vaccai and the forgotten operas of the Italian shelf](/2029/03/09/vaccai-forgotten-operas/), where Zandonai's score appears as the shelf's latest exhibit. The present article needs only the line's direction: each Italian setting descends from the novelle, each keeps the waking heroine, and each was famous in its day and forgotten in the next generation's settlement of the repertoire.

Bellini's setting is the line's monument and the standard comparison. [Bellini's I Capuleti e i Montecchi of 1830](/2028/11/03/bellini-capuleti-montecchi/) sets Romani's libretto, drawn through Luigi Scevola's tragedy from the same novelle, with Romeo sung by a mezzo-soprano in the trouser tradition and a feud recast as a war of Guelph and Ghibelline factions. Set the two operas side by side and the century between them measures itself. Bellini's hero is a woman's voice in a youth's costume, his heroine's music the long bel canto line at its purest, his ending the waking recognition that Romani had already written for Vaccai. Zandonai's hero is a spinto tenor, his heroine a dramatic soprano, his orchestra a post-Wagnerian machine, and his ending the same waking recognition carried by voices of twice the weight. The story's Italian identity survives the transformation intact, which is the strongest evidence that the identity never resided in the vocal types or the musical style. It resided in the descent: in the sources chosen, the husband's name kept, the heroine woken. Zandonai could modernise every surface of the opera because the genealogy underneath the surfaces was left untouched, and the modernising is what a fourth term in a line is for.

The comparison also disciplines the verdict on quality. Bellini's score is the greater work by the margin that separates a masterpiece from a respectable successor, and no advocacy for Zandonai is served by blurring the margin. The bel canto setting lives in the repertoire, recorded and staged across the world, while the verismo setting waits for specialists. What the comparison establishes is not equality but membership. The line that runs from Zingarelli through Vaccai and Bellini to Zandonai is the longest continuous operatic tradition attached to any literary story of the Renaissance, four settings across one hundred and twenty-six years from one pair of novelle, and Zandonai's opera is the document that proves the line survived into the age of recording, radio, and the gramophone aria. A tradition's last major document has a value its ranking cannot cancel. Museums understand the principle. The repertoire, which is not a museum, has been slower to grant it, and the slowness is the subject of the eclipse section below.

## The French Survivor: Gounod Beside Zandonai

One comparison outside the Italian line is obligatory, because the international repertoire made it for the two composers. [Gounod's opera Roméo et Juliette of 1867](/2028/10/27/gounod-opera-romeo-et-juliette/) is the setting that conquered the world's stages, and its conquest defines the space Zandonai's opera failed to take. The French score descends from the play through Barbier and Carré, keeps the five-act span, builds its architecture on four love duets, and ends with the Garrick inheritance, a heroine who wakes to sing with her dying lover before the dagger finishes what poison began. The comparison is instructive precisely where the two operas agree. Both librettos restore the tomb meeting that the play abolished, the French by way of the English stage tradition and the Italian by way of the novelle, so the waking heroine turns out to be the nineteenth and twentieth centuries' shared correction of the dramatist, reached by two roads. Both scores trust the duet as the tragedy's natural unit. Both demote the feud to a frame. The differences lie in what each nation did with the inheritance. Gounod wrote for an international lyric theatre that rewarded sheen, youth, and extractable numbers, the waltz and the cavatina travelling the world in singers' luggage. Zandonai wrote for a national theatre in a declared act of reclamation, with voices too heavy for luggage and a finale whose effect depends on an orchestra playing a storm.

The divergent fortunes followed from those choices with something like necessity. The French opera became the default sung form of the story, the version by which listeners who never read the play measure all others, and its default status is itself a fact about adaptation: the play's descent, carried by French lyricism, beat the novelle's descent, carried by Italian verismo, in the international market of the twentieth century. Zandonai's opera is the losing entry in that contest, and honesty about the loss belongs in the verdict. What the loss does not establish is the lazy conclusion that the Italian line was a dead end in 1922. The line's document ran for three decades at home, produced the century's most durable Italian tenor excerpt from the story, and returned to the stage in its centenary year in the story's largest market. A dead end does not behave like that. The French survivor won the world; the Italian restoration held its own country for a generation and kept its aria in the world's record catalogues. Both facts belong in the history of the story's music, and an account that keeps only the first has mistaken a market result for a critical judgement.

## The Premiere Quarrel: Bastianelli and Gasco Against the Gallery

The critical reception of the premiere divides with a clarity that later reception rarely matches, and the division is the opera's best-documented controversy. On one side stood the progressive wing of Italian musical opinion, speaking through two named critics. Giannotto Bastianelli, the Florentine critic and composer who had made himself the conscience of Italy's musical modernism, found, a few isolated moments apart, nothing good in the noisy and emphatic score. Alberto Gasco, writing in Rome, judged the opera overly long and loud, and singled out the third act's intermezzo, the Cavalcata, for particular censure. Bastianelli joined him on the same passage, calling the intermezzo very vulgar and clangorous. Gasco's sentence on the ride became the quarrel's most quoted artifact: the composer, he wrote, seemed to have wanted to depict not the ride of a single person but the uproarious gallop of three thousand uhlans, and he advised a substantial modification of the instrumentation. On the other side stood the gallery that fought, the audiences that encored the Cavalcata from the third performance onward, and the composer, who had announced his intention in advance and recognised the quarrel as the price of it: the younger composers affected not to write for the public, and his aim, by contrast, was to return to melodramma, to popular opera, to the type of opera he called more essentially Italian.

The disagreement is genuine and adjudicable, because both sides were describing the same sounds. The intermezzo is loud, emphatic, and vulgar in the strict etymological sense that it addresses the crowd in the crowd's own register: a gallop rendered at the scale of feeling rather than of fact. Bastianelli and Gasco measured the passage against the modernist standard their criticism existed to enforce, a standard of refinement, economy, and suspicion of effect, and by that standard the passage fails exactly as they said. The public measured it against the melodramma standard the composer had declared, the standard of *Cavalleria* and the great verismo nights, in which an orchestral storm earns its encore by carrying an emotion at full size, and by that standard the passage succeeded exactly as the encores said. The adjudication this article reaches is that the declared standard governs. A work that announces itself as a return to popular melodramma cannot be convicted of popularity as if popularity were an accident or a lapse; the critic's task is to ask whether the popular effect is achieved with craft, and on that question the record is one-sided, since the despised passage proved the most durable orchestral music in the score, detachable into a concert *Episodio* that held a place of affection in the Italian concert repertory for decades, as the musicologist Gioacchino Lanza Tomasi could still testify in 1983. Bastianelli and Gasco were accurate reporters of the sound and poor judges of its purpose. The gallery, for once, read the score's intentions more exactly than the profession did.

A second disagreement, quieter and later, runs through the modern notices and deserves the same treatment. Reviewers of the 2022 staging converged on a formula: a score short of melodic inspiration, strongest in its orchestral colour, its vocal writing declamatory and its arioso fine but its tunes unmemorable. Set against that formula stands the tenor tradition, which kept the tomb aria in the repertory of Fleta, Del Monaco, and Kaufmann across a full century. Both observations are true, and their reconciliation is the accurate criticism of the score. The melody of *Giulietta e Romeo* is concentrated rather than absent: it gathers at the crests, the tomb address above all, and thins across the long declamatory spans that connect them. Francesca had distributed its melody more evenly, which is why the earlier opera survives whole and the later one survives in its crest. The modern formula errs only in converting a distribution into an absence. A listener who knows the opera from its aria hears its best tune and mistakes the part for a promise the whole declines to keep. A listener who knows it whole hears the design: an opera that saves its song for the moments when its characters stop performing their roles and speak as spouses. The design is defensible. Its cost, in a repertoire that markets tunes, was fatal, and costs of that kind are the substance of the next two sections.

## Sixty-Seven Productions: The Interwar Currency of the Opera

The scholarly record gives the opera's stage currency in a single statistic that advocacy and detraction must both accommodate: there were no fewer than sixty-seven productions of *Giulietta e Romeo* in its first fifty years, and the work stood as standard repertory in Italy until the middle of the nineteen fifties. Sixty-seven productions is not a succès d'estime. It is the profile of a repertory opera, mounted by house after house across three decades, through the remainder of the verismo era, the fascist ventennio, the war, and the first postwar seasons. The composer's wager of 1922, judged by its first three decades, had won. Italian audiences kept faith with a popular melodramma on the national story, sung by the dramatic voices the Italian houses still cultivated, and the Cavalcata's encores became a standard practice of the run rather than a premiere novelty. The German-speaking stage took a version of the work in Alfred Brüggemann's translation, evidence that the currency, while centred in Italy, was not confined to it.

The currency also had a concert dimension, examined separately below, and a human one that the statistics conceal. A repertory opera of this kind lives through its interpreters. Dalla Rizza's identification with the heroine linked the opera to the most admired Italian dramatic soprano of the interwar stage, and Fleta's recording carried the hero beyond the theatres into the gramophone catalogues while the tenor's own career burned through its short arc to his death in 1938. The composer conducted, travelled, and revised; the *Episodio sinfonico* of 1927 shows him actively managing the opera's afterlife within five years of the premiere, extracting its most popular page for the concert hall with a showman's instinct the modernists denied him. Nothing in the record of these decades resembles a failure searching for an explanation. The explanation is required by what happened next, and it must account for a repertory work's disappearance within roughly a decade of its standard status. Explanations that reach for the score's quality alone fail the arithmetic: scores do not change in a decade, but the conditions around them do, and the conditions changed completely.

## The Postwar Eclipse: Why the Opera Left the Stage

The eclipse, when it came, had structural causes that the record supports better than the aesthetic verdict usually offered in its place. The first cause was vocal. The postwar Italian stage lost its bench of spinto-dramatic voices of the old school at the moment when the bel canto revival redirected casting prestige toward agility and youth; an opera that needs a dramatic soprano, a dramatic tenor, and a dramatic baritone in a single bill became expensive in the currency that mattered, available singers. The second cause was repertory politics. The postwar settlement of the Italian stage canonised Puccini entire, revived the ottocento masters, and admitted the modernists as classics, leaving the generation between, the post-verismo school of Zandonai, Giordano, and their peers, classified as a corridor between two destinations. Corridor composers are performed in centenary years and studied in monographs, and the classification, once fixed in programming habits, reproduces itself. The third cause was the absence of a champion. *Francesca* survived its corridor decades on the advocacy of great singing actresses, from dalla Rizza to Olivero to Scotto, each of whom made the role a personal cause. *Giulietta e Romeo* produced no equivalent advocacy at the level of the staged whole; its champions, the tenors, championed four minutes of it.

The fourth cause is the one this article has been building toward, and it is a cause about sources. The twentieth century's international imagination of the Veronese story was colonised by the play to a degree the nineteenth century never approached: school curricula, the great films, and the Gounod default taught the world a single version, with a balcony, a friar, a nurse, a dagger, and a heroine who wakes too late. An opera lacking every one of those landmarks reads to an international audience as a defective copy rather than as a parallel original, and programme committees spend their novelty budgets carefully. The Italian line's counter-version, which had needed no defence in the Italy of 1922, needed an argument everywhere by 1960, and operas that need arguments get concert performances rather than stagings. The eclipse, on this accounting, was the international victory of one descent of the story over another, ratified by casting economics and corridor classification. It was not a jury's verdict on sixty-seven productions' worth of evenings. The distinction matters for what revival can mean, since a verdict can only be appealed, while conditions can be changed. The revival record, taken up after the recording and concert sections, shows the conditions beginning to change.

## The Record Keeps the Aria: The Cetra Set and the Tenor Discs

The opera's discography is the eclipse rendered in catalogue form, and its shape deserves exact statement. The work's one widely circulated complete recording is the Cetra performance conducted by Loris Gavarini, dated 1961 in the discographies, with Angelo Lo Forese as Romeo, Antonietta Mazza Medici as Giulietta, and Mario Zanasi as Tebaldo, later reissued in the Warner Cetra reissue programme. The set is a document of the opera's last repertory decade, made by the label that served as Italy's archive of its own tradition, with voices of honest professional weight rather than international glamour. Around that single whole stands the aria's separate discography, which belongs to another class of artist entirely: Fleta's creator's record, Del Monaco's signature disc, Kaufmann's album appearance under Pappano. A browser of catalogues could reasonably conclude that the opera consists of one tenor aria with a long orchestral introduction preserved once on Cetra. The conclusion is the eclipse's most durable artifact, since recordings outlast the memories of productions and teach each new generation what a work is.

The imbalance has a critical consequence that revivalists understate. Judged from its complete recording alone, the opera sounds smaller than it is, because the Cetra set's virtues are documentary rather than theatrical: the score's orchestral protagonist registers, but the stage relations that justify the declamatory spans do not, and a listener at home meets the long arguments without the confrontations that give them point. Judged from its aria alone, the opera sounds simpler than it is, a verismo romance with a famous crest. Neither judgement is the work. The staged revivals of the centenary period therefore matter beyond their local notices, since they restore the evidence on which a fair judgement depends, the evidence of the whole in a theatre. Comparison sharpens the point for readers who organise their listening around the story's other orchestral monument: [Tchaikovsky's fantasy-overture and its love theme](/2028/11/17/tchaikovsky-fantasy-overture/) survives entire in every catalogue because an overture is its own complete object, while Zandonai's score, an opera and therefore a compound object, survives in the fragments its extractors chose. The gramophone rewarded works that fit its formats. The Veronese opera, which fits none of them except in four minutes, paid the format tax in full.

## The Episodio Sinfonico: The Opera's Second Life in the Concert Hall

Within five years of the premiere the composer had begun the extraction that gave the opera its second life. Zandonai thought about a concert arrangement of the Cavalcata as early as the summer after the first performance, in correspondence with his confidant Nicola d'Atri, the journalist whose name stands in the libretto's own source line as a collaborator in the adaptation. The resolution came in September 1926, with concerts to conduct in Rome the following year. The final plan prefaced the ride with the Danza del torchio, the Torch Dance from the start of the second act, joined the two pieces with newly composed music sketched in November 1927, and sent the completed score, titled an *Episodio sinfonico*, to Ricordi that month. The composer conducted the premiere on 11 December 1927 with the Orchestra della Regia Accademia di Santa Cecilia in the Augusteo hall in Rome. D'Atri, who had advised throughout, declared the Cavalcata a piece of such irresistible success that it would surely enter the repertory, and the prediction held for decades: the musicologist Gioacchino Lanza Tomasi, writing in 1983, could still describe the *Episodio* as having enjoyed for many years a place of affection in the Italian concert repertory.

The extraction deserves analysis rather than mere chronicle, because it shows the composer editing his own opera's meaning. The two chosen pieces are the score's most purely orchestral spans, the dance that lights the trap of the second act and the ride that carries the catastrophe of the third, and joined in sequence they narrate the opera's public half: festivity curdling into flight, the city dancing while the sentence ripens, the husband riding through the storm the city made. No voice enters. The *Episodio* is therefore the opera with its private half removed, the lovers deleted from their own tragedy, and its concert success across the decades when the staging thinned out completes the pattern the recording section described: the work's public, orchestral, message-bearing half proved portable, and its intimate half, the spouses' scenes, stayed locked in the theatre. A composer of Zandonai's theatre sense understood exactly what he was selling in 1927. He was selling the half that the format of the concert hall could hold, and the sale kept his name in the programmes while the opera waited for its conditions to change. The shrewdness is part of the record, and it complicates the portrait of the defiant traditionalist: the defender of popular melodramma was also its most clear-eyed liquidator, converting stage capital into concert capital at the rate the market set.

## New York 2022: The Centenary Staging at Battery Park

The opera's centenary year produced the staging that re-opened its file. Teatro Grattacielo, the New York company specialising in the verismo and post-verismo repertory, gave the work its New York City stage premiere on 4 and 5 June 2022, outdoors in Robert F. Wagner Jr. Park at Battery Park City, in a site-specific production directed by Stefanos Koroneos and conducted by Christian Capocaccia, with Eleni Calenos as Giulietta, Matthew Vickers as Romeo, and Franco Pomponi as Tebaldo. The billing is itself a datum: a century after the Costanzi premiere, the second city of the composer's own triumphs, the New York that had taken *Francesca* in 1916 and restored it in 1984 and 2013, had never staged the Veronese opera entire. The Grattacielo production, mounted with the Battery Park City Authority in the composer's centenary season, closed that gap under the open sky, with the harbour behind the Capuleti square and the production's projections carrying the storm.

The reviews returned the split decision this article has tracked from 1922, and the consistency is evidence rather than coincidence. The score was found short of melodic inspiration in one notice and not quite as melodically memorable as *Francesca* in another, while its orchestral colour, its string writing, its post-Wagnerian and post-Debussian atmosphere, and its archaic instruments for the story's antiquity drew agreement across the notices. The libretto drew the sharper praise for its structure: the dramaturgy was called tight and fat-free, its running span under two hours of music, and the discovery that the lovers are spouses before the curtain rises registered as the production's central revelation for more than one critic. Federica Fortunato, scientific director of the Riccardo Zandonai International Study Center at Rovereto, supplied the production's programme note, reading the story as a metaphor of love, tenderness, and friendship opposed to the logic of blood and power. A staging that earns that spread of response has done the one thing a revival must do: it has made the work arguable again on evidence. The opera left its centenary no longer a rumour attached to an aria, at least in the city that staged it, and the file it re-opened is the file this article has attempted to write.

## Verdict: A Minor Opera of a Major Line

The evidence assembles into a verdict with three clauses, matching the three tests announced at the start. On descent, the opera is exemplary: a libretto drawn from da Porto and Bandello, carrying the Italian line's fingerprints in the husband's name, the waking heroine, the daggerless death, and the lean cast, composed by the Ricordi firm's designated successor as a conscious act of reclamation. On intention, the opera succeeds on its own declared terms: a return to popular melodramma, judged by the public it addressed, who encored its most attacked passage from the third performance and kept the work in the repertory for three decades and sixty-seven productions. On survival, the opera failed for reasons external to its design: casting economics, corridor classification, the gramophone's format tax, and the international colonisation of the story by a single rival descent. A minor opera of a major line is the accurate ranking, and the ranking should be read whole. The line is major: four Italian settings across one hundred and twenty-six years, from Zingarelli's castrato hero to Fleta's spinto husband, all descending from two novelle of the Italian Renaissance. The opera is minor: a score whose melody concentrates at its crests, whose dramaturgy wagers on production, and whose whole is less than the century of its aria. Both halves of the ranking are compliments of a kind no merely fashionable work earns.

The last word belongs to the distinction the opera itself insists on. The English play ends with a prince's judgement and two golden statues, a city purchasing reconciliation at the price of its children. The Italian line ends with a wife sinking beside a husband under morning bells, a bond judged by no one and sufficient to itself. Rossato and Zandonai did not fail to reach the play's ending. They set the other ending, the older one, the one their story had carried since da Porto wrote it in Verona's shadow. Readers who want the play sung should go to the French opera, which serves that want honourably. Readers who want to know what the story was before England remade it, and what Italy made of its own tale when its greatest living opera house tradition took the tale back, have exactly one major document to study. For sustained comparison of a score against its sources, its rivals, and its own composer's earlier triumph, a researcher assembling the evidence into a presentable dossier may find a structured viewing tool such as [the ReportMedic PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) useful for keeping the comparison tables and production records in order. The document itself needs no apparatus. It needs a theatre, three dramatic voices, an orchestra willing to ride, and an audience prepared to hear a famous story told by the tradition that invented it. On the evidence of 1922 and of 2022 alike, such audiences still exist, and the opera that serves them has not finished its history.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which theatre premiered Zandonai's Giulietta e Romeo, and who conducted the first performance?

The opera was first performed at the Teatro Costanzi in Rome on 14 February 1922, with the composer conducting. The date placed the unveiling of a tragedy of young love on the feast of the saint of lovers, and the house filled with a fashionable crowd whose response divided sharply enough to produce fighting in the gallery before the evening closed in the success the composer had hoped for. The premiere came eight years after the Turin triumph of Francesca da Rimini, at the height of the composer's standing as the Ricordi firm's designated successor in the Italian line, and the occasion was treated in Rome as a plebiscite on the future of popular Italian opera as much as a first night.

### Q: Who sang Romeo and Giulietta at the 1922 premiere?

Gilda dalla Rizza sang Giulietta and Miguel Fleta sang Romeo at the Costanzi premiere. Dalla Rizza, born in 1892, was Puccini's late-career muse, the creator of Magda in La rondine, and the defining Francesca of her generation, so her presence bound the new opera to the composer's established triumph in the audience's mind. Fleta, the Spanish tenor born in 1897, brought a tempestuous fame of his own and four years later created the role of Calaf in the posthumous premiere of Turandot. The pairing announced the production's intention plainly: the new score was a star vehicle in the grand Italian manner, written for voices of spinto-dramatic weight, and the standard those two singers set at the first performance shaped every later judgement of what the roles require.

### Q: Who wrote the libretto of Giulietta e Romeo, and what were its sources?

Arturo Rossato wrote the libretto, and its sources were the Italian novelle of Luigi da Porto and Matteo Bandello. The printed text names the work a tragedy in three acts, and the source line of the adaptation also carries the journalist Nicola d'Atri, the composer's confidant, whose counsel runs through the opera's history and its concert afterlife. Da Porto's novella, printed in 1530, gave the lovers their names, their city, and their tomb; Bandello's retelling a generation later carried the tale toward France and England. Rossato worked from that Italian line directly, keeping its promised husband under da Porto's own designation, its waking heroine, and its lean cast, rather than translating the English play that the novelle had eventually produced.

### Q: Is Giulietta e Romeo based on Shakespeare's play?

No. The documented descent of Rossato's libretto runs through the Italian novelle and not through the English play, and the libretto's decisive features prove the descent wherever the two traditions divide. The promised husband is a count of Lodrone, as da Porto left him, rather than the Paris whom Bandello named and the play inherited. The heroine wakes in the tomb while her husband still lives and dies beside him without a weapon, as the novelle arrange, rather than waking to a corpse and seizing a dagger. The play's contribution to the opera is climate rather than text: a century of European fame that made the subject valuable and coloured the audience's imagination, while the page in front of the composer followed da Porto and Bandello.

### Q: Why does the opera have no Friar Laurence, no Nurse, and no parents?

The absences follow from Rossato's three-act compression and from the novelle line's leaner cast. A verismo tragedy of under two hours of music cannot nourish the play's population, so the libretto strips the story to the lovers, one articulate enemy in Tebaldo, and one accomplice in Isabella, who absorbs the Nurse's function as the heroine's friend and handmaiden. The parents vanish because the feud is carried by armed retinues and by Tebaldo's knowledge rather than by domestic tyranny. The friar vanishes because the catastrophe is engineered by messages rather than by a plan: a herald's proclamation, a singer's ballad, and a messenger's confirmation do the killing that a failed potion plot does in the play. Each absence costs a famous scene and buys speed, concentration, and the heroine's undivided agency.

### Q: Who is Count Lodrone in Zandonai's opera?

Count Lodrone is the husband chosen for Giulietta by her house, and he never appears on stage. The figure descends from Luigi da Porto's novella, where the heroine is promised to a count of Lodrone who carries no personal name; Matteo Bandello, retelling the tale, gave the man the name Paris, and the play inherited Bandello's christening. Rossato keeps da Porto's unnamed count, present only in other mouths: Tebaldo invokes the match to prosecute the heroine's shame, the street singer's ballad reports that she died on the threshold of the Lodrone wedding, and the feigned death itself is contrived to escape the ceremony. His absence is the libretto's neatest source fingerprint and a dramatic device in one, an arranged marriage experienced as pure pressure, a fate announced by herald and kinsman rather than a rival embodied in a singer.

### Q: What is "Giulietta! Son io!" and why is it famous?

"Giulietta! Son io!" is Romeo's address at the tomb in the third act, the husband's self-announcement at the locked gate of the Capuleti chapel: Juliet, it is I. The page stands at the opera's crest, where the ride's orchestral violence has spent itself and the voice must carry recognition, hope, and fatal error in a single span of arioso raised to aria pitch. Its fame is a matter of recording history. Fleta, the role's creator, left an impassioned record of the piece; Mario Del Monaco made it a signature disc in the postwar decades; Jonas Kaufmann included it on the 2015 album The Age of Puccini under Antonio Pappano. The scholarly introduction to the score materials calls the excerpt a staple of the verismo tenor repertory, and its century of tenor advocacy kept the opera's name in circulation while the staged work faded.

### Q: What is the Cavalcata in Giulietta e Romeo?

The Cavalcata is the third act's orchestral intermezzo, the ride of Romeo from Mantua back to Verona through a thunderstorm after the street singer's ballad and a messenger's confirmation have persuaded him that Giulietta is dead. The passage paints a single horseman's nocturnal gallop in orchestral tumult, and it became the opera's most argued-over page. The Roman critic Alberto Gasco heard in it the uproarious gallop of three thousand uhlans and advised the composer to modify the instrumentation, while the Florentine critic Giannotto Bastianelli dismissed the intermezzo as very vulgar and clangorous. The public gave the opposite verdict, encoring the passage from the third performance onward until the encore became standard practice. The composer later carried the piece into the concert hall as the core of the Episodio sinfonico of 1927.

### Q: Why did the first critics attack the Act 3 intermezzo that audiences encored?

The attack and the encore measured the same sounds against different standards. Bastianelli and Gasco wrote for the progressive wing of Italian musical opinion, which prized refinement, economy, and suspicion of effect, and by that standard a gallop rendered at the scale of feeling rather than of fact was noisy, emphatic, and vulgar. The audience measured the passage against the standard the composer had declared for the whole opera, a return to melodramma and to popular opera of the kind he called more essentially Italian, in which an orchestral storm earns repetition by carrying an emotion at full size. Posterity has sided with the audience on the passage's durability: the intermezzo became the opera's most detachable orchestral page, anchored the Episodio sinfonico in the concert repertory for decades, and drew from the musicologist Gioacchino Lanza Tomasi, in 1983, the testimony that the Episodio had long held a place of affection in Italian concert life.

### Q: What kills Giulietta in Zandonai's final scene?

Giulietta dies of grief beside her husband's body, without a weapon, in the opera's restoration of the Italian ending. She has taken a draught that lays her in a deathlike sleep to escape the Lodrone wedding, and she wakes in the chapel while Romeo, who has drunk poison at her bier in the belief that the sleep was death, still lives. The lovers speak, exchange the truth of the draught and of the poison, and share their last minutes in reunion. Romeo dies in her arms. Giulietta, who cannot and will not live without him, sinks down beside the body and dies entwined with it as the morning bells ring. The design follows da Porto and Bandello, whose heroines die of refusal and grief in the same posture, and it rejects the play's dagger as deliberately as the libretto rejects the play's other machinery.

### Q: How many acts does Giulietta e Romeo have, and where is each act set?

The opera has three acts, as its printed libretto declares in calling the work a tragedy in three acts. The first act occupies a small square in Verona at night, outside the Capuleti palace, where the feud's street fighting frames the masked Romeo's reunion with his wife. The second act occupies a courtyard of the Capuleti palace in late afternoon, holding the Torch Dance, Tebaldo's confrontation with Giulietta, the lovers' discovered meeting, the duel, and the flight. The third act divides into two pictures: a square in Mantua in the afternoon, where the singer's ballad and the storm launch the ride, and the chapel of the Capuleti at dawn, where the poison, the waking, and the double death complete the tragedy. The music runs to under two hours in performance.

### Q: What is the Episodio sinfonico from Giulietta e Romeo?

The Episodio sinfonico is the concert arrangement the composer drew from the opera in 1927, joining the Danza del torchio, the Torch Dance from the start of the second act, to the Cavalcata, the third act's ride, with newly composed connective music. Zandonai had contemplated the extraction in correspondence with Nicola d'Atri from the summer after the premiere, resolved on it in September 1926 with Roman concerts in view, sketched the new music in November 1927, and sent the completed score to Ricordi that month. He conducted the first performance on 11 December 1927 in the Augusteo hall in Rome with the Orchestra della Regia Accademia di Santa Cecilia. The Episodio held a place in the Italian concert repertory for many years, carrying the opera's public and orchestral half into halls where the whole work was never staged, and it remains the score's most successful act of self-liquidation.

### Q: How does Zandonai's opera differ from Bellini's I Capuleti e i Montecchi?

The two operas descend from the same novelle and differ in almost everything else. Bellini's setting of 1830, on Romani's libretto drawn through Scevola's tragedy, gives Romeo to a mezzo-soprano in the trouser tradition, recasts the feud as a war of Guelph and Ghibelline factions, and writes for the long bel canto line. Zandonai's setting gives Romeo to a spinto tenor, Giulietta to a dramatic soprano, and the feud to armed retinues under one baritone enemy, and writes for a post-Wagnerian orchestra in the post-verismo manner. The decisive features agree across the ninety-two years: the heroine wakes while her husband lives, the recognition replaces the dagger, and no scene requires the play as a source. The agreement is the point of the comparison, since it locates the Italian identity of the story in its descent rather than in any period style, and it makes the later opera the fourth term of a line rather than an eccentricity.

### Q: Why was Zandonai called Puccini's successor?

The title was a publishing house's plan before it was a critic's phrase. Tito Ricordi, whose firm had issued the Italian line from Rossini through Puccini, took the young composer up after Arrigo Boito's introduction, commissioned his first opera, and negotiated the costly d'Annunzio rights for Francesca da Rimini at a price the composer helped meet by reducing his own advance. The Turin premiere of Francesca in 1914 was received as a masterpiece, and the succession seemed secure. Puccini's own reported preference completes the picture: understanding that death would leave Turandot unfinished, he favoured Zandonai to complete the score from the sketches. Nothing came of the preference, but the episode fixes the altitude of the composer's standing in the years when Giulietta e Romeo was written, and it explains why that opera's later eclipse reads as a lost inheritance rather than a routine failure.

### Q: What complete recording of Giulietta e Romeo exists?

The opera's one widely circulated complete recording is the Cetra performance conducted by Loris Gavarini, dated 1961 in the discographies, with Angelo Lo Forese as Romeo, Antonietta Mazza Medici as Giulietta, and Mario Zanasi as Tebaldo, later reissued in the Warner Cetra reissue programme. The set documents the opera's last repertory decade in Italy, sung by voices of honest professional weight rather than international glamour, and it preserves the whole score for study. The discography's imbalance is part of the opera's history: the whole survives in one documentary set, while the tomb aria survives in the records of stars, from the creator Fleta through Del Monaco to Kaufmann. A listener reconstructing the work from discs meets two disconnected objects, a complete opera competently sung and a famous fragment magnificently sung, and the staged revivals of the centenary period matter because they reunite the two.

### Q: When did New York first stage Giulietta e Romeo?

New York first staged the opera complete on 4 and 5 June 2022, when Teatro Grattacielo gave the work its city stage premiere outdoors in Robert F. Wagner Jr. Park at Battery Park City. Stefanos Koroneos directed the site-specific production and Christian Capocaccia conducted, with Eleni Calenos as Giulietta, Matthew Vickers as Romeo, and Franco Pomponi as Tebaldo. The date carried its own commentary: a full century separated the staging from the Costanzi premiere, in the city that had taken Francesca da Rimini in 1916 and restored that opera twice at the Metropolitan. The centenary production, mounted with the Battery Park City Authority and introduced in its programme by Federica Fortunato of the Zandonai study centre at Rovereto, returned the whole score to judgement and drew the same split verdict the premiere had drawn, orchestral mastery conceded and melodic abundance disputed.

### Q: How long did Giulietta e Romeo stay in the Italian repertory?

The opera stood as standard repertory in Italy until the middle of the nineteen fifties, and the scholarly record counts no fewer than sixty-seven productions in its first fifty years. That profile is a repertory success by any measure: three decades of mountings across Italian houses, from the postwar seasons through the ventennio and the second war into the first postwar years, with the Cavalcata's encore established as standard practice inside the run. The eclipse that followed was therefore an event with causes rather than a slow verdict, and the causes were structural: the thinning supply of spinto-dramatic voices in the required combination, the postwar classification of the post-verismo generation as a corridor between Puccini and the moderns, the absence of a singing champion for the whole work, and the international triumph of the play's rival descent. The score did not change in the nineteen fifties. The conditions around it did.

### Q: Why did Zandonai describe Giulietta e Romeo as a return to melodramma?

The description was a declaration of intent against the fashion of his moment. Younger composers, the composer later explained, were affecting not to write for the public; his aim by contrast was to return to melodramma, to popular opera, to the type of opera he called more essentially Italian. The words frame the opera as an act of defiance mounted from inside the establishment: the Ricordi firm's designated successor used the firm's most famous national subject to argue that sung tragedy for a broad public remained a living form. The premiere quarrel tested the declaration immediately, since the progressive critics heard the return as vulgarity while the gallery heard it as restoration and encored the Cavalcata from the third performance. The declaration also fixes the standard by which the opera asks to be judged. A work that announces popularity as its programme cannot be convicted of popularity as though the quality were a lapse, and the fair critical question is whether the popular effects are achieved with craft.
