## The Separation in One Paragraph: What a Spin-Off Actually Does

A spin-off is a separation in which a parent company places a distinct business into a new independent public company and distributes the new company shares to existing shareholders in proportion to their holdings, without requiring payment and without an initial public offering. The parent shareholders finish with shares in two listed companies instead of one, the combined assets and liabilities are divided between the two balance sheets, and each company begins trading separately with its own board, management team, filings and capital structure. No cash changes hands in the distribution itself. A spin-off creates no value on the day it closes; it only removes the structure that was hiding value, so every gain attributed to a spin-off is really a gain from the focus, capital allocation and accountability that the combined company could not produce.

![Corporate separation and the division of one listed company into two - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-62.webp)

The mechanics begin with a board decision and a perimeter, followed by the preparation of audited carve-out financial statements for the business to be separated. The parent then files SEC Form 10 with the Securities and Exchange Commission, a registration statement that contains financial statements, risk disclosures, management discussion and a description of the separation agreements, and the form becomes effective before the distribution date is set. On the distribution date, shareholders recorded on the record date receive the new shares, often through a book entry distribution administered by a transfer agent, and the new company lists on a stock exchange through the registration rather than through a sale of shares to new investors.

Tax treatment in the United States turns on Internal Revenue Code Section 355. Under Section 355, a distributing corporation may distribute stock of a controlled corporation to its shareholders without recognition of gain at the corporate or shareholder level when statutory conditions are met, including control immediately before the distribution, an active trade or business conducted for five years, a valid business purpose, continuity of interest and continuity of business enterprise, and a showing that the transaction is not used principally as a device to distribute earnings. Internal Revenue Code Section 355(e) adds a separate restriction that can impose corporate level tax when the distribution forms part of a plan under which one or more persons acquire 50 percent or more of the stock of either company.

Separation also requires a set of contracts that replace what the combined company once supplied internally. A separation and distribution agreement allocates assets, liabilities and obligations between the two companies, while a tax matters agreement allocates tax liabilities and restricts certain transactions, an employee matters agreement allocates compensation and benefit plans, and transition services agreements cover shared functions for a limited period after closing. Debt is allocated through repayment, refinancing, assumption or the issuance of new notes by the separated company, sometimes paired with a cash distribution from the separated company to the parent before the shares are distributed. Intellectual property, real estate leases, customer contracts and regulatory licenses are assigned or replaced where consent is required. Cusatis, Miles and Wooldridge, in the Journal of Financial Economics in 1993, documented the structure and market history of spin-offs as a distinct form of restructuring, a study that helped establish the distribution without an offering as a separate category from a sale or a carve-out. The result is legal independence first, and operating independence built afterward through separate incentives, separate disclosure and separate decisions about investment.

## What the Combined Company Sells and Why It Was Combined

A combined company sells more than a collection of products because its divisions share purchasing, distribution, finance, legal support, information systems and senior management attention under one hierarchy. That breadth once promised efficiency. Alfred Chandler, in Scale and Scope in 1990, argued that large industrial firms grew by exploiting economies of scale in production and economies of scope in distribution and management, investing in plant, marketing networks and managerial hierarchies that let one organization coordinate many product lines across many markets. It was a response to technology and market reach at a time when coordinating supply, production and selling through a single hierarchy could lower unit costs and support investment that smaller rivals could not finance alone.

Transaction cost economics supplied a second explanation for why activities were brought inside one firm. Ronald Coase, in work first published in 1937, asked why firms exist at all if markets coordinate production, and answered that using the price mechanism carries costs of search, negotiation and enforcement that an organization can sometimes avoid. Oliver Williamson extended that logic in work published from the nineteen seventies onward, arguing that firms choose hierarchy over market contracting when transactions are frequent, assets are specific to the relationship and uncertainty makes complete contracts impractical. Common ownership replaced repeated bargaining between independent parties, and headquarters acted as an internal monitor that could move managers, audit performance and settle disputes without litigation or renegotiation.

The managerial hierarchy also promised a particular kind of judgment. Headquarters could compare divisions against one another, shift capital toward higher return uses and apply common disciplines in finance, hiring and controls. Diversification spread risk across industries with different cycles, a benefit that mattered more when external capital markets were less developed and information about a division was harder for outside investors to verify. Related diversification added another rationale, because divisions could share brands, research, sales channels or manufacturing knowledge. Michael Porter, named for industry structure, framed competitive advantage through the forces that shape profitability within an industry, and that framing helped managers argue that entering an attractive adjacent industry could improve the whole portfolio if the parent brought transferable skills to the new business.

The same logic carried limits that became harder to ignore as capital markets deepened and disclosure improved. A hierarchy that coordinates well at one scale can become slow and political at another, because divisional managers compete for headquarters attention as well as for capital, and headquarters lacks the detailed knowledge that specialist managers and focused investors develop. Clayton Christensen, named once alongside critics who challenged the breadth of disruption claims, argued that successful firms can fail by listening closely to established customers and sustaining technologies, and critics of that theory noted that its predictive claims were narrower than its popular reception suggested. The debate mattered for conglomerates because it exposed a general weakness in large hierarchies. Excellence in an existing business does not guarantee sound judgment about a different business with different customers, different technology and different economics.

Boards therefore inherited a structure built for an earlier set of conditions. When external finance became more available, when institutional investors could diversify on their own account and when segment reporting made divisional performance more visible, the original case for combination weakened. The combined company still sold breadth, shared services and internal coordination, but separation advocates could ask a sharper question. Whether each business still needed the other to obtain capital, customers or management, or whether the hierarchy had become a cost imposed on businesses that could contract for what they shared and govern the rest independently.

## The Unit Economics of a Division Inside a Conglomerate

The unit economics of a division inside a conglomerate are shaped less by its own sales and costs than by the headquarters decisions that allocate capital, overhead and transfer prices around it. A division may report a margin, yet that margin rests on internal charges for shared factories, distribution networks, information technology, treasury, legal services and brand support. Transfer pricing determines what one division pays another for components, services or intellectual property, and different allocation methods can make the same operating business look strong or weak without any change in customer demand or factory efficiency. Central purchasing may lower input prices, but a division that must use a central platform built for the whole group may carry costs that a focused rival would not accept.

Internal capital markets sit at the center of that system. Headquarters collects cash flow from all divisions and reallocates it according to a group plan, rather than requiring each division to raise capital externally on its own record. Jeremy Stein, in the Journal of Finance in 1997, described a potential benefit of that arrangement as winner picking, in which an informed headquarters shifts resources toward divisions with better opportunities than outside lenders can identify. When headquarters instead spreads capital evenly to preserve internal balance, funds weaker divisions from stronger ones or protects a legacy business for reasons of size and prestige, the internal market performs worse than the external market it replaces.

Cross-subsidy is the practical name for that failure. A mature division with steady cash flow can finance losses or heavy investment in another division for years, and segment disclosure may reveal the transfer only in part because corporate overhead, interest and tax effects are often reported outside divisional operating results. Hyun-Han Shin and Rene Stulz, in the Journal of Financial Economics in 1998, found evidence consistent with the view that divisional investment inside diversified firms depended on the cash flow of other divisions, a pattern that supports the concern that internal allocation does not respond only to its own opportunities.
Accountability weakens under those conditions because performance measures are negotiated inside the firm. A divisional president may meet a budget that headquarters set after adjusting for allocations, currency, restructuring charges and shared costs, while outside investors see only segment revenue and a segment profit figure defined by management. Compensation often mixes divisional results with group results, which dilutes the link between a manager's decisions and personal reward. Capital requests compete through internal presentations rather than through the test of convincing lenders or shareholders who can decline the project. That process favors managers skilled at internal advocacy and penalizes businesses whose value depends on long term investment that headquarters cannot easily compare across unrelated industries.

Separation changes the arithmetic by forcing each business to carry its own overhead, negotiate services at stated prices and raise capital against its own cash flow and prospects. A separated division must publish standalone financial statements, explain its own margins and fund its own pension, debt and capital expenditure obligations. Costs once buried in corporate allocations become explicit contracts or new internal functions. The change does not guarantee better performance, because a weak business remains weak when its subsidy ends, and a strong business may lose purchasing scale or shared knowledge that had genuine value. What changes is visibility. After separation, cross-subsidy requires an observable transaction between independent companies rather than an internal allocation, and capital allocation becomes a decision that each board must defend to its own shareholders and creditors on the record of one business alone.

## The Conglomerate Discount and How Analysts Measure It

The conglomerate discount describes the gap that can appear when the market value of a diversified company is lower than the estimated combined value of its divisions valued as focused businesses. The idea rests on a simple comparison. An analyst estimates what each segment would be worth on multiples drawn from comparable single business firms, adds those estimates together and compares the total with the market value of the actual combined company. A shortfall suggests that investors pay less for the divisions inside the conglomerate than for similar assets outside it, whether because of expected cross-subsidy, weaker incentives, complex disclosure or doubt about headquarters capital allocation. A premium is also possible, and the measurement must allow for both outcomes rather than assume that diversification always destroys value.

Berger and Ofek, in the Journal of Financial Economics in 1995, gave the discount its best known empirical estimate. Philip Berger and Eli Ofek compared the actual value of diversified firms with an imputed value built by applying the median value to sales or value to earnings multiples of single segment firms in the same industries to each segment of the diversified firm. Using segment data for the period from 1986 to 1991, Berger and Ofek estimated an average diversification discount in the range of 13 percent to 15 percent. Berger and Ofek also reported that the discount was smaller for firms with more related segments and larger where diversification coincided with overinvestment and cross-subsidization, findings that linked the valuation gap to the internal capital allocation problems that separation is meant to address.

### How is the conglomerate discount calculated?

The conglomerate discount is calculated by valuing each reported segment as if it were a standalone firm, using valuation multiples from comparable focused companies, adding the segment values into an imputed sum of the parts, and comparing that total with the diversified company actual market value. The percentage gap between the two values is the estimated discount or premium.

The method carries limits that Berger and Ofek acknowledged through the design of the estimate and that later research pressed further. Segment definitions are chosen by management, segment profit is affected by allocations, and comparable firms differ in growth, risk and asset intensity even within a narrow industry code. John Graham, Michael Lemmon and Jack Wolf, in the Journal of Finance in 2002, showed that part of the measured discount reflected the characteristics of firms and units that became part of diversified companies, rather than an effect caused by diversification itself, because units acquired by diversifying firms already traded at discounts before acquisition. That selection problem matters for spin-off analysis. A discount measured before separation does not prove that separation will close the full gap, because the gap may reflect asset quality, industry mix or measurement error as much as organizational form.

Sum of the parts analysis in practice is therefore a range, not a verdict. Analysts choose different peer sets, different multiples for different segments, different treatments of corporate costs, debt, pension obligations and tax attributes, and each choice moves the total. The same discount logic was applied to media combinations in discussions of the economics of media consolidation, and [the economics of media consolidation](/2026/10/05/why-media-companies-merge/) shows how peer selection and synergy assumptions can widen or narrow the apparent gap without any change in the underlying businesses. Used with discipline, the discount frames a question for a board. Whether focused ownership, clearer disclosure and separate capital allocation are likely to change the cash flows and the multiple investors apply to them enough to justify the costs, taxes and risks of breaking the combined company apart.

## The Separation Perimeter: Drawing the Line Around a Business

The separation perimeter is the line that determines which assets, liabilities, contracts, people, intellectual property and obligations move with the separated business and which remain with the parent. Drawing that line is the central operating task of a spin-off, because a business that looks distinct in a strategy presentation is rarely distinct in its factories, systems, leases or customer agreements. A perimeter must be complete enough that the new company can operate on the first day after distribution, and precise enough that creditors, regulators, employees and customers know which company stands behind each obligation. Every ambiguity left in the perimeter becomes a post separation dispute, a transition service or an unexpected cost for one of the two companies.

Shared sites create the first complications. Two businesses may use the same plant, warehouse, laboratory or office under one lease, with shared utilities, safety systems and maintenance staff. Separation requires a division of space, a lease assignment or sublease, capital spending to duplicate shared equipment and agreements for services that continue across the line. Mixed contracts create a second complication. A single purchasing agreement, distribution agreement or information technology contract may cover both businesses, sometimes with pricing that depends on combined volume. Those contracts must be split, renegotiated or replaced, and many contain change of control or assignment clauses that require counterparty consent before the separated business can rely on them after closing.

Brand ownership and product rights require the same precision. Trademarks may be shared across categories, licensed between the two companies or divided by product field, and quality control provisions matter when the same brand name continues on products sold by different owners. Kellogg Company supplied a clear worked example in its separation completed in 2023 into Kellanova and WK Kellogg Co. Kellogg Company separated its North American cereal business into WK Kellogg Co while the remaining company, renamed Kellanova, retained global snacking brands including Pringles and Cheez-It, along with international cereal and other businesses, according to company separation filings and announcements in 2023. The perimeter followed product category and geography rather than the legal history of the old company alone, and brand arrangements had to distinguish the Kellogg name in cereal in North America from its use in other categories and markets.

People and liabilities complete the perimeter. Employees must be assigned to the company that will employ them after separation, with payroll, equity awards, pensions and retiree obligations allocated under an employee matters agreement. Environmental liabilities, product litigation, guarantees and tax obligations that arose before separation must be assigned even when the underlying activity has ended, and indemnities determine which company bears a loss if an old claim matures. Debt follows the same exercise. Lenders may require repayment or consent when collateral or cash flow moves, and rating agencies assess each company on the assets and earnings inside its own perimeter rather than on the combined record.

A well drawn perimeter therefore does more than list assets. The perimeter tests whether the strategic case for focus survives contact with shared property, shared promises and shared history. When too much remains shared for too long, independence is nominal and transition agreements preserve the old interdependence under a new legal form. When the line is drawn cleanly, each company begins with the resources and obligations that match its stated business, and investors can judge performance without guessing which side of the old company produced a result or absorbed a cost.

## The Four Routes Apart: Spin-Off, Split-Off, Carve-Out and Sale

Boards that decide a business should leave a combined company still must choose the route, because each route distributes value, cash, tax cost and control differently. A prose comparison settles which route delivers shares without cash, which route brings cash to the parent, which route lets shareholders choose their exposure and which route transfers the business to a new owner outright. The table below settles those distinctions in one place, by route, consideration, cash effect, tax treatment and board rationale.

| Route | What shareholders receive | Cash to the parent | United States tax treatment | When boards choose it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pro rata spin-off | Shares in the separated company in proportion to existing holdings | None from the distribution itself | Generally tax free when Internal Revenue Code Section 355 conditions are met | When the board wants separation without forcing a shareholder choice or a sale |
| Split-off exchange offer | Separated company shares exchanged for parent shares that are retired | None from the exchange itself | Generally tax free when Internal Revenue Code Section 355 conditions are met | When the board wants shareholders to choose exposure and wants to reduce parent share count |
| Equity carve-out | No automatic distribution and public investors buy a minority stake in a subsidiary | Proceeds from the offering of the carved out stake | Offering proceeds are generally not taxed as a Section 355 distribution because shares are sold | When the board wants price discovery and cash while retaining control |
| Outright sale | No shares in the business and value delivered through parent use of proceeds | Full purchase price paid by the buyer | Parent generally recognizes taxable gain on the sale | When a buyer values the business most highly and the board prefers a clean exit |
| Liquidation distribution | A share of remaining assets after debts and obligations are settled | Proceeds from asset sales before distribution | Tax treatment depends on the liquidation provisions and shareholder level rules | When no viable going concern buyer or listing path exists |

Johnson and Johnson and Kenvue illustrate the split-off route in 2023. Johnson and Johnson separated Kenvue, its consumer health business, first through an initial public offering and then through an exchange offer in 2023 in which shareholders could exchange Johnson and Johnson shares for Kenvue shares, according to Johnson and Johnson separation announcements in 2023. The initial listing was a carve-out because shares were sold while the parent retained control, and the later exchange was a split-off because parent shares were retired as shareholders elected to hold the separated business instead.

A sale offers the sharpest contrast. A buyer negotiates a price, diligence tests the perimeter, and the parent receives cash, but gives up future upside and generally bears tax on the gain. In a spin-off, shareholders keep exposure to both companies and no buyer premium is collected. A split-off lets each shareholder decide the mix of exposure, at the cost of a more complex offer. A carve-out tests market value before full separation, but partial ownership can preserve conflicts and delay independence. The board choice turns on the problem being solved, because a route that maximizes cash differs from a route that maximizes shareholder choice, and a route that qualifies under Section 355 differs from a route that accepts tax for a buyer valuation.

## The Board Decision and the Strategic Review Behind It

A board that approves a spin-off does not create a new business on the day of the vote, and it does not create value on the day the distribution closes. The assets, employees, contracts and customers exist the day before in the same form, and the combined balance sheet holds the same earning power in total. What the board removes is a structure that forced unlike businesses to share one capital budget, one incentive plan and one account of performance, and the removal matters only if the separated companies then allocate capital and accept accountability in ways the combined company could not. That distinction shapes the fiduciary framing. Under the Delaware General Corporation Law and the fiduciary principles applied by the Delaware Court of Chancery, directors must inform themselves of material facts, act in good faith and place the interests of the corporation and its shareholders ahead of the interests of any manager or adviser whose compensation depends on a transaction closing. A separation presented as automatic value creation fails that framing, because the board must instead identify the specific constraint inside the combined company, whether a high return division starved of investment, a low margin division sheltered by cross subsidy, or a management team graded on blended results that hide failure in each part.

The strategic review behind such a decision normally tests several structures against that diagnosis rather than starting with separation as an answer. Management and outside advisers model a retention of the combined company with changed incentives, a sale of a division for cash, a joint venture, a tracking structure and a full distribution, and the board compares after tax proceeds, execution risk, dis-synergies and the cost of building standalone functions. Investment banks engaged for fairness work value each part by discounted cash flow, by comparable public companies and by precedent transactions, and the Internal Revenue Service treatment and rating agency analysis enter the same record. In a careful process the fairness opinion addresses whether the terms are fair from a financial point of view, not whether separation is wise, and directors who treat the opinion as a substitute for judgment misunderstand its scope. The banker work also has a known conflict that the minutes should confront directly, because a bank paid a fee contingent on completion has an interest in completion, and Delaware decisions on adviser conflicts, including the Court of Chancery ruling in the Rural Metro dispute in 2014, show that undisclosed adviser incentives can undermine the record on which directors rely.

The dated history of one announced separation illustrates the form without settling the outcome. On November 4, 2026, CVS Health announced its intent to separate into two public companies, one holding Aetna, CVS Caremark, CVS Specialty, Oak Street Health and Signify Health, the other, CVS Pharmacy, holding about 9,000 pharmacies, mail pharmacy, MinuteClinic and the Health100 technology business, with completion targeted for the first half of 2028. An announcement of intent is not a distribution, and the interval between announcement and completion is where the board case is proved or lost, in separation agreements, debt allocation, pension funding, regulatory approvals and the assignment of shared contracts. If the two resulting companies later earn higher returns, the gain will belong to clearer capital choices, to management held to a single set of margins and growth rates, and to investors able to fund one thesis without funding the other. If those changes do not follow, the separation will have added duplicate boards, duplicate audits and duplicate public company costs to the same underlying operations, and no financial engineering in the distribution itself will offset that drag.

## Section 355 and the Conditions for a Tax-Free Distribution

### What makes a spin-off tax free in the United States?

A distribution qualifies under Section 355 of the Internal Revenue Code when the parent controls the subsidiary, distributes control, satisfies the device, business purpose, active business and continuity requirements, and avoids a planned 50 percent ownership change. Each condition must hold on the facts.

Section 355 of the Internal Revenue Code provides the narrow route by which a corporation may distribute stock of a controlled subsidiary to its shareholders without the distributing corporation or the recipients recognizing gain, and the statute treats that relief as conditional rather than automatic. The first condition is control immediately before the distribution, defined through Section 368(c) as ownership of stock possessing at least 80 percent of the total combined voting power and at least 80 percent of each class of nonvoting stock. The distributing corporation must then distribute stock constituting control, or distribute all stock held if retention is not pursuant to a plan having tax avoidance as a principal purpose, a retention rule that Treasury Regulations under Section 355 examine with close attention to the reason for any stake kept after separation. Control in this sense is a legal threshold, not a loose description of influence, and a parent that has diluted its holding below the statutory line before the distribution cannot repair the defect by contract language in the separation agreement.

The statute then imposes the device prohibition, the business purpose requirement, the active trade or business test and the continuity principles developed in Treasury Regulations and case law. A distribution used principally as a device for distributing earnings and profits fails, because Congress designed Section 355 for genuine corporate separations rather than for converting a dividend into a saleable stock distribution. The active business test requires that both the distributing corporation and the controlled corporation engage immediately after the distribution in an active trade or business conducted throughout the five year period ending on the distribution date, and that business must not have been acquired in a taxable transaction during that period. Acquisitions, expansions and internal restructurings therefore receive detailed review, because a business bought for cash may support commercial logic while failing the statutory history. Continuity requires that the historic shareholders retain a continuing interest in each corporation and that the historic businesses continue, principles that connect Section 355 to the broader reorganization doctrine in Treasury Regulations Section 1.368-1.

Satisfaction of those conditions explains why tax counsel structure the record long before shares move. Counsel trace five years of business history, dividend history, shareholder composition, negotiations with third parties and the corporate reasons for separation, and the distributing board adopts resolutions that state the business purpose in operational terms. The Internal Revenue Service may issue a private letter ruling on significant legal issues in a proposed distribution, but Revenue Procedure 2017-52 and later guidance limit the scope of rulings and leave factual questions, including device and business purpose in many cases, to opinions of counsel and to examination after closing. The consequence of failure is severe and clarifies the discipline. If Section 355 does not apply, the distributing corporation generally recognizes gain as if it had sold the controlled stock at fair market value under Section 311 or Section 355(c) principles, and shareholders may face dividend treatment under Section 301, outcomes described in the Internal Revenue Service regulations governing taxable distributions. Tax freedom is therefore not a label attached to separation, and it creates no operating value by itself. It only prevents the tax system from consuming, at the moment of separation, part of the value that later focus and accountability may or may not produce.

## The Business Purpose and Device Tests in Practice

The business purpose test requires a real and substantial non tax purpose germane to the business of the distributing corporation, the controlled corporation or the affiliated group, as Treasury Regulations Section 1.355-2(b) states the standard, and the purpose must motivate the distribution in whole or substantial part. Acceptable purposes in the regulations and in Internal Revenue Service ruling practice include resolving management conflict between business lines, improving access to capital for a business whose risk profile differs from the group, reducing regulatory friction, aligning employee incentives with a single business and permitting an acquisition currency tied to one operation. A purpose framed only as increasing shareholder value states a hoped for result rather than a corporate reason, and examiners test whether the stated result could have been achieved without separation and whether contemporaneous records support the claim. Board minutes written after advisers settle on separation carry less weight than capital requests denied under the combined budget, regulatory correspondence and compensation records that predate the transaction, because the doctrine asks why the corporation needed the distribution, not why bankers preferred it.

The device test in Treasury Regulations Section 1.355-2(d) asks whether the distribution was used principally as a device for distributing earnings and profits, and the regulations weigh device factors against non-device factors rather than applying a single mechanical rule. Device factors include a subsequent sale of stock of either corporation that was negotiated or agreed before the distribution, a high proportion of liquid or nonbusiness assets in either company, and a structure in which shareholders can extract corporate earnings by selling distributed stock while retaining the underlying business interest. Non-device factors include a corporate business purpose, distribution to a widely held public shareholder base with no controlling extraction motive, and stock that is not structured to permit a bailout of earnings. The Supreme Court and lower federal courts in the line of cases following Gregory v. Helvering in 1935 supplied the substance over form background for this inquiry, and Section 355 practice retains that suspicion of form without business substance. A distribution pro rata to all shareholders, with no arranged sale and a documented operating reason, presents a different device profile from a distribution followed promptly by a prearranged disposal of one side by the historic holders.

Ruling practice translates those doctrines into a factual record that companies assemble over months. The Internal Revenue Service, under Revenue Procedure 2017-52 and the annual no rule revenue procedures, will rule on many significant Section 355 issues but generally will not rule that a distribution is not a device or that a business purpose is satisfied, leaving those determinations for examination and for reasoned opinions of tax counsel. Counsel therefore request rulings on discrete legal questions, such as the treatment of a particular restructuring step, while the device and purpose analysis rests on representations about negotiations, asset composition, earnings and profits, shareholder intent and post distribution plans. Representations are not decorative, because a ruling relies on them and a material misstatement can void reliance. In practice the two tests also discipline strategy in a useful way that supports the central economic point. A board that must articulate a corporate purpose specific enough to survive Section 355 review must name the operating problem separation solves, whether capital competition, regulatory conflict or incentive distortion, and a board unable to name such a problem has weak grounds for expecting later gains. The tests do not certify that gains will arrive. They certify only that the separation has a corporate reason beyond tax saving and earnings extraction, leaving performance to prove the rest.

## Control, Continuity and the Plans That Can Break a Finished Spin

Control and continuity protect a completed spin-off only when the plans surrounding the distribution respect limits that extend beyond the closing date. Section 355(e) of the Internal Revenue Code, enacted in 1997 in response to transactions associated with the Morris Trust pattern, provides that a distribution is taxable to the distributing corporation if it is part of a plan or series of related transactions under which one or more persons acquire stock representing a 50 percent or greater interest in either the distributing corporation or the controlled corporation. The tax falls at the corporate level on the built in gain in the distributed stock, while shareholders generally retain nonrecognition treatment, an asymmetry stated in Section 355(e) and explained in Treasury Regulations Section 1.355-7. The provision changed separation planning because a transaction that satisfied every other Section 355 condition on its closing date could still fail in economic effect if an acquisition of half or more of either company had been arranged as part of the same plan.

The Morris Trust pattern takes its name from Commissioner v. Morris Trust, decided by the Fourth Circuit in 1966, in which a bank distributed an insurance business and the distributed corporation then combined with another insurer, and later transactions refined the pattern into a method for disposing of a business without corporate level tax. A parent would distribute an unwanted business to its shareholders and the distributed company would then merge into an acquirer, with historic shareholders retaining just over half of the combined enterprise, so that the form preserved continuity while the substance transferred the business. Section 355(e) answers that pattern with the 50 percent rule rather than with a ban on all post distribution combinations, and Treasury Regulations Section 1.355-7 supplies operating rules, plan factors and safe harbors for deciding whether an acquisition and a distribution belong to one plan. Acquisitions within two years before or two years after a distribution fall inside the statutory window that triggers close analysis, and the regulations examine agreements, understandings, arrangements and substantial negotiations during defined periods to locate the plan boundary.

Continuity doctrine adds a separate constraint drawn from the reorganization provisions and Treasury Regulations Section 1.355-2(c), which require that the distribution not be part of a plan in which historic shareholders surrender their continuing interest in the separated businesses. Continuity of business enterprise requires that each corporation continue its historic business or use a significant portion of its historic assets, and continuity of interest requires that the historic owners retain a meaningful stake, principles that prevent Section 355 from sheltering a disguised sale. In practice separation teams map every approach from a potential acquirer, every banker conversation and every strategic alternative considered during the two year periods on both sides of closing, and information barriers and board protocols govern contact that might create plan evidence. A later acquisition is not automatically fatal, because an unplanned combination arising from independent business developments after separation can fall outside Section 355(e), but the burden of showing independence rests on records created near the events rather than on recollection. The lesson for valuation is direct. A spin-off that depends for its claimed success on an immediate sale of one part invites Section 355(e) risk and concedes that separation alone produced nothing, since the asserted gain comes from a change of ownership rather than from focus inside the separated companies. Durable gains, if any, must survive the two year window and arise from operations that remain owned, managed and measured on their own account.

## Building the Standalone Company: Functions It Never Needed Before

A division inside a combined company consumes functions it does not supply for itself, and separation exposes that dependence in the first budget the standalone company prepares. Treasury is the clearest example. Inside a parent, cash pools centrally, debt issues at the parent level, bank revolvers cover seasonal needs across divisions and foreign exchange and interest rate exposures net against each other, while a standalone company must set its own target capital structure, obtain its own credit rating, negotiate its own revolving facility and decide how much cash to hold against a downturn in a single business. Rating agencies including Moody's Ratings and S&P Global Ratings assess that structure on separated history and projected cash flow, and parent debt allocation often determines which company begins with investment capacity and which begins with constraint. None of that work creates revenue. It creates the conditions under which capital allocation becomes visible, because a treasurer serving one business can no longer hide a weak return behind the borrowing capacity of a stronger sibling, and a board can compare debt cost with the return on each project the company funds.

Tax, audit and control functions expand in the same way and for the same reason. A division that filed as part of a consolidated United States federal return must build standalone provision processes, manage state and foreign filings, maintain transfer pricing for transactions that continue with the former parent and operate under a tax matters agreement that allocates pre separation liabilities and the benefit of attributes such as net operating losses. Sarbanes-Oxley Act requirements, applied through Securities and Exchange Commission rules for public companies, require management assessment of internal control over financial reporting and, for larger filers, auditor attestation, and a separated company must document controls that the parent control environment previously supplied. The audit committee of the new board must retain independent auditors, oversee whistleblower procedures and approve non audit services, duties described in Securities and Exchange Commission Regulation S-X and exchange listing standards from the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq. Carve-out financial statements give those duties a history, prepared under Securities and Exchange Commission Staff Accounting Bulletin Topic 1.B and Regulation S-X allocation principles, with expenses assigned on reasonable methods, since allocations of parent overhead, interest and taxes shape the apparent profitability of the separated business.

Investor relations and the remaining corporate center complete the build and test whether separation clarifies accountability or merely duplicates cost. A standalone company must produce its own earnings releases, filings on Forms 10-K, 10-Q and 8-K, proxy statements, earnings calls and guidance, and it must explain a strategy that a segment footnote never had to defend. The Form 10 registration statement filed for many distributions, reviewed under Securities and Exchange Commission practice for spin-off information statements, contains the audited carve-out history, risk factors, management discussion and the separation agreements that define the new company for investors. Executive leadership changes in substance rather than title, because officers who once recommended projects into a parent capital process now own outcomes against public targets, and compensation committees can tie pay to one company's return on invested capital and cash conversion instead of blended group measures. The build has a measurable price in duplicate listing fees, audit fees, insurance, systems separation and stranded parent overhead, costs that separation filings disclose in pro forma adjustments and that the combined company did not bear twice. A spin-off therefore begins with a cost headwind and no automatic benefit, and it earns its justification only when sharper treasury discipline, cleaner tax and control ownership and direct investor scrutiny produce decisions the shared structure had suppressed.

## Dividing Shared Assets, Property and Intellectual Property

The division of shared assets begins with an asset register that rarely matches the way managers described the combined business. Separation agreements and their schedules allocate machinery, vehicles, inventory, contracts, permits, data and working capital item by item or by business use, and disputes arise where a plant, laboratory or distribution center served both sides. Counsel and accountants classify assets by primary use, by location, by title and by the business that will maintain them, and assets that cannot be divided physically require shared services, leases, easements or joint ownership arrangements with defined exit rights. Securities and Exchange Commission filings for major separations describe those arrangements because investors need to know whether a supposedly independent company depends on its former parent for a plant, a warehouse network or a critical system. Real estate splits carry the longest tail, since leases must be assigned or subdivided with landlord consent, owned sites may need environmental allocation, and guarantees given by the parent for a separated subsidiary lease must be released or replaced. In corporate separations the operative record is the separation agreement, together with deeds, lease amendments and indemnities assigning responsibility for contamination, deferred maintenance and code compliance.

Intellectual property requires a finer division than physical assets because a patent, trademark or software platform can serve both companies while legal title can sit in only one place or in a jointly structured holding arrangement. Patent assignment records filed with the United States Patent and Trademark Office establish chain of title for transferred patents, and trademark assignments require care to transfer associated goodwill, a requirement in Section 10 of the Lanham Act, because a trademark assigned without its goodwill can be invalid. Separation teams therefore audit registrations country by country, identify unregistered marks, trade secrets, domain names and copyrighted material, and decide whether each item transfers, remains and is licensed back, or is licensed in both directions for defined fields of use. Brand licences set royalty terms or royalty free terms, quality control obligations, territory, duration and termination, and trademark law requires the licensor to control the nature and quality of goods sold under the mark or risk weakening the right. A familiar name shared across two public companies creates a transitional problem, since customers must learn which company stands behind a product while each company needs freedom to change quality, price and strategy without injuring a mark the other still uses.

The Kellogg separation in 2023 supplies a compact illustration of brand allocation rather than a template. Kellogg Company completed the separation of its North American cereal business as WK Kellogg Co in October 2023 and the remaining global snacking and international cereal company later adopted the name Kellanova, as Kellogg Company and Kellanova disclosed in Securities and Exchange Commission filings and separation releases. The cereal names, snack names and shared heritage marks required allocation and licence arrangements so that each company could sell established products without implying that the other made them, and the filings treated brand architecture as part of the separation terms. That treatment reflects the wider principle that closes the asset division and returns to the central claim. Dividing property and intellectual property creates no new patent, no new plant and no new customer loyalty on the closing date, and a poorly drafted licence can subtract value by restricting both sides. The division only removes the ambiguity that let a combined company postpone choices about which business owned an asset, funded its upkeep and answered for its return, and any later gain must come from owners who finally carry those specific costs and rights on a single balance sheet.

## Contracts That Span the Line: Consents, Novations and Stranded Agreements

A spin-off does not move a contract by operation of a press release or a board resolution alone, and the legal separation paper must trace every material agreement to the business that will need it after the distribution. The combined company signed leases, supply arrangements, distribution agreements, licenses, credit facilities and customer contracts as a single legal person or through subsidiaries whose ownership changes in the separation, and each document carries its own language on assignment, change of control and consent. The separation and distribution agreement described in filings with the Securities and Exchange Commission typically assigns contracts primarily related to the spun business to the spun company and retains contracts primarily related to the remaining business with the parent, while shared or mixed use contracts require a different treatment, often a partial assignment, a replacement agreement or a transition service. The thesis that a spin-off removes a structure hiding value becomes practical at this point, because a customer or supplier that can block the transfer holds leverage over timing and terms, and value that looked consolidated on an organization chart may depend on permissions that do not transfer automatically.

Change of control clauses create the sharpest friction because drafting varies by contract and by the structure chosen for the separation. Consent requests therefore run party by party, and Securities and Exchange Commission disclosure in a Form 10 information statement customarily lists material consents that remain outstanding and describes the consequence if a consent is not obtained. Novation goes further than assignment because a novation replaces the original contracting party with a new party and releases the original party, whereas an assignment in many contract settings transfers rights and delegates duties without fully releasing the assignor unless the counterparty agrees. A parent guarantee can outlive the separation for the same reason, and lenders or landlords often require a replacement guarantee, a letter of credit or evidence of standalone credit strength before releasing the stronger combined balance sheet that supported the original bargain.

Customer and supplier contracts add commercial risk to the legal analysis. Government contracts follow a stricter path. Federal Acquisition Regulation Section 42.1204 provides a novation process for United States government contracts when assets are transferred, requiring documentation that the transferee can perform and that the transfer serves the government interest, and a contracting officer decides whether to recognize the successor. Stranded agreements are the residue of an imperfect map, contracts that benefit the spun business but remain legally with the parent, or the reverse, because consent failed, the document was missed or the parties chose delay. The separation documents handle that residue through agency style pass through arrangements, subcontracting, economic benefit transfers and indemnities, each of which keeps operations running while preserving the legal gap. Those workarounds prove the central point. A distribution can redraw ownership in a single closing, but contracts reveal where the combined company created convenience and where it created dependence, and only patient consent work, replacement paper and disciplined allocation turn two share registers into two companies that can actually buy, sell, lease and deliver on their own signatures.

## Allocating the Debt: Who Owes What After the Split

Debt allocation decides how much financial room each company receives on the first morning of separate life, and the choice exposes whether management expects focus alone to carry the separation or whether the balance sheet must also be rebuilt for independent decisions. The parent begins with consolidated borrowings supported by combined cash flow, combined assets and, in many cases, guarantees that run across subsidiaries in both future businesses. The separation plan assigns existing notes to one side or the other, moves borrowings attached to particular subsidiaries with those subsidiaries, refinances instruments that cannot move and raises new debt where the target structure requires it. A spin-off creates nothing in that exercise. It divides claims on cash flow that already existed, and any gain must come later from managers choosing investment, repayment and acquisition with a clearer view of the return each dollar of borrowing must support.

Push down is the most direct technique, where debt sits at or moves to the company whose assets and cash flow will service it. A subsidiary that will be spun may already be the borrower under a term loan or the issuer of notes, and that debt travels with the subsidiary when its shares are distributed. Where the parent is the borrower, documents may permit assignment to the spun company with lender consent, or the parent may refinance and the spun company may borrow anew. New issuance by the spun company shortly before distribution, followed by a cash dividend or a transfer of proceeds back to the parent, is a second common pattern described in Securities and Exchange Commission separation filings. The mechanics matter because the dividend back to the parent is not operating profit and not new wealth for shareholders as a group. It is a repositioning of borrowed cash inside a perimeter that is about to divide, valuable only if the resulting structures let each board fund high return projects without subsidizing the other side by habit.

Refinancing maturing notes forms the third leg of the negotiation, because maturities that looked comfortable inside a diversified group can cluster awkwardly once cash flows separate. Treasury teams map each maturity against the cash generation and asset life of the company that will owe it, tender for or redeem notes that carry change of control puts, and replace bank facilities that name borrowers or guarantors on both sides of the line. Comparisons with loss-absorbing instruments clarify the purpose of each layer, since the mechanics of AT1 bank capital show what a capital structure is designed to do when losses arrive, and [the mechanics of AT1 bank capital](/2026/10/16/how-at1-bonds-work/) illustrate how subordination, discretion and conversion terms allocate stress before any operating decision is taken. Spin-off debt serves a different purpose, funding ordinary operations and setting incentives rather than absorbing bank losses, but the design question is identical. Each instrument must name who bears risk, who receives cash first and who retains flexibility when earnings fall, and a structure that answers those questions cleanly gives accountability a financial form that the combined company, borrowing for an averaged enterprise, could not produce.

## Credit Ratings, Lenders and the Capital Structure Negotiation

Credit ratings and lender consents translate the separation plan into a price for borrowing, and that translation often disciplines the entire transaction more firmly than equity market commentary. S and P Global Ratings, Moody's Investors Service and Fitch Ratings each publish criteria for rating a spun entity on a standalone basis, and each agency reviews pro forma financial statements, the separation agreements, forecast cash flow, liquidity sources, management plans and the legal distance between the two companies. Analysts test whether the parent contribution of cash, the spun company dividend back to the parent, assumed pensions, lease obligations and intercompany balances leave either company with leverage outside the range for a target rating. Losing an investment grade threshold, defined in market practice and in many fund mandates by a rating at or above BBB minus from S and P Global Ratings and Fitch Ratings or Baa three from Moody's Investors Service, can raise funding costs and narrow the buyer base for new notes in a way that operating managers feel for years.

The negotiation is triangular. The parent wants to retain a rating consistent with its own strategy, often protecting an investment grade profile that supports commercial paper access under programs described in Securities and Exchange Commission filings. The spun company wants enough debt capacity to fund capital expenditure and working capital without returning to equity markets on weak terms, while lenders want covenants, collateral or guarantees that reflect the smaller perimeter that will remain after closing. Each side can trade cash for debt, debt for assets and guarantees for pricing, and the floor for the negotiation is set by cash flow under stress rather than by a headline leverage multiple in a presentation. Rating agency pro forma review makes hidden cross subsidies visible because analysts allocate corporate overhead, pension cost, tax sharing payments and transition service charges to the company that will bear them after separation. A business that looked profitable inside the group may show thin coverage once standalone costs are charged in full, and a business that looked marginal may show strong coverage once parent overhead is removed. That revelation is the mechanism by which a spin-off can help. It does not manufacture coverage. It forces a reckoning with which operation generates the cash that services the debt, and it assigns the consequence of a shortfall to a named board and a named treasury team.

Lender consent work runs in parallel and follows the documents rather than the rating letters. Bank credit agreements typically restrict assignment, mergers, asset transfers and changes in guarantors, and a spin-off can trigger mandatory prepayment, a consent solicitation or a full refinancing depending on drafting that the Securities and Exchange Commission requires issuers to summarize for material agreements. Covenant headroom after closing matters as much as the opening rating, because a structure with no room for a weak quarter pushes management toward short term cash preservation and away from the patient capital allocation that the separation was meant to enable. Capital structure negotiation therefore sets the boundary conditions for focus. A spun company loaded with debt to maximize a pre closing dividend to the parent may begin life with impressive separation proceeds and impaired judgment, while a parent stripped of cash to flatter the spun company rating may lose the capacity to invest in its remaining core. The durable gain, when one appears, comes from a structure that both lenders and boards can defend under stress, where accountability for leverage sits with the managers who chose it and where refinancing risk is spread across maturities that match the assets each company owns.

## The Form 10 and the Information Statement: Listing Without an Offering

A pro rata spin-off lists a new public company without selling shares to the public, and the Form 10 registration process supplies the disclosure that an offering prospectus would otherwise provide. The spun company files a registration statement on Form 10 with the Securities and Exchange Commission under the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, and the filing includes audited financial statements, a description of the business, risk factors, management information, pro forma financial information and the material separation agreements. The Securities and Exchange Commission staff may review the filing and issue comments, and the registration becomes effective after the review process and the satisfaction of listing and distribution conditions described in the filing itself. No underwriter markets the shares, no roadshow builds a book of demand and no offering price is set, because existing shareholders receive the new shares by virtue of shares already held.

### Why does a spin-off not need a shareholder vote?

A pro rata spin-off distributes shares already owned through the parent to existing holders in proportion to their holdings, without selling new shares or changing the underlying assets owned collectively. Under Delaware corporate law and the rules described by the Securities and Exchange Commission, that distribution functions as a dividend of property, not a merger or sale requiring approval.

The distinction between registration and approval shapes the governance of the transaction. Parent directors approve the separation under state corporate law, with the Delaware General Corporation Law Section 170 and Section 173 governing dividends and the lawful funds and property available for distribution for a Delaware corporation, and shareholders receive disclosure rather than a proxy solicitation seeking consent. Because a pro rata distribution keeps each holder in the same proportionate economic position across two companies, corporate law treats the act as a board managed disposition of property rather than a fundamental change negotiated against a third party. Securities law still imposes full disclosure and antifraud liability, and the Securities and Exchange Commission can delay effectiveness while comments remain unresolved, so absence of a vote does not mean absence of scrutiny. Litigation over fiduciary duty, disclosure adequacy and conflicts in the allocation of assets, debt and management roles remains available in state and federal court, and the information statement is written with that audience in view.

Exchange listing completes the public birth of the spun company. The New York Stock Exchange Listed Company Manual and the Nasdaq Stock Market Listing Rules each set quantitative and governance standards for listed securities, including distribution and financial criteria that a spun company must satisfy through the shares its parent holders will receive. Listing approval depends on the Form 10 becoming effective and on the exchange accepting that a public market can form in a security distributed widely to existing holders. Readers must therefore treat the history with care. Carve out profit is an accounting construction, not proof that the business earned that result as an independent company with its own treasury, board and overhead. That caution supports the central thesis. Listing creates a price, a filing record and a management team that can be judged in public, and those disciplines can expose value that consolidation obscured, but the Form 10 itself adds no plant, no patent and no customer. It removes an informational veil, and later operating and capital decisions determine whether anything durable stands behind the newly listed name.

## When-Issued Trading and the Mechanics of the Distribution Date

The distribution calendar turns a legal separation into tradable pieces, and each date in that calendar allocates risk between buyers and sellers who may not yet hold the shares being traded. The parent board sets a record date, and holders of parent shares on that date become entitled to receive spun company shares in the distribution, a sequence that the information statement in the Form 10 filing describes together with the distribution date, the ratio and the expected timing of delivery. Between announcement and closing, the spun company has financial disclosure and a proposed listing but no settled public float, so exchanges create a when-issued market for the new shares. The New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq publish notices for when-issued and ex-distribution trading in spin-offs, setting the dates on which parent shares trade with and without the right to receive the distribution and the dates on which the new shares trade on a when-issued basis.

Due bills carry the entitlement through trades in the parent shares during the window when settlement and entitlement diverge. A due bill is an instrument attached to a trade, recognized in Financial Industry Regulatory Authority rules for uniform practice governing distributions, stating that the buyer will receive the distribution even though the share trade settles around the record and distribution dates in a sequence that would otherwise create confusion. Ex-distribution trading in the parent then begins at the point set by the exchange, after which a buyer of parent shares no longer acquires the right to the spun shares, and the parent share price adjusts mechanically to reflect a business perimeter that no longer includes the distributed operation. Regular way settlement for the new shares follows the distribution, under the standard settlement cycle that the Securities and Exchange Commission Rule 15c6 dash 1 sets for broker dealer transactions in the United States, and book entry positions appear through the Depository Trust Company for eligible securities held in brokerage accounts.

The Johnson and Johnson separation of Kenvue in the year 2023 illustrates a neighboring mechanism rather than the pro rata pattern, because Johnson and Johnson used an exchange offer split-off for much of Kenvue after an initial public offering of a minority stake, a structure described in Johnson and Johnson Securities and Exchange Commission filings on the Kenvue separation. In an exchange offer split-off, tendering parent shareholders swap parent shares for spun company shares at a stated discount or ratio, the parent retires shares received in the exchange and ownership of the spun company shifts toward holders who elected the exchange, unlike a pro rata spin where every holder receives the new shares in proportion to holdings. The comparison clarifies why mechanics matter for accountability. A pro rata distribution leaves the shareholder register mirrored across both companies at closing, and divergence in ownership comes afterward through trading, while an exchange offer sorts holders at the moment of separation and changes share count for the parent. Neither mechanism creates operating value on the closing date. When-issued prices can be volatile because float, index treatment and forced selling by mandate constrained holders affect early trading, a risk that information statements disclose in their discussion of the market for the new shares. The durable question begins after settlement ends, when two shareholder bases, two boards and two capital plans replace a single register that could tolerate internal cross subsidy without a market signal naming the side that bore the cost.

## What Shareholders Receive and How Cost Basis Is Divided

A shareholder in a pro rata spin-off wakes after the distribution with two positions where one position stood before, holding parent shares that represent a smaller company and spun company shares delivered at the stated distribution ratio. The ratio may be one spun share for each parent share, one for every two parent shares or any other proportion the parent board sets and the information statement discloses, and the delivery runs through book entry credit for shares held at brokers or through the transfer agent for registered holders, in each case without payment by the receiving holder. Nothing in the delivery enriches the holder at the moment of receipt when the distribution qualifies for tax free treatment. The holder owns claims on the same underlying operations, now housed in two corporations, with two tickers, two boards and two sets of financial statements that will let future results be traced to the managers who produced them rather than blended into a consolidated total.

### How is cost basis split after a spin-off?

Cost basis is allocated between parent shares and spun company shares in proportion to their relative fair market values on the distribution date, under Internal Revenue Code Section 358 and Internal Revenue Service guidance. A holder keeps the same total basis held before the distribution, divided across the two positions according to value.

The allocation method preserves total basis while changing the basis attached to each security, and the parent statement under Internal Revenue Service Form 8937 reporting practice typically expresses the result as percentages derived from trading values around the distribution. A holder who paid a given amount for parent shares before the spin retains that aggregate basis afterward, split between the retained parent shares and the new spun shares by relative value, so a later sale of either position measures gain or loss against only the portion assigned to the shares sold. Fractional shares add a small cash element to an otherwise share based distribution. Where the ratio applied to a holding produces a fraction, the transfer agent typically aggregates fractional entitlements, sells the resulting whole shares in the market and pays cash in lieu of the fraction to each affected holder, a procedure the information statement describes in its distribution mechanics section. That cash can carry tax consequences distinct from the share distribution itself, because the holder is treated in Internal Revenue Service guidance on fractional share cash payments as having received and then sold the fractional interest, with gain or loss measured against the basis allocated to that fraction.

Basis records therefore carry a governance lesson as well as a tax function. A precise allocation forces every holder, broker and transfer agent to state what portion of an old investment now rides on each management team, ending the ambiguity of a single basis figure attached to a combined enterprise whose parts earned at different rates. Later sales reveal gains and losses company by company, and that clarity, rather than the distribution itself, is what allows capital to move toward the better allocator and away from the weaker one over time, through decisions made by thousands of holders acting on separated information rather than through any value created in the closing entries.

## Employees, Pensions and Benefit Plans Across the Line

People move across the separation line under documents that receive less public attention than the distribution ratio, yet those documents decide whether the focus promised by a spin-off survives contact with payroll, pensions and incentives. The employee matters agreement, filed as an exhibit to the Form 10 registration statement with the Securities and Exchange Commission in material separations, allocates employees to the company whose business they serve, sets the treatment of service credit, governs transfers that occur at or after closing and assigns responsibility for compensation, severance, retention and benefit obligations between parent and spun company. A spin-off that announces strategic clarity while mishandling the people who operate the assets destroys trust inside both companies at the moment each board most needs managers to accept new accountability.

Pension and retirement plan splits require actuarial precision because a promise made under the combined company must land with a sponsor able to fund it. Defined benefit assets and liabilities move according to the employee matters agreement and the plan spinoff or merger documents that plan administrators prepare under the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974, and the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation framework governs premiums, reportable events and termination insurance for covered single employer plans in the United States. Internal Revenue Code Section 414 subsection l sets conditions for a transfer of assets and liabilities between qualified plans, requiring that each participant benefit after the transfer be no less than the benefit immediately before the transfer on a termination basis, a standard the Internal Revenue Service applies in plan qualification review. Health and welfare benefits, retiree medical obligations and deferred compensation each require a named owner after closing, because an obligation left ambiguous between two former affiliates becomes a dispute that employees experience as a broken promise rather than as a drafting oversight.

Equity award conversion translates the separation into personal incentives, and the method chosen signals whether boards intend managers to profit from combined performance that no longer exists or from the results of the company each manager will actually run. Outstanding options, restricted stock units and performance shares of the parent must be adjusted or converted at closing, and Securities and Exchange Commission filings for material spin-offs describe the conversion approach in the compensation and employee matters disclosure. One approach converts a parent award into an award over shares of the employer company after closing, preserving intrinsic value at conversion through an adjustment to share count and exercise price based on relative share values. Another approach divides an award into two awards, one over parent shares and one over spun company shares, keeping the holder exposed to both companies in a manner closer to the position of a continuing shareholder. The choice matters for the thesis of the separation. Accountability sharpens when a plant manager, a divisional president and a chief executive hold awards whose value depends mainly on the cash flow and execution of the business they control, with vesting and performance periods long enough to punish short term cash extraction. A spin-off cannot create that discipline by distributing shares to outside holders while leaving insiders paid on a vanished whole. When employee allocation, pension funding and award conversion align with the new corporate boundary, the separation reaches the level where value was either hidden or consumed, inside daily decisions about hiring, investment and effort, and only then can focus stop being a presentation theme and become a measurable operating fact.

## The Complication: When Separation Destroys the Value It Claims to Release

The strongest case against a spin-off does not deny that a combined company can hide a good business. Shared purchasing gives scale that a smaller company cannot reproduce alone. Shared research, distribution, treasury functions and a shared head office spread fixed costs across revenue that no single division carries by itself. The released business steps into duplicated cost, lost discounts and thinner bargaining power. Deloitte, in its published separation guidance, describes these losses as dis-synergies, the mirror image of the synergies used to justify mergers, and warns that they arrive on a fixed schedule while the promised benefits of focus arrive only if management delivers them.

A separated company needs its own board, audit, listing, investor relations function, insurance program, treasury and tax departments and executive leadership. The parent keeps most of its own versions of the same functions, so total cost across the two companies rises even where each trims carefully. A parent that built a headquarters, a data center or a finance team for a larger group cannot shrink those commitments at the speed of the revenue that leaves. Deloitte, in its separation guidance, treats stranded cost removal as a separate program with its own timeline, because contracts, leases and employment terms outlast the closing date. Until that program succeeds, the parent reports lower earnings from a smaller base, and the separated company bears replacement services bought at market prices.

Reversals give the opposing case its evidence, because a separation that ends in recombination suggests that the split misjudged where value lay. The history of AT&T and WarnerMedia, as recorded in filings with the Securities and Exchange Commission, illustrates the difficulty without settling it. AT&T completed its acquisition of Time Warner in 2018, a transaction the Department of Justice challenged in court proceedings recorded at the time, and then reversed direction within four years. In 2022 AT&T combined WarnerMedia with Discovery through a subsidiary structure, placing the media assets into Warner Bros. Discovery, a separate listed company in which AT&T shareholders held a majority stake at closing, according to the closing announcement issued by AT&T in April 2022. Warner Bros. Discovery then carried substantial debt from the transaction, and its later annual reports filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission recorded impairment charges against network assets and extensive cost reduction programs. Stated without any verdict drawn from share prices, those facts show a large owner concluding that one structure was wrong twice in succession.

The retrospective coherence problem explains why such cases teach less than they appear to teach. After a separation fails, the story is rewritten so that failure looks inevitable, and every earlier warning becomes the explanation. After a separation succeeds, the same rewriting makes success look inevitable and erases the execution choices that produced it. Narrative tidies what evidence leaves messy, because the counterfactual, the same businesses kept together under the same managers in the same markets, never files a report. A spin-off still creates no value on the day it closes, and it still removes a structure that can hide performance, capital needs and managerial accountability. Where dis-synergies, stranded costs and duplicated public company costs exceed the gains from focus, capital allocation and accountability, separation destroys the value it claims to release, and the thesis describes a possibility that management must earn rather than a result that the transaction delivers.

## The Separation Agreement and the Web of Ancillary Contracts

A spin-off closes on paper before it closes in fact, and the paper is a web of contracts that functions as the constitution of the split. The central document, described in the Form 10 registration statements that separated companies file with the Securities and Exchange Commission, is the master separation agreement, sometimes titled a separation and distribution agreement. It identifies the assets and liabilities that move to the separated company, the assets and liabilities that stay with the parent, and the steps each party must complete before, at and after the distribution. It also sets the conditions that allow either party to stop the transaction, the representations each party makes about its business, and the covenants governing conduct between signing and closing.

Assets used mainly by the separated business move with it, assets used mainly by the retained business stay, and shared assets require a stated rule, a division, a licence or a continuing service arrangement. Liabilities follow a similar logic, tied where possible to the business that generated them, with unallocated liabilities assigned by formula or by specific negotiation recorded in schedules. The agreement covers employee transfers and the division of benefit plans, the treatment of intercompany balances that must be settled or converted into third party style debt, and the capitalization of the separated company at closing. In Form 10 filings with the Securities and Exchange Commission, companies summarize these provisions at length because the separated company has no operating history as an independent issuer, and the disclosure substitutes for a track record that investors cannot inspect elsewhere.

Around the master agreement sits a set of ancillary contracts, each governing a relationship that separation creates between parties that were once departments of one firm. An intellectual property licence allows one company to keep using patents, trademarks, software or brand names owned by the other, on stated terms covering scope, territory, duration, quality control and payment. A real estate matters agreement, or a set of leases and subleases attached to the separation documents, assigns sites, divides shared premises and sets rent and exit rights for facilities that both companies occupy after closing. An employee matters agreement allocates pension obligations, equity awards and retention commitments. A tax matters agreement and a transition services agreement, each treated separately in standard separation structures described in Securities and Exchange Commission filings, govern taxes and temporary services. Commercial supply and distribution agreements may also be required where the two businesses keep trading with each other as customers, converting internal transfers into priced contracts that auditors and tax authorities can examine.

Boards change, chief executives leave and strategies are revised, but the allocation of a legacy liability or a patent licence made at separation continues to bind both companies until its term expires or a court or renegotiation changes it. Drafting therefore carries a burden that ordinary commercial contracts do not, because the parties are dividing a shared past as well as a shared future. A liability that neither side listed, a site that both sides assumed the other had taken, or a trademark used by both sides under one registration can surface years after closing, and the answer comes from the schedules and definitions written before closing. Separation, in legal terms, is less an event than a settlement, recorded in advance, of every argument the two companies might later have about what each of them owns, owes and may use.

## Transition Services: Renting the Old Parent After Closing

On the first morning of independence, a separated company often cannot run itself. Its payroll may still sit on parent systems, its invoices may still move through parent software, its warehouses may still use parent logistics, and its employees may still enter buildings maintained by the parent. Transition services agreements solve that gap by allowing the separated company to rent the old parent after closing, buying defined services for a defined period at a defined price. The structure, described in transition services agreements filed as exhibits to Securities and Exchange Commission registration statements, turns an abrupt operational break into a staged withdrawal. Services commonly cover information technology, finance and accounting, human resources and payroll, procurement, facilities, legal support and supply chain functions. Each service is listed with a scope, a service level drawn from the standard the parent provided internally, a fee and a termination date, so that dependence is documented rather than assumed.

Transition services are commonly priced at cost or at cost plus a stated margin, a convention described in separation disclosures filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission, and the choice between those bases matters. Pricing at cost treats the arrangement as a temporary continuation of shared services, with the parent recovering expense without profit. Pricing at cost plus compensates the parent for management attention, systems capacity and risk, and gives the parent a reason to maintain quality for a customer that is also a former division. Fees also tend to rise when a service runs past its scheduled end, a design that discourages indefinite dependence.

Individual services end at different points, often within months for routine functions and over longer periods for complex systems such as enterprise software, manufacturing support or regulated record keeping, according to the schedules summarized in Securities and Exchange Commission filings. Each exit requires a replacement, either built inside the separated company, bought from an outside vendor or obtained through a new outsourcing contract, and each replacement requires testing, data migration and staff training before the old service can stop safely. Termination rights usually allow early exit once a replacement works, and extension rights usually require notice and agreement rather than automatic renewal. A parent that wants a clean break cannot withdraw a service overnight without breaching the agreement, and a separated company that misses its exit dates cannot demand indefinite support on the original terms. The schedule thus functions as a visible commitment about how long independence remains partial.

While transition services run, the parent retains people, systems and facilities sized for a larger group, and the fees it receives offset part of that retained cost. When the services end, the offset ends, but the retained cost may not fall at the same pace, leaving the parent with capacity built for work that no longer exists. Deloitte, in its published guidance on separation and transition services, identifies this timing mismatch as a central separation risk and treats stranded cost reduction as work that must begin before closing rather than after the last service expires. Replacement systems often cost more and take longer than the separation plan assumed, because internal services were never priced as products and their full complexity becomes visible only when a buyer must reproduce them. The combined company's apparent efficiency rested partly on shared services whose cost was buried, and separation forces that cost into contracts where it can be counted, compared and eventually removed or rebuilt.

## Tax Matters and Indemnities: Who Pays When the Past Is Reopened

Tax follows a separated group into independence because the years before separation were filed as one economic unit, and tax authorities examine history without regard to later corporate boundaries. The tax matters agreement assigns responsibility for taxes of the combined period, for the preparation and control of returns covering that period, for audits and litigation that reopen those returns, and for refunds that relate to them. A common allocation, described in Securities and Exchange Commission separation disclosures, makes the parent responsible for consolidated or combined group taxes for periods before the distribution and makes the separated company responsible for its own separate taxes after independence, with specific exceptions negotiated for particular filings, jurisdictions or transactions. The agreement also sets cooperation duties, because the party controlling an audit may need records held by the other, and the party bearing the cost may demand a voice in how the audit is defended or settled.

If a tax authority assesses additional tax, interest or penalties for a combined period, the indemnity states which company pays, which company reimburses the other if payment was made by the wrong party, and how disputes over the allocation are handled. The company that receives an audit notice must inform the other within a stated period, the company bearing the economic risk may participate in or control the defense under stated conditions, and settlements above stated thresholds may require consent.

Section 355 of the Internal Revenue Code allows a corporation to distribute stock of a controlled subsidiary without recognition of gain at the corporate or shareholder level when statutory conditions are met, and the Internal Revenue Service publishes those conditions in its guidance on corporate separations. Those conditions include control, active business requirements, a corporate business purpose, continuity and the rule that the distribution must not be used mainly as a device for distributing earnings. If the distribution later fails Section 355, the parent can face corporate level tax measured by the built in gain in the distributed stock, and shareholders can face treatment different from the tax free result the transaction promised. Private letter rulings and tax opinions, referenced in Securities and Exchange Commission filings, address that risk before closing, but neither removes it entirely, because later conduct can affect qualification under rules that the tax matters agreement must anticipate.

For that reason, tax matters agreements commonly restrict later conduct and assign the cost of failure to the party whose conduct caused it. Separation disclosures filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission describe covenants that limit share issuances, acquisitions and other actions during a restricted period after the distribution, because such actions can endanger the intended tax treatment when they form part of a plan connected with the distribution under Section 355 and related provisions. If a breach of those covenants causes the distribution to become taxable, the indemnity shifts the resulting tax to the breaching party, even where the tax is assessed against the other company. If failure arises without such a breach, allocation follows the general rules stated in the agreement, which may leave the parent bearing tax on its own distribution. It protects each company against paying for a past it did not control or a failure it did not cause, while it limits strategic freedom after closing, because a tax history written together cannot be unwritten by a distribution, only assigned, defended and, when reopened, paid for under rules fixed at separation.

## Regulatory Clearances and the Consents a Separation Cannot Skip

A board can approve a spin-off unanimously and still lack the power to complete it, because separation moves licensed activities, regulated assets and listed securities across legal boundaries that outside authorities control. The routes table in section six, comparing separation routes side by side, makes the point in structural form, since each route carries a different set of approvals, and a spin-off is distinguished partly by how many of those approvals arise without any sale to a third party. Banking, insurance, energy, telecommunications, health care and defense businesses may require approval for a change in the corporate parent of a licensed entity, under the licensing regimes administered by their respective regulators, even where ownership by public shareholders remains widely dispersed. A licence that moved automatically inside one group may need affirmative consent when the group divides, and closing conditions in separation agreements, summarized in Securities and Exchange Commission filings, list those consents by name.

The Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States, operating under the Foreign Investment Risk Review Modernization Act described by the Department of the Treasury, reviews transactions that could give foreign persons control over or access to sensitive United States businesses, and analogous regimes operate in the United Kingdom under the National Security and Investment Act described by the United Kingdom government, and in the European Union under its foreign direct investment screening framework described by the European Commission. A domestic spin-off between widely held companies may raise no such filing, but a separation that changes control of sensitive subsidiaries, moves data or technology rights, or accompanies investment by foreign shareholders can require notification, review or undertakings.

The separated company registers its shares on Form 10 under the Securities Exchange Act, a process administered by the Securities and Exchange Commission that requires audited financial statements for the separated business, disclosure of risk factors, management information and pro forma financial information showing the company on a stand alone basis. The Commission staff may issue comments and require amendments before the registration becomes effective, and effectiveness is a condition of distribution in standard separation agreements described in Securities and Exchange Commission filings. In parallel, the separated company applies for listing on the New York Stock Exchange or Nasdaq, each of which publishes listing standards covering distribution of shares, market capitalization, financial measures and corporate governance.

Lenders may hold change of control or asset transfer provisions that a separation triggers, landlords and major customers may hold consent rights under contracts assigned to the separated company, and pension regulators may need to approve the division of retirement schemes, as the Pensions Regulator in the United Kingdom describes in its guidance on corporate transactions. Bondholders may receive an offer or a consent solicitation where indentures restrict the transfer of assets or the incurrence of debt used to capitalize the separated company. Each consent has a different counterparty, a different standard and a different remedy for refusal, ranging from waiver fees to amended terms to exclusion of an asset from the separation perimeter. The separation agreement handles that variety through closing conditions, efforts covenants and long stop dates after which either party may abandon the transaction. Regulatory clearance is part of the decision, because a separation that cannot obtain its consents cannot deliver focus, capital allocation or accountability, and the structure that was meant to reveal value remains in place, with its costs disclosed by the attempt.

## Failure Modes: Separations That Stalled, Shrunk or Reversed

A separation can fail long before any shares change hands, and the public record of withdrawn plans shows why announcement and completion are different events. Company filings with the Securities and Exchange Commission describe boards that approved a split, only to withdraw the plan after market conditions moved, lenders balked, or the tax analysis weakened. In those filings, the stated reason often sounds narrow, a financing gap, a valuation dispute, or a regulatory delay, but the underlying problem is usually broader. The perimeter that looked clean in a press release turns out to involve shared contracts, shared pension obligations, shared information systems, and customers who bought from the combined company precisely because it was combined. When those entanglements are priced honestly, the separation cost rises, the debt that each side can carry falls, and the strategic gain that justified the work shrinks. A board that once presented division as clarity then faces a harder choice between proceeding at a worse price and retreating with credibility damaged.

A second failure mode is quieter, because the deal does close, but on a perimeter redrawn so many times that the finished companies bear little resemblance to the original thesis. Securities and Exchange Commission registration statements filed in successive amendments document this pattern, as assets move in and out of the separation group, transition services lengthen, and debt allocations shift from one side to the other. Each amendment solves a practical problem, a plant that cannot be split, a license that cannot be transferred, a brand that both sides need, but each amendment also dilutes the focus argument that launched the process. The new company may inherit the slow business plus the awkward shared asset, while the parent keeps the growth business plus the clean balance sheet, a distribution of burdens that reflects negotiating power rather than industrial logic. Clayton Christensen argued that capabilities reside in processes and values as much as in resources, and his critics, including scholars writing in the Academy of Management Review, answered that the framework can become circular when success itself defines the capability. That debate matters here because a redrawn perimeter tests both views. If performance falters after closing, defenders can blame the perimeter, critics can blame execution, and the original announcement offers little help, since the company that was described no longer matches the company that was delivered.

A third failure mode arises when separation meets insolvency, and the negotiation moves toward a court supervised process. United States Bankruptcy Court filings show distressed groups attempting to separate viable units from burdensome leases, litigation claims, and debt that the whole enterprise cannot service. The tools differ, asset sales under court approval, claim classification, and plans that distribute new securities to creditors rather than to existing shareholders, and the timetable follows the court calendar rather than the tax calendar. For that reason, distressed restructurings negotiated before a filing, covered in [the mechanics of prepackaged bankruptcy](/2026/10/22/how-prepackaged-bankruptcy-works/), often determine the real perimeter long before a judge sees the case, because creditor consent shapes which assets travel together and which liabilities stay behind. A separation pursued from weakness also reverses the normal signal. Instead of two focused companies, the result may be one smaller survivor and one entity created mainly to hold claims, and Securities and Exchange Commission risk disclosures for such cases stress dilution and cancellation of old shares. Reversal belongs in the same category, since some separations in the Meridian and FactSet transaction records were later undone through reacquisition or merger, an outcome that does not prove the original logic was false, but does prove that a boundary drawn for one set of prices and strategies may not survive a different set.

## How to Read a Separation Announcement

A separation announcement rewards slow reading, because the first sentence usually carries the decision and the later pages carry the qualifications. Start with the perimeter sentence, the line that names which businesses, brands, geographies, and liabilities move to the new company and which stay with the parent. If that sentence uses broad labels without named units, you can treat the perimeter as provisional and look for the detailed asset list in the registration statement that follows. Next comes the route, and the routes table from section 6 gives the comparison that matters, since a spin off, a split off, and a carve out distribute ownership, cash, and control in different ways even when the headline uses the same verb. A route that gives existing shareholders stock in both companies tells a different story about retained control than a route that sells a minority stake to outside investors first, and the announcement should state which path has been chosen and whether an exchange offer or an initial public offering forms part of the sequence. Where the route remains open, the board has preserved optionality, but it has also left the central economic question unanswered.

Tax language deserves the same close attention, because a single adjective can carry the condition on which the whole structure rests. Internal Revenue Service private letter rulings and company tax opinions, as summarized in Securities and Exchange Commission filings, typically describe a distribution as intended to qualify as tax free under Section 355 of the Internal Revenue Code, language that states an intention and an opinion rather than a guarantee. Look for the name of the firm giving the opinion, the assumptions on which the opinion rests, and any statement that no ruling has been requested or obtained from the Internal Revenue Service. Debt allocation then shows how the separation balances strength between the two sides. The announcement and the accompanying investor presentation, when a company files one as an exhibit, should show how much debt the new company will carry at closing, how much cash it receives or pays to the parent, and which credit rating agencies have been approached. A new company loaded with debt while the parent emerges lighter has not simply been set free, it has been financed by its own borrowing, and the interest burden will shape its first years of capital spending.

The leadership slate and the conditions list complete the document, and both reveal how settled the plan really is. A named chief executive, a named chief financial officer, and a board list with identified independent directors, as disclosed in the initial registration statement, signal that governance work has moved beyond intention, while unnamed roles and interim titles signal that recruitment and negotiation continue. You can keep the filings together as they accumulate, using a [reference tool](https://vaultbook.net) to hold the announcement, the amendments, and the investor exhibits in one place for comparison. The conditions list, usually placed near the end, names what must still happen, tax opinions delivered, debt financing closed, stock exchange listing approved, regulatory clearances received, and no law or order prohibiting the distribution. Each condition is a point at which the plan can be delayed, repriced, or abandoned, and the absence of a condition can matter as much as its presence, since a separation with no financing condition tells a different risk story than one dependent on a bond sale that has not yet launched. Read in that order, perimeter, route, tax, debt, leadership, and conditions, an announcement becomes less a celebration and more a map of unfinished work.

## What a Separation Changes and What It Cannot Change

On the morning after a separation closes, almost nothing visible has changed, a fact that Securities and Exchange Commission business descriptions for newly independent companies confirm in plain language. The same plants make the same products, the same sales teams call on the same customers, and the same patents, contracts, and environmental obligations apply, now divided by a legal boundary that customers may not notice for months. What has changed is the set of decisions that can be made inside each boundary. Capital that once competed in a single internal contest, where a division with steady cash flow funded a division with larger ambitions, now sits in two separate treasuries with two separate boards answerable for its use. That change is the heart of the matter, because capital allocation discipline depends on who bears the consequence of a poor choice. In a combined company, a weak project can survive for years on transfers from a stronger sibling, a pattern documented in research on internal capital markets published in the Journal of Finance. In a separated company, the same project must persuade its own board, its own lenders, and its own shareholders, or it ends. The boundary does not improve the project, it removes the subsidy that concealed the judgment.

Management incentives shift with the same boundary, and proxy statements filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission show how quickly compensation follows the new structure. Executives who once held equity in a large diversified parent, where their division might account for a small share of total value, now hold equity concentrated in the business they run. Michael Porter, in his work on industry structure, argued that profitability follows the forces that shape competition within an industry rather than the breadth of a corporate portfolio, and a separation aligned to that logic places managers inside one competitive arena instead of several. Acquisition currency changes in parallel. A separated company gains shares that outside investors can value against direct peers, and those shares can be offered to sellers who want exposure to that specific business rather than to a mixture. The parent loses the opposite ability, since it can no longer offer the combined breadth that once made its stock a general claim, and it can no longer use the separated division as a source of internal funding in a downturn.

The evidence on whether those changes raise returns is instructive precisely because it refuses a simple verdict. Cusatis, Miles and Wooldridge, writing in the Journal of Financial Economics in 1993, examined spin offs and their parents over the years after distribution and reported returns that exceeded matched benchmarks, with much of the gain concentrated in companies that later became takeover targets. Read carefully, that study supports the boundary argument and limits it at the same time. If gains arrived when acquirers paid premiums for newly visible assets, then separation exposed value that a buyer could price, but the operating companies alone did not automatically produce the premium. A separation cannot change demand for a product, the cost of a commodity input, or the skill of the workforce that arrives on the first day. It cannot erase a pension deficit by assigning it to the smaller company, since lenders and regulators, according to Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation guidance, still look to the strength of the sponsor. What it can change is accountability, the clarity with which a result belongs to a named team, a named board, and a named balance sheet, and that narrower change explains both the real gains and the frequent disappointment when observers expected transformation instead.

## The Significance: Why the Boundary of the Firm Keeps Moving

The boundary of the firm moves because the reasons for drawing it in one place weaken as markets, technology, and financing conditions change, a problem Ronald Coase placed at the center of economic organization in his 1937 essay in Economica. Coase asked why firms exist at all if markets can coordinate production, and he answered that using the market carries costs, finding prices, negotiating contracts, and enforcing terms, so activity moves inside a firm when internal direction costs less than market exchange. A spin off applies the same test in reverse. When a division can buy services, raise capital, and sell products more cheaply as an independent company than as an internal unit, the old boundary has become the expensive option, and separation reprices the choice Coase described. The test never settles permanently, because the costs on both sides keep moving. Cheap external capital favors independence, since a focused company can borrow on its own record, while scarce capital favors combination, since a diversified parent can fund a weak division through a credit squeeze. Shared services that once justified combination can later be rented from outside providers.

History, as recorded in company histories and Securities and Exchange Commission filings, shows a pendulum rather than a line, with conglomerate assembly in the twentieth century giving way to focused separation when investors priced diversified groups at a discount to the sum of their parts. The activist campaigns of the twenty first century sharpened that pricing argument, and the separation of eBay and PayPal in 2015 stands as the clearest case in that record. eBay announced the separation in 2014 after a public campaign by activist investor Carl Icahn, who argued, as reported in eBay filings with the Securities and Exchange Commission, that the payments business would command a higher valuation and move faster as an independent company. PayPal completed its distribution in July 2015 and began trading as a separate listed company, while eBay continued as a marketplace business with a smaller perimeter. The case matters not because the outcome settled the argument for all time, but because the mechanism was visible. An outside investor forced a Coase style question onto a board agenda, what does combination buy that separation cannot, and the board, after first resisting, concluded that the honest answer had changed.

General Electric offers the longer closing case, because its separation unfolded in stages that company announcements and Securities and Exchange Commission filings document in full. General Electric announced in November 2021 a plan to form three companies from its health care, energy, and aviation businesses, unwinding a structure assembled over more than a century. GE HealthCare completed its spin off in January 2023 and listed on the Nasdaq, and GE Vernova, holding the energy businesses, completed its spin off in April 2024, leaving the remaining company renamed GE Aerospace. Each stage repriced the same underlying question, which assets gain from shared ownership and which gain from a board, a balance sheet, and a share price of their own. The factories did not move, the engineers did not change employers in any simple sense, and the customers in hospitals, utilities, and airlines still bought equipment and services from people they already knew. What moved was accountability for capital, for strategy, and for results that a combined share price had averaged into invisibility. A separation, in the end, pays nothing at the closing bell, it only strips away the structure that kept value out of sight, and any gain must still be earned afterward through focus, through disciplined capital allocation, and through accountability that the combined company could not produce. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is a corporate spin-off in simple terms?
A corporate spin-off is a transaction in which a parent company separates one business unit into a new independent company and distributes shares of that new company to the parent shareholders. The parent does not sell the unit for cash in the basic form. Ownership divides into two parts, with the same investors holding stakes in both firms after distribution. Each company then has separate management, separate financial reporting, and a separate listing on a stock exchange in most cases. The Internal Revenue Service treats a qualifying distribution under Section 355 as a reorganization of ownership rather than a sale, provided statutory conditions are met. Boards use the structure when a combined company no longer fits a single strategy, capital plan, or investor base, and when separation allows each business to set budgets, incentives, and investment priorities without competing for resources inside one large group.

### Q: What is the difference between a spin-off and a split-off?
A spin-off distributes shares of a new company to all parent shareholders on a pro rata basis, so every holder receives new shares in proportion to existing ownership and keeps the parent shares as well. A split-off works through an exchange offer instead. Parent shareholders may choose to surrender some or all parent shares in return for shares of the separated business, and holders who decline keep only parent shares. The Internal Revenue Service describes both forms under the Section 355 framework, but the mechanics change the shareholder base. A spin-off preserves the same register in both companies at distribution, while a split-off allows the separated company to start with a self selected register and allows the parent to reduce its share count through the shares tendered in the exchange.

### Q: What is the difference between a spin-off and a carve-out?
A spin-off distributes shares of a separated business directly to parent shareholders and creates a fully independent company without raising cash for the parent through a public sale. A carve-out, also called an equity carve-out, sells a minority stake in a subsidiary to public investors through an initial public offering, as the Securities and Exchange Commission filing record for carve-out transactions shows. The parent usually retains control after a carve-out and receives the offering proceeds, while the subsidiary gains a market price and a separate shareholder base for part of its capital. A carve-out can serve as a first step toward a later full separation, since the listed stake establishes trading history and public financial statements, but control, consolidation, and shared governance often remain with the parent until a further distribution occurs.

### Q: Why would a company spin off a profitable division?
A profitable division can still fit poorly inside a larger group when its growth rate, capital needs, margins, or risk profile differ sharply from the rest of the portfolio. Separation allows each board to set a dividend policy, debt level, and investment pace matched to one business rather than averaging across several unlike operations. Management incentives also become cleaner, because equity awards can track the performance of the unit that a team actually runs. The Securities and Exchange Commission requires detailed separation disclosure in the Form 10, and that disclosure often cites focus, capital allocation, and strategic clarity as stated reasons. A profitable unit may also attract a different investor base as a standalone company, including funds that avoid conglomerates or that specialize in a single industry.

### Q: Do shareholders pay tax when they receive spin-off shares?
Under Section 355 of the Internal Revenue Code, a qualifying spin-off is generally tax free at the shareholder level and at the corporate level, meaning shareholders do not recognize gain or loss on receipt of the new shares and the parent does not recognize gain on the distribution. Qualification depends on statutory conditions rather than on the label applied to the transaction. When a distribution fails those conditions, the Internal Revenue Service treats it under the general dividend and sale rules that apply to corporate distributions, and tax consequences follow that treatment instead. Basis in the original shares is allocated between the parent shares and the new shares according to relative fair market values after a tax free distribution, following the allocation method described in Treasury regulations under Section 358.

### Q: What is Section 355 of the Internal Revenue Code?
Section 355 of the Internal Revenue Code is the federal tax provision that allows a corporation to distribute shares of a controlled subsidiary to its shareholders without recognition of gain or loss, provided every statutory requirement is satisfied. The Internal Revenue Service and the Treasury regulations under Section 355 require, among other conditions, distribution of control, a valid business purpose, continuity of interest and business enterprise, and satisfaction of the active trade or business test for five years before distribution. The provision also contains a device prohibition, which bars use of the distribution as a device to distribute earnings and profits, and Section 355(e) imposes corporate level tax when a distribution forms part of a plan involving a 50 percent or greater change in ownership of either company within the statutory period.

### Q: What happens to the parent company's share price on the distribution date?
On the distribution date, the parent share price typically adjusts downward by an amount that reflects the value of the business leaving the group, because the parent no longer owns the assets and earnings that moved to the new company. Exchange mechanics support that adjustment, since orders for parent shares on and after the ex-distribution date carry no right to receive the distributed shares. The combined value of one parent share plus the distributed shares can rise, fall, or stay near the prior combined value depending on market judgment, trading liquidity, index treatment, and forced selling by funds whose mandates do not permit holding the new company. The adjustment is a repricing of a smaller company, not a loss created by the distribution itself, and price history series are often restated by data providers to reflect the separation.

### Q: Can a spin-off fail after it has been announced?
A spin-off can be delayed, restructured, or abandoned after announcement, because completion depends on board approval, regulatory clearance, tax analysis, financing arrangements, and the effectiveness of the Securities and Exchange Commission registration process. Market conditions can also change the calculation, since a sharp fall in valuation or a closure of credit markets can make planned debt placement or standalone capitalization impractical. Companies disclose material changes through amended Form 10 filings and current reports on Form 8-K, which the Securities and Exchange Commission requires for specified corporate events. A transaction that fails a Section 355 condition can also lose tax free status even if the distribution proceeds, which is a tax failure distinct from abandonment. Announcement alone creates no obligation to complete a separation on the original timetable or terms.

### Q: What is a Form 10 in a spin-off?
Form 10 is the registration statement filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission to register the shares of the new company under Section 12 of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, and its information statement serves as the main disclosure document for shareholders who receive distributed shares without a vote or an investment decision. The filing contains audited financial statements for the separated business, pro forma information, risk factors, management biographies, compensation arrangements, related party agreements, and a description of the separation terms. The Securities and Exchange Commission staff may issue comments, and the registrant amends the filing until the staff review is complete and the registration becomes effective. Because no prospectus for a cash offering is involved in a pure spin-off, Form 10 carries the disclosure burden that an offering prospectus would carry in a sale.

### Q: Who decides how the debt is divided in a spin-off?
The parent board, advised by management, bankers, and counsel, decides the target capital structure for both companies and allocates existing debt, new borrowing, cash, and pension and lease obligations between them before distribution. Rating agency criteria published by firms such as Moody's and S&P Global Ratings assess leverage, interest coverage, and liquidity for each standalone profile, and those assessments influence how much debt each balance sheet can support. Lenders and bondholders also constrain the choice, because credit agreements and indentures may require consent, repayment, or refinancing when assets move to a new issuer. The allocation appears in the Form 10 filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission, in pro forma balance sheets and capitalization tables. Debt follows expected cash flow rather than asset location alone, since serviceability after separation governs creditor acceptance.

### Q: What is a separation agreement?
A separation agreement, often titled a separation and distribution agreement, is the master contract between the parent and the new company that sets the legal terms for dividing a combined group into two independent firms. It identifies which assets transfer, which liabilities each side assumes, how shares will be distributed, and what conditions must be satisfied before distribution occurs. Related schedules and companion contracts cover tax matters, employee matters, intellectual property licenses, real estate transfers, and indemnification for pre separation claims. The agreement is filed as an exhibit to the Form 10 submitted to the Securities and Exchange Commission, so its material terms enter the public record. After distribution, the separation agreement functions as the reference point for disputes about ownership of an asset, responsibility for a liability, or the scope of an indemnity between the two companies.

### Q: What is a transition services agreement?
A transition services agreement is a contract under which one company, usually the parent, continues to provide specified services to the other company for a defined period after separation, allowing both firms to operate while standalone systems are built. Covered services commonly include payroll, accounting, information technology hosting, procurement support, and facilities access, each priced according to a schedule in the agreement filed with the separation documents. The Securities and Exchange Commission disclosure in Form 10 describes material transition arrangements so investors can judge dependence between the firms after distribution. Terms set service levels, fees, extension options, and termination rights service by service, rather than as one indefinite arrangement. The structure prevents an abrupt operational break on the distribution date, but it also creates a deadline, since the receiving company must replace, insource, or outsource each service before the agreed period expires.

### Q: How long does a corporate spin-off usually take?
A corporate spin-off commonly takes between six months and 12 months from public announcement to distribution, although complex separations involving multiple jurisdictions, regulated assets, or extensive systems work can take longer. The timetable reflects several parallel workstreams: preparation of audited carve-out financial statements, Securities and Exchange Commission review of Form 10, tax analysis under Section 355, debt financing, listing approval, and drafting of separation contracts. Internal Revenue Service private letter rulings, when a company seeks one, add another review track with its own submission and response cycle. The period before announcement is often substantial as well, because boards typically evaluate alternatives and prepare financial information before any public statement. Each stage depends on the prior stage in part, so a delay in audit completion or regulatory comment resolution moves the distribution date for the entire transaction.

### Q: What is stranded cost in a corporate separation?
Stranded cost in a corporate separation refers to overhead expense that remains with one company after the business that helped support that expense has left, leaving fixed cost spread over a smaller revenue base. Central functions such as headquarters staff, enterprise software contracts, audit fees, insurance programs, and leased office space rarely shrink in exact proportion to a departing division, so the remaining company carries cost that no longer matches its scale. Separation planning in Form 10 disclosure filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission often identifies dis-synergies, the negative counterpart to merger synergies, as a risk of separation. Management addresses stranded cost through headcount reduction, contract renegotiation, facility consolidation, or redesign of shared services, but those actions take time and may require cash charges. Until elimination occurs, stranded cost depresses margins and complicates comparison of post separation results with combined historical results.

### Q: Why do some spin-offs get reversed or re-merged?
Some spin-offs are reversed or re-merged when the expected benefits of independence fail to offset the cost of running two public companies, or when strategy, leadership, or market conditions change after separation. A smaller standalone company can face limited trading liquidity, higher borrowing cost, and greater vulnerability to a downturn, and a merger can restore scale and shared services. Tax law shapes timing, because Section 355(e) of the Internal Revenue Code can impose corporate level tax when an acquisition of 50 percent or more forms part of a plan that existed at distribution, so parties often wait beyond the two year presumption period described in Treasury regulations. Recombination also requires fresh board approval, shareholder votes where applicable, and Securities and Exchange Commission disclosure, so reversal is a new transaction rather than a simple undoing of the original distribution.

### Q: What is the Morris Trust structure?
The Morris Trust structure combines a tax free spin-off under Section 355 with a subsequent merger of the spun company with a third party, arranged so that the original parent shareholders retain more than 50 percent of the combined company and continuity of interest is preserved. The name comes from Commissioner v. Mary Archer W. Morris Trust, a 1966 decision of the United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit that upheld a separation followed by a merger. Congress later added Section 355(e) in 1997, which imposes corporate level tax when a distribution and an acquisition form part of one plan and the acquisition crosses the 50 percent threshold, so modern Morris Trust transactions are structured to keep former parent shareholders above that threshold in the merged firm. A reverse Morris Trust applies the same logic with the spun entity as the surviving acquirer in form.

### Q: Do employees move with the business in a spin-off?
Employees who work primarily in the separated business generally transfer to the new company at distribution, while employees in central functions are assigned according to the employee matters agreement that forms part of the separation documents. That agreement addresses offers of employment, service credit, compensation continuity, benefit plan sponsorship, equity award conversion, and responsibility for accrued obligations such as vacation and severance. The Form 10 filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission describes material employee arrangements and any expected workforce changes connected with separation. Transfers must also comply with employment law in each jurisdiction where staff work, including consultation duties that apply in parts of Europe under local implementations of transfer rules. Not every role moves, since duplicate leadership, finance, and support positions are often redesigned, and some employees remain with the parent to run its retained operations.

### Q: What does pro rata mean in a share distribution?
Pro rata in a share distribution means allocation in proportion to ownership, so each shareholder receives new company shares according to the size of an existing holding rather than according to need, tenure, account type, or any request for extra shares. A distribution ratio states the proportion in operational terms, for example one share of the new company for every two shares of the parent held on the record date, and fractional entitlements are handled under the method disclosed in the information statement, often through aggregation and sale with cash paid in lieu. The Internal Revenue Service treats a pro rata distribution as the standard form for a Section 355 spin-off, distinct from a split-off exchange in which participation varies by election. The record date fixes the eligible register, and entitlement follows the holding on that date alone.

### Q: How do analysts value a company that is about to be spun off?
Analysts commonly value a pending separation through a sum of the parts approach, estimating a standalone value for the parent business and for the unit leaving, then adding those values and comparing the total with the combined market value before distribution. Inputs include the carve-out financial statements and pro forma adjustments in the Form 10 filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission, peer trading multiples for comparable listed companies, discounted cash flow models, expected standalone cost, tax attributes, and the debt and cash allocated to each balance sheet. When-issued trading, once it begins, supplies a market based check on those estimates for both securities. Differences between the sum of estimated parts and the observed combined price are often described as a conglomerate discount or premium, although that gap also reflects separation cost, tax risk, liquidity, and uncertainty about final terms.

### Q: What is when-issued trading in a spin-off?
When-issued trading is a market mechanism that allows shares of the new company, and in many cases adjusted parent shares, to trade on exchanges before the distribution date on a conditional basis, with settlement occurring after the shares are actually issued. The New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq publish listing notices that set the start of when-issued trading, the ex-distribution date, and the regular way trading date for each security. Prices formed in that period give investors an early indication of how the market values each standalone company, separate from the combined parent price. Trades are conditional because a cancelled distribution cancels the trades as well, under exchange rules for when-issued securities. Once distribution occurs, when-issued positions convert into ordinary holdings and both stocks move to regular way settlement for subsequent transactions.
