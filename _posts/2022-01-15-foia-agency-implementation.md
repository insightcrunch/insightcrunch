A FOIA request enters a processing system with fixed legal stages, and the outcome of the request depends less on persistence alone than on fit between the request as drafted and the stages through which an agency must carry the request. The Freedom of Information Act grants any person a right to seek records from executive branch agencies, and the statute then governs every step that follows, from the description of records in the opening letter to the standard a court applies after litigation has begun. The central fact of the system, stated at the outset, is that the clock runs on a decision, not on delivery. An agency faces a twenty working day period for a determination whether to comply, while production of records after a favorable determination follows a separate promptness obligation. Confusion between those two events explains a large share of disappointment with the statute, and precision about the distinction gives a requester a realistic map of timing, cost, and leverage at each stage.

The governing statute is [the Freedom of Information Act of 1966](/2021/10/01/freedom-of-information-act-1966-guide/), codified at 5 U.S.C. 552, as amended across later decades. Companion transparency statutes operate beside the Act in adjacent fields, including the regime for [financial disclosure records under the ethics in government framework](/2021/10/15/ethics-in-government-act-1978-guide/), and comparison among those regimes shows a shared pattern: a right stated in broad terms becomes practical only through definitions, deadlines, fee rules, and review procedures written in operational detail. The present article is general information about how the statute works rather than legal advice for any specific request. A specific request turns on the records sought, the component holding the records, the category claimed, and the administrative record created during processing, and advice about a specific dispute requires examination of that record by a qualified professional. General information has a different use. General information explains the machinery in advance, so that drafting choices, fee decisions, and appeal arguments can be made with knowledge of the stage that follows each choice.

The pathway begins with drafting. A request must reasonably describe the records sought, and a description built from record type, component, date range, subject terms, and custodians allows an agency employee familiar with the subject area to locate records with reasonable effort. Drafting also fixes the benchmark for later stages, because search adequacy is measured against the description, fee estimation follows from the scope described, and an appeal tests the determination reached on the request as presented. A request addressed to the appropriate component starts the determination clock without internal travel, while a request addressed to a department in general or to a component holding no responsive records spends time in routing before search work begins. Referral and consultation enter at the same early point in more complex cases, since records originated by one component and held by another carry the equities of the originating office into the processing path.

After drafting and receipt, the determination clock, fees, and expedition decide the pace and price of processing. The determination clock allows twenty working days, with narrow tolling for one request for information or for clarification of fee assessment, and with a written extension of no more than ten working days for defined unusual circumstances. Fees follow category: commercial use requesters pay search, review, and duplication; educational institutions, noncommercial scientific institutions, and news media representatives pay duplication only; all other requesters pay search and duplication, with free allowances for noncommercial categories and a public interest waiver where disclosure is likely to contribute significantly to public understanding and is not primarily in the commercial interest of the requester. Expedited processing moves a request ahead where compelling need is shown, either through imminent threat to life or physical safety or through urgency to inform the public concerning actual or alleged federal government activity by a person primarily engaged in disseminating information.

The processing machine inside the agency then performs search, review, consultation, referral, and segregability analysis, under the supervision of a Chief FOIA Officer and with FOIA Requester Service Centers, a FOIA Public Liaison, and the Office of Government Information Services available for status, assistance, and dispute resolution. An adverse determination opens an administrative appeal to the head of the agency, decided within twenty working days, with at least ninety days allowed for filing the appeal. Litigation follows exhaustion or constructive exhaustion, and two judicial devices organize most court disputes: the Vaughn index, an itemized correlation of withheld material with exemption claims and justifications, and the Glomar response, a refusal to confirm or deny the existence of records where existence itself is protected. Each stage in that sequence rewards preparation made at the stage before, and the sections that follow examine each stage in statutory order.

## Drafting a Request an Agency Can Actually Process

A Freedom of Information Act request is processed or stalled long before any lawyer reads an exemption. The decisive events happen at the front of the pipeline: the words a requester chooses to describe the records, the component to which the request is addressed, and the way an agency interprets the description it receives. The statute sets a deliberately low threshold for entry. Any person may file a request, and no requester must state a reason, a purpose, or a need for the records. What the statute does require, at 5 U.S.C. 552(a)(3)(A), is that the request reasonably describe the records sought and otherwise comply with the agency's published procedural rules. That single adjective, reasonably, carries most of the practical law of request drafting, because everything downstream, the search, the twenty working day clock, the fee estimate, and the eventual appeal, depends on how the description performs at intake.

### The Reasonable Description Standard in Practice

A request reasonably describes records when the description enables a professional employee of the agency familiar with the subject area to locate the records with a reasonable amount of effort. The test is functional rather than formal: a description succeeds if agency staff can identify which record systems to search and what to look for inside them, and it fails when the agency would have to guess at the subject, the office, or the period the requester means. Courts have sustained the requirement as a genuine condition of processing, not a courtesy, and an agency that cannot understand what is being asked is entitled to seek clarification rather than to conduct an unguided excavation of its own files.

A workable description is built from identifiable parts, and each part removes a distinct kind of guesswork. The record type comes first: memoranda, email correspondence, meeting minutes, inspection reports, contract files, grant records, or data extracts each live in different systems and are held by different custodians. The office or component comes next, because custody follows organizational lines. A date range bounds the search in time and converts an open-ended trawl into a finite one. Subject terms, the program name, the docket number, the contract number, the name of the matter, give searchers strings and files to match. Named custodians, where a requester knows them, narrow the field further. A request that supplies several of these elements reads to an intake analyst as a task; a request that supplies none reads as a research project, and the difference shows up directly in processing time.

Breadth defeats description in predictable ways. A request for all records concerning a broad topic, across all components, across an unbounded period, may be grammatically clear and still describe nothing searchable, because no single custodian holds the topic and no single system indexes it. Courts have treated requests of that scale as failing the reasonable description standard or as presenting the voluminous character that the statute elsewhere recognizes as grounds for extended and negotiated processing. The recurring drafting error is the opposite of vagueness: a requester who suspects an agency of bad faith drafts wider and wider, on the theory that a broad net cannot be evaded, and produces a request so large that delay becomes a mechanical consequence of its own design rather than a choice the agency made. Precision is the requester's protection. A narrow request that an agency processes slowly is easy to litigate; a vast request that an agency processes slowly is easy to defend.

### Why does the component a request is sent to matter so much?

The federal government is not one records office, and large departments are not even one records office each. A cabinet department processes FOIA requests through dozens of separately staffed components, bureaus, field structures, and offices, each with its own FOIA intake, its own queues, and its own record systems.

The Department of Justice, the Department of Defense, and the Department of Homeland Security each operate this way, with component-level FOIA offices handling the records their component creates and holds. A request addressed to the department generally must be identified, routed, and re-logged before any search begins, and a request addressed to the wrong component must be forwarded or referred, with the receiving component starting its own intake process. Misdirection is, on the record of agency FOIA operations, among the most common causes of early delay, and it is the cause most fully within the requester's control.

The clock rules sharpen the point. The twenty working day determination period under section 552(a)(6)(A)(i) commences when the request is first received by the appropriate component of the agency, and in any event not later than ten days after the request is first received by any component the agency has designated in its regulations to receive requests. A request mailed to an undesignated office, or filed with a component that plainly holds no responsive records, spends its first days traveling rather than being processed, and the travel happens before the timeline a requester is watching has meaningfully begun. Requesters who identify the component whose work they are investigating, using agency FOIA reading rooms, component directories, and prior releases to locate custody, remove that dead time from their own cases. Requesters who write to the government in general build it in.

### How must an agency interpret the description it receives?

The duty runs in both directions, and an agency may not defeat a request by reading it with hostile literalism. Agencies are obliged to construe a request liberally and to read the description as a whole, giving effect to what a requester plainly means rather than to the narrowest construction the words permit.

Where a description is ambiguous in a way the agency can resolve from context, the agency resolves it and proceeds. Where the ambiguity is genuine, the agency's remedy is a clarification request, and the statute gives that remedy a price: the twenty working day clock may be tolled while the agency awaits the requester's answer to one request for information, or while it seeks clarification of fee issues, under section 552(a)(6)(A)(i). Clarification is therefore a negotiation conducted under time pressure, and a requester who answers promptly and narrows intelligently converts a threatened stall into a smaller, faster search.

The search that follows interpretation is governed by a standard the courts built for exactly this setting. In Weisberg v. Department of Justice, 705 F.2d 1344 (D.C. Cir. 1983), the court articulated the formulation that has governed since: an agency search must be reasonably calculated to uncover all relevant documents. The standard does not guarantee that every responsive record will be found, and it does not require an agency to search every system it maintains. It requires a search method fitted to the description, directed at the files and systems where responsive records are reasonably likely to live, conducted in good faith. In litigation, agencies defend searches through declarations that must be relatively detailed and nonconclusory, identifying the files searched, the systems consulted, and the search terms or methods used, and a declaration that recites effort without specifying method fails the standard the Weisberg line of cases established. The drafting lesson closes the loop: the description a requester writes becomes, in any later dispute, the benchmark against which the adequacy of the search is measured, so a description that names systems, offices, and date ranges gives a court something concrete to test the agency's effort against.

Three limits on the agency's duty belong in any accurate account of what a request can demand. An agency must search for and produce records; it is not required to create records that do not exist, to compile data into a new document assembled for the requester, or to conduct research. A request framed as a set of questions, asking why a decision was made or what an official believes, asks for answers rather than records and will be redirected or answered with whatever records exist, because the statute grants access to records and not to explanations. And the duty attaches to agency records, documents created or obtained by the agency and under its control when the request arrives, so material held by contractors outside agency control, by private parties, or by components of government outside the executive branch agency structure falls outside the search altogether. Requests that ignore these boundaries consume correspondence without producing records.

Routing does not end at intake for any request of consequence. A search in one component surfaces records originated by another, and the originating office's equities in the material trigger the referral and consultation practices that structure multi-office processing: the holding component may refer the record outright to the component that created it, which then makes its own determination, or may consult the interested office while retaining the decision. Each transfer restarts a measure of internal review, each consultation waits on another office's workload, and the statute's unusual circumstances provision recognizes the pattern expressly by counting inter-component and inter-agency consultation among the grounds for extending the determination period. The requester sees none of this machinery directly. What the requester sees is elapsed time, which is why the drafting choices at the front of the process, a bounded description, the correct component, identifiable custodians and systems, matter more than any argument made later: they determine how many offices the request must visit before it becomes a production.

## The Twenty Working Day Clock and What It Actually Governs

The Freedom of Information Act promises speed in a single sentence of 5 U.S.C. 552(a)(6)(A)(i): each agency, upon a request for records, shall determine within twenty days, Saturdays, Sundays, and legal public holidays excepted, whether to comply with the request, and shall immediately notify the requester of the determination and the reasons behind it. Nearly every misunderstanding of the statute begins with a misreading of that sentence. The twenty-day clock measures the time an agency has to reach and announce a decision. It does not measure the time an agency has to place records in a requester's hands. That gap between decision and delivery, written into the operative verb of the provision, explains more about the lived experience of the Freedom of Information Act than any other feature of the law, and understanding it is the difference between reading the statute and merely quoting it.

### Does the twenty working day deadline mean the records arrive in twenty days?

No. The deadline in section 552(a)(6)(A)(i) governs the agency's determination, the decision whether to comply with the request, together with notice of that decision and its reasons. A separate sentence in section 552(a)(6)(C)(i) then requires that records be made promptly available once an agency has determined to comply.

Decision and delivery are two distinct statutory events, and only the first carries the twenty-day count.

The distinction is not a technicality layered over an otherwise clear promise. It is the structure Congress chose. The determination deadline forces an agency to commit itself, on a short timetable, to a position that a requester can accept, appeal, or challenge in court. Delivery is governed by a different and deliberately less precise word, promptly, whose meaning the courts have had to work out case by case. A requester who reads the twenty days as a delivery promise will conclude that the statute is broken whenever a large production takes months. A requester who reads it as a decision deadline will see a system performing two separate functions on two separate clocks, one tight and enforceable, the other elastic and contested.

### The Start of the Twenty Working Day Clock

The period commences on the date the request is first received by the appropriate component of the agency, the office actually charged with processing requests for the records sought. As an outer bound, the period commences in any event not later than ten days after the request is first received by any component of the agency that the agency's regulations designate to receive requests under the statute. A letter that arrives at the wrong desk inside the right agency therefore cannot be left indefinitely uncounted; the ten-day backstop caps the cost of internal misdirection, although a requester who addresses the correct component from the outset avoids the delay altogether. A request sent to the wrong agency entirely stands on different footing, since the statutory clock runs against the agency that holds the records and has received the request through a designated channel.

Once running, the clock is difficult to pause. The statute permits tolling in only two situations: the agency may make one request to the requester for information and toll the period while awaiting the answer, or the agency may toll the period where necessary to clarify issues regarding fee assessment. In either case, the agency's receipt of the requester's response ends the tolling period. There is no general authority to stop the clock because a search is difficult, a component is busy, or a consultation is slow. The narrowness of the tolling provisions is itself a policy choice. Congress enumerated the pauses it was willing to allow, and everything outside the enumeration leaves the count running, which is why the deadline is breached so often in practice and why the consequences of breach, examined below, matter as much as the deadline itself.

### The Determination as Construed by the Court of Appeals

The controlling construction comes from Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington v. Federal Election Commission, 711 F.3d 180, decided by the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit in 2013. The court held that for an agency to make a determination within the meaning of the statute, the agency must at a minimum gather and review the documents in question, determine and communicate the scope of the documents it intends to produce and the scope it intends to withhold, together with the reasons for withholding any portion, and inform the requester of the right to appeal the adverse portion of the determination. An acknowledgment letter, a queue number, or a statement that a request has been logged and assigned satisfies none of these requirements. The determination is a substantive act, completed only when the agency has examined the material and taken a communicable position on it.

The same decision drew the line that gives the determination its practical significance. The court held that an agency need not produce the records at the same time it communicates the determination, because the statute separates the two events and imposes promptness, rather than simultaneity, on production. At the same time, the court made plain that promptly available is a real obligation with a short horizon, indicating that a delay of days, or at most a few weeks, between the determination and production is what the statute contemplates. A determination followed by silence for months is not the model the court described. The ruling thus cuts in both directions: it rejects the requester's argument that decision and delivery are one event, and it rejects the agency's implicit argument that a timely decision purchases unlimited time to deliver.

Two consequences flow from the construction. First, an agency cannot manufacture compliance by issuing paper decisions on records nobody has reviewed; the review itself must precede the determination, which is the holding's principal discipline on agency practice. Second, the determination is the event that opens the rest of the statutory machinery. It triggers the right of appeal, starts the timetable for an appeal decision, and, if the agency fails to make one in time, supports the requester's claim to have exhausted administrative remedies under section 552(a)(6)(C)(i). The determination is therefore less a letter than a jurisdictional fact, the point at which the request matures into something the legal system can act upon.

### Unusual Circumstances and the Ten Working Day Extension

Section 552(a)(6)(B) permits a limited extension, and its limits are as instructive as its permission. In unusual circumstances, the time limits for a determination, and equally for a decision on an appeal, may be extended by written notice to the requester setting forth the unusual circumstances and the date on which a determination is expected to be dispatched. No notice may specify a date that would result in an extension of more than ten working days. The extension is an addition to the original twenty, producing a statutory outer frame of thirty working days for the ordinary case, beyond which the agency must rely on the requester's agreement to an alternative schedule or on a court's willingness to allow more time.

Unusual circumstances is a defined term, available only to the extent reasonably necessary to the proper processing of the particular request, and the definition reaches three situations. The first is the need to search for and collect the requested records from field facilities or other establishments separate from the office processing the request, a recognition that records of a national agency are physically and administratively dispersed. The second is the need to search for, collect, and appropriately examine a voluminous amount of separate and distinct records demanded in a single request, the statute's one acknowledgment that the size of a request bears on the time it legitimately takes. The third is the need for consultation, conducted with all practicable speed, with another agency having a substantial interest in the determination, or among two or more components of the same agency having substantial subject-matter interest in it.

Related provisions discipline the edges of the device. An agency may by regulation aggregate certain requests by the same requester, or by a group of requesters acting in concert, where the agency reasonably believes the requests constitute a single request that would otherwise satisfy the unusual circumstances definition and involve clearly related matters, while multiple requests involving unrelated matters may not be aggregated. Where an extension leaves the agency unable to process a request within the extended limit, section 552(a)(6)(B)(ii) requires the agency to notify the requester and to offer an opportunity to limit the scope of the request so it may be processed within the limit, or to arrange an alternative time frame for processing the request or a modified version of it. Refusal to reasonably modify a request or accept an alternative frame is a factor a court later weighs in deciding whether exceptional circumstances justify still more time.

### What help must the agency offer when it cannot meet the extended deadline?

The FOIA Public Liaison is the statute's designated intermediary for precisely this impasse. Section 552(a)(6)(B)(ii) requires each agency to make its FOIA Public Liaison available to aid the requester when a request cannot be processed within the extended time, and the liaison assists in resolving disputes between the requester and the agency.

Every notification of an adverse determination must, under the amended text of section 552(a)(6)(A)(i), inform the requester of the right to seek assistance from the FOIA Public Liaison and, for adverse determinations, of the right to seek dispute resolution services from the liaison or from the Office of Government Information Services. The liaison mechanism converts a missed deadline from a wall into a negotiation, at least in design: scope can be narrowed, priorities among records can be set, and an agreed schedule can replace the statutory one. Where negotiation fails, the same facts that produced the impasse, including the requester's refusal to narrow, become the record on which a court later rules.

### A second and separate safety valve in the courts

The unusual circumstances extension is not the only mechanism by which the twenty days expand, and confusing the two has produced persistent error. A distinct doctrine governs what a court may do once a requester sues. Section 552(a)(6)(C)(i) provides that a requester is deemed to have exhausted administrative remedies if the agency fails to comply with the applicable time limit, which opens the courthouse door. The same provision adds that if the government can show exceptional circumstances exist and the agency is exercising due diligence in responding to the request, the court may retain jurisdiction and allow the agency additional time to complete its review. Unusual circumstances justify an agency's own ten-day extension by notice; exceptional circumstances persuade a court, after litigation has begun, to withhold immediate judgment while the agency works.

The foundational decision is Open America v. Watergate Special Prosecution Force, 547 F.2d 605, decided by the District of Columbia Circuit in 1976. The case arose from the extraordinary backlog at the Federal Bureau of Investigation in the period after the 1974 amendments took effect, when requests arrived in numbers the Bureau had no staff to process and the statute then allowed only ten working days for a determination. The court accepted that an agency overwhelmed despite diligent effort, processing requests in an orderly first-in, first-out sequence, presented exceptional circumstances, and that a district court could retain jurisdiction and grant additional time rather than order an impossible immediate production. Open America decided a question about judicial patience in the face of demonstrated diligence and genuine overload. It did not define the term determination, and readings that cite the case for the content of a determination misplace its holding by nearly four decades, since the governing construction of that term arrived in the Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington decision of 2013. The two cases answer different questions: one specifies what an agency must do to decide, the other specifies what a court may do when deciding on time has become impossible despite honest effort.

The statute also polices the boundary of the exceptional circumstances doctrine. Section 552(a)(6)(C)(ii) confirms that a delay resulting from a predictable agency workload does not qualify as exceptional circumstances unless the agency demonstrates reasonable progress in reducing its backlog of pending requests. Chronic understaffing, standing alone, is therefore not an excuse the statute accepts, and an agency invoking Open America must show both an unusual burden and measurable movement against it.

### Why the count is twenty days rather than ten

The twenty-day figure is itself an amendment artifact, and its history confirms that the determination framework was deliberate. The original 1966 Act borrowed no such clock; the modern timetable descends from the 1974 amendments and was recalibrated a generation later. The Electronic Freedom of Information Act Amendments of 1996, Public Law 104-231, lengthened the determination period from ten working days to twenty, effective with the broader modernization that same statute carried, which included electronic reading rooms, expedited processing, and multi-track processing. Congress in 1996 thus conceded, in statutory text, that the ten-day standard had been set below what a functioning disclosure system could meet, and doubled the period while simultaneously giving requesters new tools, expedition chief among them, to escape the ordinary queue where the need was urgent. The twenty days that requesters now treat as the statute's central promise is the product of that recalibration: a legislative judgment that a somewhat longer decision deadline, honestly specified, served disclosure better than a shorter one honored mainly in the breach. The sequence of amendments that set and reset the deadlines is traced in [the history of the FOIA amendments](/2021/12/01/foia-amendments-history/).

### Why compliance statistics and requester experience diverge

Agency reporting on deadline compliance and requester testimony about delay describe the same system from opposite ends of the determination distinction, and both can be accurate at once. An agency measures itself against the event the statute times, the determination, and reports the share of requests determined within the statutory window or within an extension. A requester measures the system against the arrival of usable records, an event the statute governs with the word promptly and the courts supervise through production schedules negotiated or imposed in litigation. Where a determination is made on time and production follows on a rolling basis over subsequent months under an agreed or court-supervised schedule, the agency records compliance and the requester experiences delay, and neither account is false.

The months and years that separate request from completed production in large cases are therefore real, but their legal source must be stated precisely. They are not a period the statute grants. They arise from backlog, the condition in which the number of pending requests exceeds the staff available to gather and review records, and from the schedules that courts set when litigation converts a statutory promptness obligation into a monthly page rate the agency can actually sustain. The Open America line of authority tolerates that conversion where diligence and exceptional burden are shown; the Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington line forbids treating the determination as a formality that purchases indefinite time. Between those poles the working law of the deadline is found: twenty working days to decide, promptly thereafter to deliver, extensions measured in days rather than seasons, and every longer interval traceable either to the requester's agreement or to a court's supervised indulgence rather than to anything the twenty-day sentence itself allows.

## Fees, the Three Requester Categories, and the Waiver Standard

Fees shape FOIA practice as directly as exemptions and deadlines shape it. A request that costs nothing to file can become expensive to process when responsive records fill many boxes, require hours of searching across separate systems, or demand page by page examination before release. The statute answers the cost question in 5 U.S.C. 552(a)(4)(A), and the answer turns on three variables. Category determines which kinds of charges an agency may impose at all. Relief from payment, through free allowances, a public interest waiver, limits on advance payment, and a bar on fees after a missed deadline, restricts what an agency may collect even after a charge has been calculated correctly.

The governing standard is restrictive by design. Each agency must promulgate regulations specifying a schedule of fees applicable to the processing of requests, and those fees are limited to reasonable standard charges for document search, duplication, and review. The charges must be tied to direct costs only. Rent, general management salaries, and ordinary overhead are not the direct cost of searching for, duplicating, or reviewing a particular set of records. Direct cost follows the work on the request itself, and reasonableness requires conformity to the published schedule rather than an ad hoc calculation invented for one requester.

### What fees can an agency charge for a FOIA request?

An agency may charge only reasonable standard fees tied to direct costs of search, duplication, and, for commercial use requesters, review. Educational, scientific, and news media requesters pay duplication alone. All other requesters pay search and duplication, with initial allowances that commercial requesters do not receive.

Search charges compensate an agency for time spent looking for records responsive to a request, including locating the files and systems where responsive material is held. Duplication charges compensate the agency for making copies, whether the copy is a paper reproduction, a scanned image, or another production format. Review has the narrowest definition, written into the statute. Review means the initial examination of a record to determine whether the record must be disclosed or may be withheld in whole or in part. Review charges exclude time spent resolving legal or policy issues raised in connection with processing. Consultation about a difficult exemption question and deliberation over a policy consequence are not review, even though such work consumes staff and attorney time, because the exclusion holds the charge to the act of examination itself.

The statute states the three categories precisely. A commercial use requester pays search, review, and duplication. An educational institution, a noncommercial scientific institution, and a representative of the news media pay duplication only. All other requesters pay search and duplication. No other combination is authorized, so an agency may not charge review fees to a journalist or search fees to a university. The category finding therefore precedes arithmetic, and every later calculation inherits any error made at that step.

Commercial use is the most expensive category because the requester seeks the records for a purpose from which the requester expects a commercial benefit, and the tier rests on cost recovery from the party positioned to profit. Educational and noncommercial scientific institutions occupy the preferred tier because teaching and noncommercial research return value through scholarship and instruction rather than through sale of the information. News media representatives share that tier because reporting distributes government information to a broad audience. The residual category captures individual citizens, private researchers, and any requester whose use is personal or civic and neither commercial nor within the preferred classes. A residual category requester pays for search and duplication, but never for review.

The free allowances sharpen the distinctions. No fee is charged for the first two hours of search time or the first 100 pages of duplication, except for commercial use requests. For a preferred category requester, a production of 100 pages or fewer therefore costs nothing, because duplication is the only charge and the allowance absorbs the whole charge. For a residual category requester, the first two hours of searching and the first 100 pages are likewise free, and charges begin only beyond those thresholds. A commercial requester receives no allowance of either kind. The first hour of searching, the first page copied, and the initial examination all count from the start.

Because classification controls the charges available and the allowances applied, identification of the correct category at the outset materially changes cost, and the request itself is where that identification begins. A requester claiming news media status documents the claim by identifying the news organization served, describing the editorial function performed, and explaining the intent to use the records in reporting disseminated to the public. A freelancer documents status through a record of publication or an agreement with a news outlet, since the question is whether the requester gathers information of potential interest to a segment of the public and uses editorial skills to turn raw material into a distinct work. A requester claiming educational status documents affiliation with an institution of teaching and identifies the course, program, or scholarly project the records will serve. A noncommercial scientific institution documents its institutional character, the research purpose, and the absence of a commercial end. Silence about category leaves classification to the agency on incomplete information and risks assignment to a more expensive category by default.

Category follows use as well as identity. An institution with an educational name that seeks records to support a commercial product does not hold the preferred tier on the strength of a charter alone. A reporter seeking records for a private business venture unrelated to reporting does not carry news media status into that venture. Agencies deciding a disputed classification examine the explanation supplied with the request, the nature of the organization, and the stated plan for the material. A specific, concrete statement of use carries more weight than a bare assertion of a title, and correction through administrative appeal costs time even when correction succeeds.

Even a correctly classified requester may owe nothing, because no fee may be assessed where the cost of collecting the fee equals or exceeds the fee itself. Collection consumes staff time, correspondence, and accounting, and a small balance for search time beyond the second hour or duplication beyond the first 100 pages can cost more to collect than the balance is worth. The provision prevents the fee system from operating at a loss in the name of cost recovery, and very small balances are therefore written off rather than billed.

Fee estimates and advance payment are the points at which money enters the process before records change hands, and the statute confines both. An estimate tells the requester what full processing will cost under the category assigned, so the requester can proceed at that scope, narrow the request to reduce searching and duplication, or seek a waiver before charges accumulate. Advance payment is permitted only where the requester previously failed to pay a fee in a timely fashion, or where the fee will exceed 250 dollars. Outside those situations, the agency processes first and bills afterward. The restriction prevents prepayment from becoming a general screening device, and the threshold reserves advance payment for requests large enough to present a genuine risk of substantial uncompensated work. Narrowing the scope to bring an estimate below 250 dollars, where narrowing is possible, removes the basis for advance payment on the large fee ground.

Fee clarification holds a distinctive place in the timing scheme. The twenty working day period for a determination may be tolled, meaning paused, on only two grounds: one request to the requester for information, or clarification of fee assessment issues with the requester. No other ground authorizes tolling, so a long backlog, an unavailable reviewing official, or slow consultation does not pause the clock. Fee clarification qualifies because an unresolved fee question can leave the authorized scope of work undefined, as when a requester has not agreed to pay, has not established a category, or has not answered an estimate above the advance payment threshold. Tolling ends when the requester responds. The clock then resumes, requester deliberation time does not count against the agency, and agency time before and after the clarification counts in full.

### How does a requester qualify for a fee waiver?

A fee waiver requires a showing that disclosure is in the public interest because the records are likely to contribute significantly to public understanding of government operations or activities, and that disclosure is not primarily in the commercial interest of the requester seeking the waiver or reduction.

The standard in 5 U.S.C. 552(a)(4)(A) is conjunctive, and both prongs must be satisfied. Fees shall be waived or reduced if disclosure is in the public interest because disclosure is likely to contribute significantly to public understanding of the operations or activities of the government and is not primarily in the commercial interest of the requester. The mandatory phrasing matters, because an agency that finds both prongs satisfied does not exercise a grace in excusing payment. The agency task is the factual and judgmental work of applying the two prongs, and a denial rested on a ground outside the prongs, such as backlog size or filing frequency, applies a test Congress did not enact.

Agencies apply the first prong element by element to the specific records requested. The subject must be the operations or activities of the government, so records about a private matter in an agency file, without a connection to what the government did, decided, spent, or administered, fail the subject element. Disclosure must be likely to contribute to public understanding, which requires information that is meaningfully informative in relation to what the public already knows. Records duplicating material already public, confirming an established fact, or arriving in fragments too small to build understanding from contribute little. The contribution must be significant, judged by the scale of the activity, the degree to which the records illuminate a matter not already understood, and the extent to which understanding would change after release. A marginal addition to public knowledge, asserted without explanation, falls short of that threshold.

Dissemination connects the two prongs in agency practice. A contribution to public understanding presupposes that the understanding will reach the public, so a requester who will publish, report, teach, or otherwise distribute the substance of the records presents a stronger claim than a requester who will hold the records in a private file. Agencies examine the stated plan, the capacity to carry the plan out, and the audience the plan would reach. The second prong then asks whose interest disclosure primarily serves, and a waiver is unavailable where disclosure is primarily in the commercial interest of the requester even when the first prong is satisfied. Records illuminating an agency contracting program might satisfy the first prong, while a requester seeking the records principally to underbid a competitor fails the second. A journalist employed by a commercial news outlet is not disqualified by the outlet commercial character alone, because reporting that informs the public serves the public interest even though the outlet sells subscriptions.

A waiver request succeeds or fails on the showing made to the agency. The requester bears the burden of addressing the standard, and a bare request without explanation of the subject, the gap in public understanding, the significance of the records, or the plan for dissemination gives the agency nothing to apply the standard to. A sufficient showing identifies the government activity concerned, explains what the public does not already understand, states why the particular records are likely to add significantly to that understanding, describes how the substance will reach the public, and addresses commercial interest directly. The showing belongs in the initial request or a prompt supplement, because fees accumulate during processing, and the determination is subject to administrative appeal. Waiver and reduction are distinct outcomes, and an agency may reduce a fee where the standard is satisfied for part of the records or where a commercial interest attaches to a severable portion of the use.

Fee waiver litigation follows a distinctive review rule. Review is de novo, and review is limited to the record before the agency. De novo review means the court decides the waiver question on the merits and reaches an independent conclusion under the statutory standard, without the deference ordinarily given to agency judgment. The record limitation is the counterweight, because the court decides on the material the agency had during processing and appeal. Evidence assembled for litigation and never presented to the agency does not enter the review. A complete administrative showing is therefore rewarded twice, first as the showing most likely to persuade the agency and second as the entire record if litigation follows.

### What happens to fees when an agency misses the deadline?

When an agency fails to meet a statutory time limit, the FOIA Improvement Act of 2016 generally bars assessment of search fees, and duplication fees for the preferred category, subject to narrow exceptions for noticed unusual circumstances, very large productions, and court orders finding exceptional circumstances.

The missed deadline fee bar at 5 U.S.C. 552(a)(4)(A)(viii), as reshaped by the FOIA Improvement Act of 2016, Public Law 114-185, converts a timing failure into a financial consequence. An agency that fails to comply with any time limit under the statute may not assess search fees. For a preferred category requester, the bar also removes duplication fees, which eliminates the only charge that category ever pays. For a residual category requester, search fees are barred while duplication fees survive. For a commercial requester, the bar removes search fees while review and duplication remain assessable. No showing of prejudice or bad faith is required, and the exceptions are the only routes back to fee assessment, each conditioned on conduct performed while the clock was running.

The first exception concerns unusual circumstances, the three situations in which an agency may extend the determination period by written notice. Those situations are the need to search for and collect records from field facilities or establishments separate from the processing office, the need to search for, collect, and appropriately examine a voluminous amount of separate and distinct records in a single request, and the need for consultation, conducted with all practicable speed, with another agency or component having a substantial interest. Where unusual circumstances apply and the agency gave timely written notice, the fee bar is lifted for a further ten working days. The notice is the operative act, so an agency that encountered genuine unusual circumstances but sent no timely written notice cannot invoke the exception. An agency that sent the notice but allowed the additional period to lapse falls back under the bar.

The second exception applies where unusual circumstances exist and more than 5,000 pages are necessary to respond. An agency that failed to meet the time limits may still assess fees if timely written notice was given and the agency discussed with the requester how the request could be modified to reduce the scope, or made not less than three good faith attempts to have that discussion. The discussion requirement ties fee authority to a genuine effort at scope reduction, because a production above 5,000 pages imposes burdens of a different order and Congress preserved fees only where the requester had a real opportunity to shrink the production. Attempts count only if directed to the requester through a channel the requester could be expected to receive and framed as an invitation to discuss scope. A requester who engaged in the discussion and declined to narrow the request faces the fees, while a requester who received neither a discussion nor three good faith attempts retains the protection of the bar.

The third exception is judicial. Where a court finds exceptional circumstances, the failure to comply is excused for the period the court order allows, and fees may be assessed for a request processed within that period. Exceptional circumstances differs from unusual circumstances. Unusual circumstances are the three defined situations supporting a ten working day agency extension on written notice. Exceptional circumstances are shown to a court in litigation and justify retention of jurisdiction and additional time for an agency exercising due diligence. Delay beyond the period a court order allows stands unexcused, and the bar applies to that delay.

The fee provisions allocate cost according to consistent judgments that the FOIA Improvement Act of 2016 reinforced. Congress placed full direct cost on the commercial user, duplication alone on the institutions and journalists whose work distributes the value of disclosure, and search and duplication on the residual requester, with allowances offsetting each noncommercial tier. Congress excused fees where disclosure serves the public interest under the waiver standard, where collection would cost as much as the fee, and, within the exceptions, where the agency failed to act in time. Category, use, public contribution, commercial interest, notice, discussion, and timeliness are therefore the facts that decide what a request costs.

## Expedited Processing, Compelling Need, and the Queue

Most Freedom of Information Act requests wait their turn. Expedited processing is the statutory exception, a mechanism Congress built into 5 U.S.C. 552(a)(6)(E) for the narrow set of cases in which waiting in line defeats the purpose of the request itself. The provision does not shorten the search, excuse the review, or guarantee a production date. What it changes is position: a request that wins expedition leaves the ordinary queue and is handled ahead of requests that arrived earlier. Because that privilege displaces other requesters, the statute surrounds it with a demanding definition, a fast decision deadline, and an appeal channel of its own, and the case for each of those elements has to be made on the terms Congress wrote rather than on a general claim that the records matter.

### What qualifies as a compelling need for expedited processing?

A compelling need exists in two, and only two, statutory forms. The first is danger: the failure to obtain the requested records on an expedited basis could reasonably be expected to pose an imminent threat to the life or physical safety of an individual.

The second is urgency in public information: the request is made by a person primarily engaged in disseminating information, and there is an urgency to inform the public concerning actual or alleged federal government activity. An agency may recognize additional categories through its own regulations, but no requester can manufacture a third route out of the statutory text, and a showing that fits neither form fails however sympathetic the underlying purpose may be.

Each element of the definition does independent work, because the standard Congress enacted in the Electronic Freedom of Information Act Amendments of 1996, Public Law 104-231, is considerably narrower than the phrase compelling need suggests in ordinary speech. The threat prong requires imminence, a connection between the delay itself and the danger, and a risk to life or physical safety rather than to property, reputation, or a legal or financial interest. The information prong requires three findings at once: an occupational qualification, an urgency, and a governmental subject. A requester whose need is intense but private, however reasonable the need may be, stands outside both prongs and remains in the ordinary processing tracks described below.

### Who counts as a person primarily engaged in disseminating information?

The occupation element is the gate through which most expedition requests pass or fail. The statute asks what the requester primarily does: the dissemination of information to the public must be the requester's principal activity, not an incidental byproduct of some other occupation.

Representatives of the news media are the standard case, and the same statutory scheme that defines this phrase for expedited processing also treats news media status as a fee category, so an organization that gathers information of potential interest to a segment of the public, applies editorial skill to turn raw material into a distinct work, and distributes that work to an audience presents the paradigm the drafters had in view. The category is defined by function rather than by credential, so the inquiry runs to what the requester actually does with information and whether dissemination is the main line of activity, and a requester who cannot show that function cannot borrow the urgency prong however pressing the deadline pressure may be.

Urgency, in turn, is measured against the value of the information to the public debate it would inform, and that value decays. The subject must be actual or alleged federal government activity, a matter of what the government is doing or is said to have done, rather than a topic of private or commercial concern that happens to sit in agency files. A story tied to a scheduled vote, a pending rulemaking comment period, or an unfolding official inquiry loses its informing power on a schedule the requester does not control, and that decay is what the urgency element captures; information that will be exactly as useful in eighteen months is, by statutory design, information that can wait eighteen months. The conjunction of the three elements explains the shape of the decided disputes in this area: expedition is won by requesters who can show an occupational stake in dissemination, a federal activity at the center of the records, and a clock imposed by events rather than by preference.

### The Ten Day Decision on an Expedition Request

The statute imposes the fastest decision deadline in the entire FOIA scheme. Regulations must ensure that a determination of whether to provide expedited processing is made, and notice of the determination given to the requester, within ten days after the date on which the expedition request is made, a period the administering guidance counts in calendar days rather than working days, in pointed contrast to the twenty working days allowed for the determination on the underlying request. The ten-day call is a decision about queue position only; it resolves nothing about releasability, exemptions, or fees. Its function is to tell the requester, while the urgency that justified the application still exists, whether the request has been moved forward or left in place, and a denial at this stage is immediately consequential because the ordinary queue position is the practical determinant of when the records will move.

A denial is not the end of the mechanism. The statute requires expeditious consideration of administrative appeals of expedition determinations, so the appeal travels on an accelerated path of its own rather than entering the ordinary appellate backlog. When expedition is granted, the obligation that follows is framed in deliberately unscheduled terms: the agency must process the request as soon as practicable. That phrase confers priority without conferring a date. The expedited request goes to the front of the work, ahead of non-expedited requests in the applicable processing track, but the search still has to be run, the records still have to be reviewed line by line, consultations with other components or agencies still have to be completed, and the production that results can still take weeks or months on a large or sensitive record set. Requesters and agencies dispute expedition so vigorously precisely because both sides understand this arithmetic: expedition is worth having because queue position dominates outcomes, and it is worth resisting because every request moved forward moves every other request back.

The distance between the statutory ten-day standard and administrative performance is measurable, and the measurement undercuts any assumption that the fastest deadline in the statute is the one most reliably kept. The Department of Justice Office of Information Policy, in its Summary of Annual FOIA Reports for Fiscal Year 2020, released on May 13, 2021, reported 26,476 agency adjudications of expedited processing requests across the government for that fiscal year. Those decisions took an average of 14.09 days, and only 68.3 percent of them were decided within ten calendar days. The average agency answer to the question Congress required to be answered in ten days therefore arrived more than four days late, and nearly a third of requesters who had claimed a compelling need waited beyond the statutory period merely to learn whether the claim had been accepted, by which point some part of the urgency underlying the application had already been spent.

### How do processing tracks organize the requests that are not expedited?

Expedition operates inside a larger architecture of ordered waiting that the same 1996 amendments authorized. Section 552(a)(6)(D) permits each agency to adopt regulations providing for multitrack processing, in which requests are sorted into tracks based on the amount of work or time, or both, that processing is expected to involve.

The simple request, narrow in scope and aimed at a single office, travels on a fast track; the complex request, spanning components, years, and record systems, travels on a slow one; and expedition, where granted, cuts across the arrangement. The premise of the multitrack system is that strict first-in, first-out ordering across an entire agency would punish the requester who asked for one identifiable file by placing that request behind a million-page production that arrived the day before, and the track structure converts a single queue into several queues ordered by difficulty.

The statute attaches a condition that protects the requester sorted into a slow track. Regulations under the multitrack provision may give a person whose request does not qualify for the fastest track an opportunity to limit the scope of the request in order to qualify for faster processing, which makes narrowing a request a procedural right rather than merely a negotiating suggestion. The same provision states that multitrack processing does not affect the duty to exercise due diligence, the standard a court applies when an agency that has missed its deadlines asks to retain jurisdiction and continue working rather than face an immediate production order. Within each track, order of arrival remains the default rule, and the deviations from it are the ones the system itself defines: an expedited request, a request narrowed into a faster track, or a court order that resequences production in litigation.

These features explain why expedition disputes, when they reach litigation, are so often fought on declarations about queue position rather than on the contents of any record. A court asked to review an expedition denial, or to supervise an agency that claims diligence, needs facts the requester cannot see: how many requests stand ahead in the track, how many of those are themselves expedited, what staffing the component assigns to the queue, and what rate of processing the backlog has actually sustained. Agencies supply those facts through declarations from FOIA officials describing the queue, and requesters attack the declarations for generality, arguing that aggregate numbers conceal the discretionary choices, the consultations left unanswered, the searches not yet run, that determine real movement. The queue is therefore not an administrative backdrop to the statute but one of its operative legal facts: the determination deadline, the expedition standard, the multitrack authority, and the due diligence inquiry all resolve, in practice, into the question of where a request stands in line and how fast the line is moving.

## Inside the Processing Machine: Review, Referral, and the Backlog

A FOIA request that has been written well and routed correctly enters a working system rather than a queue in the abstract, and the shape of that system explains most of what happens next, including most delay. The Freedom of Information Act does not leave the internal organization of disclosure to chance. The statute names the officers who answer for it, the offices a requester can contact, and the outside body that exists to mediate when the process breaks down, and around that statutory skeleton every agency has built a processing machine of intake units, search assignments, consultations, and review lines. Understanding the machine matters because the largest single variable in FOIA outcomes is neither the exemptions nor the courts. It is the ordinary administrative passage of a request through offices that hold other duties, other deadlines, and finite staff.

### Who inside an agency is responsible for FOIA processing?

The statute assigns responsibility at three levels. At the top of each agency sits a Chief FOIA Officer, a senior official charged with agency-wide compliance: monitoring implementation, recommending adjustments to agency practices and staffing, and answering for the disclosure program as a whole rather than for any single request.

Below that level, FOIA Requester Service Centers serve as the first point of contact for requesters, the offices that can say where a request stands, which component holds it, and what stage it has reached. Between the requester and the agency stands the FOIA Public Liaison, an official whose statutory function is supervisory and mediating at once, available to resolve disputes that the ordinary channel has not resolved and empowered to raise a stalled or mishandled request inside the agency. A requester who treats these three offices as interchangeable misses the design. The Service Center reports status, the Liaison intervenes in disputes, and the Chief FOIA Officer owns the system that produced both the status and the dispute.

Outside the agency entirely sits the Office of Government Information Services, housed in the National Archives and Records Administration. The OPEN Government Act of 2007, Public Law 110-175, created the office, and it began operating in 2009 as the FOIA ombudsman for the executive branch. Its services are voluntary on both sides: it offers mediation and other dispute resolution to requesters and agencies that agree to use it, and it reviews agency policies, procedures, and compliance, recommending changes to Congress and the President. It cannot order an agency to release a record, and its existence has not displaced either the administrative appeal or the lawsuit. What it added to the system was a party with no stake in any particular withholding, positioned between a requester who believes the machine has failed and an agency that believes it has not.

### From Search to Release: The Processing Sequence

Processing begins with a search, and the search is a distributed exercise. The FOIA office that logged the request rarely holds the records itself. It tasks the components, program offices, and field establishments likely to hold responsive material, and those offices search their own files, shared drives, email systems, and record holdings, in the places the request reasonably points to and under the search standard the courts enforce. Where records sit in field facilities or other establishments separate from the office processing the request, collection itself becomes a stage of the work, and the statute recognizes that geography in its definition of unusual circumstances, which covers the need to search for and collect records from separate facilities as one of the three grounds on which the determination deadline may be extended. A decentralized department may have dozens of components processing requests in parallel, each with its own intake and its own backlog, which is why two requests filed on the same day to the same department can move at entirely different speeds.

Search results converge on review, and review is the stage where the volume of modern FOIA work is actually felt. Responsive records are examined page by page and, within pages, line by line, because exemption analysis attaches to information rather than to documents. A single memorandum may contain releasable narrative, a deliberative passage protected by Exemption 5, a name protected by Exemption 6, and a paragraph whose disclosure an agency believes would cause foreseeable harm, and the reviewer must separate those strands before anything can move. The segregability principle governs the exercise: reasonably segregable portions of a record must be released even where other portions are properly withheld, so withholding is performed with a marker and a redaction rather than by removing the document from the production. Review of this kind is performed partly by FOIA professionals and partly by subject matter personnel, the program staff who understand what a record means and what its release would expose, and the second group is the scarcer resource, because FOIA review is an additional duty laid over the work those employees were hired to do.

Two further stages intervene between review and release in any request of size. The first is consultation and referral. Records frequently contain material that originated with another component or another agency, or in which another office holds the governing interest, and the processing office does not decide the fate of that material alone. It consults the office whose equity is implicated, or refers the record to that office for direct response to the requester, and it waits for an answer that arrives on the other office's schedule rather than its own. The statute again anticipates the friction: consultation with another agency having a substantial interest in the determination, or among two or more components with substantial subject matter interest, is the third of the unusual circumstances that justify extending the determination period, provided the consultation is conducted with all practicable speed. The second stage is the application of the foreseeable harm standard, codified by the FOIA Improvement Act of 2016, Public Law 114-185, at 5 U.S.C. 552(a)(8). Under that provision an agency may withhold information only where the agency reasonably foresees that disclosure would harm an interest protected by an exemption, or where disclosure is prohibited by law, so the reviewer must articulate a harm, tied to the specific material, rather than rest on the exemption category alone.

Where the responsive set is large, agencies commonly produce records on a rolling basis, releasing tranches as review of each tranche completes instead of holding the entire production for the last page. Rolling release serves both parties in principle: the requester receives material while the remainder is still in review, and the agency converts one enormous deadline into a sequence of manageable ones. In litigation, rolling schedules are frequently the substance of the court's order or the parties' agreement, stated as a number of pages processed per month, and the processing rate itself becomes the contested number around which the dispute turns.

### The Government-Wide Backlog in the Annual Reporting

Every agency reports its FOIA activity annually, and the Department of Justice Office of Information Policy consolidates those reports into a government wide summary. The Summary of Annual FOIA Reports for Fiscal Year 2020, released on May 13, 2021, recorded agencies receiving more than 790,000 requests and processing more than 770,000 in that fiscal year, a throughput of more than three thousand requests per working day across the executive branch. The same summary recorded a backlog, requests pending beyond the statutory time period, of 141,762 at the end of fiscal year 2020, against 120,436 at the end of fiscal year 2019, an increase of 21,326 in a single year. The backlog was also concentrated rather than evenly spread. Five departments, Homeland Security, Defense, Justice, State, and Health and Human Services, together accounted for 74 percent of all backlogged requests, reflecting the simple fact that request volume itself is concentrated in the departments that run immigration, military, law enforcement, diplomatic, and public health programs. A backlog figure therefore measures a queue, not a verdict: it counts requests that have outlasted the statutory period, without distinguishing the request delayed by a genuinely enormous scope from the request delayed because a consultation answer never arrived.

### Why do requests take so long if the statute allows twenty working days?

The honest answer has two parts, and the statute's design requires both to be stated. The first part is structural. The twenty working day period governs the determination, and the volume arriving at large agencies exceeds the review capacity the period presupposes, so queues form at the search, consultation, and review stages even in offices working steadily.

Some requests are extremely broad, sweeping in years of correspondence across many custodians, and processing them requires line by line review of enormous record sets by subject matter staff whose principal duties lie elsewhere. Delay of this character occurs under administrations of both parties, grows and shrinks with request volume and appropriations, and reflects arithmetic as much as attitude. The Government Accountability Office and the Justice Department reports that document the backlog document volume alongside it, and the same fiscal year 2020 that added 21,326 requests to the backlog also processed more than 770,000.

The second part is that arithmetic does not explain everything, and a treatment that stopped at volume would be as incomplete as one that attributed all delay to obstruction. Requests also stall because consultations go unanswered inside agencies, because components dispute which office owns a record, because review is assigned to officials with incentives to protect their programs, and because a requester has no lever short of litigation to move a request that an office would prefer to leave at the bottom of the pile. The statutory furniture described in this section exists largely because Congress recognized both halves of the problem: the Public Liaison and the Office of Government Information Services address the request that is stuck rather than merely queued, the foreseeable harm standard disciplines withholdings that rest on category rather than consequence, and annual reporting with published backlogs makes the queue itself visible. The backlog is best read as the sum of a real workload problem and a real accountability problem, in proportions that vary by agency and by request, and the processing machine the statute built is the arena in which both are worked out.

## Denials, Administrative Appeals, and the Road to Court

A processing sequence that began with receipt, search, and review often ended with less than the full record a requester sought at the outset. The Freedom of Information Act, codified at 5 U.S.C. 552, treated that outcome as a formal legal event called an adverse determination. An adverse determination withheld records in whole or in part, denied a fee benefit, denied expedited processing, or resolved another contested element against the position taken by the requester. Because the determination started the clock for an administrative appeal and framed the record for any later case in federal court, the precision of the denial letter mattered as much as the search that preceded it.

The spectrum of adverse outcomes ran from a complete denial, in which an agency withheld every responsive record, to partial denials that accounted for much of the practical work of the system. An agency might release most responsive material and withhold a discrete set of documents outright under one or more of the nine exemptions. An agency might instead release a document in redacted form, with names, figures, or paragraphs removed and exemption codes marked beside the deletions, so that a single page contained both a grant and a denial. An agency might also report that a search reasonably calculated to uncover all relevant documents produced no responsive records at all, a result that functioned as a denial of access. Each outcome was adverse in whole or in part, and each carried the same statutory notices.

### What Must a FOIA Denial Letter Include?

A valid adverse determination states the determination and its reasons, names each person responsible for the denial with title or position, gives notice of help from the FOIA Public Liaison, states the right to appeal within at least ninety days, and states the right to seek dispute resolution services.

The requirement that a notice state the determination and the reasons for it, set out at 5 U.S.C. 552(a)(6)(A)(i), prevented a bare refusal that gave a requester nothing to answer in return. Reasons meant the exemption or exemptions relied upon for each withholding and an explanation sufficient to connect the withheld material to the terms of the exemption claimed. A claim under Exemption 6 gained meaning only when the notice identified the privacy interest at stake and the absence of a countervailing public interest in disclosure. A claim under Exemption 7 gained meaning only when the notice tied the records to a law enforcement function and to one of the harms enumerated in that exemption. The stated reasons committed the component to a rationale that an appeal or a court case would later test.

Accountability formed a separate and unusually specific element of the notice. Section 552(a)(6)(C) provided that a denial notice set forth the names and titles or positions of each person responsible for the denial. The provision reached beyond the signature on the letter, because processing often involved an analyst who recommended withholding, a supervisor who approved the recommendation, and an official in a program office or a legal office who concurred. The statute required identification of each person who bore responsibility for the outcome. A notice that named the deciding officials showed whether a denial rested on a routine processing judgment or on a considered position taken at a senior level, information of direct use in preparing an appeal.

The notice also had to inform a requester of the right to seek assistance from the FOIA Public Liaison of the agency. The liaison was a supervisory official designated as a point of contact distinct from the processing staff who had handled the request. Assistance covered questions about status, explanations of the process that produced a determination, and help with disagreements that had not hardened into a formal appeal. Alongside that notice, an adverse determination had to state the right to appeal to the head of the agency. The appeal period was set by the head of the agency, subject to a statutory floor of not less than 90 days from the date of the adverse determination. The FOIA Improvement Act of 2016, Public Law 114-185, established that ninety day minimum, reflecting a judgment that a requester needed a realistic interval to obtain advice, review a redacted production, and draft grounds of challenge.

A further required notice informed the requester of the right to seek dispute resolution services from the FOIA Public Liaison or from the Office of Government Information Services. The pairing mattered, because the liaison offered resolution from inside the agency that held the records, while the Office of Government Information Services, located within the National Archives and Records Administration, offered a forum outside the agency whose decision was under challenge. The denial letter itself therefore identified three distinct paths: an appeal to the head of the agency, assistance or dispute resolution through the liaison, and dispute resolution through a government wide ombuds office. The statute placed the burden of explanation on the agency at the moment of denial.

### How Long Does a FOIA Appeal Take?

An agency must decide an administrative appeal within twenty working days after receipt under the statute. If the denial is upheld in whole or in part, the agency must notify the requester of the provisions for judicial review, so the next step toward court is identified in the appeal decision itself.

Filing an administrative appeal set a second statutory clock running. Under 5 U.S.C. 552(a)(6)(A)(ii), an agency was required to make a determination with respect to an appeal within 20 days, excluding Saturdays, Sundays, and legal public holidays, after receipt of the appeal. The period matched the twenty working day period for an initial determination, because Congress treated the appeal as a genuine second determination rather than an open ended reconsideration. The appeal went to the head of the agency, which in practice meant a designated appellate authority, often a senior official in a general counsel office or an appeals unit that had not made the initial determination. That separation supplied the central value of the stage: a fresh examination by an official with authority to reverse the component, order a further search, or narrow an exemption claim.

An appellate official could review the withheld records without the procedural apparatus of litigation and could consult the program office that created the records about the harm disclosure might cause. Material could also be released on discretionary grounds where an exemption permitted withholding but did not compel it. An appeal allowed a requester to supply argument and context that the initial request had not contained, including the public interest served by disclosure or evidence that information in the public domain undercut a withholding rationale. Because the appellate decision rested on submissions from both sides, the appeal built a fuller administrative record than the initial determination had produced. That record became the foundation for any later dispute about fees, search adequacy, or exemption claims.

Exemption claims formed the most common subject of an appeal, and the appeal gave a requester the first structured opportunity to contest those claims in argument. A requester could argue that a record fell outside the terms of an exemption altogether, or that segregable portions of a withheld document should have been released with only exempt passages removed. A requester could also argue that the foreseeable harm standard codified at 5 U.S.C. 552(a)(8) by the FOIA Improvement Act of 2016 had not been met, because the agency had identified no harm to an interest protected by the exemption. The appellate official had to engage those arguments in a written decision, producing a more precise statement of the agency position. A claim abandoned or narrowed on appeal never reached a court at all. A claim sustained in a reasoned appellate decision entered litigation in a stronger position.

Appeals also reached the financial rulings that shaped whether a requester could use the Act at all. A fee category determination could be appealed, and the distinction carried real consequences, because commercial use requesters paid search, review, and duplication charges, while representatives of the news media and educational or noncommercial scientific institutions paid duplication charges only, and all other requesters paid search and duplication charges. A denial of a fee waiver could likewise be appealed. The waiver standard turned on whether disclosure was in the public interest because the information was likely to contribute significantly to public understanding of the operations or activities of the government and was not primarily in the commercial interest of the requester. An appellate reversal changed the economics of a request before production. An appellate affirmance created the record on which a court would later rule.

Denials of expedited processing stood on a separate and faster appellate track. Expedited processing was available where a requester demonstrated a compelling need, defined through an imminent threat to the life or physical safety of an individual, or through an urgency to inform the public concerning actual or alleged federal government activity on the part of a person primarily engaged in disseminating information. An agency was required to determine a request for expedition within ten days after the date of the request. Section 552(a)(6)(E) provided that an appeal of a denial of expedition received expeditious consideration. The accelerated appeal matched the underlying benefit, because expedition granted after months of delay would have no value to a requester facing a threat to safety or working against the decay of news value. A grant on appeal moved the underlying request forward for processing as soon as practicable, without deciding any exemption claim.

When an appeal ended, a final notice duty connected the administrative process to the courts. If an agency upheld a denial in whole or in part on appeal, the agency was required to notify the requester of the provisions for judicial review of that determination. Judicial review under 5 U.S.C. 552(a)(4)(B) lay in the district where the complainant resided or had a principal place of business, in the district where the agency records were situated, or in the District of Columbia. The court determined the matter de novo, could examine the records in camera, and placed the burden on the agency to sustain the withholding. The notice told a requester, in the appeal decision itself, that the administrative road had ended and the courthouse road had opened. A partial affirmance carried the same duty, because release of some records on appeal left the right to sue over records that remained withheld.

The appeal before litigation rested on exhaustion, and the statute stated the doctrine and its central exception in a single provision. Section 552(a)(6)(C) provided that a requester was deemed to have exhausted administrative remedies if the agency failed to comply with the applicable time limit for a determination on a request or on an appeal. An agency that allowed twenty working days, and any lawful extension, to pass without a determination could not then insist that a requester wait before filing suit. Constructive exhaustion, as the rule was known, opened the courthouse door on the strength of agency inaction alone. A court could nevertheless retain jurisdiction and allow additional time on a showing of exceptional circumstances and due diligence by the government. Delay from a predictable agency workload did not by itself qualify as exceptional circumstances unless the agency demonstrated reasonable progress in reducing a backlog.

The exception to constructive exhaustion came from Oglesby v. U.S. Department of the Army, 920 F.2d 57 (D.C. Cir. 1990), and turned on the difference between an agency that had said nothing and an agency that had spoken late. In Oglesby, the agency missed the deadline for an initial determination but issued a determination before the requester filed suit. The United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit held that a requester in that posture was required to pursue the administrative appeal before litigating. The reasoning followed from the purpose of exhaustion, which was to give the agency a full opportunity to apply its expertise and correct its own errors before a court intervened. A late determination restored that opportunity, because an appeal could resolve the dispute without litigation. The deeming rule applied while no determination existed, and the obligation to appeal attached once a determination existed, even one that arrived after the deadline.

Dispute resolution offered a path between a denial and either a formal appeal or a lawsuit, and the liaison stood at the center of that path. Under Section 552(a)(6)(B)(ii), an agency that could not meet even an extended time limit was required to offer a requester an opportunity to limit the scope of a request so that the request could be processed within the limit, or to arrange an alternative time frame. The liaison was available to assist in that negotiation and to notify the requester of dispute resolution services from the Office of Government Information Services. The negotiation involved concrete trade offs: a requester might exclude a low value category of records, agree to a rolling release in which the most important records arrived first, or accept a later date for a complete production. A refusal to reasonably modify a request or arrange an alternative time frame was a factor in the exceptional circumstances analysis, which gave the negotiation legal weight.

The Office of Government Information Services supplied the external counterpart to the internal liaison. Created by the OPEN Government Act of 2007, Public Law 110-175, and operating from 2009 within the National Archives and Records Administration, the Office offered mediation and other dispute resolution services as a voluntary alternative to litigation. Mediation placed a neutral intermediary between a requester and an agency and allowed each side to explain a position without the formality of an appeal brief or a complaint. Mediation could produce outcomes that neither an appeal nor a court order could easily deliver, such as a reformulated request answerable from a different record system or a release schedule suited to both sides. Participation did not displace appeal rights. A requester who pursued mediation preserved the ability to appeal within the statutory period or to proceed to court after exhaustion.

The appeal record framed any litigation that followed. A court confronted an agency that had committed to a rationale twice, once at the component level and once on appeal, and the appellate decision often supplied the clearest statement of the grounds defended. Where an agency defended withholdings in court, the Vaughn index that took its name from Vaughn v. Rosen, 484 F.2d 820 (D.C. Cir. 1973), required an itemized correlation of each withheld document or portion with a specific exemption claim and a justification, and arguments developed on appeal shaped the entries the index had to support. Fee questions showed the framing effect in its strictest form, because fee waiver disputes were reviewed de novo but limited to the agency record. Arguments or evidence not presented during the administrative stages, including the appeal, could not be introduced for the first time in court. The appeal stage therefore set the evidentiary and argumentative boundaries of a future case.

A requester who treated an adverse determination as the start of a defined sequence held the full set of tools the statute provided. The denial letter disclosed who had decided, why records had been withheld, where help could be obtained, how long an appeal could be taken, and which dispute resolution services stood available. The appeal tested the withholding before the head of the agency under a twenty working day deadline, reached fee and expedition rulings alongside exemption claims, and closed, if the denial stood, with notice of judicial review. Exhaustion policed the boundary between the forums, excusing the appeal where an agency had defaulted on deadlines and requiring the appeal where a late determination had given the administrative process something to review, as Oglesby established in 1990. Mediation through a liaison or through the Office of Government Information Services ran beside the formal sequence throughout.

## The Two Judicial Devices and De Novo Review in District Court

Litigation marks the point at which processing of a request under the Freedom of Information Act, codified at 5 U.S.C. 552, moves from an administrative exchange controlled by agency timetables to a proceeding controlled by a federal district court. Inside an agency, the central issues concern receipt, search, review, determination, and appeal. In court, the central issue concerns proof. An agency that has withheld records, delayed a determination, or refused to confirm or deny that records exist must defend that action with evidence and justification sufficient to satisfy a judge who owes no deference to the administrative outcome. Section 552(a)(4)(B) supplies the foundation for that proceeding, and two decisions of the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit supply the devices through which most disputes are organized.

### Where Can a Requester File a FOIA Lawsuit and How Does the Court Review the Agency Decision?

A requester may file suit in federal district court where the requester resides or has a principal place of business, where the records are situated, or in the District of Columbia. The court reviews the matter de novo, and the agency bears the burden of sustaining its withholding or other action.

Venue under section 552(a)(4)(B) offers a complainant a choice among districts connected to the complainant, districts connected to the records, and the District of Columbia without regard to either connection. A complainant who resides in one district may file there even when the component that processed the request and the files that contain the records sit elsewhere. A business or organization with a principal place of business in a district may use that district on the same basis. A complainant may instead file where the records are situated, tying the forum to the location of the material in dispute. The District of Columbia option stands apart because the statute names that district directly, so a complainant need not establish residence, a principal place of business, or the location of records there to invoke it.

De novo determination distinguishes a Freedom of Information Act suit from ordinary review of agency action on a closed administrative record. A de novo proceeding asks the district court to decide the matter anew, on the evidence and argument presented in the judicial proceeding, rather than asking only whether the agency reached a permissible conclusion on the material assembled during processing. An agency cannot rest on a denial letter that recited an exemption in general terms and expect affirmance because the letter issued through proper channels. The agency must instead present a justification that persuades the court that each challenged withholding, refusal, or delay rests on the statute. De novo review converts the administrative file from a boundary on the dispute into a starting point for a judicial record built through declarations, indexes, and briefing.

The statute pairs de novo review with an express allocation of proof by providing that the burden is on the agency to sustain its action. Placement of the burden follows from the distribution of information between the parties. The agency conducted the search, examined the records, applied the exemptions, and knows the filing systems, custodians, and consultations that produced the response. The requester ordinarily knows only the wording of the request, the correspondence received, and whatever descriptions the agency supplied for material withheld. A rule requiring the requester to disprove an exemption claim for a document never seen would make review depend on speculation. Congress placed the burden on the party holding the records and the knowledge, so a gap in the agency account counts against the agency rather than against the party excluded from the underlying material.

The same paragraph authorizes the court to examine the contents of agency records in camera. In camera examination means inspection by the judge outside the presence of the requester and counsel for the requester, so the court can assess contested records without causing the disclosure the suit seeks to compel. The authority is discretionary, and the discretion matters because direct inspection is both the most reliable check on an exemption claim and a burden that displaces the ordinary adversarial method. A judge who reads a withheld passage can compare the passage against the claimed exemption with precision no description can match, yet routine inspection of every withheld page in a large case would transfer the review function from agency reviewers to the court. Courts therefore treat declarations and indexes as the primary tools of review and reserve inspection for disputes in which descriptions leave a material doubt that briefing cannot resolve.

Section 552(a)(4)(C) requires the agency to answer within 30 days after service of the complaint unless the court directs otherwise for good cause. The period is shorter than the timetable governing many categories of federal litigation, and the compression signals that a case is not meant to spend an extended period in pleading before the substantive dispute over records takes shape. An answer rarely resolves the merits, since entitlement turns on search adequacy and exemption claims that require an evidentiary submission, but the answer fixes the agency position, identifies defenses, and starts the case toward scheduling. A court may direct a different period for good cause, a qualification that gives the district court control over cases in which service or consultation among components makes the statutory period unworkable in a particular instance.

Most litigation under the statute is conducted largely on agency declarations and summary judgment rather than through trial. The pattern follows from the kind of dispute the statute creates. The material facts usually concern what files were searched, what methods were used, what records were located, what portions were withheld, and what protection an exemption addresses, and those facts can ordinarily be presented in sworn written statements without live testimony. A declaration allows an official with knowledge of processing to describe the search and the withholding decisions in a form the court can test against the request and the correspondence. Summary judgment then allows the court to resolve the case on written submissions when the declarations and opposing argument show the legal outcome without a need for fact finding through witnesses.

The standard governing declarations requires them to be relatively detailed and nonconclusory. A detailed declaration identifies the files searched, the systems consulted, and the search terms or methods used, in the formulation associated with the search standard of a search reasonably calculated to uncover all relevant documents, articulated in Weisberg v. Department of Justice, 705 F.2d 1344 (D.C. Cir. 1983). A nonconclusory declaration does more than announce that a search was adequate, that a record was exempt, or that disclosure would cause harm. Detail connects conclusion to operation by showing which repositories were included, why those repositories were the places reasonably expected to hold responsive material, and how electronic and paper holdings were approached. Conclusory language breaks that chain and leaves the court with an assertion that asks for deference the statute does not grant.

### What Is a Vaughn Index and Why Does It Control Most FOIA Litigation?

A Vaughn index is an itemized agency submission that correlates each withheld document or portion with a specific exemption and a justification for withholding. The index allows the court to test exemption claims and allows the requester to challenge those claims without access to the withheld records themselves.

The Vaughn index arose from Vaughn v. Rosen, 484 F.2d 820 (D.C. Cir. 1973), cert. denied, 415 U.S. 977 (1974). The problem the decision addressed was structural. A requester challenging a withholding could not describe the withheld material with precision, because the agency held the material and the requester had never seen it. An agency defending a withholding could describe the material in terms so general that no meaningful challenge was possible, because a category label and an exemption citation reveal almost nothing about a particular passage. A court caught between a blind challenge and a general defense could neither test the exemption claim nor conduct de novo review. The Vaughn solution required an itemized index correlating each withheld document or portion with a specific exemption claim and a justification, so the court could test the claims and the requester could argue effectively without seeing the records.

A properly constructed index functions as the central evidentiary and argumentative document in a withholding dispute. For the court, the index translates a volume of unseen records into a series of testable propositions, each asserting that identified material falls within an identified exemption for stated reasons. A judge can accept one proposition and reject another, sustain withholding of one portion and order release of a different portion, and identify the exact entry at which a justification fails without disturbing entries that succeed. For the requester, the index supplies the first usable description of the material in dispute and makes focused argument possible. Counsel can concentrate briefing on entries in which the stated justification does not match the described material. For the agency, the index imposes discipline before any judge rules, because an official who must write a justification for each withheld portion must examine that portion closely enough to state a ground.

Vaughn practice shapes briefing and staged production schedules throughout a case. Litigation commonly proceeds through an agreement or court order setting dates for production of nonexempt material, submission of the index, filing of motions, and resolution of disputes entry by entry. Staging matters when the volume of responsive records is large, because an index prepared for every withheld item at once may delay argument on material that could be resolved earlier, while an index prepared in tranches matched to productions allows briefing to begin on completed portions and allows the court to supervise progress in measurable increments. When in camera review becomes necessary, the index tells the court which records or samples correspond to contested propositions, so inspection proceeds by reference to entries rather than by an unguided reading of a file.

The limits of the Vaughn device are the limits of description. An index entry must reveal enough to justify withholding without revealing protected content, and that balance produces recurring disputes over specificity. An entry that describes a record in terms borrowed from the exemption, without facts about the record, offers correlation in form and generality in substance. An entry that describes a record in exhaustive detail may compromise the interest the exemption protects, particularly when the description is served on the requester in open briefing. A court evaluates the balance under the same burden allocation that governs the case, so an agency that chooses brevity for reasons of protection must supply enough information, in the index, in declarations, or through in camera submission, to allow the court to sustain the action.

### When Can an Agency Refuse to Confirm or Deny That Records Exist?

An agency may refuse to confirm or deny the existence of records only when the fact of existence or nonexistence is itself protected by an exemption. Under Phillippi v. CIA, confirmation alone could reveal classified information, so neither confirmation nor denial was required where that threshold fact remained classified.

The Glomar response originates in Phillippi v. CIA, 546 F.2d 1009 (D.C. Cir. 1976). Journalist Harriet Ann Phillippi sought records of Central Intelligence Agency contacts with the press about the Hughes Glomar Explorer, the vessel built to raise a sunken Soviet submarine. The agency position sustained in that litigation was that the agency could neither confirm nor deny the existence of records responsive to the request, because the existence or nonexistence of such records was itself classified. The court sustained that position under Exemption 1 and Exemption 3. An ordinary exemption claim concedes that a record exists and argues that the content may be withheld. A Glomar response argues that the threshold fact of existence carries protected information, so even an answer to the existence question would disclose the matter the exemptions guard. Confirmation that contacts occurred would reveal an operational fact, and denial would reveal the opposite operational fact.

The name Glomar, drawn from the vessel at the center of the Phillippi litigation, came to describe a category of response rather than a single ruling. The response appears at the implementation stage when an agency answers a request without confirming or denying that responsive records exist, and the response reappears in litigation when the agency must defend that threshold refusal. A Glomar defense reorganizes the usual sequence of a case. Search adequacy recedes because the agency has not acknowledged a body of records in the ordinary sense, Vaughn indexing gives way to declarations about the existence question, and the exemption inquiry focuses on a single proposition, that confirmation or denial would itself cause the harm or reveal the matter an exemption protects. Used beyond the limits Phillippi established, the response would allow an agency to place an entire subject outside inquiry by assertion, and judicial review exists in large part to police that boundary.

The limits of Glomar are as important as the doctrine itself. An agency invoking the response must justify why confirmation itself would cause the protected harm, and that justification carries the same burden the agency bears throughout a case under section 552(a)(4)(B). A bare assertion that existence is classified repeats the conclusion a court must test and fails the relatively detailed and nonconclusory standard governing agency declarations. A sufficient justification explains the connection between the existence fact and the protected interest with as much specificity as the subject allows, showing why an answer in either direction would reveal information within Exemption 1 or Exemption 3. Courts test the logic on declarations, examining whether the stated connection holds together and whether the category of records described in the request matches the category for which the existence fact is claimed to be protected. A justification that explains harm from confirmation but not from denial invites rejection, and rejection of a Glomar claim returns the agency to ordinary obligations of confirming existence, conducting a search, and defending any withholding of content through the Vaughn process.

Attorney fees and litigation costs introduce a financial consequence that operates alongside orders for release. Under section 552(a)(4)(E), a court may assess reasonable attorney fees and other litigation costs reasonably incurred against the United States in a case in which the complainant has substantially prevailed. The standard reaches beyond a final judgment ordering disclosure. A complainant may substantially prevail through a judicial order or through a voluntary or unilateral change in agency position, provided the claim is not insubstantial. The voluntary change prong recognizes litigation dynamics in which an agency releases records after suit is filed and before judgment, a sequence that would otherwise allow an agency to avoid a fee assessment by surrendering records at a late stage while leaving the requester to bear the cost of the suit that produced the release. The not insubstantial qualification performs the opposite limiting function by withholding fee eligibility from a claim that lacked a substantive basis.

The exceptional circumstances doctrine governs the situation in which an agency, rather than defending a withholding on the merits, asks the court for more time to process a request. Section 552(a)(6)(C) provides that a requester is deemed to have exhausted administrative remedies when an agency fails to comply with an applicable time limit, which allows suit to proceed, and the same provision allows a court to retain jurisdiction and allow additional time when the government shows exceptional circumstances and that the agency is exercising due diligence. Open America v. Watergate Special Prosecution Force, 547 F.2d 605 (D.C. Cir. 1976), decided July 7, 1976, is the foundational authority for that arrangement. The case arose from a Federal Bureau of Investigation backlog under the earlier ten working day determination period, and the District of Columbia Circuit accepted a structure in which the court kept the case and the agency continued processing under supervision.

Each element of the Open America showing carries independent weight. Exceptional circumstances must be exceptional, a showing that separates the case from the ordinary condition of an agency with more requests than staff. Due diligence must also be shown, through evidence of processing steps taken, the order in which requests are handled, the resources applied, and the pace at which the particular request advances. The statute expressly narrows the first element by providing that exceptional circumstances does not include delay resulting from a predictable agency workload unless the agency demonstrates reasonable progress in reducing its existing backlog. Predictability is the disqualifying feature, because a workload that recurs in the ordinary course represents a planning condition rather than an emergency, and an agency that could invoke routine volume indefinitely would convert the statutory deadline into a default that applied only when compliance was convenient. The backlog progress qualification restores a path for an agency with heavy volume, but only on proof of movement, measured through evidence that the backlog is shrinking under the efforts described.

The doctrines governing litigation interact as parts of a single system. De novo review and the agency burden establish who must persuade and by what standard of scrutiny. The 30 day answer requirement moves a case into court supervision without an extended pleading interval. Declarations and summary judgment supply the evidentiary method, the Vaughn index supplies the organizing document for withholding disputes, and in camera inspection supplies a reserve check when descriptions cannot carry the burden alone. The Glomar response marks the boundary at which even the existence of records becomes the contested fact, subject to the same burden and the same demand for logical, detailed justification.

## The Pathway as a Whole: Where Requests Are Won and Lost

Most outcomes in the Freedom of Information Act system are decided long before any court becomes involved. Drafting precision decides the first outcome, because a description naming record type, component, date range, subject terms, and custodians defines the search an agency must run and supplies the benchmark against which search adequacy is later judged. A bounded description places the burden of explanation on the agency from the start, since a narrow request processed slowly presents a clearer question on appeal and in litigation than a vast request processed slowly. The component chosen for filing carries the same weight, because receipt by the appropriate component starts the determination clock, while receipt elsewhere consumes the ten day backstop in travel between offices.

The determination distinction decides the second outcome. The twenty working day clock governs a decision whether to comply, supported under Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington v. Federal Election Commission by gathering and review of the documents, communication of the scope intended for production and withholding with reasons, and notice of appeal rights. Production follows under a promptness obligation measured in days or a few weeks in the governing case law, while longer intervals arise from backlog, agreed schedules, or court supervised production rates rather than from a grant of months or years in the statute itself. A requester who understands that structure reads each agency letter accurately: an acknowledgment is not a determination, a determination is not a production, and a missed deadline creates constructive exhaustion subject to the Oglesby qualification where a late determination issued before suit still requires an appeal.

Category and waiver choices decide the third outcome, the price of the pathway and in many cases whether the pathway remains affordable at all. Commercial use status brings search, review, and duplication charges without free allowances. Preferred category status for educational institutions, noncommercial scientific institutions, and news media representatives limits charges to duplication, with the first one hundred pages free. Residual status brings search and duplication with two free hours of search and one hundred free pages. A fee waiver turns on a documented showing about government operations, significant contribution to public understanding, dissemination, and absence of a primary commercial interest, and waiver review in court is de novo but limited to the agency record. Each financial ruling is appealable, and each ruling therefore belongs in the administrative record from the initial request onward.

The administrative record decides the fourth outcome, because appeal and litigation both test the record built during processing. An appeal to the head of the agency, filed within a period of not less than ninety days and decided within twenty working days, gives a fresh official authority to order a further search, narrow an exemption claim, or reverse a fee ruling. Dispute resolution through the FOIA Public Liaison or the Office of Government Information Services can produce narrowed scope, alternative time frames, or rolling release arrangements that neither an appeal decision nor a court order supplies as flexibly. In court, de novo review places the burden on the agency, the Vaughn index correlates each withholding with an exemption and a justification, and the Glomar response protects the fact of existence only where that fact itself falls within an exemption.

A reader who wants to go deeper has a defined study path. The first step is close reading of the statutory text at 5 U.S.C. 552, supported by guidance on [reading a federal statute as an integrated scheme](/2022/05/01/how-to-read-a-federal-statute/), with attention to the determination sentence, the fee paragraphs, the expedition provision, and the foreseeable harm standard at section 552(a)(8). The second step is comparison across the wider series through [the structured study path for the legislation series](/2022/06/15/us-legislation-study-guide/), connecting the Act to neighboring transparency statutes and to the amendment history that produced the twenty day period. The third step is practice with the pathway as examinable material, using [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html) and [ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/us-government-civics-study.html) as companion study tools for the request pathway and fee categories as standard examinable material in administrative law and journalism coursework. Mastery of the pathway rests on sequence: describe precisely, file with the right component, watch the determination clock, document category and waiver at the outset, and appeal on a complete record.

## Frequently Asked Questions



### Q: How do you write a FOIA request that works?

A workable request reasonably describes the records sought so that a professional employee familiar with the subject area can locate the records with reasonable effort. Precision comes from identifiable elements: the record type, the component or office holding the records, a bounded date range, subject terms or identifiers such as a program name or docket number, and named custodians where known. Filing with the appropriate component starts the determination clock without routing delay. Breadth undermines the description, because a request spanning all components and an unbounded period may describe nothing a single custodian can search. A narrow, complete description also creates the benchmark against which search adequacy is later measured on appeal or in court.

### Q: How long does an agency have to answer a FOIA request?

Section 552(a)(6)(A)(i) gives an agency twenty days, excluding Saturdays, Sundays, and legal public holidays, after receipt by the appropriate component to make a determination whether to comply and to notify the requester of the determination and the reasons. The period commences on receipt by the appropriate component and in any event not later than ten days after receipt by a designated component. Tolling is allowed only for one request for information or to clarify fee assessment, ending when the requester responds. Written notice of unusual circumstances can extend the period by no more than ten working days. An appeal determination carries a separate twenty working day period.

### Q: Does a determination on a FOIA request mean the records arrive?

A determination and delivery are distinct statutory events. Under Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington v. Federal Election Commission, 711 F.3d 180 (D.C. Cir. 2013), a determination requires an agency to gather and review the documents, determine and communicate the scope of documents intended for production and withholding with reasons for withholding, and inform the requester of the right to appeal the adverse portion. Production need not coincide with the determination, but section 552(a)(6)(C) requires records to be made promptly available after a determination to comply. The governing decision indicated that days or a few weeks is the expected scale for prompt availability, not months or years as a sanctioned interval.

### Q: What fees apply to a FOIA request?

Fees under section 552(a)(4)(A) are reasonable standard charges tied to direct costs only, and category controls which charges apply. Commercial use requesters pay search, review, and duplication. Educational institutions, noncommercial scientific institutions, and news media representatives pay duplication only. All other requesters pay search and duplication. No fee applies for the first two hours of search or the first one hundred pages of duplication except for commercial use requests. Review covers only initial examination to decide disclosure or withholding and excludes time resolving legal or policy issues. No fee may be assessed where collection cost equals or exceeds the fee, and a public interest waiver can waive or reduce fees.

### Q: How do you get expedited processing of a FOIA request?

Expedited processing under section 552(a)(6)(E) requires a demonstration of compelling need or a category an agency has set by regulation. Compelling need means either that failure to obtain records on an expedited basis could reasonably be expected to pose an imminent threat to the life or physical safety of an individual, or that the requester is a person primarily engaged in disseminating information and faces an urgency to inform the public concerning actual or alleged federal government activity. The agency must determine the expedition request and give notice within ten days after the date of the request. Appeals of expedition denials receive expeditious consideration, and granted requests are processed as soon as practicable.

### Q: What is a Vaughn index in a FOIA request dispute?

A Vaughn index is an itemized agency submission in litigation that correlates each withheld document or portion with a specific exemption claim and a justification for withholding. The device takes the name from Vaughn v. Rosen, 484 F.2d 820 (D.C. Cir. 1973). The index solves a structural problem: the requester has not seen the withheld records and cannot challenge a general description, while the court must test exemption claims on de novo review with the burden on the agency. A sufficient index gives the court testable propositions entry by entry and gives the requester a description detailed enough to frame focused argument without revealing protected content.

### Q: What is a Glomar response to a FOIA request?

A Glomar response is a refusal to confirm or deny the existence of records where the fact of existence or nonexistence is itself protected by an exemption. The response originates in Phillippi v. CIA, 546 F.2d 1009 (D.C. Cir. 1976), where journalist Harriet Ann Phillippi sought records of agency contacts with the press about the Hughes Glomar Explorer, the vessel built to raise a sunken Soviet submarine. The court sustained the position under Exemptions 1 and 3 because confirmation or denial would reveal a classified operational fact. An agency defending a Glomar response must justify why an answer in either direction would cause the protected harm, with detailed and nonconclusory reasoning.

### Q: Can you sue over a FOIA request denial?

Judicial review is available under section 552(a)(4)(B) after exhaustion, or after constructive exhaustion where an agency has failed to comply with an applicable time limit. Suit may be filed in the district where the complainant resides or has a principal place of business, where the agency records are situated, or in the District of Columbia. The court determines the matter de novo, may examine records in camera, and the burden rests on the agency to sustain the action. The agency must answer within thirty days after service unless the court directs otherwise for good cause. Attorney fees may be available where the complainant has substantially prevailed.

### Q: What is a FOIA Public Liaison and what can the liaison do for a requester?

A FOIA Public Liaison is a supervisory agency official designated to assist requesters and to help resolve disputes between a requester and the agency. Every adverse determination notice must inform the requester of the right to seek assistance from the liaison, and the liaison is available where a request cannot be processed within an extended time limit because of unusual circumstances. Assistance can address status questions, explanations of processing, scope limitation, and arrangement of an alternative time frame. The liaison also can notify the requester of dispute resolution services from the Office of Government Information Services. The role is mediating and supervisory within the agency rather than a substitute for a formal appeal.

### Q: What does the Office of Government Information Services do in a FOIA dispute?

The Office of Government Information Services sits within the National Archives and Records Administration and serves as the FOIA ombudsman for the executive branch. The OPEN Government Act of 2007, Public Law 110-175, created the office, and operation began in 2009. The office offers voluntary mediation and other dispute resolution services to requesters and agencies, and reviews agency policies, procedures, and compliance, with recommendations to Congress and the President. Participation is voluntary on both sides and does not displace the administrative appeal or litigation. The office cannot order an agency to release a record, and value lies in a neutral forum without a stake in a particular withholding.

### Q: How can a requester check the status of a pending FOIA request?

Status checks run through FOIA Requester Service Centers, which serve as the first point of contact for requesters about where a request stands, which component holds the request, and what stage processing has reached. Agency tracking information supplied at intake, including a tracking number and component contact information where provided, allows a requester to direct a status inquiry to the office holding the request rather than to a department in general. Where a status inquiry reveals a dispute or a stalled request, the FOIA Public Liaison is the next statutory contact, available to assist in resolving disputes and to notify the requester of dispute resolution services from the Office of Government Information Services.

### Q: What happens when an agency misses the twenty working day deadline?

Section 552(a)(6)(C) deems a requester to have exhausted administrative remedies if an agency fails to comply with an applicable time limit, opening the path to suit. A court may nevertheless retain jurisdiction and allow additional time where the government shows exceptional circumstances and due diligence. The Oglesby qualification limits constructive exhaustion in one posture: under Oglesby v. U.S. Department of the Army, 920 F.2d 57 (D.C. Cir. 1990), where an agency missed the deadline but issued a determination before suit was filed, the requester must still pursue the administrative appeal. A missed deadline also triggers the fee bar in section 552(a)(4)(A)(viii), subject to defined exceptions for noticed unusual circumstances, large productions, and court orders.

### Q: What is multi-track processing of FOIA requests?

Multi-track processing is the system authorized by section 552(a)(6)(D) under which an agency may adopt regulations sorting requests into tracks based on the amount of work or time processing is expected to require. A simple request narrow in scope travels on a faster track, while a complex request spanning components, years, and record systems travels on a slower track. The statute protects a requester placed in a slower track through the opportunity to limit the scope of the request in order to qualify for faster processing. Within each track, order of arrival remains the default, and multi-track processing does not affect the duty to exercise due diligence.

### Q: What is a rolling release of FOIA records?

A rolling release is production of responsive records in tranches as review of each tranche completes, rather than withholding of the entire production until review of the last record finishes. The practice serves both parties where the responsive set is large: the requester receives material while the remainder remains in review, and the agency converts one enormous production obligation into a sequence of manageable stages. In litigation, rolling schedules often form the substance of a court order or an agreement between the parties, stated as a number of pages processed per month, and the processing rate becomes a contested measure of due diligence and prompt availability.

### Q: What happens if a FOIA request does not reasonably describe the records sought?

A request that does not reasonably describe the records sought fails the condition in section 552(a)(3)(A) for processing, because agency staff cannot identify which systems to search or what material to locate without guessing at subject, office, or period. The agency remedy is clarification rather than an unguided excavation of agency files. The agency may make one request for information and toll the twenty working day period while awaiting the requester response. A prompt response that narrows record type, component, date range, subject terms, or custodians can convert an unprocessable description into a searchable task, while an unanswered clarification leaves the request without a defined search to perform.

### Q: Must an agency create a new record in order to answer a FOIA request?

An agency need not create a new record to answer a request. The statute grants access to existing agency records, meaning records created or obtained by the agency and under agency control when the request arrives. The duty is to search for and produce records reasonably described, under a search reasonably calculated to uncover all relevant documents. The statute does not require an agency to answer questions, conduct research for a requester, or compile data into a document assembled for the requester. A request framed as questions about why a decision was made seeks explanations rather than records and is handled through whatever responsive records exist.

### Q: What is the foreseeable harm standard for FOIA withholding?

The foreseeable harm standard sits at section 552(a)(8), codified by the FOIA Improvement Act of 2016, Public Law 114-185. Under the standard, an agency may withhold information only where the agency reasonably foresees that disclosure would harm an interest protected by an exemption, or where disclosure is prohibited by law. The standard requires a reviewer to articulate a harm tied to the specific material and to the interest an exemption protects, rather than resting on the exemption category alone. The standard also interacts with segregability, because reasonably segregable portions of a record must be released even where other portions satisfy both an exemption and the foreseeable harm showing.

### Q: How are FOIA fees estimated before processing begins?

Fee estimation begins with a category decision, because category determines which charges an agency may impose and which free allowances apply. An estimate then states what full processing will cost under the schedule of reasonable standard charges tied to direct costs for search, duplication, and, for commercial use requesters, review. The estimate allows a requester to proceed at the stated scope, narrow the request to reduce searching and duplication, or seek a public interest waiver before charges accumulate. Advance payment is permitted only where the requester previously failed to pay a fee in a timely fashion or where the fee exceeds 250 dollars. Fee clarification can toll the determination period until the requester responds.

### Q: What is the difference between a referral and a consultation in FOIA processing?

A referral transfers a record to the agency or component that originated the record for direct response to the requester, so the originating office makes the determination on that record. A consultation keeps the decision with the processing office after obtaining the views of the office or agency holding an interest in the material. Consultation, conducted with all practicable speed, with another agency having a substantial interest or among components with substantial subject matter interest, is one of the three unusual circumstances that can extend the determination period. Each transfer or consultation adds another office schedule to the pathway, which is why records with equities held elsewhere move more slowly.

### Q: Does the Freedom of Information Act apply to Congress and the federal courts?

The Freedom of Information Act applies to executive branch agencies and reaches neither Congress nor the federal courts. The statutory definition of agency covers executive departments, military departments, government corporations, government controlled corporations, independent regulatory agencies, and other establishments in the executive branch, including the Executive Office of the President with defined exclusions. Congress is a legislative branch institution outside that definition, and the federal courts are judicial branch institutions outside that definition. Records held by Congress or by a federal court therefore fall outside a FOIA search, even where the subject matter concerns government activity, and access to such material depends on rules and practices of the institution holding the material.
