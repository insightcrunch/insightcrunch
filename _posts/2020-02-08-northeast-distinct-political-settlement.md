Northeast India politics operates through constitutional machinery that exists nowhere else in the Indian Union. Eight states at the country's eastern edge, holding about 46 million people on roughly 8 percent of its land area, are governed through a layered architecture of special constitutional articles, autonomous tribal councils, a permit regime that controls the entry of other Indian citizens, and peace accords that function as constitutional events. The claim defended in this article is that these four variations, and not the insurgencies or the development deficits that standard accounts place at the centre, constitute the region's political settlement: the durable framework inside which its conflicts are fought, its governments are formed and its bargains with New Delhi are struck.

## A Region the Standard Frames Describe Badly

The region comprises Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland, Sikkim and Tripura. By the Census of 2011 the eight states held about 45.6 million people, some 3.8 percent of India's population, across about 262,000 square kilometres, close to 8 percent of the national territory. The region touches the rest of India through the Siliguri corridor, a strip of land in northern West Bengal about 22 kilometres wide at its narrowest, and it shares roughly 5,400 kilometres of international boundary with China, Myanmar, Bangladesh, Bhutan and Nepal. Its demographic composition sets it apart as sharply as its geography. Scheduled Tribes form about 28 percent of the region's population against a national share of about 8.6 percent, and the state-level figures run from about 94 percent in Mizoram and about 86 percent in Nagaland and Meghalaya down to about 12 percent in Assam. A settlement built for this terrain had to differ from the settlement built for the Gangetic plain, and it did.

Two standard treatments dominate writing on the region, and each captures a real layer of its history. The conflict-and-insurgency treatment begins with the Naga armed movement of the 1950s, follows the Mizo uprising of 1966, the Assam agitation of 1979 to 1985 and the Bodo and Tripura insurgencies, and reads the region as a sequence of armed challenges to the Indian state answered by counter-insurgency. The developmental-deficit treatment begins with per-capita income gaps, road and rail density and the fiscal dependence of the hill states on central transfers, and reads the region as a lagging periphery awaiting integration through investment, a reading institutionalised in the North Eastern Council of 1972, the Department of Development of North Eastern Region of 2001 and the full Ministry of Development of North Eastern Region of 2004. Neither treatment is false. Each is incomplete in the same way: neither can say what the region's politics actually runs on, the instruments through which a dispute in Kohima, Aizawl or Imphal is converted into a negotiation, a council election, a constitutional amendment or a signed accord.

The constitutional-architectural reading defended here names those instruments. It takes its scholarly bearings from three bodies of work. Sanjib Baruah, in India Against Itself: Assam and the Politics of Nationality, published in 1999, and again in In the Name of the Nation, published in 2020, argued that the region is governed through an exceptional apparatus in which nationality politics and coercive law are permanently entangled. Walter Fernandes, across essays written from Guwahati over four decades, documented how tribal land, customary institutions and displacement interact inside that apparatus. B.G. Verghese, in India's Northeast Resurgent, first published in 1996 and revised in 2005, offered the integrationist counterweight, treating accords, connectivity and electoral participation as evidence of a settlement consolidating. The sections below put these three readings against the primary record: the texts of Articles 371A to 371J, the text of the Sixth Schedule, the Bengal Eastern Frontier Regulation of 1873, and the signed accords of 1960, 1975, 1985, 1986, 2003 and 2020. The adjudication reached is that the architecture is neither a façade over conflict, as the strongest version of the conflict frame implies, nor a waiting room for development, as the deficit frame implies. The architecture is the settlement itself.

### Why is the Northeast governed differently from other Indian regions?

The Northeast is governed differently because its accession bargains were written into the Constitution instead of being absorbed by the uniform state model applied elsewhere. Between 1947 and 1987 the Union added state-specific clauses to Part XXI, created elected councils under the Sixth Schedule, retained a permit line from 1873 and converted negotiated accords into statehood.

One clarification belongs at the threshold, because the vocabulary of exception invites a misunderstanding. Asymmetry is not a Northeast monopoly inside the Indian Constitution. The founding text and its amendments carry special provisions for many states, and the comparative framework of state settlements developed for this pillar, [the four-dimensional comparative framework](/2019/12/21/comparative-indian-state-political-settlements/), treats asymmetry as one dimension among several. What distinguishes the Northeast is density. Nowhere else do a Part XXI clause, an autonomous-council schedule, an entry-permit regime and a living accord process operate simultaneously on the same territory, and nowhere else does a peace negotiation with an armed organisation carry the standing of unfinished constitutional business for a quarter of a century. The distinctness is a matter of combination and depth, and it is that combination the following sections take apart, variation by variation.

A word on the term settlement itself, since the article's claim stands or falls with it. A political settlement, in the usage of this pillar, is not a synonym for peace, consent or justice. It is the set of standing terms on which political conflict is conducted and governments are formed: the rules that decide whose legislature may block a parliamentary law, whose council controls a hillside, whose permit a traveller must carry and whose signature converts a ceasefire into a state. A settlement is tested not by the absence of conflict but by the address of conflict's claims. When a movement in the Northeast wants land protected, a language recognised, a boundary redrawn or a history acknowledged, it addresses the claim to the schedules, the clauses, the permit line or the accord table, and the Government of India answers at the same addresses. That shared address book, maintained across seventy-five years of otherwise discontinuous politics, is what the following sections document, and it is why the region's settlement can be called distinct without being called successful: several of its terms remain contested, one of its processes remains unfinished, and one of its states fractured along the settlement's own lines in 2023. Distinctness is a claim about where the politics happens. It is not a claim that the politics is finished.

## The Founding Design: Bordoloi and the Choice of Asymmetry

The architecture's first draft was drawn before the Constitution itself. Under the Government of India Act of 1935, the hill tracts of Assam were administered as Excluded and Partially Excluded Areas, kept outside the ordinary provincial legislature and run by the Governor in his discretion, a colonial device that combined protection of hill land and custom with exclusion of hill peoples from provincial politics. When the Constituent Assembly convened its Advisory Committee on Fundamental Rights, Minorities and Tribal and Excluded Areas, chaired by Vallabhbhai Patel, the committee appointed a sub-committee for the North-East Frontier (Assam) Tribal and Excluded Areas under Gopinath Bordoloi, the Premier of Assam. Its members included the Khasi leader J.J.M. Nichols-Roy, the strongest advocate inside the process for elected hill councils, and its interim recommendations reached the Advisory Committee in 1947; its full report was placed before the Constituent Assembly as the drafting of the schedules closed in 1949, exactly the founding-period terrain surveyed in [the founding-period long-arc context](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/).

The Bordoloi Sub-Committee's decisive move was to reject both poles of the debate it inherited. Against the colonial model, it refused continued exclusion: the hill districts would sit inside Assam, send members to the Assam legislature and vote in provincial elections. Against the assimilationist model pressed by some Assamese opinion, which would have administered the hills as ordinary districts of the plains, it refused absorption: the hills would govern their land, forests, village administration, inheritance and marriage through elected district councils with legislative and judicial powers. Bordoloi carried this design into the Constituent Assembly debates of 5 to 7 September 1949, where the Sixth Schedule was adopted, defending the councils as the instrument by which hill peoples could hold their land and customs while taking their place in the province. Nichols-Roy, himself Khasi, answered plains fears of fragmentation by pointing to the councils' bounded jurisdiction: the schedule enumerated subjects, reserved the Governor's assent and left the council inside the state's executive authority. The design that emerged, preserved in the Constituent Assembly Debates and the records of the Ministry of Home Affairs, is the under-cited primary source of the entire settlement: the region's architecture was argued for, clause by clause, by named founders facing a named objection.

Bordoloi brought to that argument a lesson his own career had just taught him about the fragility of unwritten assurances. The Cabinet Mission Plan of May 1946 had placed Assam in the grouping of provinces designated Section C, alongside Bengal, a scheme under which the sections would frame constitutions for their member provinces and Assam's representatives feared, with reason, that a Bengal-majority section would write Assam's future and open its land to the demographic pressure of the Bengal delta. Bordoloi, as Assam's Premier, led the Assam Congress's resistance to compulsory grouping, carried the case to the Congress Working Committee and to Mahatma Gandhi, whose support for Assam's right to refuse the grouping stiffened the province's stand, and watched the scheme collapse with the Mission's failure. The episode stands behind the Sixth Schedule's method. The man who designed elected councils for the hills had spent 1946 resisting a constitutional arrangement imposed on his own province by a paper scheme negotiated over its head, and the design he produced for the hills bears the mark: protections written into the constitutional text itself, institutions elected by the protected people themselves, and powers enumerated so that a later majority, in Dispur or in New Delhi, would have to amend a schedule in the open rather than reinterpret an assurance in private. The Northeast's preference for instruments over promises, visible in every later accord's demand for constitutional conversion, begins here, in the founding generation's own experience of how paper schemes behave.

The contrast with the mainland tribal belt fixes the design's meaning. For the tribal areas of the mainland states, the Constitution's Fifth Schedule, read with Article 244(1), gave the Governor reporting duties and regulation-making powers and provided Tribes Advisory Councils whose advice the executive could take or leave. No elected council received legislative power over land and custom. The Sixth Schedule, read with Article 244(2), did the opposite for the Assam hills: it made the council a legislature in miniature for its enumerated subjects and a court for disputes under customary law. Two schedules, drafted in the same Assembly in the same months, distribute power in opposite directions, and the choice between them is the founding act of the Northeast's distinct settlement. The pattern belongs to the broader logic of [the asymmetric-federal design](/2019/01/03/federalism-indian-constitution-explained/) that runs through the Indian text wherever a founding bargain required the Union to hold unlike units by unlike terms.

Elasticity was written into the design from the start. When the States Reorganisation Commission reported in 1955 and the States Reorganisation Act followed in 1956, the Northeast's hill demands were left substantially undecided: [the 1956 Northeast deferred-cases context](/2019/02/10/states-reorganisation-act-1956-india/) records a settlement postponed, with the Naga Hills administered under Assam, the North-East Frontier Agency run directly by the Union and the hill districts' statehood claims unresolved. The architecture absorbed the deferral and then converted it, step by step, into new units. The Twenty-second Amendment of 1969 inserted Article 244A, permitting an autonomous state within Assam with its own legislature and council of ministers, and Meghalaya functioned in that intermediate form from April 1970 before full statehood on 21 January 1972. Manipur and Tripura reached full statehood on the same day under the North Eastern Areas (Reorganisation) Act of 1971. Nagaland had preceded them on 1 December 1963, Mizoram and Arunachal Pradesh followed on 20 February 1987. Each conversion travelled through an instrument of the architecture, an accord, an amendment, a schedule, rather than through the ordinary reorganisation politics of the mainland. The founding design, in short, was not a concession extracted once. It was a machine for producing further settlements.

Arunachal Pradesh travelled the machine's longest track, and the track explains its clause. The territory began as the North-East Frontier Agency, administered after 1954 by the Ministry of External Affairs through the Governor of Assam acting as the President's agent, under a governing philosophy Jawaharlal Nehru articulated with the anthropologist Verrier Elwin: tribal communities were to develop along their own genius, neither swamped by plains money and settlement nor frozen as museum exhibits. The Agency stood outside the Sixth Schedule entirely, its peoples governed through political officers rather than councils, a fifth and older mode of frontier administration surviving inside the republic. The Chinese invasion of 1962, which reached the Agency's foothills, ended the arrangement's innocence without ending the arrangement; the territory became the Union Territory of Arunachal Pradesh in 1972 and a full state on 20 February 1987, carrying Article 371H's law-and-order discretion as its founding clause because its open boundary question with China, which claims the state's greater part as South Tibet, made security the state's defining anxiety rather than customary law. Even the region's great exception to the council architecture arrives, on inspection, as one of its variations: a frontier held first by administrators, then admitted to statehood on a bespoke clause fitted to the anxiety its history had assigned it.

## Article 371 and Its Variants: A Clause for Every Bargain

Article 371 stands at the head of a family of provisions in Part XXI of the Constitution, headed Temporary, Transitional and Special Provisions, through which the Union writes state-specific terms into the constitutional text itself. The original Article 371, addressed to Maharashtra and Gujarat, empowers the President to assign the Governor a special responsibility for separate development boards for regions such as Vidarbha, Marathwada, Saurashtra and Kutch. The variants that follow the lettered sequence, from 371A to 371J, extend the device across the Union. Six of the lettered variants belong to the Northeast, and they are the first of the four architectural variations: constitutional clauses that suspend, for a named state, rules that bind every mainland state, and that do so in the Constitution's own words rather than in revocable statute.

Article 371A, inserted by the Thirteenth Amendment of 1962 as the constitutional price of Nagaland's statehood, is the family's strongest member. Its text bars any Act of Parliament from applying to Nagaland, unless the Nagaland Legislative Assembly by resolution so decides, in respect of four fields: religious or social practices of the Nagas, Naga customary law and procedure, administration of civil and criminal justice involving decisions according to Naga customary law, and ownership and transfer of land and its resources. A mainland legislature legislates for its state subject to the Union list and the concurrent list. The Nagaland Assembly holds, in these four fields, a veto over Parliament itself. Article 371G, inserted by the Fifty-third Amendment of 1986 on Mizoram's statehood, repeats the same four-field protection for Mizo religious or social practices, Mizo customary law and procedure, customary justice and land. Two states of the Union thus carry, inside the Constitution, a legislative shield for customary law that no mainland state possesses, and the shield's existence predates and frames every later negotiation conducted in Kohima and Aizawl.

Article 371A carries two further devices that repay attention, because they show the clause governing transitions as well as protections. Its first part charges the Governor of Nagaland with a special responsibility for law and order for so long as, in his opinion, the internal disturbances of the period before statehood continue, to be discharged in his individual judgment after consulting the council of ministers, a discretion carried over from the Sixteen-Point Agreement's security terms and drafted to lapse with the emergency that justified it. Its transitional provisions held the Tuensang area, the former frontier division of the Naga Hills Tuensang Area, under the Governor's direct administration for a decade, governed with the aid of a regional council, until its full integration into the state's ordinary institutions, an arrangement that acknowledged in text what the map made obvious: the eastern hills had entered the state on different terms from Kohima's, and the settlement would carry the difference openly for ten years rather than pretend it away. The Eastern Nagaland demand of the 2020s, recorded in the Naga section below, is unintelligible without this founding admission that Nagaland itself was assembled asymmetrically.

The remaining Northeast variants distribute narrower but real powers. Article 371B, inserted by the Twenty-second Amendment of 1969, requires the President to constitute a committee of the Assam Legislative Assembly drawn from members elected from the state's tribal areas, a device for giving the hill and plains-tribal constituencies a collective voice inside the plains legislature. Article 371C, inserted by the Twenty-seventh Amendment of 1971 as Manipur approached statehood, does parallel work for Manipur's hill areas: a committee of assembly members elected from the hill areas, a presidential power to direct its functioning, and a Governor's special responsibility to report annually to the President on hill administration. Article 371H, inserted by the Fifty-fifth Amendment of 1986 for Arunachal Pradesh, assigns the Governor a special responsibility for law and order, to be exercised in his individual judgment after consulting the council of ministers. Each variant answers a different anxiety recorded at a different accession moment: tribal representation in Assam, hill-valley balance in Manipur, frontier security in Arunachal Pradesh. The family resemblance is the method, a bespoke clause per bargain, not the content, which varies as widely as the bargains did.

### Why does Article 371F protect Sikkim's pre-merger laws?

Article 371F protects Sikkim's pre-merger laws because Sikkim entered the Union in 1975 not as conquered or ceded territory but as a former protectorate whose accession terms, ratified by referendum, required continuity. The clause preserves the laws in force immediately before the appointed day until altered by a competent legislature, and shields that continuity from constitutional challenge.

Sikkim's variant, inserted by the Thirty-sixth Amendment of 1975 after the referendum of April 1975 and the abolition of the Chogyal's office, is the family's most intricate member. It fixes the Sikkim Legislative Assembly at not less than thirty-two seats, reserves seats for Bhutia-Lepcha and other communities, continues every law in force in Sikkim before the appointed day until altered by a competent authority, empowers the President to extend Union laws with adaptations, and bars courts from questioning the arrangement by reason of the terms of Sikkim's integration. Where 371A shields a people's customary law against Parliament, 371F shields an entire predecessor legal order against discontinuity. The two clauses mark the family's range: at one end a veto on future legislation, at the other a guarantee that accession itself changed less than accession usually changes.

The family's remaining members show that the method, once built, travelled outward from the Northeast rather than remaining its peculiarity. Article 371D, inserted by the Thirty-second Amendment of 1973, empowers the President to secure equitable opportunities in public employment and education for the regions of Andhra Pradesh, an arrangement that survived the state's bifurcation and continues to govern both successor states, which makes the clause the natural bridge to [the post-2014 state-reorganisation comparator](/2020/02/03/andhra-pradesh-telangana-bifurcation-2014/) within this pillar. Article 371E provides for a central university in Andhra Pradesh. Article 371I, inserted in 1987, assigns the Governor of Goa a special responsibility for law and order in terms parallel to Arunachal Pradesh's. Article 371J, inserted by the Ninety-eighth Amendment of 2012, creates a development board and reservations in employment and education for the Hyderabad-Karnataka region. The Northeast did not invent asymmetry, but the six Northeast variants form its densest cluster, and the cluster's dates, 1962, 1969, 1971, 1975, 1986, trace the accession calendar of the region state by state. Read as a series, Part XXI is the region's treaty shelf, kept inside the Constitution where treaties cannot ordinarily be kept.

The clauses are litigated text, not ceremonial text, and Article 371A's fourth protected field, ownership and transfer of land and its resources, supplies the proof. Successive Nagaland governments and Naga civil organisations have read that field to vest the ownership of subsoil petroleum in the land and its owners, and the state assembly framed the Nagaland Petroleum and Natural Gas Regulations of 2012 to assert the reading. The Union's reading places mineral regulation within its own legislative competence and treats the clause as protecting surface ownership and customary transfer. The Oil and Natural Gas Corporation, which began work in Nagaland in the 1970s at Changpang in Wokha district, suspended operations in 1994 amid objections from Naga organisations, and for three decades the two readings of one constitutional clause, not any shortage of oil, kept exploration closed. Discussions between the two governments in 2023 on resuming exploration in undisputed areas turned entirely on how the clause distributes the resource and its rents between Kohima, New Delhi and the landowning villages. A provision drafted in 1962 was still allocating bargaining power sixty years on, which is the behaviour of a working settlement term and not of a decorative exception.

Article 371C's career in Manipur teaches the complementary lesson, that a clause can be architecturally real and politically underweight at the same time. The clause promises the hill areas a committee of their own assembly members, a structured channel for hill legislation and an annual gubernatorial report to the President on the administration of the hills. The mechanism exists and sits; the hill districts' grievances, over funds routed through the valley-dominated departments, over the weakness of the district councils created by the Manipur statute of 1971 and over the pace of development administration, accumulated outside it. When the state fractured in May 2023, the remedies demanded from the hills were framed in the constitutional vocabulary the clause belongs to: a strengthened hill administration, Sixth Schedule status for the hill councils and, at the demand's outer edge, a separate administration altogether. The catastrophe showed the committee's failure to carry the load the founders assigned it. It also showed that even the failure was argued in the settlement's language, and that no party to Manipur's argument proposed to settle the state's future outside the Constitution's asymmetric instruments. They proposed stronger ones.

## The Sixth Schedule: Autonomous Councils as Working Governments

The second variation is the Sixth Schedule, the autonomous district council architecture that the Bordoloi Sub-Committee designed and the Constituent Assembly adopted in September 1949. The schedule applies to tribal areas in four states, Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram, and it constitutes each listed tribal area as an autonomous district governed by an elected district council of not more than thirty members, of whom not more than four are nominated by the Governor and the rest elected on adult suffrage. A mainland district is an administrative unit of a state government, run by a deputy commissioner answerable upward. An autonomous district under the Sixth Schedule is a small polity, with its own legislature for enumerated subjects, its own courts for a defined class of disputes and its own fund fed by taxes the council is empowered to levy. The difference is not one of degree in decentralisation. It is a difference in the kind of body the Constitution creates.

The councils' legislative jurisdiction, set out in paragraph 3 of the schedule, covers the allotment, occupation and use of land other than reserved forests, the management of forests other than reserved forests, the use of canals and watercourses for agriculture, the regulation of shifting cultivation, the setting up of village and town committees, matters of village administration including village police and public health, the appointment and succession of chiefs and headmen, inheritance of property, marriage and divorce, and social customs. Paragraph 8 adds taxation: taxes on land, buildings, professions, trades, animals, vehicles and boats, tolls, and a share of mineral royalties under paragraph 9. Paragraphs 4 and 5 equip the councils with village and district council courts trying suits and offences under customary law between Scheduled Tribe parties, with appeals structured upward to the Governor and, in the schedule's original design, away from the ordinary district judiciary for the covered class of cases. Acts of Parliament and of the state legislature apply to an autonomous district only as the Governor directs, with exceptions and modifications the notification may specify, and council laws take effect on the Governor's assent. No mainland institution below a state legislature holds a comparable bundle: law-making on land and custom, adjudication, taxation and a filter on the application of parliamentary law.

### How much power does an autonomous district council actually hold?

An autonomous district council holds real but bounded power. Within its district it legislates on land other than reserved forests, shifting cultivation, village administration, inheritance, marriage and social customs; it runs council courts for customary disputes, levies taxes on land, buildings and professions, and receives mineral royalties. The Governor's assent, and state control of council finances, mark its ceiling.

The inventory of councils shows the architecture at working scale. Assam carries three: the Bodoland Territorial Council at Kokrajhar for the Bodo areas of the north bank, the Karbi Anglong Autonomous Council at Diphu, in continuous existence since the district council of 1952, and the Dima Hasao Autonomous Council at Haflong for the North Cachar Hills. Meghalaya carries three: the Khasi Hills, Jaintia Hills and Garo Hills autonomous district councils, whose jurisdiction covers nearly the whole of a state whose population is overwhelmingly tribal, so that Meghalaya lives under two elected tiers with overlapping claims, an arrangement scholars of the state's politics treat as the schedule's most complete realisation. Mizoram carries three smaller councils for its southern minorities, the Chakma Autonomous District Council at Kamalanagar, constituted in 1972 out of the earlier Pawi-Lakher Regional Council, the Lai Autonomous District Council at Lawngtlai and the Mara Autonomous District Council at Siaha. Tripura carries one, the Tripura Tribal Areas Autonomous District Council, first constituted in January 1982 under a Tripura statute of 1979 and raised into the Sixth Schedule by the Forty-ninth Amendment of 1984, in force from 1 April 1985, exercising jurisdiction over some 68 percent of the state's territory, in which the state's tribal population, reduced by migration across the twentieth century to a minority of about 32 percent in its own homeland, is concentrated.

The Bodoland council's history shows the architecture absorbing an insurgency rather than merely administering a district. A first Bodo accord of 1993 created a Bodoland Autonomous Council under state law, without constitutional standing, and it failed for want of defined territory, funds and legal authority; the armed campaign of the Bodo Liberation Tigers continued until the Memorandum of Settlement of 10 February 2003 converted the council into a Sixth Schedule body, renamed the Bodoland Territorial Council, with forty elected seats, defined legislative subjects under an amended schedule and a development package. The settlement of 27 January 2020, signed in New Delhi by the Union Home Minister Amit Shah's ministry with all four factions of the National Democratic Front of Boroland, the All Bodo Students Union under its president Pramod Boro and the United Bodo People's Organisation, renamed the administered domain the Bodoland Territorial Region and promised a commission to redraw its boundaries. In the council elections of December 2020 the Bodoland People's Front, which had held the council since 2003 under Hagrama Mohilary, won seventeen of forty seats, the United People's Party Liberal twelve and the Bharatiya Janata Party nine, and on 15 December 2020 a United People's Party Liberal, Bharatiya Janata Party and Gana Suraksha Party executive took office with Pramod Boro, a signatory of the accord eleven months earlier, as Chief Executive Member. An accord, a constitutional schedule, an election and a transfer of power: the sequence is the Sixth Schedule working as a settlement machine, and no Fifth Schedule institution on the mainland has produced its like.

The councils' limits are documented as plainly as their powers, and the critics inside the region state them at their strongest. Walter Fernandes argued across his Guwahati essays that the councils were handed jurisdiction over land while the commanding instruments of land policy, reserved forests, major minerals, industrial siting and immigration, remained with state and Union governments, so that autonomy guarded the village while the forces displacing villagers operated above the council's ceiling. Council leaderships in Karbi Anglong, Dima Hasao and the Tripura council have placed parallel complaints on record for decades: functions listed in the schedule without the functionaries or the funds, state governments delaying the transfer of departments, and the Governor's assent operating as a slow veto. Paragraph 14 of the schedule itself anticipates the audit, empowering the Governor to appoint a commission to examine and report on the administration of the autonomous districts. The architectural reading does not require the councils to be strong. It requires them to be the recognised arena, and on the evidence of Bodoland's three accords and Tripura's 1985 elevation, movements that could have demanded exit have repeatedly demanded instead a better place inside the schedule.

The schedule carries its own repair mechanism, and the repair record is part of the evidence. Paragraph 21 empowers Parliament to amend the Sixth Schedule by ordinary law, adding, varying or repealing its provisions, and declares that such a law is not a constitutional amendment for the purposes of Article 368. The founding generation thereby built the one part of the tribal settlement that can be renegotiated by simple legislative majorities, and Parliament has used the power repeatedly: to carry the schedule to Tripura, to restructure councils on state reorganisation, and in 2003 to write the Bodoland council's adapted subject list into the text. The Constitution (125th Amendment) Bill, introduced in the Rajya Sabha in February 2019, proposes the deepest repair yet attempted: village and municipal councils beneath the district councils, reservation of seats for women in a system whose customary composition admitted almost no women for decades, elections conducted by the state election commissions, and a requirement that state finance commissions recommend devolution to the councils. The bill's long pendency cuts in two directions at once, and both directions support the architectural reading rather than the relic reading. A dead letter is not amended, and it is not fought over either: the councils' own associations have lobbied for the bill's passage because the schedule is where their politics lives.

The schedule's prestige travels beyond the four states the text names. After the reorganisation of August 2019 made Ladakh a Union Territory without a legislature, the Leh Apex Body and the Kargil Democratic Alliance, representing the territory's Buddhist and Muslim majorities respectively, placed the Sixth Schedule at the head of their joint demands in their negotiations with the Ministry of Home Affairs, alongside statehood, arguing that a territory whose population is overwhelmingly tribal requires the schedule's land and council protections against the exposure that direct Union administration brings. A Himalayan territory at the opposite end of the mountain chain asking to be governed by the Northeast's founding instrument is the single clearest sign that the schedule is understood, by peoples who have studied their options, as the Indian Constitution's serious offer of protected self-rule. Instruments are judged by the company that petitions to join them.

The councils' own territories remain under active renegotiation through the accord track, which keeps the schedule a living text rather than a closed list. On 4 September 2021 the Union, the Assam government and five Karbi armed organisations signed a memorandum of settlement in New Delhi promising the Karbi Anglong Autonomous Council enhanced powers, a special development package and the rehabilitation of over a thousand surrendered cadres, the fourth major attempt, after accords in the 1990s and 2000s, to settle Karbi aspirations inside the council the Karbis have held since 1952. In April 2023 the Dimasa National Liberation Army signed a parallel settlement for Dima Hasao. The pattern deserves emphasis because it repeats across forty years: an armed organisation forms around a grievance the council exists to answer, the state answers with force and negotiation in combination, and the settlement's terms are delivered as amendments to the council's powers, funds or territory. The Sixth Schedule is less a finished constitution for the hills than a standing offer, renewed at each crisis, whose terms each generation of hill politics reopens and re-prices.

## The Inner Line: A Permit Regime from 1873

The third variation is the oldest instrument in the settlement and the only one that regulates the movement of Indian citizens within India. The Bengal Eastern Frontier Regulation of 1873, a regulation of the Governor-General in Council issued under the Government of India Act of 1870, armed the administration of Bengal's eastern frontier districts to draw an Inner Line beyond which British subjects and foreign residents could not pass without a licence, and to prescribe passes for trade and residence across it. The regulation's original purposes were frontier management and the protection of the tea, timber and oil interests of the Assam plains from uncontrolled movement into the hill tracts, and of the hill peoples from plains moneylenders and land speculators; historians of the frontier read the instrument as both shield and cage, and both readings survive in the debates of the successor states. What matters for the settlement is the instrument's survival. The Constitution's freedom of movement under Article 19 was framed against the permit's continued existence, and the Inner Line Permit regime operates, in adapted form, in Arunachal Pradesh, in Nagaland and in Mizoram, each requiring a citizen from another Indian state to hold a permit, issued for a stated period and purpose, as a condition of lawful entry and stay.

### Does the Inner Line Permit apply to foreign nationals?

No. The Inner Line Permit binds Indian citizens entering from other Indian states, and it descends from the Bengal Eastern Frontier Regulation of 1873. Foreign nationals are governed by a separate instrument, the Protected Area Permit issued under the Foreigners (Protected Areas) Order of 1958, which restricts foreign entry into the same frontier belt on Union-administered security grounds.

The regime's extension to Manipur is the clearest demonstration that the permit is living architecture rather than a colonial relic kept for ceremony. Manipur had stood outside the Inner Line system through the whole post-1947 period, and Manipuri civil organisations campaigned for inclusion for years, arguing that a small state of under three million people, bordered by Myanmar and ringed by permit states, could not protect its land and demographic balance while remaining the region's only open door. In December 2019, as Parliament passed the Citizenship (Amendment) Act and protests spread across the Northeast, the President issued the Adaptation of Laws (Amendment) Order adapting the Bengal Eastern Frontier Regulation to Manipur, and the Inner Line Permit system took effect in the state on 1 January 2020. The sequence matters for the adjudication this article defends. A colonial regulation of 1873 was demanded from below, extended by constitutional adaptation in 2019 and received in Imphal as a protective achievement. Whatever the line's origins, its political meaning in the successor states is set by the states that ask for it.

The permit regime also draws the boundary of the region's demographic politics, and it interlocks with the other variations in ways the single-instrument accounts miss. The same December 2019 settlement that extended the line to Manipur wrote the line's states, and the Sixth Schedule's areas, out of the Citizenship (Amendment) Act's operation, a carve-out examined in the post-2014 section below and placed in its full political context in [the post-2019 CAA Northeast-contestation context](/2019/08/05/caa-nrc-contestation-2019-2020/). A Naga citizen's entry into Arunachal Pradesh requires a permit; an Arunachali student's entry into Nagaland requires one; the permit states guard each other's citizens as carefully as they guard the plains. The architecture thereby creates a region-wide category of protected insiderhood that no other part of the Union reproduces, and it gives every subsequent debate on migration, citizenship registers and land sale in the eight states a common legal grammar: the question is never whether the boundary exists, but where it is drawn and who administers it.

Two live disputes show the permit's working edges. Dimapur, Nagaland's commercial capital and its only railhead, grew as a trading town at the hills' foot and sat for decades in an ambiguous position under the state's permit regime; the Nagaland government resolved to apply the Inner Line to the district in 2019, and the question of its enforcement, against the claims of the town's long-settled trading communities and the demands of Naga organisations for strict application without exception, remained contested through the following years, a dispute conducted wholly within the instrument's own terms. Meghalaya, a Sixth Schedule state across nearly its whole territory, passed a unanimous assembly resolution in December 2019 demanding the Inner Line for Meghalaya as well, judging its councils' protections insufficient against migration pressure without the permit at the border; the Union has not granted the demand. A state already holding the second variation petitioning for the third is the settlement thinking in its own vocabulary, and the unanswered petition records the system's other property: entry to its protections is itself allocated by negotiation, not by entitlement.

The permit's economics are argued in the same vocabulary. Hospitality and investment voices inside the permit states have long contended that the line taxes the region's integration, deterring the tourists and employers whose arrival the fiscal layer's programmes subsidise, and the permit states have answered not by repealing the line but by administering it more lightly, moving issuance online and easing short tourist permits for festivals such as Nagaland's Hornbill. The protection, that is, is retained as the settled term and its friction is negotiated at the counter, which is how a settlement treats an instrument it regards as permanent.

## Accords as Constitutional Events: The Naga Process

The fourth variation is the accord process itself, the standing practice by which negotiated settlements with armed movements are converted into constitutional text, new states and new councils. The Naga case is its longest running instance and the one that defines its outer limit. The Naga National Council, founded in 1946 under Angami Zapu Phizo, declared Naga independence on 14 August 1947 and conducted a plebiscite in May 1951 whose result the organisation reported as overwhelmingly for sovereignty. The Government of India never accepted either claim, and the dispute's terms have not moved since: a Naga position, stated at its strongest by the National Socialist Council of Nagaland (Isak-Muivah) and its predecessors, that the Naga people never acceded to India and negotiate as a distinct political entity; and a Union position, stated by every government since 1947, that any settlement lies within the Indian Union while the Naga history and identity are unique and entitled to asymmetric recognition. The architecture exists to hold exactly this disagreement inside a process, and the process's stations mark the variation's method.

The first station converted negotiation into a state. After the creation of the Naga Hills Tuensang Area in 1957 and the conventions of the Naga People's Convention at Kohima in 1957 and 1959, a convention delegation led by the Convention's president, Dr Imkongliba Ao, negotiated in New Delhi the Sixteen-Point Agreement of July 1960 with Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru's government. The agreement's points fixed the terms the state still carries: a state of Nagaland inside the Union, a ministry for its affairs in the Ministry of External Affairs and later the Home Ministry, the protection of Naga customary law and land that the Thirteenth Amendment then wrote into Article 371A, and safeguards for the Naga areas administered from Tuensang. Parliament enacted the State of Nagaland Act in 1962, and Nagaland became the Union's sixteenth state on 1 December 1963. The Naga National Council rejected the settlement and the underground war continued, but the precedent was set and it has never been displaced: in the Northeast, a sufficiently organised political claim does not merely win concessions. It is offered the Constitution's own machinery, statehood with a bespoke clause attached.

The second station showed the method's limit. The Shillong Accord of 11 November 1975, signed in Shillong between the Governor of Nagaland, L.P. Singh, and representatives of the underground organisations, recorded the representatives' decision, in the text's words, of their own volition to accept the Constitution of India and to deposit arms. The accord ended one phase of the war and split the movement. Phizo, in exile in London, repudiated it; the younger commanders who had trained in China, among them Isak Chishi Swu and Thuingaleng Muivah, condemned it as a surrender; and on 31 January 1980 the dissidents founded the National Socialist Council of Nagaland, which split in 1988 into the Isak-Muivah and Khaplang factions. The lesson the movement drew, and which every subsequent negotiator on the Union side has had to respect, is that an accord signed by representatives who cannot carry their own organisation settles nothing. The architecture's later stations were built to avoid a second Shillong: agreements would be negotiated with the organisation that holds the ground, in the open, with the constitutional conversion spelled out.

### What did the 2015 Framework Agreement actually settle?

The Framework Agreement of 3 August 2015 settled the basis, not the substance, of a final settlement. Signed in New Delhi by the National Socialist Council of Nagaland (Isak-Muivah) and interlocutor R.N. Ravi, with Prime Minister Narendra Modi present, it recorded shared-sovereignty language and the uniqueness of Naga history. Its text remains unpublished; the final agreement has not been concluded.

The third station is the process in its mature form. A ceasefire between the Union and the National Socialist Council of Nagaland (Isak-Muivah) took effect on 1 August 1997 and has been renewed since; a parallel ceasefire with the Khaplang faction followed in 2001, and seven other Naga groups, organised as the Naga National Political Groups, signed an Agreed Position with the Union on 17 November 2017 accepting a settlement within the Indian Constitution. Talks with the Isak-Muivah organisation were conducted, in the formula agreed in 2001, without territorial limits, and Prime Minister Atal Bihari Vajpayee stated at Kohima in 2003 that the Government of India recognised the unique history and culture of the Nagas. The Framework Agreement of 3 August 2015 was presented by Prime Minister Narendra Modi, who attended its signing, as the foundation of an honourable settlement. The process then stalled on the settlement's symbolic core: the Naga negotiators' demand for a separate Naga flag and a Naga constitution, which they hold to be implied by the recognition of unique history, and the Union's position, stated by the interlocutor and Governor R.N. Ravi in October 2019, that the talks had concluded on all substantive issues and that a single Indian constitution and the absence of a separate flag were not negotiable. Ravi resigned as interlocutor in September 2021, the former Intelligence Bureau official A.K. Mishra continued the engagement as adviser, and the dispute's terms, flag and constitution against integration within one text, remained open through 2024. A new front opened inside the state in parallel: the Eastern Nagaland People's Organisation, representing six eastern districts including Mon, Tuensang and Longleng, demanded a separate Frontier Nagaland Territory arrangement on grounds of developmental neglect by Kohima, and its call for abstention emptied polling across the eastern districts in the 2024 parliamentary election. Even the incompleteness is architectural in form. The argument proceeds through agreements, interlocutors, frameworks and proposed territories, the vocabulary of the settlement itself.

The process's interlocking character, the way one state's settlement becomes another state's grievance, was demonstrated in June 2001, when the ceasefire's extension brought Manipur into the street. The Union and the Isak-Muivah organisation agreed at Bangkok that month to extend their ceasefire without territorial limits, a formula intended to cover Naga areas beyond Nagaland's borders. In Imphal the formula was read as a step toward the dismemberment of Manipur, whose eastern districts are Naga-inhabited; protests engulfed the valley, the state assembly complex was attacked and burned on 18 June 2001, and security forces firing on the crowds killed eighteen protesters, a date Manipur still observes. The Union withdrew the territorial formula in July 2001 and confined the ceasefire to Nagaland. The episode wrote the rule that has governed every station since: no Naga settlement can be drafted as if the neighbouring states were not parties to it, the territorial question is the process's heaviest item precisely because the architecture's units interlock, and the Manipur assembly's repeated resolutions in defence of the state's territorial integrity belong to the same record as the Kohima negotiations. A settlement system is recognisable by exactly this property, that its parts cannot be renegotiated one at a time without the others answering.

## Accords as Constitutional Events: Mizoram and Assam

If the Naga process defines the variation's limit, the Mizo accord defines its complete life cycle, the one case in the region where an armed movement travelled the full distance from insurgency to statehood to ordinary electoral politics inside the architecture. The Mizo National Front grew out of the Mizo National Famine Front, founded by Laldenga in 1961 amid the mautam famine of 1959 and the Assam government's failure to answer it, and on 1 March 1966 the Front launched its uprising, seized Aizawl and declared independence; the Government of India retook the town with air support in the only instance of the Indian Air Force striking inside Indian territory, and the counter-insurgency that followed regrouped the Mizo population into protected villages. Twenty years of negotiation, exile politics and ceasefire followed, and on 30 June 1986 the Government of India, the Government of Mizoram and the Mizo National Front signed the Memorandum of Settlement in New Delhi under Prime Minister Rajiv Gandhi. The accord traded the demand for sovereignty for statehood with protection: the Constitution (Fifty-third Amendment) Act of 1986 inserted Article 371G, the State of Mizoram Act carried the Union Territory to full statehood on 20 February 1987, and the Front surrendered arms and entered elections.

The accord's weight is best measured against what preceded it. The counter-insurgency after March 1966 regrouped the Mizo countryside into Protected and Progressive Villages, roadside settlements into which the population of hundreds of villages was moved under guard so that the hills between them could be treated as free-fire country; by the early 1970s the great majority of rural Mizos had passed through the grouping centres, an experience Mizo memoir and scholarship record as the insurgency's deepest wound, deeper than the Aizawl air strikes of 5 March 1966 themselves. A movement whose constituency had been regrouped at gunpoint accepted, twenty years on, a settlement that left sovereignty aside and took statehood with a customary-law shield, and then kept the settlement through nine changes of government. The architectural reading rests its Mizo case on that sequence: the text of 1986 and 1987, and not the fatigue of 1986, is what the following decades inherited, because the text is what a Mizo voter of 2023, born after the accord, still lives under.

The settlement's durability is a matter of electoral record. The Mizo National Front won the first assembly election of the new state in 1987 and Laldenga took office as chief minister of a state his organisation had fought to leave the Union; after a spell of President's Rule in 1988, the Congress under Lal Thanhawla won office in 1989, and for three decades the state's politics alternated between the Front and the Congress as an ordinary two-party competition conducted under the accord's terms, until the Zoram People's Movement under Lalduhoma won twenty-seven seats in December 2023 and formed the government. No Mizo political formation of consequence has reopened the sovereignty demand. Critics of the architectural reading, who treat accords as truces purchased by exhaustion, owe an account of this case, and the account available to them, that Mizoram is small, linguistically compact and predominantly Christian and therefore easy to settle, concedes the article's point by another route: the architecture works where the bargain is written into constitutional text, because the text, and not the exhaustion, is what later generations inherit.

Assam supplies the accord process in its mass-movement form. The Assam Movement of 1979 to 1985, led by the All Assam Students Union and the All Assam Gana Sangram Parishad, demanded the detection and removal of foreign nationals from the state's electoral rolls, after the discovery of large numbers of contested names in the Mangaldoi parliamentary constituency rolls in 1979 converted a long-running anxiety over migration from East Bengal and East Pakistan into a mass agitation. The movement's darkest day came on 18 February 1983 at Nellie in Nagaon district, where, during an election the movement had called on Assam to boycott, mobs killed more than two thousand people, most of them Bengali-speaking Muslims, in a few hours; the Tiwari Commission appointed to inquire into the Nellie killings submitted its report to the state government, which was never officially placed before the public. The Assam Accord of 15 August 1985, signed by the Union Home Secretary R.D. Pradhan and the movement's leaders, among them Prafulla Kumar Mahanta and Bhrigu Kumar Phukan, closed the agitation with a calibrated citizenship settlement: persons who entered Assam before 1 January 1966 were to be regularised, those who entered between 1 January 1966 and 24 March 1971 were to be detected, struck off the rolls for ten years and then restored, and those who entered after 24 March 1971 were to be detected and expelled. Parliament wrote the bargain into the Citizenship Act as Section 6A. The movement's political wing, the Asom Gana Parishad, organised in October 1985, won sixty-four of the assembly's one hundred and twenty-six seats that December and Mahanta, at thirty-three, became chief minister. The Supreme Court in October 2024, by a majority of four to one in a Constitution Bench led by Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud, upheld Section 6A as a valid legislative bargain, with Justice J.B. Pardiwala dissenting; the Assam settlement of 1985 was still being litigated, and upheld, four decades on, because it had been drafted as constitutional-grade text rather than as a press statement.

Tripura and the later Assam settlements show the accord method running on after its founding generation. In Tripura, the Tripura National Volunteers under Bijoy Kumar Hrangkhawl signed an accord with the Union in August 1988 and surrendered arms, in a settlement whose terms ran through the autonomous council the state had already raised into the Sixth Schedule in 1985; later Tripura agreements, with the All Tripura Tiger Force in the 1990s and with the National Liberation Front of Tripura factions in 2019 and September 2024, repeated the pattern of surrender, rehabilitation and council-centred politics. In Assam itself, the pro-talks faction of the United Liberation Front of Asom signed a memorandum of settlement on 29 December 2023 in the presence of the Union Home Minister and Chief Minister Himanta Biswa Sarma, closing, on paper, the oldest insurgency of the plains as the paresh-Baruah faction stood outside it. The method's reach and its limit appear in the same frame: accord follows accord because the architecture converts armed claims into administrable terms, and a residue stands outside each accord because no signature has yet been devised that binds those who did not sign.

## The Four Variations on One Map

The four variations are usually studied separately, Article 371 by constitutional lawyers, the Sixth Schedule by scholars of tribal administration, the Inner Line by historians of the frontier, and the accords by students of conflict resolution. The settlement becomes visible only when the four are laid on the same territory. Table 1, the four-architectural-variation map, sets out each variation with its operating mechanism, the states and instruments through which it runs, and the documented stress test that reveals how it behaves under pressure.

| Variation | Operating mechanism | Where it operates | Documented stress test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Article 371 variants | State-specific constitutional clauses in Part XXI suspending ordinary rules | Nagaland (371A), Assam (371B), Manipur (371C), Sikkim (371F), Mizoram (371G), Arunachal Pradesh (371H) | The Meitei Scheduled Tribe demand of 2023 pressed past the 371C hill-areas committee rather than through it |
| Sixth Schedule | Elected autonomous district councils with legislative, judicial and taxation powers | Ten councils across Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram | The Bodo settlement of 2020 and the council election that transferred power at Kokrajhar that December |
| Inner Line Permit | Permit for entry and stay of Indian citizens from other states, descending from the Bengal Eastern Frontier Regulation of 1873 | Arunachal Pradesh, Mizoram, Nagaland, and Manipur from 1 January 2020 | Extension to Manipur in December 2019, demanded from below during the citizenship amendment crisis |
| Accord settlements | Negotiated memoranda converted into statehood, councils and constitutional clauses | Nagaland 1960 and 1963, Assam 1985, Mizoram 1986 and 1987, Bodoland 1993, 2003 and 2020 | The Naga Framework Agreement of 2015, its text unpublished and its final settlement unconcluded |

Table 1 repays a horizontal reading. Each row has, at some documented point, been asked to do the work of another: the Inner Line was extended in 2019 to perform the protective function that Manipur's absence of a Sixth Schedule council and the narrowness of 371C had left undone; the Bodoland insurgency was closed by promoting a state-law council into the Sixth Schedule; the Naga state was created by accord and immediately fitted with a 371 clause as its armour. A settlement whose instruments substitute for one another under pressure is a system, and it is the system's existence, not any single instrument's elegance, that the namable claim of this article asserts: the Northeast operates through constitutional-architectural variations producing a distinct political settlement, and the comparative framework for Indian states requires this architectural augmentation to describe the region at all.

The map also reads vertically, state by state, and the vertical reading explains why no single-instrument account of any one state suffices. Nagaland carries the strongest clause, the permit and the unfinished accord, but no Sixth Schedule council, its village and area councils running instead under state law and customary authority beneath 371A's shield. Mizoram carries the matching clause, the permit and three small councils for its southern minorities, its accord the only one fully closed. Assam carries the committee clause, three of the ten councils, two plains accords of the first magnitude and no permit, the region's demographic giant governed by the thinnest protective bundle relative to its exposure, which is why Assam's politics, alone in the region, is fought primarily over the citizenship register rather than over councils or clauses. Tripura carries a single council over two-thirds of its land and no clause and no permit, its settlement resting on one instrument that the state spent the 1980s raising into the Constitution. Meghalaya carries three councils across nearly its whole territory and petitions for the permit it lacks. Manipur carries the committee clause, statutory hill councils and, since 2020, the permit, and its crisis is legible as the one case where every instrument was present in a weakened form: a committee without weight, councils without constitutional standing or timely elections, and a permit that arrived in the same month as the citizenship statute it was meant to answer. Sikkim carries its merger clause alone, the settlement's quietest file. Arunachal Pradesh carries the security clause and the permit over a territory whose district councils were never created, its tribal governance running through customary village institutions the Constitution mentions only in passing. Eight states, eight bundles, no two identical: the variation is not noise around a regional pattern. The bundles are the pattern, assembled state by state from the same four instruments across forty years of bargains.

## Special Category: The Fiscal Terms of Union Membership

One layer of the settlement lies outside the four variations, and the architectural reading must account for it honestly, because it is the layer the developmental-deficit frame built and the layer through which New Delhi's money reaches the eight states. From 1969, applying criteria associated with the Gadgil formula and decisions of the National Development Council, the Union classified the northeastern states, with the other hill states, as special category states for the purposes of central assistance. The classification carried a funding grammar: plan assistance divided ninety to ten between grant and loan for special category states, against seventy to thirty the other way for the general states, a ratio later carried into the funding pattern of centrally sponsored schemes, where the northeastern and Himalayan states contribute ten percent against the Union's ninety. The region's state budgets, small in own revenue and large in committed expenditure, were thereby written into permanent fiscal asymmetry matching the constitutional asymmetry of the schedules and clauses: the Union holds the region's states by unlike fiscal terms for the same reason it holds them by unlike constitutional terms, because like terms, applied to this terrain, would not hold them at all.

The fiscal layer acquired its own institutions on the deficit frame's schedule. The High Level Commission appointed to examine the region's backwardness under the chairmanship of S.P. Shukla submitted its report, Transforming the North East, in 1997, documenting infrastructure backlogs and recommending that unspent central funds earmarked for the region should cease to lapse. The recommendation produced the Non-Lapsable Central Pool of Resources from 1998 and 1999, fed by the rule that ten percent of the plan budgets of Union ministries stood earmarked for the Northeast, with unspent balances flowing to the pool rather than back to the Consolidated Fund. The Department of Development of North Eastern Region followed in 2001 and a full ministry in 2004. The Fourteenth Finance Commission's award of 2015, raising the states' share of the divisible tax pool to forty-two percent, dissolved the planning-era apparatus within which special category status had operated, and the label lost its formal home when the Planning Commission gave way to the NITI Aayog; the funding pattern for the region's schemes, ninety to ten, was nonetheless retained, and the earmarking norm for ministry budgets continued. Fiscal asymmetry survived the machinery that had named it, which is the behaviour of a settlement term rather than of a scheme.

The fiscal layer is also where the deficit frame's critique lands hardest against the architecture, and the critique belongs in the adjudication. Walter Fernandes documented, in his studies of development-induced displacement in the region, how large projects financed through the developmental apparatus, dams, mines, oil installations and highways, repeatedly drew their land from the same tribal territories whose land the Sixth Schedule and Article 371A exist to protect, so that the settlement's protective layer and its fiscal layer work against each other on the ground: the council guards the village's land while the project acquires the district's. Verghese's integrationist programme in India's Northeast Resurgent answered from the other direction, treating roads, power and market access as the settlement's missing substance and the accords as incomplete until connectivity made the region's economy a going concern, a programme the Union's Act East policy, announced in 2014 as the successor to the Look East policy of 1991, adopted with the Kaladan multi-modal project and the India-Myanmar-Thailand trilateral highway as its flagships. Both critics are describing real machinery. What neither the displacement critique nor the integrationist programme displaces is the constitutional layer beneath them, because the region's arguments about dams, highways and oil are themselves conducted through the settlement's instruments: a council's consent, a clause's land shield, an accord's rehabilitation clause. Money is the settlement's fifth subject. It is not a fifth variation, because it allocates no authority. The authority it must pass through belongs to the four.

The Finance Commission channel tells the same story in the language of tax devolution. The Fifteenth Finance Commission's horizontal formula for 2021 to 2026 assigned a weight of ten percent to forest and ecology, a criterion whose arithmetic runs strongly in favour of the eight states, whose forest cover is the Union's densest; the northeastern states stand, in consequence, among the largest per-capita recipients of tax devolution and of grants under Article 275, a distribution their governments defend, in their memoranda to successive commissions, in the settlement's own vocabulary of terrain, sparsity and the cost disabilities of hill administration. Fiscal federalism's most technical documents thus reproduce the founding judgment of 1949: that equal treatment of this terrain would be unequal in effect, and that the Union's terms must be written unevenly if they are to bind evenly.

The fiscal layer's two regional bodies also record, in their rivalry, the difference between planning for the region and settling with it. The North Eastern Council, statutory since 1971, was designed as the region's own planning table, its members the states themselves; the ministry created in 2004 sits in New Delhi and administers the earmarked funds. Students of the region's administration have long noted the duplication, and the council's share of the region's investment decisions has shrunk relative to the ministry's pool and the line ministries' earmarks. The point is not administrative tidiness. A settlement's fiscal terms endure because they are ratios and rules, ninety to ten, ten percent, non-lapsable, which no single budget can quietly repeal; the bodies that administer them rise and fall with governments. The Northeast's fiscal asymmetry has outlasted the Planning Commission that named its category, and it will outlast the ministry that administers its pool, for the same reason the Sixth Schedule has outlasted the governments that amended it: the terms are load-bearing for the states' finances, and load-bearing terms are renegotiated, never simply removed.

## The Post-2014 Test: Expansion, Citizenship and Manipur

A settlement's reality is tested when a national party decides to govern through it rather than around it. The decade after 2014 supplied that test in three waves. The first wave was electoral. In May 2016 the Bharatiya Janata Party led an alliance to power in Assam under Sarbananda Sonowal, a former president of the All Assam Students Union who had carried the movement generation's politics into the state's highest office, giving the party its first government in the region as the leading partner. In March 2017 a Bharatiya Janata Party government took office in Manipur under N. Biren Singh although the Congress had emerged as the largest single party. In March 2018 the party won Tripura under Biplab Kumar Deb, closing twenty-five years of Left Front government under Manik Sarkar, joined the National Democratic Alliance government of Nagaland under Neiphiu Rio of the Nationalist Democratic Progressive Party, and entered the Meghalaya coalition led by Conrad Sangma of the National People's Party. In May 2019 it won Arunachal Pradesh outright under Pema Khandu. The North-East Democratic Alliance, convened from 2016 by Himanta Biswa Sarma, organised the expansion as a single regional front. The reading of this wave divides scholars in the familiar way. Its defenders, including the alliance's own leadership, present it as integration achieved through the settlement's own electoral channels. Its critics, in the line of argument associated with Baruah's In the Name of the Nation of 2020, present it as the Centre learning to operate the exceptional apparatus for partisan ends. Both readings agree on the point that matters here: the expansion ran through the architecture, its alliances, councils and state governments, and not through any suspension of it.

The second wave was the citizenship crisis of 2019, the sharpest single test the architecture faced in the decade. The Citizenship (Amendment) Act of December 2019, whose full regional politics is treated in [the post-2019 CAA Northeast-contestation context](/2019/08/05/caa-nrc-contestation-2019-2020/), offered a fast track to citizenship for six non-Muslim communities from Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan, and the Northeast's protests against it were driven by the fear, stated by the All Assam Students Union and the region's student and civil organisations at their strongest, that any regularisation of migration from Bangladesh would dissolve the demographic protections on which the region's bargains rested. Five protesters died in police firing in Assam in December 2019. The Act as passed carried the architecture inside itself: its benefits were withheld from the tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram and Tripura covered by the Sixth Schedule and from the states covered by the Inner Line, so that the protected zones the founding generation had built were written into the new citizenship law as its boundary. The Manipur Inner Line extension of the same month, recorded above, was the crisis's other architectural product. When the Act's rules were finally notified in March 2024, the carve-outs stood. The critics' counter-argument deserves its full weight: a protection that the Union can write into a statute, the argument runs, the Union can write out, and the carve-outs therefore prove the architecture's subordination rather than its strength. The reply is on the record of December 2019 itself: the carve-outs were not a gift volunteered in Delhi but a price extracted by mobilisation in the region, and a settlement is not shown to be unreal by the fact that its terms must be defended. It is shown to be real by the fact that defending them works.

The third wave was Manipur, and it is the hardest evidence in this article, to be stated with corresponding care. Manipur's internal architecture divides the state between a valley, holding about two-fifths of its area and the Meitei majority, and hills, holding the rest and protected for Scheduled Tribe communities including the Naga and Kuki-Zo peoples: hill land cannot be transferred to non-tribals, the hill districts are administered through autonomous district councils created by a Manipur statute of 1971 rather than by the Sixth Schedule, and Article 371C's hill-areas committee of the assembly stands between the two zones. The second government of N. Biren Singh, in office from March 2022, pursued eviction drives in reserved forests and a campaign against poppy cultivation that Kuki-Zo organisations stated, at their strongest, amounted to the targeting of one community under administrative cover; the government stated, at its strongest, that the drives enforced forest and narcotics law against encroachment regardless of community. In March 2023 a single judge of the Manipur High Court directed the state to send a recommendation on the long-standing Meitei demand for Scheduled Tribe status, a direction the Supreme Court later described as factually and legally problematic and which a division bench of the High Court deleted in February 2024. On 3 May 2023 a Tribal Solidarity March organised by the All Tribal Students' Union of Manipur protested the demand; by nightfall, violence between Meitei and Kuki-Zo groups had ignited on the Churachandpur and Bishnupur border, and the state divided along communal lines with a speed that displaced, by the Union Government's figures placed before Parliament, some sixty thousand people, and killed, by the same official count as of November 2024, two hundred and fifty-eight. Kuki-Zo organisations and legislators demanded a separate administration, holding that coexistence inside the existing state had failed; Meitei organisations opposed any territorial division of Manipur and accused Kuki armed groups under Suspension of Operations agreements of driving the conflict; each position is recorded here as its holders state it, and the dispute between them is not resolved by the evidence available to this article. The institutional sequelae ran through the architecture's channels: the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act, withdrawn from parts of the valley before the violence, was reimposed in six valley police-station areas in November 2024; Biren Singh resigned on 9 February 2025; and President's Rule was imposed on 13 February 2025. The Manipur catastrophe does not refute the architectural reading, because the conflict's very terrain, the hill-valley land regime, the Scheduled Tribe list, the 371C committee, the demand for Sixth Schedule status for the hill councils, was drawn by the architecture. It refutes any complacent version of the reading, in which the settlement figures as a solved problem rather than as the arena in which the region's deepest disputes continue to be fought.

One measure of the arena's decay in Manipur predates the violence and belongs to the comparison this article has drawn throughout. The hill districts' councils, created by the Manipur (Hill Areas) District Council Act of 1971, are statutory bodies elected for five-year terms; their last completed elections before the crisis were held in 2015, and the elections due in 2020 were postponed repeatedly on grounds that moved from the pandemic to the demand, pressed by hill organisations themselves, that the councils first be given the greater powers of an amended act. The hills thus entered 2023 with their elected tier expired and their districts run by administrators and caretaker arrangements, while across the border in the Sixth Schedule states the councils at Kokrajhar, Diphu, Shillong and Agartala renewed themselves by election on schedule. The contrast isolates the variable this article has argued from throughout: where the autonomous tier is constitutional, it reproduces itself; where it is statutory, a state government can let it lapse, and a lapsed tier leaves a state's grievances with no elected address short of the street. The Kuki-Zo demand for Sixth Schedule status for the hill councils, raised after May 2023, is that comparison stated as a programme.

Two quieter features of the settlement framed the Manipur crisis before it broke, and both belong to the architecture's less visible machinery. The first is the Suspension of Operations regime in the hills: on 22 August 2008 the Union, the Manipur government and the Kuki armed groups organised in the Kuki National Organisation and the United People's Front signed a tripartite Suspension of Operations agreement, under which the groups' cadres were housed in designated camps under ground rules and the security forces suspended operations against them. The device is a ceasefire without an accord, an architectural halfway house of a kind the region has multiplied, and its terms became contested ground in March 2023 when the state government announced its withdrawal from the agreement in respect of two signatory groups, the Kuki National Army and the Zomi Revolutionary Army, alleging their cadres' involvement in agitation over forest evictions; Kuki bodies answered that a state government could not unilaterally exit a tripartite instrument to which the Union was the senior party. The argument, conducted entirely over who could sign and unsign what, is the accord process's grammar operating below the threshold of war and peace.

The second feature is the delimitation freeze. Parliamentary and assembly constituencies in the four states of Assam, Arunachal Pradesh, Manipur and Nagaland were excluded from the delimitation completed for the rest of India in 2008, the exercise deferred by presidential order on grounds that included the sensitivity of tribal representation and the unsettled census figures in parts of the region. A Delimitation Commission for the four states was constituted in March 2020 under Justice Ranjana Prakash Desai, yet the exercise proceeded, in the event, only for Assam, where the Election Commission completed the redrawing in August 2023 under Section 8A of the Representation of the People Act of 1950; in the other three states delimitation remained deferred. A freeze on the redrawing of constituencies is a settlement term like any other: it holds representation constant lest population-based revision disturb the hill and valley, tribal and non-tribal balances on which the states' internal bargains rest, and its persistence into a third decade records how much of the region's politics the architecture governs by deliberate postponement.

The security law's longer arc closes the section. The Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act of 1958, first applied to the Naga Hills and extended across the decades, is the settlement's coercive floor, and its record is documented rather than rhetorical: the Jeevan Reddy Committee, appointed in November 2004 after the killing of Thangjam Manorama in Manipur, recommended the Act's repeal in its report of June 2005, and no government accepted the recommendation. Irom Sharmila fasted against the Act in Manipur from November 2000 to August 2016. Against that record stands the documented contraction of the Act's footprint as accords held: withdrawal from Tripura in May 2015 and from Meghalaya in 2018, and the reductions announced on 31 March 2022 by Home Minister Amit Shah, removing the disturbed-area designation from twenty-three Assam districts in full and one in part, from fifteen police-station areas in six Manipur districts and from fifteen police stations in seven Nagaland districts, with further withdrawals notified in April 2023. The December 2021 killing of thirteen civilians by security forces at Oting in Mon district, and the committee the Union then appointed on phased withdrawal, frame the 2022 decision's timing. The two facts coexist and the adjudication must hold both: a settlement that still requires a special powers act in parts of its territory is incomplete, and a special powers act retreating district by district as settlements accumulate is evidence that the architectural track, and not the coercive one, is carrying the region's politics.

A fourth test, electoral and still unfolding, qualifies the expansion story in a way that strengthens the architectural reading against both partisan accounts of the decade. In Mizoram's assembly election of December 2023 the Zoram People's Movement, a regional formation outside both the national alliances, defeated the Mizo National Front government and took twenty-seven of forty seats, an outcome no Delhi headquarters scripted. In the parliamentary election of 2024, held while Manipur remained divided, the Congress won both of Manipur's Lok Sabha seats, the Inner Manipur seat and the Outer Manipur seat reserved for Scheduled Tribes, a verdict delivered by valley and hill voters alike against the state government the Bharatiya Janata Party had led since 2017. In Nagaland's eastern districts the abstention organised by the Eastern Nagaland People's Organisation emptied the poll. Expansion, counter-verdict and boycott were all executed through the settlement's own channels, state elections, reserved constituencies and a territorial demand addressed to the Home Ministry in the established idiom. The decade's lesson is not that the region was captured by a national party or that it resisted capture. It is that the region's politics, in every direction it moved, moved through the architecture.

## The Counter-Reading: Responses to Conflict, or a Settlement in Their Own Right?

The strongest counter to the constitutional-architectural reading is causal, and it should be given in its strongest form. On the counter-reading, the four variations are not a settlement designed but a residue accumulated: Article 371A answered the Naga insurgency, the Sixth Schedule's Bodo extension answered the Bodo insurgency, the Inner Line is a colonial control instrument retained by inertia, and the accords are the receipts issued when armed campaigns ended. If conflict and developmental failure caused the architecture, then the conflict-and-insurgency and developmental-deficit framings are causally prior, and the architectural reading mistakes the scar tissue for the body. Baruah's India Against Itself pressed a version of this case in 1999, reading the region's special arrangements as the apparatus of a state managing nationalities it could not integrate, and In the Name of the Nation renewed the argument in 2020 against the post-2014 order. The case is formidable because its history is accurate. Almost every clause in Part XXI's Northeast cluster can be dated to a crisis, and this article has dated them.

The reply concedes the history and disputes the inference. A variation's origin in conflict does not fix its function across the following decades, and the post-founding record shows the architecture operating independently of the crises that produced it, structuring claims that no insurgency forced. The Manipur Inner Line extension of 2019 was demanded by civil organisations through electoral politics and granted as an adaptation order, not extracted by arms. The Citizenship (Amendment) Act's Sixth Schedule and Inner Line carve-outs were negotiated inside a parliamentary process by governments and movements using the architecture as their leverage. The Bodo settlement of 2020 closed a conflict, but it closed it by deepening the schedule, renaming the territory, commissioning a boundary review and holding an election that transferred power to the accord's own signatory, a sequence in which the instrument plainly outruns the occasion. Even the Manipur catastrophe of 2023 was fought over the architecture's categories, Scheduled Tribe status, hill land, the powers of councils, rather than over the state's existence. Scar tissue, to stay with the metaphor, does not adjudicate land disputes, conduct elections or exempt territories from citizenship law. Institutions do, and the four variations are institutions at work. The comparison with the Union's other asymmetric arrangements sharpens the point. When Jammu and Kashmir's special position was dismantled in August 2019, [the J&K Reorganisation as constitutional-architectural-shift comparator](/2019/07/31/article-370-abrogation-august-2019/), the change was executed by presidential order and parliamentary resolution in a single week, possible because that position rested on a provision its holders could not defend through any elected regional institution of their own. No equivalent one-week operation is available against Article 371A or the Sixth Schedule, not because the Union lacks the amending power, but because the Northeast's variations are load-bearing: governments, councils, parties and movements across eight states are organised through them, and dismantling them would require dismantling the region's working politics.

The reply is strengthened by the settlement's demonstrated capacity for self-repair, a property neither rival framing predicts. Dead letters are not amended; the Sixth Schedule has been amended by ordinary law under its own paragraph 21 whenever a settlement required it, most elaborately for Bodoland in 2003. Superseded instruments are not extended by popular demand; the Inner Line was extended in 2019. Closed chapters are not reopened as negotiating frameworks; the accord form closed the Bodo question for a third time in 2020 and the Tripura and Assam plains insurgencies in the same idiom. Even the critics' evidence is generated inside the architecture they criticise: Fernandes's displaced villagers petition councils and courts the settlement created, Baruah's exceptional apparatus is the settlement's own coercive floor, documented in its own committee reports, and Verghese's integration proceeds through its state governments and its accords. A framing that explains why its rivals' evidence exists, and where that evidence is filed, has the stronger claim on the material.

The reading also states its own falsifier, as an argued claim should. The constitutional-architectural reading would fail if the region's political actors ceased to address their claims to the settlement: if a major movement declined the accord form and prospered outside it, if a state surrendered a 371 clause or a council without exacting a price, if a citizenship or land question of the first magnitude were settled in the region by ordinary legislation opposed by its protected institutions and the settlement simply absorbed the defeat. None of these has occurred across the period this article surveys, which includes the most centralising decade in the Union's recent history and a citizenship statute aimed directly at the region's founding anxieties. The clause, the schedule, the line and the accord table are where the Northeast's claims continue to be filed, including the claims of those who hold the settlement responsible for their grievances. A framework that even its failures must petition is not a façade. It is the address of power in the region, and addresses of power are what political settlements are.

What, then, does the architectural reading add to the comparative study of Indian states? This pillar's hub constructed [the four-dimensional comparative framework](/2019/12/21/comparative-indian-state-political-settlements/) for reading state political settlements, and the framework's dimensions organise the mainland cases well. The Northeast is the case that bends it. The region's settlements cannot be read off party systems, leadership patterns or fiscal relations alone, because a fifth fact conditions all four: the constitutional terms on which the state exists, bargains struck, differ from every mainland case and continue to allocate land rights, legislative competence, entry rights and negotiating standing. The augmentation the hub framework requires is therefore not decorative. It is the difference between describing the Northeast as eight small states with turbulent histories and describing it as the Union's most fully articulated experiment in holding unlike peoples by unlike terms. Teachers who carry Table 1 into a classroom can project the map through [an online PPT viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) without installed software, and the projection makes the article's claim in one screen: four instruments, eight states, one settlement, and no mainland analogue. The claim defended throughout can be stated at its full strength. The Northeast operates through constitutional-architectural variations producing a distinct political settlement; the four-dimensional comparative framework requires this architectural augmentation; and the variations, tested by insurgency, citizenship crisis and the Manipur catastrophe, have bent under every load placed on them without any party to the region's politics proposing to govern without them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is Article 371?

Article 371 is the head of a family of special provisions in Part XXI of the Indian Constitution through which the Union writes state-specific terms into the constitutional text. The original article concerns Maharashtra and Gujarat, empowering the President to charge the Governor with special responsibility for regional development boards for Vidarbha, Marathwada, Saurashtra and Kutch. The lettered variants that follow, Articles 371A to 371J, extend the device to individual states with terms fitted to each state's accession bargain. Six variants belong to the Northeast: 371A for Nagaland, 371B for Assam, 371C for Manipur, 371F for Sikkim, 371G for Mizoram and 371H for Arunachal Pradesh. The remaining variants serve Andhra Pradesh, Goa and Karnataka, which makes Part XXI a general Indian instrument whose densest cluster is northeastern.

### Q: What is the Sixth Schedule?

The Sixth Schedule is the part of the Indian Constitution, read with Article 244(2), that creates autonomous district councils for tribal areas in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram. Designed by the Bordoloi Sub-Committee of the Constituent Assembly and adopted in September 1949, it constitutes each listed tribal area as an autonomous district governed by an elected council of up to thirty members. The councils legislate on land other than reserved forests, forest management outside reserved forests, water for agriculture, shifting cultivation, village administration, inheritance, marriage, divorce and social customs. They run council courts for customary disputes, levy taxes on land, buildings and professions, and receive a share of mineral royalties. Ten councils operate under the schedule, from the Bodoland Territorial Council at Kokrajhar to the three small councils of southern Mizoram.

### Q: What is the Inner Line Permit?

The Inner Line Permit is the permit an Indian citizen from another state must hold to enter and stay in Arunachal Pradesh, Mizoram, Nagaland and, from 1 January 2020, Manipur. It descends from the Bengal Eastern Frontier Regulation of 1873, by which the colonial administration drew an Inner Line across the Assam frontier and licensed passage beyond it, in part to protect tea, timber and oil interests and in part to shield hill peoples from plains moneylenders and land speculators. The successor states retained the instrument and converted its meaning: the permit is defended by the region's governments and civil organisations as demographic and land protection for small peoples. Permits are issued for stated periods and purposes by the resident state, and separate Protected Area Permits govern foreign nationals.

### Q: What was the 1986 Mizo Accord?

The 1986 Mizo Accord, formally a Memorandum of Settlement signed in New Delhi on 30 June 1986 by the Government of India, the Government of Mizoram and the Mizo National Front under Laldenga, ended the insurgency the Front had launched on 1 March 1966. The Front surrendered its demand for sovereignty and its arms; the Union granted full statehood, enacted through the State of Mizoram Act, effective on 20 February 1987, and inserted Article 371G into the Constitution to shield Mizo customary law, religious and social practices, customary justice and land from parliamentary legislation unless the Mizoram Assembly resolved otherwise. The Front then contested and won the new state's first assembly election in 1987, and Laldenga became chief minister of the state his movement had fought to leave the Union.

### Q: What is the Naga Peace Process?

The Naga Peace Process is the standing negotiation between the Government of India and Naga political organisations over the Naga claim to a distinct political status, a claim first organised by the Naga National Council under Angami Zapu Phizo, who declared independence on 14 August 1947 and held a plebiscite in May 1951. Its stations include the Sixteen-Point Agreement of July 1960, which produced the state of Nagaland in December 1963 with Article 371A's protection of Naga customary law and land; the Shillong Accord of November 1975, repudiated by the movement's younger commanders; the ceasefire with the National Socialist Council of Nagaland (Isak-Muivah) effective 1 August 1997; the Agreed Position signed by seven other Naga groups in November 2017; and the Framework Agreement of 3 August 2015, whose unpublished terms frame a final settlement that remains unconcluded.

### Q: What is the Bodoland Territorial Council?

The Bodoland Territorial Council is the Sixth Schedule council governing the Bodo areas of northern Assam from its headquarters at Kokrajhar, created by the Memorandum of Settlement of 10 February 2003 between the Union, the Assam government and the Bodo Liberation Tigers after an earlier state-law Bodoland Autonomous Council of 1993 failed for want of territory, funds and legal standing. The council has forty elected seats and five nominated members and legislates for the territory on the Sixth Schedule subjects, adapted to Bodo conditions. A further settlement of 27 January 2020, signed with all four factions of the National Democratic Front of Boroland and the All Bodo Students Union, renamed the administered area the Bodoland Territorial Region, and in December 2020 the council's first election under the new accord transferred power to a United People's Party Liberal-led executive under Pramod Boro.

### Q: What happened in Manipur in 2023?

In 2023 Manipur divided in ethnic violence between the Meitei majority of the Imphal valley and the Kuki-Zo tribal communities of the hills. The immediate sequence ran from a Manipur High Court direction of March 2023, later deleted by a division bench, that the state send a recommendation on the Meitei demand for Scheduled Tribe status, to a Tribal Solidarity March on 3 May 2023 protesting that demand, after which clashes on the Churachandpur and Bishnupur border spread across the state. By the Union Government's figures placed before Parliament, some sixty thousand people were displaced and two hundred and fifty-eight killed as of November 2024. Kuki-Zo organisations demanded a separate administration; Meitei organisations opposed any division of the state; the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act was reimposed in six valley police-station areas in November 2024, and President's Rule followed in February 2025.

### Q: What is AFSPA and how has its footprint in the Northeast changed?

AFSPA is the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act of 1958, which empowers the armed forces of the Union to operate in areas declared disturbed, with authority to fire on unlawful assemblies, arrest without warrant and enter and search premises, and protection from prosecution without the Union's sanction. First applied in the Naga Hills, it became the security floor of the region's politics, and its repeal was recommended by the Jeevan Reddy Committee in June 2005 without acceptance by any government. Its footprint has contracted as settlements accumulated: withdrawal from Tripura in May 2015 and from Meghalaya in 2018, and reductions announced on 31 March 2022 that removed the disturbed-area designation from twenty-three Assam districts in full, fifteen police-station areas in six Manipur districts and fifteen police stations in seven Nagaland districts, with further withdrawals in April 2023.

### Q: What was the Assam Accord of 1985?

The Assam Accord, signed on 15 August 1985 by Union Home Secretary R.D. Pradhan and the leaders of the Assam Movement, including Prafulla Kumar Mahanta and Bhrigu Kumar Phukan, closed the six-year agitation led by the All Assam Students Union for the detection and removal of foreign nationals from the state's electoral rolls. Its citizenship clauses graded entrants by date: those who came before 1 January 1966 were regularised; those who came between 1 January 1966 and 24 March 1971 were to be detected, removed from the rolls for ten years and then restored; those who came after 24 March 1971 were to be detected and expelled. Parliament enacted the formula as Section 6A of the Citizenship Act, the movement's wing, the Asom Gana Parishad, won the December 1985 assembly election, and a Supreme Court Constitution Bench upheld Section 6A in October 2024.

### Q: What was the Sixteen-Point Agreement of 1960?

The Sixteen-Point Agreement of July 1960 was the settlement negotiated in New Delhi between Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru's government and a delegation of the Naga People's Convention led by its president, Dr Imkongliba Ao, which converted the Naga Hills into a state of the Indian Union on agreed terms. Its sixteen points covered the state's name and place within the Union, the administration of the Tuensang area, financial arrangements, the consolidation of Naga areas as a matter left open, and protections for Naga customary law, land and resources that Parliament then wrote into Article 371A by the Thirteenth Amendment of 1962. The State of Nagaland Act followed in 1962 and Nagaland became the Union's sixteenth state on 1 December 1963. The Naga National Council rejected the agreement and the armed conflict continued, but the agreement set the region's precedent: organised claims are answered with statehood and constitutional protection.

### Q: What was the Shillong Accord of 1975 and why did it fail?

The Shillong Accord of 11 November 1975 was an agreement signed in Shillong between L.P. Singh, the Governor of Nagaland, and representatives of the Naga underground organisations, by which the representatives accepted, in the text's words of their own volition, the Constitution of India and agreed to deposit their arms. It failed because the representatives could not carry the movement they claimed to speak for. Angami Zapu Phizo, in exile in London, repudiated the accord; the younger commanders who had undergone training in China, among them Isak Chishi Swu and Thuingaleng Muivah, condemned it as surrender; and on 31 January 1980 the dissidents founded the National Socialist Council of Nagaland to continue the armed campaign. The accord's lesson shaped every later negotiation: settlements must be signed by the organisation that holds the ground.

### Q: What was the Bordoloi Sub-Committee?

The Bordoloi Sub-Committee was the sub-committee on the North-East Frontier (Assam) Tribal and Excluded Areas appointed by the Constituent Assembly's Advisory Committee on Fundamental Rights, Minorities and Tribal and Excluded Areas, which was chaired by Vallabhbhai Patel. Chaired by Gopinath Bordoloi, the Premier of Assam, and including the Khasi leader J.J.M. Nichols-Roy, it reported in stages from 1947 to 1949 and designed the Sixth Schedule. Its decisive recommendation rejected both colonial-style exclusion of the hills and their absorption as ordinary districts of Assam, proposing instead elected autonomous district councils with legislative and judicial authority over land, forests, village administration, inheritance, marriage and social customs. Bordoloi defended the design in the Constituent Assembly debates of 5 to 7 September 1949, when the schedule was adopted, and the sub-committee's report remains the founding document of the region's settlement.

### Q: How does the Sixth Schedule differ from the Fifth Schedule?

The two schedules distribute power in opposite directions. The Fifth Schedule, read with Article 244(1), governs tribal areas in the mainland states through the Governor, who reports to the President on their administration and may direct that laws apply with modifications, advised by a Tribes Advisory Council whose counsel the executive may accept or set aside; no elected body under the Fifth Schedule legislates. The Sixth Schedule, read with Article 244(2), constitutes the tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram as autonomous districts under elected councils that make laws on land, forests, water, shifting cultivation, village administration, inheritance, marriage and social customs, operate council courts for customary disputes, levy taxes and receive mineral royalties. The mainland model administers tribal areas from above; the northeastern model equips them to govern enumerated subjects themselves.

### Q: How did Sikkim become part of India in 1975?

Sikkim, a Himalayan kingdom under the Chogyal and an Indian protectorate since the treaty of 1950, moved toward integration after elections in April 1974 brought to power the Sikkim National Congress under Kazi Lhendup Dorjee on a platform of closer association with India, and after disorder in Gangtok brought an Indian-administered government under a chief executive. In April 1975 the Sikkim Assembly resolved to abolish the Chogyal's office and to merge Sikkim with India, and a referendum held that month endorsed the resolution. Parliament then enacted the Thirty-sixth Amendment, which made Sikkim the twenty-second state of the Union on 16 May 1975 and inserted Article 371F, reserving assembly seats for Bhutia-Lepcha and other communities, continuing all laws in force before the appointed day until altered by a competent authority, and shielding the integration's terms from challenge in court.

### Q: What special responsibility does Article 371H give the Governor of Arunachal Pradesh?

Article 371H, inserted by the Fifty-fifth Amendment of 1986 as Arunachal Pradesh moved to full statehood, charges the Governor of Arunachal Pradesh with a special responsibility for law and order in the state. The clause directs the Governor, in discharging that responsibility, to exercise his individual judgment after consulting the council of ministers, and it makes the Governor's decision on whether a matter falls within that individual judgment final, beyond question in any court on the ground that he ought or ought not to have acted in his discretion. The device mirrors Article 371I for Goa, enacted in the same period, and it marks the outer edge of the 371 family's Northeast cluster: not a shield for customary law or land, as in Nagaland and Mizoram, but a security discretion held by the Union's representative inside the state's executive.

### Q: Why was the Inner Line Permit extended to Manipur in 2019?

The Inner Line Permit reached Manipur in December 2019 through the collision of a long state campaign with the citizenship crisis of that winter. Manipuri civil organisations had demanded inclusion for years, arguing that a state of under three million people, bordered by Myanmar and surrounded by permit states, could not protect its land and demographic balance as the region's only open state. When Parliament took up the Citizenship (Amendment) Bill in December 2019 and protests spread across the Northeast, the demand acquired decisive leverage: the President issued the Adaptation of Laws (Amendment) Order adapting the Bengal Eastern Frontier Regulation of 1873 to Manipur, and the permit system took effect there on 1 January 2020. The same settlement withheld the citizenship amendment's benefits from Inner Line and Sixth Schedule areas, so Manipur entered the permit regime as the crisis's architectural price and product.

### Q: What did the Bodo Peace Accord of 2020 change?

The Bodo Peace Accord of 27 January 2020, signed in New Delhi by the Union and Assam governments with all four factions of the National Democratic Front of Boroland, the All Bodo Students Union and the United Bodo People's Organisation, closed the armed Bodo movement that three earlier settlements, in 1993, 2003 and by stages after, had not fully closed. Its signatory factions surrendered arms and dissolved, the administered territory of the Bodoland Territorial Council was renamed the Bodoland Territorial Region, a commission was promised to redraw the region's boundaries to include Bodo villages and exclude non-Bodo ones, and rehabilitation and development packages were committed. The accord then passed through the settlement's own machinery: the council elections of December 2020 ended seventeen years of Bodoland People's Front rule at Kokrajhar and installed a United People's Party Liberal-led executive under Pramod Boro, himself a signatory of the accord as president of the students' union.

### Q: What are the unresolved issues in the Naga talks?

The Naga talks remain unconcluded on the symbolic core of the settlement. The National Socialist Council of Nagaland (Isak-Muivah) holds that the Framework Agreement of August 2015, with its recognition of the uniqueness of Naga history, implies a separate Naga flag and a Naga constitution, symbols it treats as the substance of shared sovereignty. The Government of India has held, through the interlocutor R.N. Ravi's statement of October 2019 and after, that one Indian constitution admits no second text and that no separate flag forms part of a settlement. Around that core stand secondary questions: the integration of Naga-inhabited areas of Manipur, Assam and Arunachal Pradesh, which those states oppose; the parallel Agreed Position of the seven Naga National Political Groups, who accept a settlement within the Constitution; and the Eastern Nagaland People's Organisation's demand for a Frontier Nagaland Territory for six eastern districts, which has proceeded as its own negotiation.

### Q: What is the North Eastern Council?

The North Eastern Council is the statutory regional planning body for the eight northeastern states, created by the North Eastern Council Act of 1971 and functioning from 1972, with its secretariat at Shillong. Conceived alongside the reorganisation that carried Manipur, Tripura and Meghalaya to full statehood on 21 January 1972, the council was designed to plan for the region as a unit in sectors no single small state could carry alone: transport and communications, power, flood control and inter-state coordination. Its membership joins the Governors and Chief Ministers of the member states under a Union chairmanship, and a 2002 amendment recast it as the regional planning body for the North Eastern Region and admitted Sikkim as the eighth member. The council belongs to the settlement's developmental layer, the institutional expression of the deficit frame, and its limits mark that frame's limits: it plans and funds, but the region's political questions are settled elsewhere, in the schedules, clauses and accord tables.

### Q: When did Manipur, Tripura and Meghalaya become full states?

Manipur, Tripura and Meghalaya became full states of the Indian Union on 21 January 1972, under the North Eastern Areas (Reorganisation) Act of 1971. Manipur and Tripura, princely states that had merged with India in 1949 and been administered as Union Territories from 1956, received legislatures and full state status together. Meghalaya arrived by the architecture's intermediate step: carved out of Assam as an autonomous state within the state under Article 244A, inserted by the Twenty-second Amendment of 1969, it functioned from April 1970 with its own legislature and council of ministers before graduating to full statehood on the same January day. The triple statehood of 1972 was the founding generation's deferral of 1956 finally discharged, and each of the three states carried forward its own protections: Manipur its hill-areas committee under Article 371C, Tripura its tribal areas later raised into the Sixth Schedule, Meghalaya its three autonomous district councils covering nearly the whole state.
