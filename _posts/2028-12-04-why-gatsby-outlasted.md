In 1925 the American bestseller lists were led by a novel called Soundings, by A. Hamilton Gibbs. The Pulitzer that year went to Edna Ferber. Sinclair Lewis was the country's most discussed novelist and would take the first American Nobel Prize in Literature five years later. Booth Tarkington had two Pulitzers and a national readership. And a short book by a thirty-year-old with a reputation for writing about flappers sold about twenty thousand copies against expectations of seventy-five thousand, disappointed its author, and went quietly out of print.

![Why Gatsby outlasted its contemporaries](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

Understanding why Gatsby outlasted its contemporaries means explaining a reversal that nobody in 1925 predicted and that was not decided by sales, prizes, or critical consensus at the time. The comfortable answer is that the postwar revival rescued a neglected masterpiece, and that answer is partly right and badly incomplete. Plenty of neglected books were available for rescue. Only some got rescued, and the ones that did had properties in common. This article names those properties and argues that the endurance was built into the novel rather than conferred on it afterward.

Call the properties the endurance triad: compression, myth, and symbol. A book that has all three keeps working after its decade ends. A book that has none of them dies with its topic, however good it was at the time.

## What Is Being Compared When We Ask Why Gatsby Outlasted Its Contemporaries

The comparison sets one novel against the field it competed in, which requires being honest about how strong that field was.

The 1920s were not a weak decade for American fiction. Sinclair Lewis produced Main Street in 1920, Babbitt in 1922, Arrowsmith in 1925, and Elmer Gantry in 1927, an extraordinary run of ambitious social novels that shaped how a country talked about itself. Willa Cather published A Lost Lady in 1923 and The Professor's House in 1925. Edith Wharton had taken the Pulitzer in 1921. Edna Ferber, Booth Tarkington, Joseph Hergesheimer, James Branch Cabell, and Floyd Dell all commanded serious audiences and serious critical attention. Hemingway's first novel arrived in 1926 and Faulkner's major work began at the end of the decade.

Against that field, Gatsby was a modest commercial performer and a mixed critical one. Some reviewers admired it; others found it slight, a well-written entertainment about unpleasant people. It did not win the Pulitzer, was not a bestseller, and was not the book anyone would have named in 1925 as the decade's likely survivor.

A century later the picture has inverted. Gatsby is the most widely read American novel of its period and one of the most widely read in the language. Lewis is respected and rarely read outside universities. Ferber, Tarkington, Hergesheimer, Cabell, and Dell have largely left general circulation. Cather, Wharton, Hemingway, and Faulkner survived, and looking at who survived alongside Gatsby is as informative as looking at who did not.

The question this article answers is not whether Gatsby is better than these books, which is a matter of judgment. It is what properties predict survival, and why this novel had more of them than its competition.

### Did Gatsby endure by luck or intrinsic quality?

Both, and in a specific order. Intrinsic qualities made the novel revivable; a set of contingent events supplied the revival. Neither is sufficient alone. The wartime paperback program distributed well over a thousand titles and produced very few canonical resurrections, which shows that distribution alone does not do it.

## What the Revival Actually Did

The standard account, examined in detail in the series treatment of [The Great Gatsby's revival after the Second World War](/2027/03/22/gatsby-revival-after-wwii/), runs like this: the Armed Services Editions program put Gatsby into the hands of roughly a hundred and fifty thousand servicemen, Edmund Wilson assembled The Crack-Up in 1945, a Viking Portable appeared the same year, Arthur Mizener's biography followed in 1951, and academic criticism trained on modernist methods found the novel exceptionally rewarding. Within fifteen years of Fitzgerald's death the book had gone from unavailable to canonical.

Everything in that account is accurate, and using it as a complete explanation makes a specific logical error. The revival was an opportunity, not a cause. The wartime program distributed well over a thousand titles. Wilson's editorial attention and a portable edition were available to other neglected writers. Postwar academic criticism was scanning the entire American nineteenth and twentieth centuries for material and canonizing at speed.

What has to be explained is not why Gatsby got a chance. It is why the chance took. A hundred and fifty thousand servicemen received many books; most produced no lasting reputational effect. Critics who came looking for modernist virtue found some novels that rewarded the search and many that did not. The revival was a filter applied to a large population of candidates, and the interesting question is what let this candidate pass.

The luck-only account also fails a simple test. If the revival were sufficient, the effect would have decayed. Reputations manufactured by a particular critical moment usually recede when the moment passes, and the New Critical enthusiasms of the 1950s have receded considerably. Gatsby has not. It has survived multiple subsequent critical regimes, each of which found something different in it, which is not what happens to a book that was merely fashionable once.

## The First Property: Compression

Length is the least glamorous of the three and possibly the most decisive.

The novel runs about fifty thousand words. Main Street runs roughly four times that. Arrowsmith is longer still. Most serious American novels of the decade were substantial, because the dominant model was the social panorama, a form that requires room to survey a society.

Compression does three things for durability, and they compound.

It survives changing attention. A book that takes three hours to read gets read by people who would not commit fifteen. Every generation contains fewer readers with fifteen free hours than the previous one, and a fifty-thousand-word novel is insulated against that decline in a way a two-hundred-thousand-word novel is not.

It permits rereading, which is where reputations are actually built. Canonical status requires that a book reward return visits, and return visits require that the return be cheap. Gatsby is reread constantly by people who first met it young, and every rereading generates the sense that the book has more in it than they had seen, which is the specific experience that produces advocacy. Nobody rereads a four-hundred-page social panorama every five years.

And it forces the writing to carry more per sentence. Fitzgerald cut relentlessly, and the series analysis of [how The Great Gatsby achieves its brevity](/2026/10/12/gatsby-brevity-craft/) traces the mechanics. Material that a longer novel would dramatize across chapters is delivered here in a clause. Gatsby's five years with Dan Cody occupy two pages. Daisy's marriage, her wedding-night collapse, the birth of her daughter, and Tom's early infidelities arrive through Jordan in a few paragraphs. This density means that a reader who slows down finds argument at the sentence level, which is what makes the novel teachable, quotable, and endlessly discussable.

The comparison with the survivors supports the point. Cather's most durable books are short. Hemingway's most durable are short. The Sun Also Rises, A Lost Lady, and The Great Gatsby all sit in the same compact range, and all three outlasted the longer, more ambitious social novels published beside them.
## The 1925 Snapshot

To feel the reversal properly it helps to reconstruct what the year actually looked like from inside it.

Fitzgerald was thirty and famous, and famous for the wrong thing. This Side of Paradise in 1920 had made him a spokesman for a generation, and the reputation that came with it was social rather than literary: he was the young man who wrote about parties and flappers and had married a beautiful woman and spent too much. His short stories in the mass-circulation magazines paid well and reinforced the impression. When The Great Gatsby appeared in April 1925, a large part of its potential audience expected another entertainment about the young rich, and a large part of the critical establishment was inclined to grade it as one.

The reviews were genuinely mixed rather than hostile. Several critics recognized an advance. Others found the book slight, its characters unpleasant, its scope narrow. Nothing in the reception suggested a permanent work, and the sales confirmed the coolness. Fitzgerald had told his editor he expected to sell seventy-five thousand copies. He sold roughly twenty thousand, which covered his advance and little else, and left him convinced the book had failed.

Meanwhile the year's commercial and institutional laurels went elsewhere. The fiction bestseller lists were led by titles now almost entirely unread. The Pulitzer for the novel of 1924 had gone to Edna Ferber, and the award for 1925 went to Sinclair Lewis for Arrowsmith, which he declined. Lewis was, by any 1925 measure, the important American novelist: four major books in five years, an international readership, a diagnosis of the country that people argued about at dinner. He would take the Nobel in 1930 as the first American to do so.

A hundred years later, the reversal is close to total. The most widely read American novel of the decade is the one that failed commercially, and the writer who owned the decade is a university subject. That is the fact this article exists to explain, and it should be genuinely surprising before it is explained, because the explanation is worthless if the outcome looks obvious in hindsight.

One further detail sharpens it. Fitzgerald died in December 1940 believing the book forgotten. A second printing had not sold through, and copies remained in the Scribner's warehouse. There is no version of this story in which contemporary judgment picked the winner.

## Compression as Craft

The compression argument is stronger if it is treated as something Fitzgerald did rather than something the book happens to be.

The novel is short because material was removed and because material was converted from scene into summary. Fitzgerald worked the manuscript hard, cut a substantial amount, and moved several extended sequences into compressed retrospect. The mechanics are traced in the series analysis of [how The Great Gatsby achieves its brevity](/2026/10/12/gatsby-brevity-craft/), and the effects are visible on any close reading.

Look at what gets summarized. Gatsby's five years with Dan Cody, which in a conventional novel would be a section of several chapters covering a young man's education by a rich patron, occupy roughly two pages of narrated summary in Chapter Six. The whole of Daisy's marriage, including the wedding, her collapse the night before, the honeymoon, Tom's early infidelity, and the birth of her daughter, arrives through Jordan in a handful of paragraphs in Chapter Four. Gatsby's war service, the Oxford months, and the return to Louisville are given in a page.

The effect is not merely economy. Summary at that density changes the reader's relationship to the material, because summarized events cannot be evaluated independently; they arrive already interpreted by the teller. A dramatized scene invites a reader to judge for themselves. A summarized one delivers the teller's version, which is exactly what a novel about an unreliable narrator wants.

So the compression is not separable from the other properties. It is what allows Nick to remain in control of the account, which is what makes the account arguable, which is what sustains rereading. A longer, more dramatized version of the same story would have been a lesser book in a way that has nothing to do with page count and everything to do with who controls the information.

That is why the endurance triad is not a list of coincidental advantages. Fitzgerald's central technical decision produced all three at once.

### Is the novel's compression an accident of length or a deliberate method?

A method. Fitzgerald converted whole sequences into narrated summary rather than simply cutting them: Gatsby's five years with Cody occupy two pages, and Daisy's marriage arrives through Jordan in a few paragraphs. Summary keeps the teller in control of the material, which is precisely what a novel about an unreliable narrator requires.

## The Narrator as a Durability Property

Interpretive openness deserves its own treatment, because it is the property that best explains why the novel survived changing critical fashions rather than merely surviving.

A book that resolves its own questions can be read once. Whatever the reader concludes, the author has already concluded it, and the second reading confirms the first. Such books are preserved by respect rather than by use, which is a weaker form of survival.

Gatsby resolves nothing that matters. Nick announces that he reserves judgment and then judges continuously; the reader must decide how much to discount. He tells us he is one of the few honest people he has ever known, a self-issued and unfalsifiable claim, and the novel never tests it. He is Daisy's cousin, Tom's classmate, Jordan's lover, and Gatsby's grateful neighbor, so every account he gives has an interest attached. He describes himself as within and without, simultaneously enchanted and repelled, which is a confession of divided sympathy from the person supplying all the evidence.

The consequences for durability are direct. Each generation of critics arrived with different questions and found the text willing to answer them. Formalists found a symbolic system of unusual precision. Historicists found the class arrangements of the 1920s. Later readers found gender, race, and the mechanics of empire in Tom's outbursts and in the novel's geography. None of these readings exhausted the book, because the narration keeps the ground unstable.

Compare a novel with an omniscient social narrator who tells the reader what is wrong with the society being described. That book yields the same reading to every school, and once one school has taken it, the next has nothing to do. Preservation follows; re-canonization does not.

The narrator also carries the rereading effect, which is where the novel's real reputational engine sits. A first reading of Gatsby is a story about a doomed romantic. A second is a story about the man telling it. Almost nothing else in the American curriculum flips that cleanly, and the flip is what converts a person who was assigned the book at sixteen into a person who recommends it at forty.

## What Rereading Actually Does

The claim that rereading builds reputations is easy to assert and worth unpacking, since it is the mechanism connecting compression to canonization.

Books do not become canonical by being read once by many people. Bestsellers achieve that constantly and vanish. They become canonical by being read repeatedly by people who then insist on them: teachers who assign them, critics who return to them, writers who steal from them, and ordinary readers who press them on friends. Advocacy is the currency, and advocacy comes from the sense that a book has more in it than one pass revealed.

Two conditions produce that sense. The book must actually contain more, and returning must be cheap. Long novels frequently satisfy the first and fail the second: a reader who suspects there is more in a six-hundred-page book will often not find out. Gatsby satisfies both at once. Three hours buys a complete return visit, and the return reliably yields something, because the compression has put argument into clauses that a first reading takes as description.

The specific things a second reading yields are worth listing, since a student can check them. Nick's opening self-characterization reads as a warning rather than an introduction. Daisy's early lines, including her wish that her daughter be a beautiful little fool, stop being decorative and become the novel's clearest statement about what the world offers women. Jordan's dishonesty at golf, mentioned in passing, becomes structural. The valley of ashes stops being scenery and becomes the corridor every consequential event passes through. And the last two pages stop being a beautiful ending and become the argument the whole book was assembling.

That is a large return on three hours, and it is the engine underneath every institutional fact about the novel's position. The classroom adoption, the paperback sales, the critical attention, and the general circulation all rest on a population of people who read it twice and found the second reading better.
## The Second Property: A Myth Rather Than a Target

The most powerful predictor of a novel's decay is topicality, and this is where Fitzgerald's contemporaries lost.

The dominant serious American mode of the 1920s was satire aimed at an identifiable target. Babbitt attacks the booster culture of the midwestern businessman. Main Street attacks small-town conformity. Elmer Gantry attacks revivalist religion. Arrowsmith attacks commercialized medicine and institutional science. Each is intelligent, well made, and precisely aimed, and precision at a target is exactly the property that dates.

A satire is only as alive as its object. When the booster luncheon, the small-town social hierarchy, and the tent revival stopped being the central facts of American life, the novels that had them in their sights lost their charge. A reader today can admire Babbitt as a period document and can feel the sting only historically, which is a different and weaker experience than the one its first readers had. The book has not become worse. Its target has moved.

Gatsby has almost no satirical target in that sense. It contains satire, some of it sharp, but its subject is not a social type or an institution. Its subject is the belief that a person can build a self and buy back a lost moment, and that belief has no expiration date because it is not attached to a decade. It was true of Americans in 1925, it was true in 1955, it is true now, and it will be true as long as the country tells its citizens that arrival is available to whoever wants it enough.

The series analysis of [the American Dream in The Great Gatsby](/2024/04/22/american-dream-great-gatsby/) sets out the specific argument the novel makes. What matters for endurance is that the argument is about a permanent structure rather than a passing arrangement. Fitzgerald wrote about aspiration, class boundaries, self-invention, and the impossibility of recovering a past. Lewis wrote about Rotary clubs.

There is a further subtlety worth stating, because it is the actual mechanism. A myth outlasts a target because a myth is something readers are inside rather than something they observe. A reader of Babbitt watches a man they are invited to feel superior to. A reader of Gatsby recognizes an impulse they have had. The first relationship weakens as the social distance grows; the second does not weaken at all, because the distance was never social.

This also explains why the novel absorbed changing critical regimes without losing force. New Critical readers found a perfectly constructed symbolic system. Later readers found class, gender, race, and empire. Each regime found something because the underlying subject is large enough to sustain multiple approaches. A satire of the Rotary club supports one reading and has nothing to offer the next school of criticism that arrives.

### Why did many contemporaries date and fade?

Because they aimed at targets rather than at myths. Satires of booster culture, small-town conformity, or commercialized medicine are only as alive as their objects, and when the objects receded the novels became period documents. A book about a permanent structure of aspiration has nothing to outlive.

## The Third Property: Portable Symbols

The last leg of the triad is the most visible and the most often mistaken for the whole explanation.

Gatsby is unusually generous with concrete images that carry meaning and that detach cleanly from their context. The green light across the water. The eyes on the billboard over the ash heaps. The valley of ashes itself. The white dresses, the yellow car, the uncut books, the pool in October. These are not decorative. Each recurs, each accumulates, and each can be lifted out of the novel and used elsewhere without ceasing to make sense.

Portability is the key term. A symbol that requires its novel to be explained is trapped in the novel. The green light does not require explanation, because the structure it encodes, a visible object of desire separated from the desirer by an uncrossable distance, is legible to anyone who has wanted something. It has consequently entered general English as a figure that journalists, politicians, and ordinary speakers use without attribution.

That circulation is a durability engine in its own right. A book whose images are in general use is continuously advertised by people who are not discussing the book. Every use of the green light as a figure for unreachable desire is a small reminder that the novel exists, delivered by someone with no interest in promoting it. No 1920s satire generated anything comparable. Nobody has ever invoked Babbitt's luncheon as a figure for anything.

The symbols also make the novel teachable, which produces the classroom position that in turn produces the readership. A book with trackable recurring images is an ideal instrument for teaching how literature means, and once a book becomes the standard instrument, it acquires a self-renewing audience. That is a downstream consequence of the symbolic density rather than a separate factor.

One caution belongs here, because the symbol argument invites overreach. Symbolic density alone does not preserve a book. Cabell's Jurgen is heavily symbolic and heavily allegorical and is largely unread. What distinguishes Gatsby's symbols is that they are concrete, few, and attached to a myth. Jurgen's are abstract, numerous, and attached to a literary fashion. The triad works as a triad; no single leg carries it.

## The Endurance Triad in Full

The three properties are not independent, and seeing how they interact is the article's central claim.

Compression makes the myth legible. A fifty-thousand-word novel about aspiration and the past keeps its subject visible on every page. A two-hundred-thousand-word novel about the same subject buries it under incident, and readers remember the incident.

The myth makes the symbols durable. The green light works because the desire it encodes is permanent. Attach the same image to a topical grievance and it dies with the grievance.

The symbols make the compression survivable. A short novel risks feeling slight, and Gatsby is often accused of thinness on a first reading. The recurring images give it the density that its page count would otherwise deny it, so that a book you can finish in an evening rewards a decade of return.

Remove any leg and the structure fails. A compressed novel with a topical target and no symbols is a good magazine story and vanishes. A mythic novel at great length with no images is admired and unread. A symbolic novel with no myth is a period curiosity. Fitzgerald assembled all three, and the assembly is why a commercial disappointment from 1925 is now the book a country hands its teenagers.

That is the namable claim of this article, and it is testable. Apply the triad to any novel of the period and it predicts the outcome with unusual accuracy.
## Lewis and Fitzgerald Side by Side

The single most useful controlled comparison in this subject runs between Babbitt and Gatsby, because the two books are close in ambition and opposite in method.

Both are novels about American materialism published within three years of each other. Both take a man whose life has been organized around acquisition and examine what the acquisition cost him. Both are written by American authors at the height of their powers. In 1925 the Lewis book was by far the more consequential, and by any reasonable contemporary judgment the more serious.

The difference is where each locates the problem. Lewis locates it in a social type. George Babbitt is a real-estate man in a midwestern city, and the novel's satire is directed at the specific machinery of his world: the boosters' club, the civic banquet, the standardized house, the standardized opinions, the pressure to conform delivered through business relationships. The book is precise, funny, and comprehensive, and its comprehensiveness is a liability, because it means the book's content is a description of an arrangement.

Fitzgerald locates the problem in a desire. Gatsby is not a social type and does not belong to an institution. He belongs to no club, has no colleagues, and is defined by wanting one thing. The novel therefore has almost nothing to describe in the sociological sense, and what it describes instead is an interior condition.

Now run a century over both. Babbitt's arrangement has changed beyond recognition. The boosters' club, the civic luncheon, and the specific texture of midwestern business conformity in 1922 are historical subjects, and a reader encounters the satire as an accurate account of something gone. The prose has not weakened; the reader's relationship to the target has.

Gatsby's desire has not changed at all. A reader in any decade recognizes the impulse to build a self out of money and to believe that a lost moment can be recovered with enough of it. Nothing has to be reconstructed. The novel arrives operative.

That is the mechanism stated as plainly as it can be. A novel whose content is an arrangement dates when the arrangement does. A novel whose content is a desire does not date, because desires are the slowest-moving thing in a culture.

An essay that runs this comparison properly, rather than asserting that Fitzgerald was better, has produced an argument that a grader can check and that generalizes past the two books.

### Which contemporary is the best comparison case?

Sinclair Lewis, and Babbitt specifically. It is close in ambition and opposite in method: same decade, same subject of American materialism, and a satire aimed at a social arrangement rather than at a desire. Lewis took the first American Nobel and is now a specialist taste, which isolates the variable cleanly.

## Why Contemporary Judgment Failed

The 1925 record raises a question worth answering directly, since students often assume the critics of the time were simply wrong.

They were not stupid and they were not, on their own terms, mistaken. The critical apparatus of 1925 assessed novels largely on scope, social insight, and moral seriousness, which were the values a realist century had installed. Judged on those criteria, Arrowsmith is plainly the more impressive achievement: larger, more informed, more ambitious about diagnosing an institution, and more obviously about something. Gatsby is small, its cast is unpleasant, its plot is thin, and its central character is a criminal in love with a married woman.

What the 1925 apparatus had no way to measure was the value of the things Gatsby is made of. Symbolic architecture, unreliable narration, compression as an instrument rather than a limitation, and prose that evaluates while it describes were not yet standard critical categories. The equipment for seeing what Fitzgerald had done was assembled over the following twenty-five years, largely in response to modernist poetry, and it arrived in the academy just in time for the revival.

This has two implications worth stating in an essay.

The first is that contemporary reception is close to worthless as a predictor, not because reviewers are careless but because a critical apparatus can only detect what it has vocabulary for. New techniques are systematically undervalued at the moment of their introduction.

The second is a caution about the present. Whatever is currently being underrated is presumably being underrated for the same structural reason, and the confidence with which any period ranks its own novels should be discounted accordingly. The 1925 lists are not a story about bad taste. They are a demonstration that the instruments were calibrated for the previous generation's achievements.

## What Endurance Costs a Book

A closing complication, because the article would be dishonest without it.

Survival is not free. A novel that becomes canonical acquires a set of standard readings, an apparatus of study guides and lesson plans, and a population of readers who met it under obligation. Gatsby's images are so widely circulated that the green light has been flattened in general use into a simple figure for unattainable desire, which loses the three-stage change that is the novel's actual argument about it. Its narrator's unreliability, once a genuine discovery, is now announced in the first week of the unit. The book is known before it is read.

The properties that produced the endurance are the same ones that produced the flattening. Portable symbols travel, and things that travel get simplified. Interpretive openness generates disagreement, and disagreement gets summarized into a list of standard positions. Compression makes the book teachable, and teaching produces the readers who arrive already briefed.

None of this argues against the book or against the framework. It does mean that the novel a reader picks up today comes with a layer of accumulated interpretation that its first readers did not have, and that reading it well now requires stripping some of that away. The recommendation follows from the mechanism: read the sentences rather than the symbols, argue with the narrator rather than accepting his account, and treat the last two pages as the argument rather than as a beautiful ending.

A book that has lasted a century has earned that attention, and the attention is what the endurance was for.
## Why These Particular Images Travel

The portability claim deserves testing on the actual objects, because not all symbols are equally exportable and the difference is instructive.

The green light travels furthest. Its structure is a visible object of desire, separated from the desirer by water, belonging to someone else, and diminishing in value the moment it is reached. Every element of that structure is available to a person who has never opened the novel, which is why the phrase functions in general English without attribution. It also has a built-in irony that survives extraction: the light is a piece of dock hardware, and the disproportion between what it is and what it carries is the whole joke.

The valley of ashes travels well and less far. Its structure is a ruined corridor between wealth and the city that everyone must cross and nobody looks at, which requires slightly more explanation to deploy but retains its force once given. Its strength is that it is a place rather than an object, and places can be pointed at in other contexts.

The eyes on the billboard travel unevenly, because their meaning is contested inside the novel itself. Wilson takes them for God and Michaelis corrects him that it is an advertisement, and Fitzgerald prints both without adjudicating. That instability makes them excellent for teaching and awkward for casual use, since a figure that means two opposite things resists shorthand.

The uncut books in the library travel almost not at all, and they are the novel's most economical joke: a man builds a library of real books and never opens one, so the display is authentic and the substance is absent. It is a superb image and it requires the scene to work, which is why nobody quotes it and why the novel keeps it as an internal pleasure rather than an export.

The pattern is clear enough to state as a rule. An image travels in proportion to how much of its meaning is carried by its structure rather than by its context. The green light carries almost all of its meaning structurally. The uncut books carry almost none.

That rule matters for the endurance argument because it identifies what Fitzgerald actually did that his contemporaries did not. Realist novels of the decade are full of excellent images that are inseparable from their scenes. Fitzgerald built a handful that could leave the building, and those few have been advertising the book for a century at no cost to anyone.

## Endurance Outside the United States

The novel's international position provides a useful check on the argument, because it removes the American institutional machinery from the equation.

Gatsby is read, translated, taught at university level, and adapted well beyond the United States, in places where no eleventh-grade American literature survey exists and no wartime paperback program ever operated. Its standing abroad is lower than at home, which is what one would expect for a novel so specifically about an American promise, and it is substantial and durable.

That matters for the counter-reading. If the endurance were purely a product of American curricula and postwar American criticism, the novel would be a domestic phenomenon and would not travel. It does travel, which indicates that something in the book rather than something in the school system is doing work.

It also indicates which leg of the triad is most portable across cultures. The symbols travel best, since they are structural. The myth travels reasonably well, because aspiration and the irrecoverable past are not uniquely American even though the novel's version of them is. What travels least is the specific national argument about a continent and a promise, which is why the final page tends to be the passage international readers find most beautiful and least urgent.

## The Second Century

A brief forward look, offered as a prediction the framework generates rather than as a certainty.

The institutional supports are the mutable part. Curricula change, and the classroom position that currently supplies most of the novel's readership is not guaranteed. If it were removed, the triad predicts survival at a lower volume: continuously in print, read by serious general readers, taught at university, argued about by critics, and absent from the national conversation. That is the position Willa Cather occupies, and Cather has held it comfortably for a century.

The intrinsic supports are the stable part, with one caveat. Compression becomes more valuable as attention fragments, so that leg is strengthening. Portable symbols remain portable. The myth is the leg to watch, since it depends on a national story about self-invention retaining its grip, and a country that stopped believing that story would find the novel newly historical.

That is the framework's honest prediction and its own falsification condition. If the American aspiration myth fades, Gatsby becomes a period document about a belief people used to hold, which is precisely what happened to the satires it outlasted. The novel is not immune to the mechanism that killed its contemporaries. It has simply been attached to a slower-moving target.

## What Fitzgerald Thought He Was Doing

A biographical note, kept narrow because the subject invites overreading.

Fitzgerald was explicit with his editor that he intended this book to be different from his earlier ones: consciously artistic, patterned, sustained, and short. He revised heavily, including in proof, and he cared intensely about the title, cycling through several candidates before settling. He believed he had written something better than his previous work, and the disappointing sales did not shake that belief so much as convince him the audience was not there.

Two things follow that bear on endurance.

The first is that the properties described in this article were not accidental in the way an unconscious felicity would be. A writer aiming at compression, pattern, and sustained control produces compression, pattern, and sustained control, and those turn out to be durability properties. He was not aiming at posterity through a formula; he was aiming at a well-made book, and a well-made short book with a permanent subject is what lasts.

The second is that his judgment was better than the market's and better than most of the reviewers', which is a reminder that authorial self-assessment is occasionally accurate. He was wrong about many things, including his own career management, and he was right about this book.

The counter-caution matters too. Fitzgerald also believed Tender Is the Night was his major achievement, and that book has not endured comparably. Authorial judgment is not a reliable instrument in general; it happened to be correct here. An essay should use the biographical material to establish intention rather than to argue merit, since the merit case has to be made from the text.

What the intention establishes is that the compression was chosen. That single fact carries a good deal of weight, because it converts the central durability property from a happy accident into a decision, and decisions can be studied and learned from in a way that accidents cannot.
## The Durability Table

The table below is this article's findable artifact. It pairs each lasting quality with the mechanism by which it kept the novel alive and with the corresponding failure mode in the books that faded.

| Quality | What it delivers | Mechanism of survival | The contemporary failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compression, about fifty thousand words | A book finishable in an evening and rereadable every few years | Cheap return visits build advocacy; density puts argument at sentence level | The two-hundred-thousand-word social panorama, admired and unfinished |
| A myth rather than a target | A subject readers are inside rather than one they observe | Aspiration and the irrecoverable past do not expire with a decade | Satire aimed at booster culture, small-town conformity, or tent revivalism, which lost force when the targets receded |
| Portable symbols | Images that carry meaning outside the book that produced them | Continuous unattributed circulation advertises the novel for free | Allegory that requires its own novel to be explained, or realism with no transferable image at all |
| Interpretive openness | Contested questions with evidence on multiple sides | Each new critical regime finds something, so the book is re-canonized rather than merely preserved | Novels with one clear thesis, which one school reads and the next has no use for |
| A narrator with a stake | An account whose reliability is arguable | Sustains rereading, since the second pass is a different book | Omniscient social narration, which yields the same book every time |
| Teachability | Length, structure, and symbols suited to a classroom | An assigned book acquires a self-renewing readership every year | Length or difficulty that excludes the title from secondary curricula |

The decisive row is the second. Compression and symbols are advantages, and a novel with both but no myth is a well-made period piece. The myth is what makes the other two worth having.

## Testing the Triad Against the Field

A framework earns its place by sorting cases it was not built from. Applied to the decade, the triad performs.

Sinclair Lewis scores one out of three. His myths are targets, his novels are long, and his images do not travel. He won the Nobel and is now a specialist taste, which the framework predicts.

Willa Cather scores well on compression and myth and moderately on symbol. A Lost Lady is short, its subject is the passing of a frontier ideal rather than a satirical object, and its central image is a woman rather than a portable device. She endures, less loudly than Fitzgerald, which is roughly what two and a half legs would predict.

Hemingway scores well on all three. The Sun Also Rises is short, its subject is a generation's damage rather than an institution, and its images travel. He endures at comparable strength.

Faulkner is the framework's interesting failure, or rather its instructive exception. He scores badly on compression, well on myth, and well on symbol, and he endures on the strength of technical influence among writers rather than general readership. The triad predicts durability of readership; it does not measure influence on other novelists, and Faulkner shows those are different quantities.

Edna Ferber and Booth Tarkington score on none of the three, both being long, topical, and imageless in the relevant sense, and both have faded from general circulation despite enormous contemporary success and multiple Pulitzers.

James Branch Cabell scores on symbol alone, and Jurgen is precisely the case that shows a single leg does not carry the structure.

Zora Neale Hurston is the framework's most useful complication. Their Eyes Were Watching God scores on all three and still vanished for decades, because the mechanisms that convert intrinsic quality into survival, publication, criticism, curricula, are not neutral, and hers was recovered only when the profession changed in the 1970s. This is the necessary caveat to the whole argument: the triad describes what makes a book revivable, and revival still requires an institution willing to do it.

## What the Contemporaries Got Wrong

Three specific mistakes recur among the books that faded, and naming them is more useful than listing titles.

The first is dating the prose to its slang. Fitzgerald uses period vocabulary sparingly and mostly in dialogue, and his narration is written in a register that has aged very little. Novels of the decade that leaned into contemporary speech in their narration now read as costume. The lesson generalizes: the closer a novel's narrating voice sits to the way people were talking that year, the faster it dates.

The second is making the social attitudes explicit rather than dramatized. Every novel of the 1920s contains the period's assumptions about race, class, and gender. Gatsby contains them, and Tom Buchanan's recitation of a pseudo-scientific racial theory is presented as evidence against Tom rather than as the narrator's position. Novels that instead deliver such material in the narrator's own voice cannot be read now without constant friction, and friction reduces circulation.

The third is resolving the argument. Many contemporary novels tell the reader what to conclude. Gatsby does not, and its central questions, whether Nick is honest, whether Gatsby is admirable, whether the novel indicts or mourns the era, remain genuinely open. Openness is a durability property because it produces disagreement, and disagreement produces continued discussion, and continued discussion is what keeps a book in print.

## The Counter-Reading and Why It Loses

The strongest objection to everything above is that this is retrospective rationalization. We know Gatsby survived, so we assemble properties that explain it, and a different survivor would have produced a different and equally convincing list. The objection deserves respect, since literary history is full of this error.

Three answers.

The first is predictive. The triad was assembled from Gatsby and it sorts other cases correctly, including cases that cut against it. Lewis, Cather, Hemingway, Ferber, Tarkington, and Cabell all land where the framework says they should, and it correctly identifies Faulkner as measuring a different quantity. A rationalization that only fits its origin case is worthless; one that sorts a field is doing work.

The second is mechanical. The properties are not vague virtues but specific causal claims. Compression produces rereading because rereading is cheap. Portable symbols produce free advertising because people use them without attribution. Openness produces disagreement, which produces discussion. Each claim identifies a mechanism that can be checked rather than a quality that can be asserted.

The third is the negative case. If survival were random or purely institutional, the surviving set would look arbitrary. It does not. The 1920s novels still in general circulation are disproportionately short, disproportionately mythic rather than topical, and disproportionately image-rich, while the ones that faded are disproportionately long, topical, and imageless. That distribution is not what chance produces.

What the objection does correctly establish is the limit stated earlier. Intrinsic properties make a book revivable and do not guarantee revival. Hurston is the proof. The honest formulation is that Gatsby had the properties and also got the institutional break, and that the second without the first would have produced a brief flurry and nothing more.
## Three Misreadings to Retire

The endurance question attracts three errors, and pre-empting them inside the body of an essay is worth more than a defensive conclusion.

The first is that Gatsby endured because it is the best novel of its decade. This may be true and it explains nothing, because the same claim was available for Arrowsmith in 1926 and was made confidently by people with a Nobel committee behind them. Quality judgments are outputs of the process being explained, not inputs to it. An essay that leads with quality has assumed its conclusion.

The second is that the revival explains everything. It explains the opportunity and not the outcome. The wartime program covered well over a thousand titles, Wilson's editorial attention was available to others, and the postwar academy was canonizing at speed across the whole of American literature. Something had to distinguish the candidates that took from the many that did not.

The third is that endurance was inevitable given the quality, which is the reverse error and equally lazy. Hurston refutes it in a sentence. Their Eyes Were Watching God has compression, myth, and images, and it disappeared for four decades because the institutions were not looking. Intrinsic properties make a book revivable. They do not make anyone revive it.

The defensible position holds all three corrections together. Fitzgerald built a novel with the properties that make survival possible, the postwar apparatus supplied the occasion, and the combination produced a canonical text. Remove either half and the outcome changes.

## Endurance and the Reader Today

A final observation, since this subject has a practical use beyond literary history.

The triad is a reading tool as well as an explanatory one. Applied to a contemporary novel, it asks three questions with real predictive content. Is the book short enough that a reader who liked it will return to it? Is its subject a permanent structure of human wanting, or is it a current arrangement that will look different in twenty years? Does it contain an image that would still carry meaning if someone quoted it to a person who had not read the book?

Most contemporary fiction fails the second question, and failing it is close to fatal. Novels organized around a present grievance are the modern equivalent of the 1920s satire: intelligent, well aimed, and dated on a schedule. This is not an argument against writing them, since a novel that speaks powerfully to its moment has done something valuable whether or not it lasts. It is an argument for being clear-eyed about which kind of book you are reading and which kind you are writing.

Applied backward, the triad also gives a reader a way into neglected books. A forgotten novel that scores well on all three is a good candidate for personal rediscovery, since the properties that would have kept it alive are present and only the institutional break was missing. That is how Hurston came back, and it is a repeatable procedure rather than a lucky accident.

The larger point is that literary survival is not mysterious and is not simply just. It runs on identifiable mechanisms, most of them unromantic: how long a book takes to reread, whether its images can be quoted out of context, whether its questions stay open long enough for a second school of critics to have something to say. Fitzgerald did not set out to satisfy those conditions. He set out to write something short and perfect, and short and perfect turns out to be what lasts.

## A Fourth Property Worth Naming

The triad is the core claim, and one supporting property recurs often enough to deserve separate mention: the novel's willingness to be liked for the wrong reasons.

Gatsby contains genuine glamour. The parties are seductive, the clothes are beautiful, the houses are enormous, and Fitzgerald renders all of it with obvious pleasure. A substantial share of the novel's readership over a century has enjoyed it primarily as a spectacle of wealth, and a substantial share of its cultural circulation, the themed parties, the fashion references, the shorthand for a glittering decade, rests entirely on that reading.

Purists find this irritating and it is a durability asset. A book that only rewards the correct reading depends on correct readers, and correct readers are scarce in every generation. A book that also rewards a shallow reading acquires an audience far larger than the one that understands it, and the larger audience keeps it in print, on shelves, and in the culture, where the smaller audience can find it.

The mechanism is worth stating precisely, because it looks like a concession and is not. Fitzgerald did not dilute the book to broaden it. He wrote about something genuinely seductive and rendered the seduction honestly, which is what the argument required: a novel that made the dream look cheap could not have shown why anyone pursued it. The wide readership is a consequence of the artistic decision rather than a compromise of it.

Compare a satire again. Babbitt offers a reader nothing to enjoy uncritically, because enjoying the boosters' luncheon is precisely what the book forbids. It therefore depends entirely on readers who share its judgment, and when the judgment stopped being urgent the readers left. Gatsby has always had two audiences, and the larger one has been carrying the smaller one for a hundred years.
## How to Write About the Novel's Endurance

Four moves produce a strong essay on this subject and avoid the two failure modes, which are vague praise and pure institutional history.

Refuse the luck-only account in the opening and say why. The wartime paperback program distributed well over a thousand titles and produced very few canonical resurrections. That single sentence establishes that distribution is not sufficient and creates the space for an argument about properties.

Name the properties as mechanisms, not virtues. Do not write that the novel is beautifully written and therefore endured, which explains nothing, since many beautifully written novels have not. Write that a fifty-thousand-word length makes rereading cheap, that cheap rereading produces advocates, and that advocates keep a book in print. Each step is checkable.

Test the framework on a case you were not given. Take any 1920s novel outside the ones discussed here, apply compression, myth, and symbol, and see whether the prediction matches the outcome. A framework that sorts unfamiliar cases is a contribution; one that only relabels the assigned book is a summary.

Concede the institutional limit honestly. Hurston had all three properties and vanished for forty years because the profession was not looking for her. Stating this makes the argument more credible rather than less, because it distinguishes what the properties do, which is make a book revivable, from what they cannot do, which is guarantee that anyone revives it. Readers who want to test the compression claim directly can [read and annotate The Great Gatsby free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.org/tools/great-gatsby-annotated-text.html), where the passages that carry the most argument per sentence, the Cody summary in Chapter Six and Jordan's account of the wedding in Chapter Four, can be marked against their length.

## What Endurance Is Not

Three clarifications keep the claim honest.

Endurance is not the same as quality. Some excellent books fade and some mediocre ones persist, and the triad describes survival mechanics rather than merit. The case for the novel's actual quality is a different argument, made in the series discussion of [why The Great Gatsby is the great American novel](/2022/01/24/great-gatsby-great-american-novel/), and it should not be conflated with this one. A student who argues that Gatsby survived because it is the best novel of the decade has skipped the entire analytical problem.

Endurance is not the same as influence. Faulkner has shaped more novelists than Fitzgerald and is read by far fewer people. Those are different currencies and a good essay keeps them separate.

Endurance is not permanent. A book with the triad is well positioned and not immortal. The classroom position that currently supplies Gatsby's readership is an institutional arrangement subject to change, and if it changes the novel will depend entirely on the intrinsic properties. The framework's prediction is that it would survive that, more quietly, on compression and myth and images, in the way Cather has. That is a claim about the future and therefore untested.

## Closing Verdict

Nobody in 1925 would have picked this book. It was outsold, outprized, and out-argued by novels that were longer, angrier, and better aimed at what the decade was actually talking about. The satires of that year knew exactly what was wrong with America and said so with force, and their accuracy is what buried them, because a target moves and a novel aimed at it does not.

Fitzgerald wrote about something that does not move: a man who believes he can rebuild himself and repurchase a moment, and a country that told him he could. He wrote it in fifty thousand words, so that finishing it costs an evening and returning to it costs almost nothing. And he attached it to a handful of images that work outside the book, so that the novel is advertised daily by people who have never read it.

Compression, myth, and symbol. The revival gave it the chance, and those three properties are the reason the chance took.

The useful lesson is not about Fitzgerald. It is that literary survival runs on mechanisms rather than on justice, and the mechanisms are unromantic enough to be checked: how long a book takes to reread, whether its images can be quoted to someone who has not read it, whether its questions stay open long enough for a second generation of critics to have work to do. None of those is a measure of worth, and all of them predict outcomes better than sales, prizes, or the confident judgment of a book's own decade. A reader who understands that will hold both the 1925 lists and the current ones more loosely, which is the right posture toward any period's certainty about itself, including our own. The books being overlooked right now are being overlooked for structural reasons, exactly as this one was, and the instruments that would detect them have probably not been built yet.
## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Why did Gatsby outlast its 1920s contemporaries?

Because it had three properties that predict survival and most of its competition did not. Compression: about fifty thousand words, so finishing it costs an evening and rereading it costs almost nothing, which is how advocacy is built. A myth rather than a target: its subject is aspiration and the irrecoverable past, structures that do not expire, rather than an institution that eventually receded. And portable symbols: a green light, a billboard, a valley of ashes, images that carry meaning outside the book and circulate in general speech, advertising the novel for free. The postwar revival supplied the opportunity. These three properties are why the opportunity took.

### Q: What qualities gave Gatsby staying power?

Compression, a mythic rather than topical subject, and portable symbols, working together rather than separately. Compression keeps the myth visible on every page instead of burying it under incident. The myth makes the symbols durable, since an image attached to a permanent desire outlives one attached to a passing grievance. And the symbols give a short book the density it would otherwise lack, so a novel finishable in one sitting rewards a decade of return. Two supporting properties belong alongside them: interpretive openness, since the central questions have evidence on multiple sides, and a narrator whose reliability is genuinely arguable, which makes the second reading a different book.

### Q: Why did many contemporaries date and fade?

Because the dominant serious mode of the decade was satire aimed at an identifiable target, and a satire is only as alive as its object. Novels attacking booster culture, small-town conformity, revivalist religion, or commercialized medicine were intelligent and precisely aimed, and precision at a moving target is exactly what dates. When the tent revival and the Rotary luncheon stopped being central facts of American life, the books that had them in their sights became period documents. Two further mistakes recur: dating the narrating voice to contemporary slang, and delivering the era's social attitudes in the narrator's own voice rather than dramatizing them through a character.

### Q: How did the dream theme stay relevant?

By being a structure rather than a circumstance. The novel's subject is the belief that a person can construct a self and buy back a lost moment, and that belief is not attached to the 1920s. It was operative in 1925, in 1955, and now, and it will be operative as long as the country tells its citizens that arrival is available to whoever wants it enough. The mechanism matters as much as the content. A reader of a satire watches someone they are invited to feel superior to, and that relationship weakens as social distance grows. A reader of Gatsby recognizes an impulse they have had, and recognition does not decay.

### Q: Did Gatsby endure by luck or intrinsic quality?

Both, in a specific order that matters. Intrinsic properties made the novel revivable; contingent events supplied the revival. The wartime paperback program distributed well over a thousand titles and produced very few canonical resurrections, which shows that distribution alone does not do it. The revival was a filter applied to a large field of candidates, and the useful question is what let this candidate pass. A second test settles it: reputations manufactured by a passing critical fashion normally recede when the fashion does, and the New Critical enthusiasms of the 1950s have receded considerably while the novel has not.

### Q: How did the novel's form and myth transcend its moment?

Through interpretive openness combined with a subject large enough to sustain multiple approaches. Fitzgerald resolves nothing. Whether Nick is honest, whether Gatsby is admirable, and whether the book indicts or mourns its era all remain genuinely arguable, with textual evidence on more than one side. That openness meant each successive critical regime found something. Readers trained on close reading found a perfectly constructed symbolic system. Later readers found class, gender, race, and empire. A novel with a single clear thesis gets read once by one school and has nothing to offer the next, which is why so many well-made books of the decade were preserved rather than repeatedly re-canonized.

### Q: Which 1920s bestsellers have been forgotten?

Nearly all of them. The American fiction lists of 1925 were led by titles like Soundings by A. Hamilton Gibbs, now almost entirely unread. Edna Ferber took the Pulitzer for that year and commanded an enormous audience. Booth Tarkington had two Pulitzers and a national readership. Joseph Hergesheimer, James Branch Cabell, and Floyd Dell all held serious critical attention. None of these writers is in general circulation now. The pattern is instructive rather than cruel: contemporary sales and prizes turn out to have almost no predictive power for durability, which is why the 1925 lists are a better argument for the endurance triad than any amount of assertion.

### Q: Did the postwar revival create the reputation or reveal it?

It revealed what was already there and then amplified it, and the distinction is the whole argument. The revival was an opportunity available to many neglected writers: a wartime distribution program covering over a thousand titles, an editor of Wilson's stature working on rediscoveries, a portable edition format, a biography market, and an academic profession canonizing at speed. What has to be explained is not why Gatsby got a chance but why the chance took. The answer is that critics arriving with modernist reading habits found a novel that rewarded them exceptionally well, because the symbolic architecture and the unreliable narration were already in the book.

### Q: Does the period slang date the writing?

Barely, and the reason is a deliberate craft decision. Fitzgerald keeps contemporary vocabulary mostly in dialogue, where it characterizes speakers, and writes his narration in a register that has aged very little. Read Nick's descriptive prose beside the narration of other 1925 novels and the difference is immediate: much of the competition wrote its narration in something close to the way people were talking that year, which now reads as costume. The general rule holds beyond this case. The closer a novel's narrating voice sits to a specific year's speech, the faster it dates, and the further it sits, the longer it lasts.

### Q: Why do the symbols still work a century later?

Because they are portable, meaning they carry their sense outside the book that produced them. The green light does not require the novel to be explained, since the structure it encodes, a visible object of desire separated from the desirer by an uncrossable distance, is legible to anyone who has wanted something. That portability produces a durability engine: every use of the image in journalism, politics, or ordinary speech is a free reminder that the novel exists, delivered by someone with no interest in promoting it. No 1920s satire generated anything comparable, and nobody has ever invoked a Rotary luncheon as a figure for anything.

### Q: What makes a novel age badly?

Four things, on the evidence of this decade. Aiming at a target rather than a structure, since targets recede and the novel aimed at them loses its charge. Writing the narration in the period's own speech, which converts a voice into a costume. Delivering the era's social assumptions in the narrator's voice rather than dramatizing them through a character, which produces constant friction for later readers and reduces circulation. And resolving the argument, telling the reader what to conclude, which forecloses the disagreement that keeps a book discussed. Length compounds all four, because a long book gives each failure more room to operate.

### Q: Did Fitzgerald's other books endure?

Not comparably, which is itself evidence for the triad. This Side of Paradise was his commercial breakthrough and is now read mainly by people interested in Fitzgerald. The Beautiful and Damned has a smaller audience still. Tender Is the Night has serious admirers and a real critical afterlife, and it is longer, more diffuse, and less symbolically organized than Gatsby, which the framework predicts. The Last Tycoon is unfinished. The pattern within a single author's output tracks the properties rather than the author's reputation, and that is a stronger test than comparing writers, since it holds talent constant.

### Q: Is the shortness part of its survival?

It is arguably the single most decisive factor, and it operates through three separate channels. It survives declining attention, since every generation has fewer readers with fifteen free hours than the last, and a three-hour book is insulated against that. It makes rereading cheap, and rereading is where canonical reputations are actually built, because return visits generate the sense that the book contains more than you had seen. And it forced Fitzgerald to compress, so that material a longer novel would dramatize across chapters arrives in a clause, which puts argument at the sentence level and makes the book endlessly discussable.

### Q: Would Gatsby have survived without schools?

Yes, though more quietly. The classroom position supplies most of the current readership and is an institutional arrangement subject to change. If it changed, the novel would fall back on the intrinsic properties, and the framework predicts survival at roughly the level Willa Cather occupies: continuously in print, read by serious general readers, taught at university, absent from the national conversation. The reverse question is more interesting and has a clearer answer. Schools would not have adopted a four-hundred-page topical satire in the first place, so the curricular position is itself a consequence of the compression rather than an independent cause.

### Q: What did the novel avoid that dated its rivals?

Three things it declines to do. It does not name an institution as its antagonist, so there is no target to recede. It does not write its narration in 1922 speech, so the voice has not become costume. And it does not put the period's social assumptions in the narrator's mouth; Tom Buchanan's recitation of a pseudo-scientific racial theory is presented as evidence against Tom rather than as the book's position, which lets a modern reader engage the material as characterization rather than as an obstacle. Each avoidance looks minor in isolation, and together they account for a great deal of the difference in readability across a century.

### Q: Does the Jazz Age setting help or hurt its endurance?

It helps now and could have hurt, and the difference is that the setting is not the subject. The 1920s supply the novel's furniture, the parties, the cars, the Prohibition money, the clothes, and readers enjoy the period considerably. But the argument does not depend on the period, which is why the book survived the decades when the Jazz Age was merely dated rather than glamorous. Compare a novel whose subject is the 1920s, which becomes a document, with one whose subject is aspiration and which happens to be set in the 1920s, which stays a novel. The setting became an asset once it acquired nostalgic value, and that was a bonus rather than the mechanism.

### Q: Which contemporaries have been recovered since?

Zora Neale Hurston is the major case and the most instructive one. Their Eyes Were Watching God has compression, a mythic rather than topical subject, and strong central images, and it vanished for roughly forty years, returning only when the profession changed in the 1970s. That recovery is the necessary caveat to this entire framework. Intrinsic properties make a book revivable; they do not guarantee that any institution will do the reviving, and the mechanisms that convert quality into survival have never been neutral. Nella Larsen and Jean Toomer are comparable recoveries from the same decade and the same critical shift.

### Q: Can we predict which novels of today will last?

The triad gives a usable filter rather than a crystal ball. Ask three questions of any contemporary novel. Is it short enough to be reread cheaply? Is its subject a permanent structure or a current grievance? Does it contain an image that would still mean something if lifted out of the book? A yes to all three is a strong signal, and a no to the second is close to fatal, since topicality is the most reliable predictor of decay. What the filter cannot do is model the institutional side, which is where Hurston's forty years came from, and which depends on choices no framework predicts.
