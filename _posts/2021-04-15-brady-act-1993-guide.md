## What the Brady Act Built

### What problem was the Brady Act written to solve?

The Brady Act was written to replace a paper certification with a verification performed before transfer. Under the prior system, a buyer signed a statement claiming eligibility, a dealer accepted it without checking, and federal enforcement followed only after officials discovered that the statement was false.

Before November 30, 1993, the federal screen at a licensed dealer's counter rested on the buyer. A handgun buyer at a licensed importer, manufacturer, or dealer signed a certification of falling outside the prohibited categories: no felony indictment or conviction, no status as a fugitive from justice, no unlawful use of a controlled substance, no adjudication as a mental defective and no commitment to a mental institution, no unlawful alien status, no dishonorable discharge, and no renunciation of citizenship. The dealer took the signature, and the transaction proceeded. No federal provision required the dealer, a state officer, or a federal agency to test the answers against any record system before the handgun left the counter.

![Brady Act background checks, the interim waiting period and the permanent instant check system - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The consequences of that design were structural. A false statement exposed the buyer to prosecution once the falsity came to light, and possession by a prohibited person exposed the possessor to prosecution once the possession was discovered, but both forms of enforcement operated after the transfer had occurred. Between signature and discovery the statute interposed nothing: no waiting interval keyed to a records search, no official charged with checking the answers, and no federal database built to answer a dealer's inquiry. Federal law defined who could not lawfully receive a handgun and punished false claims to eligibility, yet the law supplied no procedure for testing eligibility at the only moment when a test could prevent the transfer, the moment before the licensed sale was completed.

That statute was the Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act, Public Law 103-159, 107 Stat. 1536, enacted by the 103rd Congress as H.R. 1025 and signed by President Bill Clinton on November 30, 1993. The act amended the Gun Control Act of 1968, the parent statute treated at length in [the Gun Control Act of 1968 complete guide](/2021/04/01/gun-control-act-1968-complete-guide/), and its operative provisions sit in section 922 of title 18 of the United States Code, with subsection (s) carrying the interim procedure and subsection (t) carrying the permanent system. Section 103 of the act directed the Attorney General to establish the national instant criminal background check system, and section 925A of title 18 supplies a remedy for a person denied a firearm because of erroneous information. The act's three titles, in order, are Title I, Brady Handgun Control, at sections 101 to 107; Title II, Multiple Firearm Purchases to State and Local Police; and Title III, the Federal Firearms License Reform Act of 1993.

The architecture those provisions created had two phases and a later third layer. Phase one was interim: from February 1994 to November 1998, handgun transfers by licensees ran through a signed statement, a check by the chief law enforcement officer of the buyer's place of residence, and a five business day waiting structure. A Supreme Court decision then interrupted the design, removing the federal command directed at local officers and leaving the rest of the interim structure intact. Phase two was permanent: from November 30, 1998, a federal instant check applied to all firearms, handguns and long guns alike, with the licensee contacting a federal system or a state point of contact before transfer. The timing mechanism that survived into the permanent phase was the default proceed rule, under which silence from the system at the end of a defined number of business days permits the licensee to complete the transfer. The third layer, added by the records amendments of 2007 and 2018, addressed the completeness of the records that the system searches. The organizing judgment on the constitutional episode can be stated plainly: the Court struck the method, not the requirement.

The interim phase began on February 28, 1994, ninety days after enactment, and ran until November 30, 1998, when the permanent provisions took effect and the interim provisions ceased to apply under a sunset fixed at sixty months after enactment. The interim procedure covered handguns only, and only sales, deliveries, and transfers by licensees to nonlicensees. The licensee received from the transferee a signed statement, the Brady form, certifying that the transferee fell outside the prohibited categories the statement listed. The licensee verified the transferee's identity against a photographic identification document. The licensee transmitted a copy of the statement to the chief law enforcement officer of the transferee's place of residence. The licensee then waited: transfer could proceed on notice from the officer that the transfer would not be unlawful, or once five business days elapsed without notice that the transfer would be unlawful.

The officer's duty sat in subsection (s)(2). Within that period, the officer was to make a reasonable effort to ascertain whether the transferee had a criminal record or any other legal impediment existed, including research in available state and local recordkeeping systems and in a national system designated by the Attorney General. A transferee could present a statement from the officer, issued within the previous ten days, recording a threat to the life of the transferee or a family member. Transfers were excepted in states whose law required verification before transfer under an approved state system, transfers under a qualifying state permit issued within the previous five years were excepted, and transfers that the Secretary of the Treasury certified as impracticable to subject to the procedure were excepted. At the start, the federal procedure applied in 32 states, while the remaining 18 states and the District of Columbia held qualifying alternatives and were exempt; the administration's one year progress report of February 1995 counted 27 states and territories subject, as additional states qualified.

The Bureau of Justice Statistics, in a report of June 15, 1999 on presale handgun checks for the Brady interim period of 1994 to 1998, measured the scale of the first phase. The Bureau of Justice Statistics estimated 12,740,000 presale checks from March 1, 1994 through November 29, 1998, and an estimated 312,000 rejections, about 2.4 percent of the total. About two thirds of the rejections rested on a felony conviction or a pending felony indictment, according to the same 1999 report; domestic violence misdemeanor convictions accounted for roughly one rejection in ten, and protection orders accounted for a small additional share.

The constitutional interruption arrived before the interim phase ended. In Printz v. United States, 521 U.S. 898, decided on June 27, 1997, the Supreme Court considered challenges brought by Jay Printz, the sheriff and coroner of Ravalli County, Montana, and Richard Mack, the sheriff of Graham County, Arizona, each the designated chief law enforcement officer for his jurisdiction. District courts in Montana and Arizona had held the command unconstitutional but severable in 1994, leaving checks voluntary, and the Ninth Circuit had reversed in 1995. By five to four, in an opinion by Justice Scalia joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, and Thomas, with dissents by Justices Stevens, Souter, and Breyer, the Court held the command unconstitutional. The reasoning rested on the structure of dual sovereignty: historical practice and precedent, above all New York v. United States, 505 U.S. 144 (1992), which had barred Congress from commanding state legislatures, established that Congress could not compel state executive officers to administer a federal regulatory program, and conscription blurred political accountability because voters could not tell which level of government to blame.

The holding's boundaries define the interruption. The unconstitutional provision was subsection (s)(2), and the decision extinguished with it the implied duty of an officer to accept completed Brady forms from dealers under subsection (s)(1)(A)(i)(III) and (IV). Everything else survived: the waiting period, every licensee duty covering the statement, identification, notice, and wait, the state alternative systems, the permits exception, and the command to the Attorney General to build the federal system. Nothing in the decision touched the permanent provisions, which bind licensees and the federal system rather than state officers. From the decision until November 30, 1998, checks continued voluntarily where officers chose to run them, and the interim provisions expired on the statutory schedule.

The permanent phase rested on section 103, which had directed the Attorney General to build the national instant system during the interim years. The Department of Justice published the final rule establishing the National Instant Criminal Background Check System on October 30, 1998, and the Federal Bureau of Investigation activated the system on November 30, 1998. The permanent provisions applied to all firearms, handguns and long guns alike, and, unlike the interim provisions, the permanent provisions covered the redemption of a pawned firearm. In operation, the transferee completes Form 4473 of the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives, and the licensee contacts the system: directly to the Federal Bureau of Investigation in states where the Bureau runs the check, through a state point of contact in states that perform their own checks, or through a partial point of contact, under which the state runs handgun checks while the Bureau runs long gun checks. The search runs against three databases managed by the Criminal Justice Information Services Division of the Federal Bureau of Investigation: the Interstate Identification Index, the National Crime Information Center, and the NICS Index, which holds records on prohibited persons not captured in the other two.

The system's answers, under 28 C.F.R. 25.6, are three in number: proceed, under which the licensee receives a NICS Transaction Number; delayed; or denied. The default proceed rule, at section 922(t)(1)(B)(ii), supplies the timing: if the system has not notified the licensee within three business days after contact that receipt would be unlawful, the licensee may transfer the firearm. The licensee remains free to wait, and a denial received before transfer bars the transfer. A business day, under 28 C.F.R. 25.2, is a 24 hour day beginning at 12:01 a.m. on which state offices are open in the state of the transaction; Saturdays, Sundays, state holidays, and the day the check is initiated do not count.

Research does not stop at the three day mark. The NICS Section continues researching a delayed transaction until a determination is reached or the record reaches its purge point, within a 90 day window. A denial arriving after transfer is a delayed denial and produces a firearm retrieval referral to the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives, which bears responsibility for retrieval. The Federal Bureau of Investigation referred 6,361 such cases to that Bureau in 2020, according to the NICS Operations Report for 2020 and 2021. Alternatives to a fresh check survive under section 922(t)(3): a qualifying state permit issued within five years after verification that included, for a permit issued after November 29, 1998, a national instant check; transfers of National Firearms Act firearms approved by the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives; and transfers that Bureau certified as impracticable.

A denied person may challenge the denial with the Federal Bureau of Investigation or the point of contact agency and seek correction of the record; section 925A of title 18 allows a person denied because of erroneous information to sue the United States or the responsible state to correct the information or obtain the firearm. The Voluntary Appeal File of the Federal Bureau of Investigation lets persons repeatedly matched to records that are not theirs store clarifying information and a Unique Personal Identification Number for later transactions. Federal Bureau of Investigation figures record 103,592 federal denials in 2019 and 1,700,558 cumulative federal denials from the November 1998 launch through December 31, 2019.

Section 103(i) forbids any federal department, agency, officer, or employee to use the system to establish a registration system for firearms, firearm owners, or firearm transactions, except with respect to persons prohibited from receiving a firearm. Congress legislated the retention schedule in section 617 of Public Law 108-199, the fiscal 2004 Consolidated Appropriations Act signed on January 23, 2004, requiring destruction of identifying information on persons determined not to be prohibited no more than 24 hours after the licensee was notified of approval.

The third layer began with the NICS Improvement Amendments Act of 2007, Public Law 110-180, signed by President George W. Bush on January 8, 2008. The act followed the April 16, 2007 shooting at Virginia Tech in Blacksburg, Virginia, in which 32 people were killed; in the account given by the Federal Bureau of Investigation, an adjudication that prohibited the shooter was never transmitted to the NICS Index, and licensees received approvals for the purchases. Federal agencies must furnish the Attorney General electronic records on prohibited persons at least quarterly, must update, correct, or remove records, and face an annual compliance report from the Attorney General to Congress. States face completeness benchmarks, with grant money through the NICS Act Record Improvement Program, administered by the Bureau of Justice Statistics, withholding consequences for missed benchmarks, a requirement to establish relief from disabilities programs for full grant support.

The Fix NICS Act of 2018, enacted as Title VI of Division S of the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2018, Public Law 115-141, and signed by President Donald Trump on March 23, 2018, extended the same records logic. The 2018 act followed the November 5, 2017 shooting at the First Baptist Church of Sutherland Springs, Texas, in which 26 people were killed; the shooter, convicted by an Air Force court martial in 2012 of assault on his spouse and child, passed the checks because the Air Force failed to submit the conviction records to the Federal Bureau of Investigation. A Department of Defense Inspector General report of December 2017 found the military services failing to report final disposition outcomes to the Federal Bureau of Investigation in an average of 31 percent of 2015 and 2016 cases: the Air Force at 14 percent, the Army at 41 percent, and the Navy and Marine Corps at 36 percent. Under the 2018 act, the head of each federal agency must certify compliance in writing to the Attorney General semiannually, each agency must publish an implementation plan with benchmarks for maximum coordination and automated reporting, the Attorney General establishes an implementation plan for each state and tribal government, and the Department of Justice reports to Congress semiannually; the act also reauthorized the National Criminal History Improvement Program. The Department of Justice, in the semiannual reporting the 2018 act required, recorded total records in the three searched databases rising from 100,849,193 in April 2018 to 112,352,109 by February 2021.

Title II addressed multiple firearm purchases reported to state and local police, and Title III, the Federal Firearms License Reform Act, addressed dealer licensing and carrier labeling. The act also held defined limits: no new possession offense or prohibited category, no check on transfers between unlicensed persons, no federal waiting period in the permanent phase, and no registration system.

## The Man the Act Is Named For and the Seven Years to Passage

On March 30, 1981, outside the Washington Hilton Hotel, John Hinckley Jr. attempted to assassinate President Ronald Reagan and shot four men. James S. Brady, press secretary to President Reagan, was among the four men shot. Brady was struck in the head, and the wound left Brady permanently partially paralyzed. Those facts form the origin of the statute that carries the Brady name. Public Law 103-159 does not narrate the shooting. The statute instead builds a procedure for transfers by licensed importers, manufacturers, and dealers, and the statute attaches the name of a wounded press secretary to that procedure. The connection between the man and the mechanism came through years of public campaigning by James Brady and Sarah Brady, and through a legislative effort that consumed three Congresses before a bill reached a President for signature on November 30, 1993.

The injury defined the public identity that James Brady carried into the campaigns that followed. After March 30, 1981, Brady held a different kind of public position, that of a survivor whose paralysis was permanent and whose experience gave a personal name to an argument over how handgun transfers should be checked before completion. The operative text of the statute is impersonal: categories of prohibited persons, statements signed by transferees, and duties placed on licensees. The name on the act supplied the human reference point that the operative text lacked, and opponents in Congress were required to vote on a measure identified with a specific man, a specific date, and a specific wound, on the record, in committee and on the floor, across the 100th Congress, the 102nd Congress, and the 103rd Congress.

Brady and his wife Sarah campaigned for the bill for years. The campaign was sustained, organized, and institutional rather than episodic. Sarah Brady joined the board of Handgun Control, Inc. in 1985, four years after the shooting outside the Washington Hilton Hotel. Sarah Brady became chair of Handgun Control, Inc. in 1989. A board seat in 1985 placed Sarah Brady inside an existing organization devoted to the issue. The chairmanship in 1989 placed Sarah Brady at the head of that organization during the period when the bill was reintroduced in the 102nd Congress and again in the 103rd Congress. James Brady and Sarah Brady campaigned together as the public faces of the measure, and the organizations that carried the campaign were renamed in the couple's honor in 2001, eight years after enactment.

James Brady attended the White House signing ceremony on November 30, 1993, when President Bill Clinton signed H.R. 1025 and the measure became Public Law 103-159. The man shot on March 30, 1981, was present on November 30, 1993, when the bill bearing the Brady name became law. James Brady died on August 4, 2014, aged 73. A medical examiner ruled after the death that the death was a homicide caused by the 1981 gunshot wound. That ruling extended the legal consequences of the March 30, 1981, shooting across more than three decades, and the ruling placed the cause of death in the same event that had set the legislative campaign in motion. The act had operated in two distinct phases by the date of that death, an interim phase built around a waiting period and a permanent phase built around a national instant system, and the design that joined those two phases was part of the reason the bill passed in 1993 after failing in earlier Congresses.

### Why did the Brady Act take seven years to pass?

The bill took seven years because support in Congress and the executive branch shifted across three Congresses, early versions failed on the House floor or ended without presidential action, and passage in 1993 required a design that paired a temporary waiting period with a federally built instant check system scheduled to replace it.

The legislative road began on February 4, 1987, in the 100th Congress. Representative Edward F. Feighan introduced the measure in the House of Representatives, and Senator Howard M. Metzenbaum introduced the measure in the Senate. Introduction in both chambers gave the proposal a bicameral start. The original form proposed a seven day waiting period. That single feature defined the first version: a transferee would wait, and during the wait the transfer would not be completed. The seven day figure deserves attention because the enacted statute did not preserve that exact design. The interim provision that took effect on February 28, 1994, used a period of five business days, measured against notice to a chief law enforcement officer and against the absence of notice that a transfer would be unlawful. The permanent provision that took effect on November 30, 1998, imposed no federal waiting period as such and substituted contact with a national instant system, with a default rule measured in three business days. The movement from seven days in the 1987 proposal to five business days in the interim provision and then to an instant system traces the compromise in numerical form.

The first Congress to consider the measure carried the bill part of the distance and then stopped the bill on the House floor. The House Judiciary Committee approved the measure in June 1988, as part of omnibus drug legislation. Committee approval in June 1988 showed that the proposal could clear the committee stage in the House. Approval as part of omnibus drug legislation also showed a procedural reality that shaped the entire seven years: a single issue measure could advance by attachment to a larger vehicle, or a single issue measure could be forced to stand alone on the floor and face a direct vote on its own terms. On September 15, 1988, the House defeated the measure on the floor by 228 to 182. A floor defeat ends a bill in practical terms for that Congress, because a defeated measure has no path to the Senate, to conference, or to the President without a separate successful action that the record of the 100th Congress did not produce. The 1988 sequence established the pattern that repeated in altered form in the 102nd Congress: progress at one stage, followed by failure to complete every stage required before presentment to the President.

The second attempt came in the 102nd Congress. Representative Charles E. Schumer reintroduced the measure in March 1991 as H.R. 7. Sponsorship had changed from Representative Feighan in 1987 to Representative Schumer in 1991. The House passed H.R. 7 on May 8, 1991. House passage represented the furthest point the proposal had reached to that date, beyond committee approval and beyond the floor defeat of September 15, 1988. Passage in one chamber, however, completes only one stage. A measure must pass both chambers in identical form, resolve any differences between the House text and the Senate text, and reach the President before the Congress that produced the measure ends. No vehicle carried the measure to the President in the 102nd Congress. The measure died without reaching the President, and the end of the 102nd Congress ended H.R. 7 as a legislative vehicle. Every gain recorded in 1991 had to be rebuilt in a new Congress under a new bill number, with new committee action and new floor action in both chambers.

The third attempt began on February 22, 1993, when Representative Schumer reintroduced the measure as H.R. 1025 in the 103rd Congress. Reintroduction came early in the first session of the new Congress, and the sequence that followed moved faster than the sequences of 1987 to 1988 or 1991 to 1992. The House passed H.R. 1025 on November 10, 1993, by 238 to 189. Set against the House tally of September 15, 1988, when 228 members voted against the measure and 182 members voted for the measure, the November 10, 1993, tally showed a reversal of the floor result in the same chamber that had defeated the proposal five years earlier. The House that passed H.R. 1025 differed in composition from the House that had defeated the predecessor measure, because House membership changes at each biennial election. The comparison between the two House tallies is the clearest numerical evidence in the passage record for the proposition that changing congressional composition changed the outcome.

The Senate passed H.R. 1025 on November 20, 1993, by 63 to 36. Senate passage on November 20, 1993, ten days after House passage on November 10, 1993, meant that both chambers had approved the measure during a single month, a pace that neither the 100th Congress nor the 102nd Congress had achieved. Senate passage also created the need for the conference stage, which reconciles differences between House action and Senate action and produces a single text that both chambers then accept or reject without amendment. A conference report was filed on November 22, 1993, two days after Senate passage, and the two chambers acted on the conference report on consecutive days.

The House agreed to the conference report on November 23, 1993, by 238 to 187. The Senate agreed to the conference report on November 24, 1993, by voice vote, after two cloture motions on the conference report were withdrawn by unanimous consent. The Senate sequence of November 24, 1993, carries procedural weight that a simple tally cannot capture. A cloture motion is a motion to end debate, and the filing of two such motions indicated that extended debate had been anticipated or threatened. Withdrawal of both motions by unanimous consent indicated that no senator objected to withdrawal, and agreement by voice vote completed Senate action without a recorded roll call tally on the final question. The measure therefore cleared the Senate for the second time in four days, first by a recorded tally of 63 to 36 on November 20, 1993, and then by voice vote on November 24, 1993. President Bill Clinton signed the measure on November 30, 1993, six days after Senate agreement and twenty days after House passage, and the signed measure became the Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act, Public Law 103-159, 107 Stat. 1536, an amendment to the Gun Control Act of 1968.

Considered as a whole, the passage record describes a bill that failed at a different stage in each unsuccessful Congress. In the 100th Congress, the measure cleared the House Judiciary Committee in June 1988 and failed on the House floor on September 15, 1988, by 228 to 182. In the 102nd Congress, the measure passed the House on May 8, 1991, and failed to reach the President at all. In the 103rd Congress, every stage occurred in the same Congress, in the correct order, before that Congress ended. Committee approval did not guarantee floor passage in 1988, and floor passage in one chamber did not guarantee presentment in 1991. The seven years from February 4, 1987, to November 30, 1993, were therefore not a single continuous consideration of one bill. The seven years were three separate legislative efforts, each starting again from introduction, and the elapsed time measured the difficulty of completing a full sequence rather than the length of debate on any one version.

Executive support formed the second variable in that sequence. A bill that reaches a President requires a signature, or a veto override by two thirds of both chambers, before the bill becomes law. In the 102nd Congress, the measure never reached the President, so no signature and no veto occurred, and executive support had no formal occasion to decide the outcome. In the 103rd Congress, President Clinton signed the measure on November 30, 1993, in the sequence that placed James Brady at the White House signing ceremony. The signature was the final act in the legislative process and the first act in the administrative process, because section 103 of the act directed the Attorney General to establish the national instant system that the permanent provisions would use. The interim provisions took effect on February 28, 1994, ninety days after enactment. The Federal Bureau of Investigation activated the permanent system on November 30, 1998, the date on which the interim provisions ceased to apply. A President who signed the bill in 1993 therefore committed the executive branch to a five year construction task with a statutory deadline, and that commitment was part of the enacted design rather than an addition made after passage.

The enacted design formed the third variable, and the design functioned as the compromise that carried the bill in 1993. The original proposal of February 4, 1987, rested on a seven day waiting period as the central mechanism. A waiting period operates through delay: a licensee holds a transfer while time passes and while a check, if a check occurs, has time to occur. The enacted statute kept a waiting period but bounded that period in time and attached a replacement to the same text. Under the interim provision in section 922(s) of title 18, a licensee received a signed statement from the transferee, verified the transferee's identity against a photographic identification document, transmitted a copy of the statement to the chief law enforcement officer of the transferee's place of residence, and waited five business days unless that officer notified the licensee earlier that the transfer would not be unlawful. Under section 103 of the act, the Attorney General was directed to build a national instant system during the same period. Under the permanent provision in section 922(t) of title 18, which took effect on November 30, 1998, the licensee would contact the system before a transfer, and the system would respond with a direction to proceed, a delayed response, or a denial. The waiting period was therefore enacted as a temporary bridge with a stated end, and the instant system was enacted as the permanent replacement for that bridge.

That structure answered objections from more than one direction without adopting the full position of any single holder. A legislator who supported a check before transfer received a check in both phases, performed by a chief law enforcement officer under the interim provision and by the national system under the permanent provision. A legislator who opposed an open ended waiting period received a waiting period with a fixed terminus, because the interim subsection ran by its own text to the day before the date sixty months after enactment. A legislator who doubted that a national instant system could be built received a five year construction interval during which the interim procedure operated. None of those positions carried in pure form. The seven day waiting period of the 1987 proposal did not survive as proposed, a permanent waiting period did not survive, and an instant system standing alone, with no interim procedure, did not take effect in 1994. The statute that President Clinton signed on November 30, 1993, combined elements that earlier bills had presented separately, and combination rather than repetition distinguished H.R. 1025 from H.R. 7 and from the measure defeated on September 15, 1988.

Time itself imposed a further constraint. A Congress lasts two years, and a bill introduced in one Congress expires with that Congress, so sponsors must begin again in the next Congress regardless of how far the earlier bill advanced. The measure introduced on February 4, 1987, belonged to the 100th Congress. H.R. 7, introduced in March 1991, belonged to the 102nd Congress. H.R. 1025, introduced on February 22, 1993, belonged to the 103rd Congress. Sponsors therefore worked inside three separate two year windows, with a gap between the House defeat of September 15, 1988, and the reintroduction of March 1991, and another gap between the end of the 102nd Congress and the reintroduction of February 22, 1993. Within each window, sponsors had to secure committee consideration, floor time in the House, floor time in the Senate, agreement between the chambers, and presentment, in competition with every other measure seeking the same floor time. A single issue bill has no omnibus vehicle of its own, and the one occasion on which the measure advanced as part of omnibus drug legislation, in June 1988, ended in floor defeat on September 15, 1988.

The namesake gave sponsors a resource that partly offset those structural disadvantages, and the passage record shows where that resource reached its limits. James Brady and Sarah Brady campaigned for years, Sarah Brady held a board seat at Handgun Control, Inc. from 1985 and the chairmanship from 1989, and the couple supplied a continuous public presence across three Congresses. Continuity of advocacy, however, did not by itself produce a tally. The House tally of 228 to 182 on September 15, 1988, was recorded after Sarah Brady had held a board seat for three years. The House passage of May 8, 1991, was recorded after Sarah Brady had held the chairmanship for two years, and that passage produced no law because the measure never reached the President in the 102nd Congress. The tallies of November 1993 were recorded in a Congress whose composition and whose President differed from those of 1988 and 1991. Personal advocacy supplied persistence across the gaps between Congresses. Votes supplied the rest, and votes depended on chamber composition and on a text that enough members of both chambers could accept in the same session.

The final month of 1993 shows how narrow the completed sequence was even after the tallies turned favorable. Twenty days separated House passage on November 10, 1993, from signature on November 30, 1993, and a large share of those days was consumed by Senate passage, conference, and agreement. A delay at any point in that month, a failure to withdraw the two cloture motions of November 24, 1993, a rejection of the conference report by either chamber, or a decision by President Clinton to withhold a signature, would have returned sponsors to the position occupied at the end of the 100th Congress and at the end of the 102nd Congress, with progress recorded and no statute enacted. The statute that emerged carried the structure the long process had produced: Title I addressed handgun transfers through licensees, first through an interim statement and waiting procedure and then through a permanent instant system, and the operative provisions amended section 922 of title 18. The name attached to Public Law 103-159 recorded the man whose wounding on March 30, 1981, had supplied the campaign with a beginning, and the organizations renamed in the couple's honor in 2001 traced back to that same beginning.

## The Interim Phase: Five Business Days and the Local Officer

The Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act built its first screening system as a temporary structure with a fixed end written into the statute itself. The interim provisions took effect on February 28, 1994, ninety days after enactment on November 30, 1993, and the operative text of subsection (s) of section 922 of title 18 ran to the day before the date sixty months after enactment. That arithmetic placed the end of the interim phase at November 30, 1998, when the permanent provisions took effect and the interim provisions ceased to apply. The phase therefore lasted a defined span of four years and nine months, bounded at the front by a ninety day preparation interval and at the back by a self executing expiration that required no further act of Congress to bring the temporary procedure to a close.

The scope of the interim procedure was narrow in two directions at once. The statute regulated handguns only, leaving long guns outside the presale procedure that subsection (s) created, and the duties of the phase attached only to transfers by licensed importers, manufacturers, and dealers to persons who were not licensees. A sale, delivery, or transfer that fell outside either boundary fell outside the interim check as well. The design placed the federal screening obligation on the licensed trade at the point of sale, and it placed the handgun, the firearm type at the center of the legislation from its introduction onward, at the center of the temporary system. Every element that followed, the statement, the identification check, the notice to a local officer, and the wait, operated only inside that defined perimeter.

### How did the interim Brady check actually work?

A licensed dealer first took a signed statement from the buyer certifying that the buyer fell outside seven prohibited categories, verified photographic identification, sent a copy to the chief law enforcement officer of the buyer's residence, and then waited for clearance or for five business days to pass without an adverse notice.

The sequence began with paper rather than with a database. Before a licensee could transfer a handgun to a non licensee, the licensee had to receive from the transferee a signed statement, the form associated with the act and known as the Brady form, in which the transferee certified a personal status under the categories the statement listed. The signature mattered to the structure of the procedure because the first screen in the interim system was a sworn style attestation by the buyer, made at the counter and preserved as part of the transaction. The licensee did not generate the certification and could not supply it on the transferee's behalf. The transferee supplied the statement, and the licensee received it, verified identity against it, transmitted a copy onward, and held the handgun while the remainder of the procedure ran its course.

The seven certifications inside the statement tracked the prohibited categories that the form enumerated, and each one required the transferee to certify a negative. The transferee certified that the transferee was not under indictment for a felony and had not been convicted of a felony, joining the pending charge and the completed conviction in a single attestation about criminal status. The transferee certified that the transferee was not a fugitive from justice, a category directed at flight from legal process rather than at any underlying offense. The transferee certified that the transferee was not an unlawful user of a controlled substance, extending the attestation beyond court records into personal conduct. The transferee certified that the transferee had not been adjudicated as a mental defective and had not been committed to a mental institution, two distinct legal events that the statement treated together as one certification about adjudicated mental status.

The remaining certifications completed the set along lines of status rather than conduct. The transferee certified that the transferee was not an alien unlawfully in the United States, the statement using the period's statutory vocabulary of unlawful alien status. The transferee certified that the transferee had not been discharged from the armed forces under dishonorable conditions. The transferee certified that the transferee had not renounced United States citizenship. Taken together, the seven certifications converted the prohibited categories listed on the statement into a personal declaration that the licensee could collect, file, and forward, and a false attestation placed the defect in the transaction at its first step, in a document bearing the transferee's own signature. The statement asked the transferee to speak to every listed category, so silence or refusal on any one of them left the licensee without the document the procedure required before any transfer could proceed.

The second step tied the paper statement to a physical person. The licensee verified the transferee's identity against a photographic identification document, matching the individual standing at the counter to the name and particulars entered on the statement. That verification served a distinct function from the certification itself. The certification recorded what the transferee declared, while the identification check established that the person making the declaration was the person named in it. Without that link, a statement could travel through the rest of the procedure detached from the individual who would receive the handgun, and the copy sent to the chief law enforcement officer would describe a name rather than an identified buyer. The statute placed the verification duty on the licensee, and the duty sat alongside the receipt of the statement as one of the obligations that belonged to the dealer in every covered transaction.

The third step moved the transaction out of the shop and into a local office. The licensee transmitted a copy of the statement to the chief law enforcement officer of the transferee's place of residence. The destination was defined by residence rather than by the location of the sale, so a handgun purchased away from home generated a notice directed to the officer responsible for the jurisdiction where the transferee lived. The transmitted copy carried the transferee's certifications into the hands of the one official the interim system charged with testing them against records. The licensee's act of transmission started the practical clock of the phase, because the officer's opportunity to respond ran within the same five business day window that governed the licensee's wait, and the two duties, the dealer's patience and the officer's inquiry, proceeded in parallel from that point.

The wait could end in either of two ways, and the statute defined both. The transfer could proceed if the chief law enforcement officer notified the licensee that the transfer would not be unlawful, an affirmative communication that closed the interval early and allowed the licensee to complete the sale without consuming the full period. The transfer could also proceed once five business days had elapsed without notice that the transfer would be unlawful. Silence therefore had a legal consequence under subsection (s). The expiration of the period without an adverse notice permitted the licensee to transfer the handgun even though no officer had affirmatively approved the transaction, while a notice that the transfer would be unlawful, delivered inside the window, blocked completion. The interim wait was accordingly not a fixed five day detention of every sale. It was a window whose length depended on the officer's response, short where the officer answered promptly in the transferee's favor and full where the officer did not answer at all.

The officer's side of the procedure rested on subsection (s)(2) of section 922, which directed the chief law enforcement officer to make a reasonable effort to ascertain, within five business days, whether the receipt or possession of the handgun by the transferee would be unlawful. The object of the inquiry had two branches. The officer was to determine whether the transferee had a criminal record, and the officer was to determine whether any other legal impediment to the transfer existed. The standard was one of reasonable effort rather than of guaranteed discovery, a formulation that measured the officer's obligation by the diligence of the search instead of by the completeness of the underlying records. The same five business days that limited the licensee's wait also limited the officer's inquiry, so the federal procedure asked local offices to fit a records search of every covered handgun transfer in the jurisdiction inside a single working week.

The search the statute contemplated ran through the record systems that existed at the state and local level together with a federal component. Subsection (s)(2) described research in whatever state and local recordkeeping systems were available to the officer, a phrase that made the reach of each check depend in part on the records infrastructure of the jurisdiction conducting it, and research in a national system designated by the Attorney General. The combination joined decentralized records, held by the governments closest to the transferee's residence, to a designated national source, and the officer conducting the check was the bridge between the two. The interim design thus assigned the verification function to local officers precisely because the relevant records sat in state and local systems, while the licensee's transmitted statement supplied the identifying information that allowed the officer to run the transferee against those systems within the allotted days.

Congress wrote exceptions into the interim procedure for transactions in which the standard sequence was unnecessary, duplicative, or unworkable, and the first exception addressed urgent personal danger. A transferee who presented a statement from the chief law enforcement officer, issued within the previous ten days, attesting that the transferee required a handgun because of a threat to the life of the transferee or to the life of a member of the transferee's family was excused from the ordinary wait. The exception was tightly bounded in time and in content. The officer's statement had to be recent, no more than ten days old at presentation, and the threat it recorded had to run against the transferee or the transferee's family rather than against property or against the public generally. The provision recognized that a five business day interval could defeat the purpose of a purchase made under a documented and immediate threat, and it substituted the officer's prior attestation for the check that the interval would otherwise have carried.

A second exception removed whole categories of transfers from the federal procedure where a state system performed the verification function. Transfers in states whose law required verification before transfer, under an approved state system, were excepted from the interim sequence, because a buyer in such a state passed through a state check that served the same screening purpose. A third exception operated at the level of the individual buyer rather than the state. A transfer made under a qualifying state permit issued within the previous five years was excepted, on the ground that the permit process had already subjected the holder to a determination of eligibility at the time the permit issued. The five year limit kept the exception tied to a comparatively recent state determination, and a permit older than that window could not carry the exception. A fourth exception covered the residual class of transactions that the ordinary sequence could not reach, namely transfers that the Secretary of the Treasury certified as impracticable to subject to the procedure.

The geography of the interim phase followed from the exceptions for state systems and permits. At the start of the phase in 1994, the federal procedure applied in 32 states. The remaining 18 states and the District of Columbia had qualifying alternatives, in the form of permit systems or state operated checks, and were exempt from the federal sequence. The map of the interim system was therefore a divided one from its first day of operation. A handgun buyer in a state subject to the federal procedure encountered a dealer who collected the signed statement, verified photographic identification, notified the chief law enforcement officer of the buyer's residence, and observed the five business day window, while a buyer in an exempt jurisdiction encountered the state alternative instead. The identity of the screening authority, federal procedure administered through local officers or state system administered under state law, depended on the state in which the transferee resided and bought.

The divided map shifted as additional states qualified for exemption during the phase. By February 1995, the administration's one year progress report counted 27 states and territories subject to the federal interim procedure, a reduction from the 32 states counted at the start in 1994 as more states established qualifying alternatives. Each state that qualified moved its handgun transfers out of the federal sequence and into its own system, contracting the territory in which chief law enforcement officers carried the subsection (s)(2) inquiry. The movement ran in one direction during the period the progress report covered, from federal coverage toward state substitution, and it reflected the structure Congress had built, in which the interim federal procedure operated as a default for states without an approved alternative rather than as a uniform national method imposed on every jurisdiction regardless of state law.

Measured across its full span, the interim system processed a volume of transactions that the Bureau of Justice Statistics later reconstructed in its report on the Brady interim period, released on June 15, 1999. The Bureau of Justice Statistics estimated that from March 1, 1994 through November 29, 1998, presale handgun checks under the interim provisions totaled 12,740,000. Against that volume, the Bureau of Justice Statistics estimated 312,000 rejections during the same period from March 1, 1994 through November 29, 1998, a figure the bureau reported as about 2.4 percent of the checks conducted. The reporting window the bureau used began the day after the interim provisions took effect and closed the day before the permanent provisions took effect, so the bureau's reconstruction covered the operative life of subsection (s) from its first full day to its last. The great majority of transactions, by the bureau's 1999 accounting, ended without a rejection and proceeded once the applicable wait or clearance was satisfied.

The composition of the rejections showed the check operating principally against criminal history categories. The Bureau of Justice Statistics reported in its 1999 reconstruction that about two thirds of the 312,000 rejections rested on a felony conviction or a pending felony indictment, the first certification on the transferee's statement and the category most directly visible in the criminal records that officers searched. Domestic violence misdemeanor convictions accounted for roughly one rejection in ten, according to the Bureau of Justice Statistics figures released in 1999, and protection orders accounted for a small additional share of the rejections the bureau counted. The distribution illustrates the dual character of the interim screen. The statement asked the transferee to certify against every listed category at once, while the records inquiry behind the statement found its largest class of disqualifications in felony status and a substantial minority class in domestic violence misdemeanor convictions that a felony focused search alone would have missed.

The administration offered its own accounting of the interim record before the phase closed. On October 29, 1998, Attorney General Janet Reno, announcing that the permanent instant system would open on schedule, stated that the interim checks had prevented almost a quarter of a million over the counter sales to prohibited persons. The figure, presented by Attorney General Reno on October 29, 1998 as an administration statement, differed from the Bureau of Justice Statistics estimate published in 1999, which counted 312,000 rejections across the period from March 1, 1994 through November 29, 1998. The two numbers measured the interim record from different positions, one delivered by the Attorney General a month before the phase expired and one reconstructed by the Bureau of Justice Statistics after the permanent system had replaced the local officer procedure, and the historical record of the interim phase carries both, each attached to the source and the date that produced it.

## Printz: The Court Strikes the Method, Not the Requirement

The constitutional challenge that reached the Supreme Court in Printz v. United States came from two county officers who occupied a precise place in the interim system created by the Brady Act. Jay Printz served as sheriff and coroner of Ravalli County, Montana. Richard Mack served as sheriff of Graham County, Arizona. Each man was the designated chief law enforcement officer for his jurisdiction, and that designation mattered more than any personal connection to a particular handgun transfer. The interim provisions of section 922(s) of title 18 assigned duties by office rather than by name. A licensed importer, manufacturer, or dealer who transferred a handgun to a person who was not a licensee transmitted a copy of the transferee statement to the chief law enforcement officer of the transferee place of residence. The officer who received that statement then carried the duty described in section 922(s)(2) to make a reasonable effort, within five business days, to ascertain whether the transferee had a criminal record or whether another legal impediment barred the transfer.

That design placed a federal task inside county government. The officer was expected to conduct research in whatever state and local recordkeeping systems were available and in a national system designated by the Attorney General. The product of that research affected the timing of a transaction between a licensee and a prospective transferee. If the officer notified the licensee that receipt would not be unlawful, the transfer could proceed on that notice. If five business days elapsed without notice that receipt would be unlawful, the transfer could also proceed. The officer therefore stood between the completed statement and the expiration of the waiting period, even though the officer had no part in drafting the federal standard or enacting the prohibition categories that controlled the answer.

Printz and Mack challenged the duty that the interim provision assigned to the office each man held. The litigation did not ask the courts to erase every part of the Brady Act, to excuse licensees from collecting statements, to abolish the waiting period as a condition on licensees, or to forbid a state from operating a qualifying system of its own. The challenge centered on the federal command directed at chief law enforcement officers. That distinction shaped every stage of the case, because the statute operated through several actors performing different steps. The licensee received the statement, verified identity, transmitted a copy, and waited. The officer received the copy and conducted the check. A state with a qualifying alternative performed verification under state law or issued a qualifying permit. The Attorney General carried a separate duty to establish the permanent federal system. Printz tested only the proposition that Congress could conscript the state executive officer into administering the federal check.

The first judicial answers came from two federal district courts in 1994. The Montana district court, in the decision reported at 854 F. Supp. 1503, held the background check command unconstitutional. The Arizona district court, in the decision reported at 856 F. Supp. 1372, reached the same conclusion on the command. Neither district court treated that conclusion as a reason to disable the entire interim arrangement. Each court held the unconstitutional command severable from the remainder of the statute. After the district court judgments, background checks did not become forbidden acts. Officers who chose to perform them could continue on a voluntary basis. The defect identified at that stage lay in compulsion, not in the information contained in a record search and not in cooperation between a county office and a federal licensee. The distinction between a duty imposed by Congress and a service a state officer elected to provide ran through the remainder of the litigation and through the period after the final judgment.

The United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit reversed in 1995, in the decision reported at 66 F.3d 1025. The court of appeals upheld the provisions that the district courts had condemned. That reversal restored the legal force of the command while review continued toward the Supreme Court. The sequence matters because the case did not move in a straight line from enactment to invalidation. District judges in Montana and Arizona found a constitutional defect and severed it, while a regional appellate court disagreed and sustained the federal direction to state officers. The Supreme Court therefore confronted a divided lower court record on a narrow structural question: whether Congress, having created a federal regulatory program for handgun transfers by licensees, could require state executive officers to conduct the background checks on which the interim version of that program depended.

### What exactly did the Supreme Court strike down in Printz?

The Supreme Court struck down section 922(s)(2), the interim provision commanding chief law enforcement officers to conduct background checks within five business days, and extinguished the implied duty to accept completed Brady forms from licensees under section 922(s)(1)(A)(i)(III) and (IV).

Printz v. United States, 521 U.S. 898, was argued on December 3, 1996, and decided on June 27, 1997. The decision was five to four. Justice Scalia wrote for the majority, joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, and Thomas. Dissents came from Justices Stevens, Souter, and Breyer. One side treated the interim duty as an unconstitutional transfer of federal administrative responsibility to state officers. The other side treated that same duty as a limited federal direction compatible with the relationship between the national government and the states. The majority view controlled, and the legal effect was exact rather than general. Section 922(s)(2) could not command chief law enforcement officers to conduct background checks.

Precision about the holding requires attention to a second, related duty. Section 922(s)(1)(A)(i)(III) and (IV) concerned transmittal of the transferee statement to the chief law enforcement officer and the sequence that followed from that transmittal. The Supreme Court holding extinguished the implied duty of an officer to accept completed Brady forms from dealers. That consequence followed from the invalidation of the check itself. A form could be sent under the licensee side of the procedure, but federal law could not convert receipt into an obligation for the state officer to take the form as the starting point of a compulsory federal investigation. Licensees had duties concerning the statement. State officers could not be forced, by implication from the licensee provisions, to accept the statement for the purpose of performing a check that section 922(s)(2) had no valid power to impose.

The holding did not rest on a finding that background checks were undesirable, inaccurate, or outside the subject matter Congress had addressed in the Brady Act. The majority did not hold that a chief law enforcement officer lacked knowledge of local records, that county record systems were unsuitable sources, or that a check performed voluntarily produced a different legal answer from a check performed under compulsion. The constitutional defect lay in the source of the obligation. A check performed because a state officer chose to cooperate rested on state discretion. A check performed because section 922(s)(2) commanded the officer to perform it rested on federal conscription of a state executive. Printz invalidated the second arrangement. The federal requirement that a licensee pause, collect a statement, verify identity, transmit notice, and wait survived as a rule for licensees. The federal method of staffing the interim check with commanded state officers did not survive.

The majority opinion by Justice Scalia began from an absence. No constitutional text answered the question directly. The Constitution did not contain a sentence expressly authorizing Congress to direct state executive officers to administer a federal background check program, and no sentence expressly forbade that direction in the terms presented by the interim provision. The majority therefore resolved the case through historical practice, the structure of the federal system, and precedent. That order of reasoning placed weight on inference from the design of the Union. The question was not whether one isolated provision could be read to grant the power. The question was whether the constitutional structure, understood through practice and earlier decisions, permitted Congress to use state officers as administrators of a federal regulatory program.

Historical practice supplied one part of the answer for the majority. The opinion treated the record of earlier federal and state relations as failing to establish a settled practice of Congress compelling state executive officers to administer federal programs in the manner attempted by section 922(s)(2). The absence of a direct textual command and the absence of a convincing historical pedigree left the statute dependent on a broader claim about congressional power. The dissent read the same historical terrain differently, and that disagreement over practice was central rather than incidental. For the majority, history helped define the limits that structure imposed when the text was silent.

Dual sovereignty supplied the structural core. Under that structure, the federal government and each state government retained distinct authority, and each answered through its own political processes. A state officer was not a field employee of the United States who happened to draw a county salary and occupy a county office. The sheriff of Ravalli County and the sheriff of Graham County held state and local responsibilities under the laws that governed those offices. When Congress assigned the background check duty to the chief law enforcement officer, Congress selected an officer of another government and directed the performance of a federal task. The majority treated that selection as constitutionally significant. Federal law could regulate licensees directly, and federal officers could administer a federal system, but the interim provision chose a different administrative instrument.

Precedent converted that structural concern into a governing rule. In New York v. United States, 505 U.S. 144 (1992), the Supreme Court had barred Congress from commanding state legislatures to enact or administer a federal regulatory program by directing the legislative process itself. Printz asked whether the protection recognized in New York stopped at the legislative door. The majority answered that it did not. State executive officers stood in a comparable position when Congress compelled them to administer a federal program. The reasoning extended the anti-commandeering principle from the making of state law to the execution of federal law by state personnel. A legislature ordered to legislate and an executive officer ordered to administer both faced displacement of independent governmental judgment by a federal directive.

Political accountability gave the structural rule a practical explanation. When Congress compelled state officers to administer a federal program, citizens who objected to the program, its delays, its errors, or its local administration could not readily tell which government deserved responsibility. A voter dissatisfied with a background check might blame the sheriff who performed it, although the sheriff had not designed the federal criteria, chosen the period of five business days, or decided that county personnel would conduct the research. Conversely, a voter pleased with the program might credit state officers for a policy that Congress had created. The majority treated that blurring as a constitutional cost, not a mere public relations problem. Elections discipline governments only when responsibility can be traced to the government that made the governing choice.

The dissent by Justice Stevens, joined by three colleagues, would have upheld the duty. The dissent read historical practice differently from the majority. In the dissent view, federal statutes assigning record and reporting tasks to state officers provided a stronger foundation for the interim arrangement than the majority acknowledged. That reading mattered because the case turned in part on what silence in the constitutional text permitted when earlier practice was consulted. The dissent found no textual prohibition on the modest interim duty imposed by section 922(s)(2). A duty limited in duration, tied to the period before the permanent federal system began, and confined to a reasonable effort to check records did not, in that view, cross a constitutional line. The majority converted state office and mandatory language into unconstitutional commandeering. The dissent treated history and the limited character of the task as reasons to sustain a cooperative federal arrangement.

The inventory of what survived Printz demonstrates the narrowness of the judgment. The waiting period itself survived. The interim statute imposed a sequence on licensees transferring handguns to unlicensed persons, and that sequence included a pause before delivery. Printz did not remove the pause from the licensee obligations. The decision removed the federal power to compel the officer check that operated inside the timetable. The timetable, as a condition governing licensee conduct, remained part of the interim law until the interim provisions expired on November 30, 1998.

Every licensee duty survived as well. A licensee had to receive from the transferee a signed statement, the Brady form, certifying that the transferee was not within the prohibited categories listed in the statement. Those categories, as the statement described them, covered a felony indictment or conviction, fugitive status, unlawful use of a controlled substance, adjudication as a mental defective or commitment to a mental institution, unlawful alien status, dishonorable discharge, and renunciation of citizenship. A licensee had to verify the transferee identity against a photographic identification document. A licensee had to transmit a copy of the statement to the chief law enforcement officer of the transferee place of residence. A licensee had to wait, proceeding after notice that the transfer would not be unlawful or after five business days elapsed without notice that receipt would be unlawful. Printz changed the obligation on the receiving officer, not the obligations that Congress had placed directly on the federally licensed seller.

The state alternative systems survived untouched. From the start of the interim period, the federal procedure did not apply in the same way in every jurisdiction. Transfers in states whose law required verification before transfer proceeded under an approved state system. A transferee could also proceed under the permit exception by presenting a qualifying state permit issued within the previous five years, a route that depended on the state system that issued the permit rather than on a compelled check for that transaction. A state could choose a system, maintain a system, and have that system recognized by federal law. The constitutional problem arose when Congress ordered the state chief law enforcement officer to perform the federal check as a federal duty.

The order to the Attorney General to build the federal system also survived. Section 103 of the Brady Act directed the Attorney General to establish the national instant criminal background check system that would replace the interim arrangement. That direction ran to a federal officer within the federal executive branch, not to a sheriff or another state executive. Printz therefore left the permanent design intact. Nothing in the decision touched the permanent provisions, which bound licensees and the federal system rather than state officers. The permanent provisions applied after November 30, 1998, to firearms generally under section 922(t), while the interim provisions in section 922(s) ceased to apply on the schedule Congress had set. The judgment changed only who could be compelled to perform checks during the months that separated the decision from the scheduled end of the interim phase.

During that interval, voluntary checks continued in willing jurisdictions. An officer who received information and chose to search available records could provide the assistance that the district courts had contemplated after severance in 1994 and that the Supreme Court judgment made the only lawful basis for officer participation under the interim scheme. An officer who declined could not be held to the section 922(s)(2) command that the Court had invalidated. Licensees in jurisdictions without a voluntary check or a qualifying state alternative followed the licensee sequence and the passage of five business days as the statute provided. The result was variation in administration during the remainder of the interim period, produced by state and local choice rather than by a uniform federal command.

The interim provisions expired on schedule on November 30, 1998. That date had been fixed by the statute design, running to the day before the date sixty months after enactment, and the permanent system began as the interim system closed. Printz did not create the expiration date, extend it, or convert a temporary defeat for one administrative method into a permanent defeat for background checks by licensees through a federal system. The case decided the constitutional status of a bridge mechanism built for the period between February 28, 1994, when the interim provisions took effect, and November 30, 1998, when the permanent provisions took effect.

The doctrinal footprint of Printz extended beyond the handgun provisions that produced the case. The anti-commandeering principle, applied in New York to state legislatures and in Printz to state executive officers, became a standard reference point whenever a federal statute assigned mandatory administrative work to state personnel rather than regulating private conduct directly or offering a state a genuine regulatory choice. Later disputes in other fields tested the same boundary between federal direction and state administration, including the field surveyed in [the guide to federal drug law against state legalization](/2018/09/01/federal-drug-law-vs-state-law/). The enduring legal point of Printz was institutional. Congress retained power to govern licensees, to build a federal checking system, and to recognize state systems that states chose to maintain. Congress lacked power to make a county sheriff the compulsory administrator of the federal interim program.

## The Permanent Phase: An Instant Check in Place of a Wait

The permanent phase began with an administrative act rather than with a new vote in Congress. Section 103 of the Brady Act had directed the Attorney General to establish a national instant criminal background check system, and the Department of Justice discharged that direction by publishing a final rule on October 30, 1998, establishing the National Instant Criminal Background Check System, known by the acronym NICS. The Federal Bureau of Investigation activated the system on November 30, 1998, the date on which the interim provisions of section 922(s) of title 18 ceased to apply and the permanent provisions of section 922(t) took their place. The handover therefore closed one statutory design and opened another on a single day, with no interval in which a licensed transfer proceeded without a governing federal procedure. Administration passed from a dispersed structure built around statements transmitted to chief law enforcement officers toward a centralized records search conducted at the point of sale.

The scope of the check changed on the same day, and the change altered the daily work of every licensed importer, manufacturer, and dealer. The interim provisions had reached handguns alone. The permanent provisions applied to all firearms, handguns and long guns alike, so a rifle or shotgun transaction that had stood outside the interim procedure entered the federal check on November 30, 1998. The permanent provisions also covered a transaction the interim provisions had not covered, the redemption of a pawned firearm, so a person recovering a firearm from pawn passed through the same search as a person purchasing a firearm across a counter. A fuller account of [how the NICS background check law works](/2021/06/15/nics-background-check-law/) treats the statutory structure behind that search in full.

A permanent-phase transaction began with paper before it reached a database. The prospective transferee completed ATF Form 4473, the firearms transaction record maintained by the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives, and the information supplied on that form gave the licensee the identifying particulars that the check required. The licensee then contacted the system before transferring the firearm, and the transfer depended on the answer returned through that contact. The form fixed the transaction in a record created at the place of sale and tied the inquiry to a named transferee and a specific proposed transfer, while the contact submitted that identity to a search of federal and state records held far from the counter. The statute placed both duties within the licensee's part of the transaction, a placement with constitutional significance after the litigation over the interim phase, because the permanent design bound licensees and the federal system rather than commanding state officers to administer a federal program.

The route of the contact depended on the state in which the transaction occurred, and the system operated through more than one channel from its first day. In states where the FBI ran the check, the licensee contacted the FBI directly, and federal examiners conducted the search and returned the response. In states that performed checks of their own, the licensee contacted a state point of contact, an agency designated to receive the inquiry, run the search, and answer the licensee. The distinction between the channels concerned the institution handling the inquiry rather than the standard governing the transfer, because section 922(t) supplied the governing rule in either case. A state point of contact also brought state record systems within reach of the inquiry in a manner that a purely federal channel organized differently.

A third arrangement stood between the two principal channels. Some states acted as partial points of contact, running handgun checks through the state agency while the FBI ran long gun checks for transactions in the same state. A licensee's route therefore depended not only on geography but also on the category of firearm proposed for transfer, with a handgun inquiry and a long gun inquiry in a partial point of contact state traveling to different examiners. The arrangement reflected the staged history of the statute itself, under which handguns had formed the whole subject of the interim phase and long guns entered the federal check only on November 30, 1998. The phrase instant check therefore described a family of procedures with a common statutory destination rather than a single uniform path: direct federal checks, full state points of contact, and partial points of contact dividing the work by firearm type.

### What does the instant check search, and how does an answer come back?

The instant check searches three FBI databases, the Interstate Identification Index, the National Crime Information Center, and the NICS Index, for records matching the transferee. The licensee receives one of three responses under federal regulation: proceed with a transaction number, delayed for further research, or denied.

The three databases stood under one administration. The FBI's Criminal Justice Information Services Division managed each of them, and a single inquiry ran against all three, so the licensee made one contact while the search examined three distinct bodies of records. The architecture treated the databases as complementary rather than interchangeable. A record present in one body of records and absent from the others could determine the outcome, and the absence of a matching record in one database carried no answer about the contents of the remaining two. Consolidation at the point of inquiry therefore coexisted with separation at the point of storage, and the examiner's task consisted in reading the combined result, resolving whether any record returned belonged to the transferee named on ATF Form 4473, and deciding whether such a record established a prohibition.

The Interstate Identification Index formed the first component of the search. As its name recorded, the index served an interstate identification function within the FBI's criminal justice information structure, linking the inquiry to identification records maintained through cooperation among jurisdictions rather than within a single state file. Its contribution to the transaction was reach across state lines at the moment of sale. A prohibition arising from events in a state other than the state of purchase depended for its effect on records that traveled, and the index supplied the mechanism by which the check looked beyond the records of the jurisdiction where the licensee stood.

The National Crime Information Center formed the second component, and it contributed the records of an established national information center to the same inquiry. The center's place in the search meant that the check drew on records of different kinds held in different systems, because the statute required an answer about whether receipt of the firearm would be unlawful. The examiner receiving results from all three databases had to weigh the combined return as one answer to one statutory question. The design placed the burden of synthesis on the examining agency, federal or state, rather than on the licensee, whose duty ended with making the contact accurately and honoring the response returned.

The NICS Index, also called the NICS Indices, formed the third component and the component built for the system itself. The index held records contributed by federal and state agencies on prohibited persons not captured in the other two databases. That residual function defined its importance. A person could fall within a prohibited category under section 922(g) or section 922(n) of title 18 without a corresponding record surfacing through the Interstate Identification Index or the National Crime Information Center, and the NICS Index existed to receive records aimed at exactly that gap. Contributions arrived from two levels of government, so the completeness of the index depended on transmission practices inside federal agencies and inside state agencies alike, a dependence that later legislation on records improvement addressed directly.

The response itself took one of three forms under the governing regulation. Section 25.6 of title 28, Code of Federal Regulations, supplied the vocabulary of proceed, delayed, and denied, and each term carried a distinct instruction for the licensee. A proceed response authorized the transfer to go forward, and the licensee received a NICS Transaction Number, the identifier that connected the approval to the audited record of the transaction. The number mattered beyond the moment of sale, because the privacy architecture described below preserved the transaction number and the date in the audit log after other identifying information on an approved transferee faced destruction. A denied response barred the transfer, communicating that the system had determined that receipt of the firearm by the transferee would be unlawful. Between those two poles stood the third response, and the third response accounted for a substantial share of the system's work.

A delayed response meant that the initial search had neither cleared the transferee nor established a prohibition, and that further research was required before a determination could issue. A delay could arise from a record that matched the transferee in part, from an incomplete record that left a prohibition unresolved, or from any return that an examiner could neither dismiss as belonging to another person nor treat as conclusive against the transferee. The delayed status therefore represented an intermediate evidentiary state rather than a judgment about the transferee, and it placed the transaction in suspense while the examining agency pursued the underlying record. The consequences of an extended delay, including the rule that permitted a licensee to transfer a firearm after three business days without a notification that receipt would be unlawful, receive separate treatment in the discussion of the default proceed rule, and that discussion owns the subject in full.

The operating record of the first month showed the system working at national scale through both its federal and state channels. The Bureau of Justice Statistics reported that December 1998 brought about 893,000 applications, with the FBI conducting about 507,000 checks and state agencies conducting about 386,000 checks during that month. The same Bureau of Justice Statistics account recorded about 20,000 rejections in December 1998, a rejection share of 2.2 percent of applications. The distribution between federal and state examiners confirmed that the point of contact structure was no formality, because state agencies carried well over two fifths of the first month's volume between them.

Two decades of operation produced a record of a different order, and the FBI's figures for 2019 illustrated both the scale of denial activity and the prevalence of delay. The FBI recorded 103,592 federal denials in 2019, a figure confined to denials issued through the federal channel rather than a combined national total across every point of contact state. The FBI also reported that about 30 percent of transactions processed by the NICS Section in 2019 received an initial delayed status, while 1.27 percent of the transactions processed by the NICS Section in 2019 ended in denial. Read together, the FBI's 2019 figures described a system in which outright denial formed a small fraction of outcomes and delay formed a routine stage in a large minority of transactions. The FBI placed cumulative federal denials from the November 1998 launch through December 31, 2019, at 1,700,558.

Congress also preserved routes by which a transfer could proceed without a fresh check, and section 922(t)(3) of title 18 defined those alternatives. The first alternative rested on a qualifying state permit. A permit issued not more than five years before the transfer could stand in place of the check where an authorized official had verified, before issuing the permit, that the holder was not within a prohibited category. The five year limit tied the exception to verification of some recency in statutory terms, so a permit of greater age could not perform the substituting function. Congress tightened the condition for permits of later issue. A permit issued after November 29, 1998, qualified only if the verification behind the permit had included a NICS check, a requirement that folded the permanent system into the exception itself and prevented the permit route from becoming a path around the federal search for permits issued once the system operated.

The remaining alternatives addressed narrower classes of transfer. Transfers of National Firearms Act firearms approved by the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives fell outside the requirement of a fresh NICS inquiry, because those transfers passed through a separate federal approval process administered by that bureau. Transfers that the bureau certified as impracticable to subject to the check formed the third alternative, carrying into the permanent phase a form of exception that the interim provisions had also recognized in certification by the Secretary of the Treasury. Each alternative shared one structural feature: none of them rested on the licensee's judgment alone. A permit depended on a prior verification by an authorized official, a National Firearms Act transfer depended on a federal approval, and an impracticability claim depended on a federal certification, so the exceptions displaced the check with another official act rather than with private discretion at the counter.

A denial did not end the denied person's recourse, and the system's design included mechanisms for correction and appeal directed at the accuracy of the underlying record. A denied person could challenge the denial with the FBI or with the point of contact agency that had handled the check and could seek correction of the record on which the denial rested. A denial could rest on a record that was accurate about another person, on a record that was inaccurate about the transferee, or on a record whose disposition left a prohibition in doubt, and each defect called for a different remedy. Section 925A of title 18 supplied a judicial remedy alongside the administrative one, allowing a person denied because of erroneous information to bring an action against the United States or against the responsible state to correct the information or to obtain the firearm. The existence of that action placed a court behind the administrative process, so an agency's handling of erroneous information faced review outside the agency itself.

Repeated error received a mechanism of its own. The FBI's Voluntary Appeal File allowed persons who were repeatedly matched to records that were not theirs to store clarifying information with the FBI for use in later transactions. A person enrolled in that file received a Unique Personal Identification Number, which the person could cite in a later transaction to assist the examiner in distinguishing the transferee from the subject of a similar record. The file addressed a recurring cost of a name based search conducted at national scale, in which a lawful transferee with identifying particulars resembling those of a prohibited person could face repeated delay or erroneous denial across successive purchases. Enrollment was voluntary in structure and prospective in effect, since the stored information operated on later inquiries rather than reopening a completed denial.

The final element of the permanent design concerned what the system could retain, and Congress surrounded the search with a privacy architecture that limited the records an approval could leave behind. Section 103(i) of the Brady Act forbade any department, agency, officer, or employee of the United States to use the system to establish a system for the registration of firearms, firearm owners, or firearm transactions, except with respect to persons prohibited from receiving a firearm. That prohibition governed the purpose for which the system's information could be organized, and it stood apart from the retention rules that governed individual records.

The retention rule arrived through appropriations law rather than through the 1993 act itself. Section 617 of Public Law 108-199, the Consolidated Appropriations Act for fiscal year 2004, signed on January 23, 2004, required the destruction of identifying information on persons determined not to be prohibited no more than 24 hours after the licensee received notification of the approval. Congress repeated the requirement in later appropriations acts, and in the fiscal 2012 appropriations cycle Congress made the requirement permanent through the word hereafter in the governing text. The legal path of the rule therefore ran from an annual appropriations condition to a standing instruction, and the 24 hour period it fixed became the central fact of the approved transaction's documentary life. Identifying information on an approved transferee had a lifespan measured from the notification of approval, and expiration of that period carried a duty of destruction rather than a discretionary records decision.

Destruction had defined limits, and the architecture specified what survived it. Under section 25.9 of title 28, Code of Federal Regulations, and the FBI's privacy impact assessment for NICS, the audit log retained the NICS Transaction Number and the date of the transaction after identifying information on an approved transferee faced destruction. Records of denied transactions were retained rather than destroyed, a distinction consistent with the registration prohibition's own exception for persons prohibited from receiving a firearm and with the practical requirements of appeals, in which a retained denial record allowed the denied person and the reviewing agency to address the same underlying material. Information on transactions that remained open was purged within 90 days, so an unresolved inquiry also carried a fixed outer limit on the retention of its information. The resulting structure sorted records by outcome: a minimal audit trace for approvals, retention for denials, and a bounded period for transactions that had reached neither determination.

The destruction rule carried a documented institutional cost, and the objection entered the record through an oversight body rather than through inference. The Government Accountability Office reported in July 2002 that next day destruction of audit log information would impair auditing of the system and oversight of dealers, a concern recorded before Congress enacted the 24 hour requirement in the fiscal 2004 appropriations act. The FBI had previously retained audit log information on approved transfers for a period, set at 90 days under the administration in office in 2001, for the purpose of permitting audits of the system's own accuracy and use. The sequence therefore presented two documented positions in statutory and oversight form: a congressional direction toward prompt destruction of identifying information on approved transferees, and a Government Accountability Office finding that prompt destruction weakened the audit trail on which oversight depended.

## The Default Proceed Rule: Three Business Days and a Dealer's Discretion

### What happens if the check is not finished in three business days?

If the system has not notified the licensee by the end of the third business day that receipt would be unlawful, section 922(t)(1)(B)(ii) permits the licensee to transfer the firearm. The licensee may also wait. A denial received before any transfer bars the transfer absolutely.

The default proceed rule operates inside the permanent system that the Brady Act directed the Attorney General to establish and that the Federal Bureau of Investigation activated on November 30, 1998. That system, the National Instant Criminal Background Check System, applies to firearms generally rather than to handguns alone. A prospective transferee completes ATF Form 4473, and the licensee contacts the system either directly through the Federal Bureau of Investigation or through a state point of contact in a state that performs its own checks. Some states are partial points of contact for handgun checks. The response framework in 28 C.F.R. 25.6 gives the licensee one of three answers: proceed, delayed, or denied. A proceed response carries a NICS Transaction Number and authorizes the transaction to move forward on the basis of the completed check. A denied response closes the transaction. A delayed response occupies the middle ground that makes the default rule necessary, because the system has neither cleared the transfer nor determined that the receipt of the firearm would be unlawful.

Section 922(t)(1)(B)(ii) addresses that middle ground with a conditional permission rather than an instruction. Paraphrased in full, the provision states that where the system has not notified the licensee, by the end of the third business day after the licensee contacted the system, that the receipt of the firearm by the transferee would violate federal law, the licensee may lawfully transfer the firearm to the transferee. The trigger is the passage of a defined interval without a particular notification. The notification that matters is a notification that receipt would be unlawful, not a general statement that research remains incomplete. The consequence is permission for the licensee to transfer, expressed through the word may, rather than a command that the transfer occur. Under the default rule, an unfinished check is not the same thing as a prohibited transfer.

The absence of notification also differs from an affirmative clearance. A proceed response communicates that the system has allowed the transaction to advance, and the licensee receives the transaction number associated with that response. The default rule supplies no equivalent finding that the transferee is eligible. It supplies a point at which the legal barrier created by the pending check falls away if the system has not reported that receipt would be unlawful. The licensee who transfers under the default acts on statutory permission, not on a federal representation that the record is clean. The licensee who declines to transfer likewise acts within the statute, because the permission creates an option and imposes no duty to exercise it. The system, for its part, has not exhausted its authority or its inquiry merely because the calendar point has passed.

The calendar point itself depends on a regulatory definition that excludes more days than a casual reading of the phrase three business days might suggest. Under 28 C.F.R. 25.2, a business day is a 24-hour day beginning at 12:01 a.m. on which state offices are open in the state where the transaction is to take place. Saturdays do not count. Sundays do not count. State holidays do not count. The day on which the licensee initiates the check does not count either, even when that day otherwise satisfies the definition. The definition is tied to the transaction state rather than to a uniform federal office calendar, so the count follows the days on which offices are open in that state. A check therefore does not mature into a default transfer opportunity simply because three calendar days have elapsed.

The Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives illustrated the count with a worked example that removes most ambiguity from the sequence. In that example, a licensee begins a check on a Thursday, and Thursday does not count. Friday becomes the first business day, provided state offices are open on that Friday in the transaction state. Saturday and Sunday fall outside the definition and contribute nothing to the count. Monday becomes the second business day, and Tuesday becomes the third business day. If the system has not notified the licensee by the end of Tuesday that the receipt of the firearm would be unlawful, the earliest point at which the transfer may occur under the default is the start of business on Wednesday. The example fixes two practical points at once: the licensee counts forward from the day after initiation, and the permission opens after the third business day has ended.

That sequence also explains why the default rule interacts with the delayed response rather than replacing it. A delayed response tells the licensee that the system requires additional research before a final answer can issue. The research may involve records that the initial search located but did not complete, and the examiner may need to determine whether a located record establishes a prohibition. The statute places a limit on how long the unresolved status can hold the transaction in place without a notification of unlawfulness, but the limit runs against the suspension of the transfer rather than against the research itself. When the third business day ends without the specified notification, the licensee acquires the option to proceed. The delayed status has not been converted into a proceed response, and no finding about the transferee has issued. The only change is legal rather than evidentiary.

Licensee discretion is consequently a substantive part of the rule rather than an incidental qualification. The statute permits the transfer; the statute does not command the transfer. A licensee who receives no determination by the deadline may wait for the research to conclude, may decline to complete the transaction, or may transfer the firearm under the permission the provision grants. Nothing in section 922(t)(1)(B)(ii) penalizes a licensee for waiting beyond the third business day, and nothing in the provision converts the deadline into an instruction from the federal system to hand over the firearm. The choice belongs to the licensee, who remains free to wait, in the formulation the regulatory record uses to describe the permanent system. That discretion preserves a role for judgment at the point of sale in the narrow class of transactions that reach the deadline without an answer.

Discretion has a firm outer limit, and a late denial defines it. A denial that reaches the licensee at any time before the transfer bars the transfer, even when the denial arrives after the third business day has ended. The default permission therefore lasts only while two conditions coincide: the deadline has passed, and no notification of unlawfulness has intervened before the firearm changes hands. A licensee who delays a voluntary transfer in order to await further word does not lose protection by waiting, but a licensee who receives a denial during that interval loses the ability to transfer altogether. Permission ripens at the end of the third business day, and a pre-transfer denial extinguishes that permission before the transaction occurs. A denial after the deadline but before delivery is not a delayed denial in the retrieval sense, because the bar operates in advance of possession.

Research on a delayed transaction continues after the third business day. The NICS Section continues researching the transaction until a determination is reached or the transaction record reaches its purge point, and the Federal Bureau of Investigation works to a 90-day window for that purpose. The deadline therefore does not instruct examiners to close a file, discard an unresolved lead, or treat silence as eligibility. An examiner who has located a potentially prohibiting record may continue pursuing the material needed to resolve it during the period after the licensee has acquired the transfer option. If that research produces a determination of unlawfulness before the licensee transfers the firearm, the denial bars the transaction under the sequence described above. If the research produces a determination after the licensee has transferred the firearm, a different mechanism begins, one that operates after possession rather than before it.

A denial that arrives after a transfer has occurred is a delayed denial, and a delayed denial generates a firearm retrieval referral. In that event, the NICS Section notifies the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives that a prohibited person is in possession of a firearm, and the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives bears responsibility for retrieval of the firearm. The referral does not unwind the transaction by itself. The roles divide along institutional lines: the background check system identifies the late determination and makes the referral, and the firearms bureau receives the task of recovering the firearm from a person whom the completed record has shown to be prohibited. The existence of that referral mechanism is a central premise in the arguments on both sides of the rule, because it defines the remedy that survives a default transfer and the gap that the remedy must close.

The case for the default begins with the allocation of delay. A background check places the machinery of the federal and state record systems between a licensee and a transferee, and the party least able to accelerate that machinery is the person waiting for an answer. The examiner depends on databases, custodians, and records. The transferee cannot assemble the government file or correct the pace of a records search. A rule without a deadline would assign the cost of institutional delay to the two private parties at the counter, even though neither party directs the institutions whose records decide the question. After the interval expires without a notification that receipt would be unlawful, the unfinished research of the government no longer holds the transaction suspended.

The alternative to a deadline gives the rationale its sharpest form. Without the default, an unresolved record could hold a lawful transfer in suspension indefinitely. A slow records custodian could postpone the answer beyond any predictable horizon. An incomplete database entry could leave the examiner unable to confirm or exclude a prohibition. None of those conditions would require an adjudication that the buyer is a prohibited person, and none would produce a denial that the buyer could appeal as a determination on the merits. The transfer would fail in practice while the legal record contained no finding that the transfer was forbidden. The default distinguishes between a prohibition established through the record and a prohibition merely suspected because the record remains unfinished. It allows the second condition to delay a transfer for three business days, and it refuses to let that condition function as a permanent denial.

The structure of the permanent rule also has a lineage inside the Brady Act itself. During the interim phase, section 922(s) required a licensee to transmit the transferee statement to the chief law enforcement officer and then to wait. The transfer could proceed when the officer notified the licensee that the transfer would not be unlawful, or when five business days had elapsed without notice that the transfer would be unlawful. Silence after the defined interval carried legal meaning under the interim arrangement. The permanent system changed the checking institution, widened the coverage from handguns to firearms generally, and shortened the interval from five business days to three, without changing the underlying judgment that government silence at the end of a stated period permits the transaction to move forward.

A deadline of that kind does not abandon the check, and the case for the rule relies heavily on the remedies that operate after the deadline passes. Research continues after the third business day until the NICS Section reaches a determination or the transaction record reaches the purge point at the end of the 90-day window described by the Federal Bureau of Investigation. A denial that arrives during that continued research, at any moment before the transfer, bars the transfer outright. The default therefore creates only a conditional and revocable permission during the interval between the deadline and the delivery of the firearm. If research produces the adverse determination after delivery, the delayed denial generates a firearm retrieval referral. The default is a deadline with a remedy, not an abandonment of the check.

Dealer discretion completes the rationale by adding a private screen after the federal deadline has run. The statute says the licensee may transfer, and the conditional wording leaves the final decision with the person conducting the transaction. A licensee who is uncomfortable with an unresolved record may wait for a determination, and a licensee who prefers not to rely on the default may decline the transfer. That discretion means the end of the third business day does not produce an automatic release of every delayed transaction. Each delayed transaction returns to the judgment of an individual licensee who must decide whether the permission should be exercised.

The record against the default begins with the mirror image of the same transaction. When research runs past three business days, a licensee may complete a transfer to a person whom the completed record would have excluded. The check has functioned as a delay rather than a screen in that transaction, because the determination that would have barred the sale arrives only after the legal permission to sell has opened. The remedy is retrieval after the fact, which differs in kind from prevention before the fact. A pre-transfer denial leaves the firearm with the licensee and the prohibited person without possession. A post-transfer referral requires the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives to locate and recover a firearm that a prohibited person has already received. The objection rests on that sequence: research unfinished, transfer permitted, prohibition established afterward, and enforcement reduced to recovery.

Federal figures establish that the sequence describes a recurring class of transactions rather than an isolated possibility. The Federal Bureau of Investigation made 6,361 firearm retrieval referrals to the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives in 2020, according to the NICS Operations Report for 2020 and 2021 published by the Federal Bureau of Investigation. The Department of Justice reported in its semiannual reporting that delayed denials represented 0.048 percent of federal transactions in 2018, 0.0365 percent of federal transactions in 2019, and 0.0486 percent of federal transactions in 2020. The percentages place delayed denials within the full volume of federal transactions and show a rate measured in hundredths of one percent during each of the three years reported by the Department of Justice. The referral total for 2020 supplies the corresponding count of cases in which the late determination produced a referral for recovery during that year, as reported by the Federal Bureau of Investigation. Each referral represents a transfer that the completed record would have prevented.

The documented case that gave the class a public examination arose from a South Carolina transaction in 2015, and the findings stated by Federal Bureau of Investigation Director James B. Comey on July 10, 2015 define the record of that case. In April 2015, a dealer in South Carolina initiated a background check on a buyer, and the initial search returned an arrest record from February 2015. The examiner required the arrest report itself to determine whether the arrest supported a prohibition. The examiner worked from a contact list that did not identify the correct custodian for the report, requested the report from the wrong local agency, and never received the report. Comey stated that the unobtained report recorded the admission by the buyer to possessing a controlled substance and that the report would have supported a denial on the ground that the buyer was an unlawful user of a controlled substance. The evidentiary gap was therefore not an absence of any record. A record existed, and the missing document contained the admission on which a denial could have rested.

The three business day interval expired before that step could be completed, and the default supplied the legal path for the transfer that followed. No denial issued within three business days of the initiation of the check. On April 16, 2015, the dealer transferred the pistol to the buyer under the permission the default rule provides after the interval runs without a notification that receipt would be unlawful. The buyer was the man later convicted of the June 17, 2015 murders of nine worshippers at Emanuel African Methodist Episcopal Church in Charleston. Comey ordered an internal review of the examination process. At the level of official findings, the case joins each element of the objection in a single sequence: a relevant arrest located, a dispositive report never obtained, a deadline expired, a transfer completed, and a record that would have supported a denial.

Congress later revisited the default rule for the youngest purchasers through [the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act](/2021/06/01/bipartisan-safer-communities-act-2022/). That later statute extended the review window for buyers under twenty-one to as much as ten business days, allowing the system to complete an enhanced check of juvenile and mental health records before a transfer may proceed by default.

## Repairing the Record: The Later Amendments and What the Act Never Did
### What did the later amendments change, and what did they leave alone?
The amendments of 2007 and 2018 changed the flow of records into the system, adding furnishing duties, compliance reporting, completeness benchmarks, grant incentives, agency certifications, and implementation plans, while leaving the transfer procedure, the prohibited categories, and the limits on private transfers and registration untouched in substance.

The Brady Act built a check that depended on records held elsewhere. Section 103 of the 1993 act directed the Attorney General to establish the national instant system, and section 922(t) of title 18 placed the duty to contact that system on the licensee at the point of transfer. Neither provision, on its own terms, created the underlying criminal history, court, or agency records against which a name would be searched. A system designed to answer within a short statutory window could therefore be no more complete than the files that federal departments, state agencies, and courts had transmitted into the databases behind the search. The two later amendments addressed that dependency directly.

The earlier amendment was the NICS Improvement Amendments Act of 2007, Public Law 110-180, enacted as H.R. 2640 and signed by President George W. Bush on January 8, 2008. The gap that produced the statute was stated through the account of the Federal Bureau of Investigation. According to the Federal Bureau of Investigation account preserved in the record of the 2007 act, the man who killed 32 people at Virginia Tech in Blacksburg, Virginia, on April 16, 2007, had been adjudicated in a manner that prohibited him from receiving a firearm, but the record of that adjudication was never transmitted to the NICS Index, and the purchases from licensees were approved. The remedy in Public Law 110-180 therefore began at the point of transmission.

For federal custodians, the 2007 act converted transmission into a recurring statutory duty. Federal departments and agencies were required to furnish the Attorney General with electronic versions of records on prohibited persons not less frequently than quarterly under Public Law 110-180 of 2007. The duty covered more than an initial delivery of files. Each department and agency was also required to update, correct, or remove records as the underlying status changed, so that a record in the system would track the adjudication, conviction, or other determination on which the prohibition rested. Accountability for that duty took the form of a report to Congress. The Attorney General was required under the NICS Improvement Amendments Act of 2007 to report annually to Congress on the compliance of each federal agency.

For states, the 2007 act used benchmarks and money. The statute set benchmarks for the completeness of the records that states made available to the system, and it attached consequences to performance against those benchmarks. Grant money flowed through the NICS Act Record Improvement Program, administered by the Bureau of Justice Statistics under the 2007 act, to support state efforts to supply more complete records, and missing a benchmark carried withholding consequences for grant support. Completeness therefore operated as a condition of federal assistance as well as a stated objective. Benchmarks gave grant administration a measurable standard tied to the records a state made available, rather than to effort or intention alone. To qualify for full grant support under the NICS Improvement Amendments Act of 2007, a state was also required to establish a program for relief from disabilities, a mechanism through which a person subject to a prohibition could seek restoration under procedures the state established. That condition linked record completeness to record correction from the opposite direction, because a more complete database increased the importance of a defined route for addressing a disability that no longer applied. The act also funded improvements to state court systems for transmitting mental health adjudications and commitments, the category of record at issue in the Virginia Tech account.

The Fix NICS Act of 2018 returned to the same structural problem after a different record, held by a different custodian, failed to travel. The measure was enacted as Title VI of Division S of the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2018, Public Law 115-141, and signed by President Donald Trump on March 23, 2018. The standalone bill, S. 2135, had been introduced by Senators John Cornyn and Chris Murphy. The official findings associated with the 2018 act concerned the November 5, 2017, shooting at the First Baptist Church of Sutherland Springs, Texas, in which 26 people were killed. According to those official findings from 2017 and 2018, the shooter had been convicted by an Air Force court-martial in 2012 of assault on his spouse and child, the Air Force failed to submit the conviction records to the databases of the Federal Bureau of Investigation, and the shooter passed the checks.

A report from the Department of Defense Inspector General in December 2017 gave the Sutherland Springs failure a measured institutional context. The Department of Defense Inspector General reported in December 2017 that during 2015 and 2016 the military services failed to report final disposition outcomes to the Federal Bureau of Investigation in an average of 31 percent of cases. The service figures in the December 2017 report of the Department of Defense Inspector General were 14 percent for the Air Force, 41 percent for the Army, and 36 percent for the Navy and Marine Corps in the reporting on final dispositions for 2015 and 2016.

The mechanisms of the Fix NICS Act of 2018 pressed on certification, planning, and recurring accountability. The head of each federal agency was required by Public Law 115-141 of 2018 to certify in writing to the Attorney General semiannually whether the agency was in compliance with the record submission requirements. Certification by the agency head, rather than by a subordinate records office, placed responsibility for the answer at the top of the institution and required that answer twice each year. Each certification created a recurring point at which an agency had to examine its own submission pipeline and state the result in writing to the Attorney General. Each agency was also required to publish an implementation plan with benchmarks for maximum coordination and automated reporting.

The 2018 act extended the planning structure beyond federal agencies. Under Public Law 115-141 of 2018, the Attorney General, coordinating with states and tribal governments, established an implementation plan for each state and tribal government. The Department of Justice was required by the Fix NICS Act of 2018 to report to Congress semiannually, a cadence that matched the semiannual certification cycle for agency heads and gave Congress a recurring account of compliance. The act also reauthorized the National Criminal History Improvement Program, the established federal grant program for improving criminal history record systems, connecting the new certification and planning duties to a continuing source of support for the record infrastructure on which the plans depended.

The Department of Justice measured substantial growth in the record base during the period covered by its semiannual reporting under the 2018 act. The Department of Justice reported that total records in the three databases searched by the system rose from 100,849,193 in April 2018 to 112,352,109 by February 2021. The Department of Justice reported in the same semiannual reporting that records in the NICS Indices rose by 31.4 percent across the span from April 2018 to February 2021. Those figures measured volume rather than an abstract claim of completeness. The sharper percentage growth in the NICS Indices mattered because that index held the contributed records on prohibited persons that the other two databases did not capture. Growth in the index therefore spoke directly to the class of gap identified after Virginia Tech in 2007 and after Sutherland Springs in 2017.

Titles II and III of the original 1993 act operated beside the background check provisions, and each addressed a discrete piece of the regulatory structure surrounding licensed transfers. Title II, styled Multiple Firearm Purchases to State and Local Police, amended section 923(g)(3) of title 18. Under Title II of the 1993 act, a licensee who sold or disposed of two or more handguns to one unlicensed person within five consecutive business days was required to forward a copy of the multiple-sale report to the department of state police or to the local law enforcement agency of the jurisdiction where the sale occurred. That notice carried a short statutory life. The receiving agency under Title II of the 1993 act was required to destroy the form and any record of its contents within 20 days, except for a purchaser who was prohibited, and to certify the destruction to the Attorney General at six-month intervals. Destruction within 20 days limited retention of information about a purchaser against whom no prohibition applied, while the exception preserved the record where the purchaser fell within a prohibited category, and semiannual certification gave the Attorney General a recurring confirmation that the destruction duty had been performed without extending the retention period itself.

Title III, the Federal Firearms License Reform Act of 1993, changed the price of the dealer license and restricted a labeling practice by carriers. The General Accounting Office reported in its letter report GGD-94-111R of March 14, 1994, that the dealer license application fee rose from 30 dollars for three years, the rate carried from the Gun Control Act of 1968 at 10 dollars per year, to 200 dollars for a three-year initial license and 90 dollars for a three-year renewal. Title III also prohibited common and contract carriers from requiring a label, tag, or other written notice on the outside of a container indicating that the container contained a firearm.

Measured against the scale sometimes attributed to the statute in public argument, the 1993 act drew narrower lines. Public Law 103-159 created no new possession offense and added no new prohibited category. The categories of persons prohibited from receiving or possessing firearms came from section 922(g) and section 922(n) of the Gun Control Act of 1968, the statute that the Brady Act amended. By supplying a procedure for checking a prospective transferee against records of those categories, the act left the definition of the categories themselves in the earlier statute. That distinction between defining a prohibition and screening for a prohibition runs through the amendments as well, because neither the 2007 act nor the 2018 act used record completeness legislation to rewrite the underlying list of prohibited categories.

The act also imposed no check on transfers between unlicensed persons. Its transfer duties bound licensees, the licensed importers, manufacturers, and dealers whose transactions passed through the statement, identification, notice, and system contact requirements that Congress placed in section 922 of title 18. A transfer in which neither party acted as a licensee stood outside that procedural chain. The same restraint governed timing after the permanent system opened on November 30, 1998. The Brady Act imposed no federal waiting period once the permanent system opened. The interim arrangement, under which a licensee waited for notice from a chief law enforcement officer or for five business days to elapse, ceased to apply on November 30, 1998, and the federal timing rule in the permanent phase was the three business day default in section 922(t) of title 18, under which a licensee could proceed after the window closed without a denial. Registration stood on a separate statutory prohibition. Section 103(i) of the 1993 act forbade a federal department, agency, officer, or employee to use the system to establish a system for the registration of firearms, firearm owners, or firearm transactions, except with respect to persons prohibited from receiving a firearm. Claims that treat the check itself as a form of registration, together with related claims about waiting periods, private transfers, and newly created categories, gather around the system in forms addressed by [the federal gun law myths guide](/2021/08/15/federal-gun-law-myths/), and the statutory text supplies the boundaries against which each claim is tested.

The measured-effect literature on the act requires the same care with attribution that the record growth figures require. Jens Ludwig and Philip J. Cook published a study in the Journal of the American Medical Association in 2000 that compared states subject to the interim provisions with states that had qualifying alternatives and were exempt during the interim period. Ludwig and Cook reported in the Journal of the American Medical Association in 2000 that the comparison produced no statistically significant reduction in homicide rates attributable to the act. The same analysis by Ludwig and Cook in 2000 suggested a possible reduction in firearm suicide among persons aged 55 and older. The comparison concerned the interim period that ran from 1994 to 1998 under section 922(s) of title 18, before the permanent system opened on November 30, 1998. Presented at that strength, the study by Ludwig and Cook in 2000 stands as one examination within a contested literature on measured effects, not as a settlement of the question, and disagreement among assessments does not enlarge a single comparison into a general verdict on the permanent system that opened after the interim period closed.

The statute, taken as a whole, functioned as a machine for moving information rather than as a prohibition in its own terms. Public Law 103-159 changed who checks, because the licensee rather than the transferee or a local officer in the permanent phase initiated the contact with the federal system. The statute changed the records against which the check ran, because the search reached the Interstate Identification Index, the National Crime Information Center, and the NICS Index, and the amendments of 2007 and 2018 pressed federal agencies, states, tribal governments, and courts to make those databases more complete. The statute changed the deadline on which a transaction could proceed, first through the five business day structure of the interim phase under section 922(s) of title 18 and then through the three business day default of the permanent phase under section 922(t) of title 18. Its litigation history concerned the machinery, in the command to state officers during the interim phase, and its amendments concerned the machinery, in the transmission, certification, planning, and reporting duties that determined whether a record would be present when the machinery ran. A prohibition defined who could not receive a firearm, and that definition remained in the Gun Control Act of 1968. The Brady Act and its amendments determined whether that definition would be tested, against what information, by whom, and within what time.

The profile established a statute defined by procedure rather than by new prohibitions. The Brady Act moved the federal screen to the moment before transfer, first through a statement, identification, notice to a chief law enforcement officer, and a five business day wait, and then through contact with a federal instant system covering all firearms and answering proceed, delayed, or denied. Printz limited the method of the first phase without removing the licensee duties, the three business day default governed silence in the second phase, and the amendments of 2007 and 2018 addressed the completeness of the records behind the search. A reader following those terms further turns to section 922(s) and section 922(t) of title 18, section 103 on the system and its registration prohibition, section 925A on correction of erroneous information, and a place to [keep your statute notes, citations, and case chronologies together free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html)

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the Brady Act require?

The Brady Act required a check before a licensed importer, manufacturer, or dealer transferred a firearm to a person who was not a licensee. During the interim phase the licensee received a signed statement from the transferee certifying that the transferee fell outside the prohibited categories listed on the statement, verified identity against a photographic identification document, transmitted a copy to the chief law enforcement officer of the transferee place of residence, and waited for notice or for five business days to elapse. During the permanent phase the licensee contacts the national instant system before transfer and honors a proceed, delayed, or denied response. Section 103 of the act directed the Attorney General to establish that system.

### Q: Was there a waiting period under the Brady Act?

A waiting period operated during the interim phase and not as a fixed federal wait during the permanent phase. The interim provisions took effect on February 28, 1994 and ceased to apply on November 30, 1998. A licensee waited for notice from the chief law enforcement officer that a handgun transfer would not be unlawful, or for five business days to elapse without notice that the transfer would be unlawful. Silence at the end of that period permitted the transfer. The permanent system substituted an instant check for the wait. The federal timing rule in the permanent phase is the three business day default in section 922(t) of title 18.

### Q: What did Printz decide about the Brady Act?

Printz v. United States, 521 U.S. 898, decided on June 27, 1997, held unconstitutional the interim provision in section 922(s)(2) of title 18 that commanded chief law enforcement officers to conduct background checks. Justice Scalia wrote for a majority of five, joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, and Thomas, with dissents by Justices Stevens, Souter, and Breyer. The majority reasoned from historical practice, the structure of dual sovereignty, and precedent, above all New York v. United States, and held that Congress may not compel state executive officers to administer a federal regulatory program. Conscription of state officers also blurred political accountability, because voters could not tell which level of government to blame.

### Q: Did Printz strike down the whole Brady Act?

Printz did not strike down the whole Brady Act. The decision invalidated section 922(s)(2) and extinguished the implied duty of an officer to accept completed Brady forms from dealers under section 922(s)(1)(A)(i)(III) and (IV). The waiting period survived, and every licensee duty survived, covering the signed statement, verification of identity, transmission of notice, and the wait. The state alternative systems survived, the permits exception survived, and the command to the Attorney General to build the federal system survived. Nothing in the decision touched the permanent provisions, which bind licensees and the federal system rather than state officers. From the decision until November 30, 1998, checks continued on a voluntary basis where officers chose to run them.

### Q: What is the three day default proceed in the Brady Act?

The three day default proceed is the rule in section 922(t)(1)(B)(ii) of title 18. If the system has not notified the licensee at the end of three business days after the licensee contacted the system that receipt of the firearm would be unlawful, the licensee may transfer the firearm. The choice belongs to the licensee, who remains free to wait, and a denial received at any time before the transfer bars the transfer. A business day under 28 C.F.R. 25.2 is a 24 hour day beginning at 12:01 a.m. on which state offices are open in the state of the transaction. Saturdays, Sundays, state holidays, and the day the check is initiated do not count.

### Q: Who was the Brady Act named for?

The act was named for James S. Brady, press secretary to President Ronald Reagan. On March 30, 1981, outside the Washington Hilton Hotel, John Hinckley Jr. attempted to assassinate Reagan and shot four men, including Brady, who was struck in the head and left permanently partially paralyzed. Brady and his wife Sarah campaigned for the bill for years. Sarah Brady joined the board of Handgun Control, Inc. in 1985 and became its chair in 1989, and the organizations were renamed in honor of the Bradys in 2001. James Brady died on August 4, 2014, aged 73, and a medical examiner ruled the death a homicide caused by the 1981 gunshot wound.

### Q: How long did the Brady Act take to pass?

The measure took seven years from first introduction to signature. Representative Edward F. Feighan and Senator Howard M. Metzenbaum introduced the measure on February 4, 1987, in the 100th Congress, in a form proposing a seven day waiting period. The House Judiciary Committee approved the measure in June 1988, and the House defeated the measure on the floor on September 15, 1988, by 228 to 182. Representative Charles E. Schumer reintroduced the measure in March 1991 as H.R. 7, and the House passed H.R. 7 on May 8, 1991, but no vehicle carried the measure to the President in the 102nd Congress. Schumer reintroduced the measure on February 22, 1993, as H.R. 1025, and President Bill Clinton signed the measure on November 30, 1993.

### Q: What replaced the Brady Act interim system?

The National Instant Criminal Background Check System replaced the interim system. Section 103 of the act directed the Attorney General to establish the system during the interim years. The Department of Justice published the final rule establishing the system on October 30, 1998, and the FBI activated the system on November 30, 1998, the date on which the interim provisions ceased to apply. The permanent provisions applied to all firearms, handguns and long guns alike, and covered the redemption of a pawned firearm, a transaction the interim provisions had not covered. The licensee contacts the FBI directly or a state point of contact, and the search runs against three federal databases.

### Q: What is the Brady Act's public law number, and where is it codified?

The Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act is Public Law 103-159, 107 Stat. 1536, enacted by the 103rd Congress as H.R. 1025 and signed by President Bill Clinton on November 30, 1993. The act amended the Gun Control Act of 1968. The operative provisions sit in section 922 of title 18 of the United States Code. Subsection (s) carried the interim procedure, and subsection (t) carries the permanent system. Section 103 of the act directed the Attorney General to establish the national instant system. Section 925A of title 18 supplies a remedy for a person denied a firearm because of erroneous information.

### Q: Does the Brady Act cover rifles and shotguns, or only handguns?

Coverage changed between the two phases. The interim provisions covered handguns only, and the duties attached to sales, deliveries, and transfers by licensed importers, manufacturers, and dealers to persons who were not licensees. Long guns stood outside the interim presale procedure. The permanent provisions, in force from November 30, 1998, applied to all firearms, handguns and long guns alike, so a rifle or shotgun transaction entered the federal check on that date. The permanent provisions also covered the redemption of a pawned firearm. A transferee in the permanent phase completes ATF Form 4473, and the licensee contacts the system before transfer, either through the FBI or through a state point of contact.

### Q: Who must run a Brady background check, and which transfers are exempt?

The licensee initiates the check, and the FBI or a state point of contact runs the search. In the permanent phase a licensed importer, manufacturer, or dealer contacts the system before transferring a firearm to a person who is not a licensee. Section 922(t)(3) of title 18 supplies alternatives to a fresh check: a qualifying state permit issued not more than five years earlier after verification that the holder was not prohibited, with a permit issued after November 29, 1998 qualifying only if the verification included a NICS check; transfers of National Firearms Act firearms approved by ATF; and transfers ATF certifies as impracticable to subject to the check. The act imposed no check on transfers between unlicensed persons.

### Q: Which databases does a NICS check search?

A NICS check searches three databases managed by the Criminal Justice Information Services Division of the FBI. The Interstate Identification Index supplies an interstate identification function, linking the inquiry to identification records maintained through cooperation among jurisdictions. The National Crime Information Center contributes the records of an established national information center to the same inquiry. The NICS Index, also called the NICS Indices, holds records contributed by federal and state agencies on prohibited persons not captured in the other two databases. A single contact by the licensee runs against all three databases, and the examiner resolves whether a returned record belongs to the transferee and establishes a prohibition under section 922(g) or (n) of title 18.

### Q: What happens when a delayed check ends in a denial after the firearm was transferred?

Research on a delayed transaction does not stop at three business days. The NICS Section continues researching until a determination is reached or the transaction record reaches its purge point, and the FBI works to a 90 day window. A denial that arrives after the firearm has been transferred is a delayed denial, and a delayed denial produces a firearm retrieval referral. The NICS Section notifies ATF that a prohibited person is in possession of a firearm, and ATF bears responsibility for retrieval. The FBI referred 6,361 firearm retrieval cases to ATF in 2020, according to the NICS Operations Report for 2020 and 2021 published by the FBI.

### Q: Can a person appeal a NICS denial?

A denied person may challenge the denial and seek correction of the underlying record. The challenge goes to the FBI or to the point of contact agency that handled the check. Section 925A of title 18 supplies a judicial remedy alongside the administrative process, allowing a person denied because of erroneous information to sue the United States or the responsible state to correct the information or to obtain the firearm. The FBI Voluntary Appeal File addresses repeated error. Persons who are repeatedly matched to records that are not theirs may store clarifying information in that file and receive a Unique Personal Identification Number to cite in later transactions, assisting an examiner in distinguishing the transferee from the subject of a similar record.

### Q: Does the Brady Act create a national registry of firearms or owners?

The act creates no national registry and forbids the system to be used as one. Section 103(i) of the act forbids any federal department, agency, officer, or employee to use the system to establish a system for the registration of firearms, firearm owners, or firearm transactions, except with respect to persons prohibited from receiving a firearm. Section 617 of Public Law 108-199, signed on January 23, 2004, required destruction of identifying information on persons determined not to be prohibited no more than 24 hours after the licensee was notified of the approval. The audit log retains the transaction number and the date, records of denied transactions are retained, and information on transactions that remain open is purged within 90 days.

### Q: What did the NICS Improvement Amendments Act change?

The NICS Improvement Amendments Act of 2007, Public Law 110-180, signed by President George W. Bush on January 8, 2008, addressed the completeness of records transmitted to the system. The act followed the April 16, 2007 shooting at Virginia Tech in Blacksburg, Virginia, in which 32 people were killed; in the FBI account, an adjudication that prohibited the shooter was never transmitted to the NICS Index. Federal departments and agencies must furnish the Attorney General electronic records on prohibited persons not less frequently than quarterly and must update, correct, or remove records. The Attorney General must report annually to Congress on the compliance of each federal agency. States face completeness benchmarks, grant money through the NICS Act Record Improvement Program, withholding consequences for missed benchmarks, and a requirement to establish relief from disabilities programs for full grant support.

### Q: What did the Fix NICS Act require federal agencies and states to do?

The Fix NICS Act of 2018 was enacted as Title VI of Division S of the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2018, Public Law 115-141, signed by President Donald Trump on March 23, 2018. The standalone bill, S. 2135, was introduced by Senators John Cornyn and Chris Murphy. The head of each federal agency must certify in writing to the Attorney General semiannually whether the agency is in compliance. Each agency must publish an implementation plan with benchmarks for maximum coordination and automated reporting. The Attorney General, coordinating with states and tribal governments, establishes an implementation plan for each state and tribal government. The Department of Justice reports to Congress semiannually, and the act reauthorized the National Criminal History Improvement Program.

### Q: Did the interim waiting period apply in every state?

The interim federal procedure did not apply in every state. At the start of the phase in 1994, the federal procedure applied in 32 states. The remaining 18 states and the District of Columbia had qualifying alternatives, in the form of permit systems or state operated checks, and were exempt from the federal sequence. By February 1995, the one year progress report of the administration counted 27 states and territories subject to the federal interim procedure, as additional states qualified for exemption. Separate exceptions covered a transferee presenting an officer statement issued within the previous ten days recording a threat to life, transfers under a qualifying state permit issued within the previous five years, and transfers certified as impracticable.

### Q: What did Titles II and III of the Brady Act do?

Title II amended section 923(g)(3) of title 18. A licensee who sells or disposes of two or more handguns to one unlicensed person within five consecutive business days must forward a copy of the multiple sale report to the department of state police or the local law enforcement agency of the jurisdiction where the sale occurred. The receiving agency must destroy the form and any record of its contents within 20 days, except for a purchaser who is prohibited, and must certify the destruction to the Attorney General at six month intervals. Title III, the Federal Firearms License Reform Act of 1993, raised the dealer license application fee from 10 dollars per year to 200 dollars for a three year initial license and 90 dollars for a three year renewal, and prohibited carriers from requiring an external label indicating that a container contains a firearm.

### Q: What should a student remember about the Brady Act for an exam?

A student answer gains precision from four distinctions. First, the act built a check in two phases: an interim statement and five business day procedure run through chief law enforcement officers for handguns from 1994 to 1998, and a permanent instant system run through the FBI or a state point of contact for all firearms from November 30, 1998. Second, Printz struck the method of the first phase, the command to state officers, and left the licensee duties and the permanent system intact. Third, silence carries legal effect in both phases, after five business days in the interim phase and after three business days in the permanent phase. Fourth, the act defined no new prohibited category, imposed no check between unlicensed persons, and forbade registration.
