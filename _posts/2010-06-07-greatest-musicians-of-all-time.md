## Why the Question Is Asked at All

One billion records. That figure followed Elvis Presley through decades of record company publicity, repeated by journalists who rarely asked who had counted, and it is exactly the kind of number that appears to end arguments about the greatest musicians of all time while settling none of them. A sales figure of that size, even if it could be audited, would answer a question about manufacturing, distribution and broadcasting. The question people bring to a phrase like this one is different. They want to know whose music deserves their limited listening life, whose work will repay study, and whose name a newcomer should learn first. Publicity answers with a number because a number looks like a verdict. The honest answer is an argument, and arguments can be examined, weighed and improved in ways that numbers pasted onto reputations cannot.

![Greatest musicians of all time compared across eras and traditions - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-99.webp)

The question gets asked because music is one of the few arts in which a lifetime is not long enough to hear everything that matters. A reader who gives an hour to one composer gives it away from another, and ranking is the crude instrument people reach for when they allocate scarce attention. Record shops organised their shelves by it. Radio stations built countdown formats on it. Magazine editors discovered generations ago that a ranked list sells the issue and starts arguments that continue in letters pages and, later, in comment threads, long after the printing bill was paid. Underneath the commerce sits a genuine intellectual need. Every tradition of music carries an internal sense of its own peaks, and the moment two traditions meet in one listener's education, the peaks have to be compared somehow.

There is also a plainer motive, which is loyalty. To name the greatest musician is often to declare membership in a tribe of listeners, and the name chosen signals an age, a place, a class background and a theory of what music is for, all compressed into a single word. A jazz collector who answers with Louis Armstrong and a conservatory student who answers with Johann Sebastian Bach are not only reporting taste. Each is defending a world in which a particular kind of excellence counts as the central kind. That is why these arguments generate heat out of proportion to their stakes. Nobody is merely wrong about a ranking. Somebody's education, heroes and youthful discoveries are being marked down in public.

The article that follows treats the question as worth taking seriously precisely because the easy versions of it are so bad. A ranking that hides its criteria is marketing. A ranking that announces that comparison is impossible and stops there is an abdication, because listeners will compare anyway, with worse tools. What can be built, and what the next thirty three sections build, is a method: six criteria stated separately, the distortions that corrupt each one examined in the open, the strongest case for each major tradition given its full hearing, and finally the method applied to four named figures with its weights declared, so that a reader who disagrees can change a weight rather than merely change the subject.

## What People Actually Mean by Greatest

The word greatest performs at least five different jobs, and most arguments about the greatest musicians of all time are really two people using the word in different senses and talking past each other for an hour. In one sense it means the most technically accomplished performer, the person whose command of an instrument or a voice left other professionals shaking their heads. In a second sense it means the most consequential, the musician after whom the music itself went differently. In a third sense it means the maker of the greatest body of work, judged the way a library is judged, by the shelf of things that endure. In a fourth sense it means the most beloved or the most widely reached. In a fifth sense, the slipperiest of all, it means the musician a speaker personally cannot imagine music without, which is autobiography wearing the costume of judgment.

### Who is the greatest musician of all time?

No single name answers it, because the question quietly contains several different tests at once: the finest technician, the deepest influence on later musicians, the largest body of durable work, the widest cultural reach. A name that passes one test, such as Elvis Presley on reach, can fail another, such as compositional craft.

Each sense has its own evidence and its own failure modes. Technical accomplishment can be judged by peers within a tradition, but it says nothing about whether the accomplishment changed anything. Consequence can be traced through named successors, but it rewards musicians who worked at a tradition's bottleneck and punishes those who perfected a form so completely that nobody could follow them usefully. The body of work test rewards longevity and punishes the musician who died at twenty seven with twenty nine songs. Reach rewards whoever stood nearest the largest broadcasting machinery. The autobiographical sense is not false, exactly, but it is evidence about the speaker, not about the musician, and it becomes useful only when the speaker admits that is what it is.

Other fields escape this confusion because they keep score. The fastest sprinter is a fact about a stopwatch, and even there the argument resumes the moment someone asks whether a sprinter from the era of cinder tracks can be compared with one who ran on synthetic surfaces with modern shoes. Music has no stopwatch. It has testimony, sales ledgers, scores, recordings, teaching lineages and the stubborn survival of some works in performance while others vanish from the repertory. Every one of those sources measures something real, and none of them measures greatness whole. The first step toward an honest ranking is therefore grammatical. Before naming anyone, a ranking has to say which of the five jobs the word is doing, and an article about the greatest musicians of all time that skips that step has decided its answer in secret and spent its remaining pages arranging the applause.

This confusion also explains why the argument is so durable across generations. Each era inherits the previous era's winners under criteria it did not choose, then rebels by silently swapping the criterion rather than disputing the names. A generation raised on recordings demoted composers it could not hear performed and promoted improvisers whose work the microphone had preserved. A generation raised on stadium tours promoted reach and called the promotion honesty about popularity. Nobody announced the change of standard, so each side experienced the other as simply deaf. Naming the senses of the word, as this section has done, does not end the argument. It makes the argument findable, which is the precondition for everything that follows.

## The Criteria Problem Stated Properly

A criterion is a decision about what counts, made before the counting starts. The scandal of most greatest lists is not that they choose the wrong musicians but that the decision about what counts is never shown, so the reader cannot tell a considered judgment from a mood. Richard Taruskin, whose five volume Oxford History of Western Music appeared in 2005, built much of his career on exposing exactly this manoeuvre inside classical music. His argument, held consistently across his work, is that the canon of masterworks was assembled by institutions with interests: conservatories that needed a syllabus, publishers who needed a back catalogue, and a nineteenth century German musical culture that presented its own preferences as the laws of music itself. Taruskin does not claim the canon is worthless. He claims it is made, and that anything made can be examined, criticised and remade with its workings visible.

Once that is accepted, the structure of an honest ranking becomes clear. First the criteria are separated, because a musician can be enormous under one and negligible under another, and collapsing them into a glow of general importance is how reputations escape examination. Then each criterion needs its evidence described: what would count as a measurement, what corrupts the measurement, and how far the surviving record can be trusted. Then the traditions have to be heard at their strongest, because a criterion drawn from one tradition will quietly rig the contest against the rest. A durability test built on written scores favours literate traditions before a note is compared. A reach test built on record sales favours musicians born after the phonograph and inside the empires that distributed it. Fairness in this subject is not a matter of goodwill. It is a matter of noticing which evidence each tradition was able to leave behind.

There is also the question of the unit being ranked. A ranking of musicians slides constantly among three different objects: the person, the body of work, and the performances. The person includes the career, the influence on contemporaries and the myth. The body of work can be weighed and reweighed by people who never met its maker. The performances, which for most of music history were the whole art, survive only in testimony until the age of recording, and testimony is the most corruptible evidence of all. An honest method states its unit and sticks to it. This article ranks musicians, but it treats the body of work and the documented effect on other musicians as the primary evidence, because those are the parts of a musical life that strangers can still inspect. Charisma, however real its effects on the audiences who felt it, enters only where it left documents behind.

Six criteria survive this scrutiny as genuinely distinct, and they structure the next six sections. Innovation asks how far the music moved. Influence asks who carried the movement onward. Craft asks how completely the musician commanded the medium as its own practitioners judge it. Body of work asks what quantity and consistency of durable material remains. Cultural reach asks how far the music travelled across the boundaries of its home community. Durability asks whether the work still functions when its world is gone. Each will now be taken in turn, given its strongest formulation, and then shown its characteristic corruption, because a criterion that cannot describe its own failure mode will fail silently when it is used.

## Criterion One, Innovation

Innovation is the distance between the practice a musician inherited and the practice that existed after them. It is the most romantic of the six criteria and the one most often invoked by a single adjective attached to a name, as though being first were a halo rather than a historical relationship that has to be demonstrated. Demonstrating it requires three things. A state of the art before the musician has to be described, which is harder than it sounds, because the predecessors who define that state are usually less famous than the innovator. The change has to be located in identifiable material: a rhythmic practice, a harmonic device, a way of building a solo, a studio technique, an instrument's new role. And the change has to be shown entering the common stock, because a novelty that nobody could use is a curiosity, not an innovation.

Louis Armstrong is the standard exhibit because his case clears all three hurdles with documents. The New Orleans practice he inherited, as Ted Gioia recounts in The History of Jazz, was a collective improvisation in which the soloist was one voice in a woven front line. In the Hot Five and Hot Seven sessions recorded between 1925 and 1928, Armstrong moved the solo to the centre of the record, stretched its length, and gave it a dramatic architecture, rising to climaxes that organised everything around them. The opening of West End Blues in 1928, an unaccompanied trumpet cadenza, announced the new order in seconds. What followed was not admiration from a distance. Singers, saxophonists and pianists rebuilt their phrasing on his rhythmic placement, and the swing era's entire solo economy descended from those sides. Innovation of that order is rare because it changes what every later practitioner of the form has to learn.

The criterion has a built in corruption, and it is severe. History credits innovation to the first musician who recorded the change, published it, or stood where the journalists were, and those are accidents of documentation as often as they are facts of invention. Buddy Bolden, the New Orleans cornettist who led the band Armstrong's generation grew up hearing, made no recordings at all, and his innovations survive only in the recollections of men who heard him. Inside the written tradition the same distortion applies in reverse. Johann Sebastian Bach is routinely described as a conservative because he perfected inherited forms rather than smashing them, yet the keyboard writing of the Well Tempered Clavier and the fugal procedures of his late work enlarged what the forms could hold so substantially that later composers studied them as new territory. A criterion that counts only visible ruptures will underrate the innovator who worked by deepening, and overrate the innovator whose rupture was mostly a change of costume.

Applied carefully, innovation is therefore a claim about a documented before and after, stated at the level of specific devices, with the precursor problem admitted in the same breath. Where the before cannot be documented, as with most orally transmitted music before the phonograph, the honest verdict is not that innovation was absent but that its evidence is thinner. That asymmetry will return throughout this article, because it is the single largest structural unfairness in every ranking ever printed.

## Criterion Two, Influence

Influence is innovation's slower and more provable sibling. Where innovation looks at the moment of change, influence looks at the receipts: named musicians who took named elements from an earlier musician and put them to work in their own music. The standard of proof is higher than resemblance. Two musicians can sound alike because both drank from the same source, and the history of popular music is littered with genealogies drawn by fans who heard an echo and promoted it into a bloodline. Real influence leaves tracks. Pupils name their teachers. Performers describe in interviews the record that rearranged their ambitions. Composers leave sketches, dedications and letters. A technique appears in one player's hands for a decade and then, after contact, appears everywhere.

The classical tradition offers the cleanest paper trails because it wrote everything down. Joseph Haydn taught Beethoven informally and employed the young Mozart's respect as a colleague; the string quartets Haydn and Mozart dedicated to each other document a two way transmission between generations. Beethoven's sketchbooks, and the testimony of pupils such as Carl Czerny, let a historian watch influence being metabolised almost page by page. In the recording traditions the trail runs through testimony instead. The guitarists who built postwar blues and rock said, on the record and by name, what Robert Johnson's 1936 and 1937 sessions did to them. Hip hop producers named James Brown's records as their quarry, and the claim can be audited in the most literal way, because Clyde Stubblefield's drum break from Funky Drummer, recorded in 1970, was lifted bodily into hundreds of later tracks. Influence that can be sampled can be counted.

Influence also has a multiplier that the other criteria lack. A musician who teaches changes the students of their students, and a musician whose recordings teach changes players they never met in countries they never visited. Armstrong's rhythmic language reached musicians on every continent through shellac. Bach's counterpoint reached musicians he could not have imagined through the conservatory curriculum that later centuries built on his exercises. The reach of influence is therefore partly a property of transmission machinery, which means the criterion quietly favours musicians whose work entered teaching institutions and recording catalogues. A master inside a tradition transmitted face to face, in a language the cataloguers did not speak, may have shaped five hundred musicians whose names no archive kept.

The corruption of the criterion is flattery and its reverse. Musicians name prestigious ancestors to borrow stature, and they omit embarrassing ones, the dance band jobs and the pop singers who actually taught them their trade. Elijah Wald, whose work consistently relocates musical change from lone geniuses to the audiences and working musicians around them, has documented how blues genealogies were tidied by revivalists into lines of pure descent that the working record contradicts. Influence, used honestly, is a claim about documented transmission, held at the confidence the documents support. Used lazily, it is the word generations does duty for, and any ranking that leans on it without names and mechanisms is leaning on fog.

## Criterion Three, Craft

Craft is command of the medium as the medium's own practitioners judge it, and it is the criterion least visible to the voters who compile popular lists. A lay listener can hear that a performance is exciting. Only another pianist can fully register what Art Tatum's left hand was doing, which is why Tatum's reputation has always been highest among pianists. The story musicians retell, of Fats Waller announcing to a club audience that he only played the piano but that God was in the house that night, is testimony about a hierarchy that the public could sense but not measure. Craft hierarchies inside every tradition are real, stable and largely invisible outside the profession, and any ranking that ignores them is grading musicians by their publicity surfaces.

Craft also refuses to sit still as a single scale, because each tradition defines command differently. The classical guitarist Andrés Segovia built a concert craft out of repertoire that largely did not exist, commissioning new works and transcribing Bach so that the instrument could be judged at the level of the piano and the violin. Django Reinhardt, after a caravan fire in 1928 injured the fretting hand that should have ended his career, rebuilt a technique around two working fingers and produced a fluency that guitarists with five fingers still study as a reproach. A Carnatic violinist's craft is measured by improvisational command inside a raga over long spans. A qawwali singer's craft is measured by the ability to build and hold a devotional intensity across an entire night. These are not lesser or greater crafts. They are crafts whose examinations are set by different boards, and the only examiners qualified to grade them are the practitioners inside each hall.

Two corruptions attend the criterion. The first is the confusion of difficulty with merit. Music that is hard to play advertises its craft on its surface, and musicians who favour display have always been suspected by other musicians of spending technique on effect. The second corruption is the reverse snobbery, in which simplicity of means is treated as proof of sincerity and craft is discounted as mere schooling. Both errors come from grading the advertisement rather than the command. The evidence that survives for craft is professional testimony across generations, the continued use of a musician's solutions by other practitioners, and, in the recording age, the performances themselves, which can be re examined by any later player willing to do the work. Of the six criteria, craft is the one where the verdict of practitioners deserves the most weight and the verdict of polls the least, and a ranking that inverts that weighting has told you it is measuring fame.

Peer examination is the machinery that keeps the criterion honest, and several traditions built formal versions of it. The jazz cutting contest, in which pianists and saxophonists challenged each other on a bandstand before an audience of rivals, was a craft tribunal disguised as entertainment, and the reputations it minted, for players such as Art Tatum and Charlie Parker, carried the authority of having been awarded by the defeated. Conservatories run a slower version of the same tribunal in their juries and competitions, where a young musician's command is graded by older practitioners against a standard the public never sees applied. Hospital corners of a tradition, the accompanists and section players, sit their examinations nightly: a singer's pianist who cannot transpose, follow a rubato or rescue a memory slip is finished in the profession's ledger whatever the posters say, and the ledger is kept by the only voters who were in the room.

## Criterion Four, Body of Work

A body of work is what remains when the career is over and the anecdotes have stopped earning their keep: the compositions that still get performed, the recordings that still get played, the songs that other musicians still need. The criterion sounds like simple arithmetic and is not, because it contains two variables that pull apart. One is volume, the sheer amount of durable material. The other is consistency, the density of first rate work inside the whole. Ludwig van Beethoven scores on both counts in a way few musicians match: nine symphonies, sixteen string quartets, thirty two piano sonatas and a single opera, with the centre of gravity of each genre shifted by the examples he left in it. A catalogue like that is not an accumulation. It is an argument sustained across thirty years, and its survival in the repertory is the closest thing music has to a controlled experiment in durability, since every generation of performers has had the chance to drop it and has declined.

Set against that shape the case of Robert Johnson, whose entire recorded legacy is twenty nine songs from two sessions. Under a body of work criterion Johnson nearly vanishes, and under influence and innovation he looms over musicians with forty albums. Neither verdict is an error. They are the same life measured by different instruments, and a ranking that reports only one of them has misled its readers. The single work musician sharpens the problem further. Johann Pachelbel wrote church music in respectable quantity, but his public existence rests on one canon, and almost nobody who can hum it can name another piece he wrote. A body of work test that cannot say whether Pachelbel's one ubiquitous composition outweighs a shelf of solid forgotten ones has not finished defining itself.

Preservation corrupts the count in both directions. Composers who published in their lifetimes, or whose employers archived them, present inflated catalogues next to improvisers whose work evaporated nightly. Bach, employed by churches and courts that kept his manuscripts, still lost roughly a third of his output; the standard estimate is some three hundred cantatas composed, of which about two hundred survive. What the bonfires, floods, damp storerooms and indifferent heirs did to less protected musicians can only be guessed, and the guess matters, because the body of work we rank is the body of work that happened to survive a series of accidents. For recorded musicians the corruption changes shape rather than vanishing. Studio outtakes, alternate takes and posthumous compilations can stretch a small catalogue into the appearance of a large one, while the estates of musicians who recorded little but performed constantly have almost nothing to sell. The criterion, honestly used, ranks surviving work and says so. The word surviving does the heavy lifting, and dropping it is how catalogues become destinies.

## Criterion Five, Cultural Reach

Reach is the criterion the record business can measure, and the one it therefore overvalues. It describes how far a musician's work travelled beyond its home community: across languages, classes, borders and generations of listeners who had no other connection to the world the music came from. The strongest cases are not in dispute. When Elvis Presley appeared on American network television in 1956, the audience was measured in the tens of millions, and the records, films and merchandise that followed carried a style built in Memphis into countries whose radio systems had never programmed anything like it; the full account belongs to [the life and career of Elvis Presley](/2012/09/07/elvis-presley-life-and-career/), but his function in this argument is simple. Presley demonstrates the maximum of what reach alone can do, because his fame vastly exceeded the circle of people who could describe his musical contribution in any detail. The Beatles pressed the same advantage further a decade later, with the additional machinery of a mature global record industry, and their claimed sales, in the hundreds of millions of units by the industry's own estimates, describe a distribution achievement as much as a musical one.

Reach is real, and dismissing it as mere popularity is its own kind of evasion. A musician whose work crosses languages has done something technically difficult: the music carried meanings that survived translation, or shed the meanings that could not travel and kept the ones that could. The rhythmic and timbral intelligence of Cuban dance music, the melodic architecture of Indian film song, the congregational lift of gospel: each crossed oceans because something in the construction was portable. Listeners who never learned a word of Arabic wept at Umm Kulthum's broadcasts. That is evidence about the music, not only about the marketing.

The corruption is that reach follows infrastructure, and infrastructure follows power. The musician standing at the junction of a film industry, an export minded record company and a dominant language begins the race halfway down the track. A singer of equal gifts inside a tradition served by one regional radio station and no export catalogue is not less great. She is less exported. Reach also decays oddly. It measures the peak of distribution, which can be a single decade's work, and it cannot distinguish the fame of a musician from the fame of a song attached to a film, an advertisement or a football chant. Used as one criterion among six, reach corrects the scholar's bias toward the documented and the difficult. Used alone, it crowns whoever the twentieth century's distribution empires happened to carry, and calls the coronation a discovery.

Reach had a long prehistory before the microphone, and its first industrial form was print. Stephen Foster's songs in the middle of the nineteenth century travelled the United States as sheet music, sung in parlours by people who had never heard a professional sing them, and a hit in that economy was measured in copies sold to amateur hands rather than in audiences reached. Print reach rewarded music that households could perform, which bent composition toward the singable and the playable, and it built reputations of a slow, distributed kind that no single performance could create or destroy. The sheet music era also demonstrates the criterion's neutrality about quality in its purest surviving form: the songs that travelled farthest were those best fitted to the distribution technology of the parlour piano, and the fitting was a craft of its own that rankings rarely think to grade.

## Criterion Six, Durability

Durability is the criterion that takes the longest to measure and the only one that cannot be faked by a living publicity machine. It asks whether the work still functions when the world that produced it has disappeared: whether musicians still need it, audiences still choose it, and students still have it set before them as a standard. Bach is the extreme case and therefore the instructive one. At his death in 1750 his music was admired by professionals and largely unknown to the public, his published output was small, and the style he had perfected was being dismissed by his own sons' generation as learned and old fashioned. Eighty years later he stood at the centre of the Western canon, where he has remained. Whatever durability is, it survived a near total eclipse, which means it cannot be the same thing as continuous fame.

The mechanisms of durability are partly musical and partly institutional, and confusing the two produces bad rankings in both directions. Music survives when it is written down or recorded in a form strangers can re enter, when institutions exist that profit from teaching it, when each generation of performers finds unsolved problems in it worth their ambition, and when it can change function without breaking: a dance idiom becoming concert music, a devotional repertory becoming national heritage, a tavern song becoming a string quartet's encore. The separate question of [what makes music last for centuries](/2026/10/21/what-makes-music-last-for-centuries/) takes those mechanisms one by one. For the ranking problem, the crucial point is the negative space. Vast quantities of admired music failed every institutional test while succeeding at every musical one, and the ranking that treats survival as a pure quality signal inherits the biases of every archive, syllabus and record catalogue that did the surviving.

Durability also has a time horizon problem that no method can remove. Judged in 1760, the most durable composer of the age was arguably Georg Philipp Telemann, whose works were published, performed and respected across Europe while Bach's sat in manuscript. Judged in 1860, the answer had reversed. Judged in 2010, the question is still open for every musician whose work is less than a century old, which is most of the musicians any modern list wants to crown. A record can be certified durable only by surviving circumstances its maker never imagined, and the youngest work on any list is, by definition, still on probation. This does not make durability useless. It makes it a criterion that must be reported with the age of the evidence attached, the way a wine merchant reports a vintage, and any ranking that applies it silently to a thirty year old catalogue has confused a prediction with a measurement.

Institutions do much of durability's quiet work, and two kinds deserve naming. Examination syllabi, the graded ladders of the music schools and boards, conscript each new generation of students into performing a fixed ancestral repertory, so that a work adopted by the examiners receives thousands of competent performances a year from candidates who never chose it. Liturgy is the older version of the same machinery. Plainchant survived a millennium not because audiences voted for it but because an institution needed it daily and trained men to carry it, and the chant's durability tells us as much about the durability of the Roman rite as about the melodies. Whenever a ranking cites survival as evidence, the careful question is which institution was doing the surviving on the work's behalf, and whether the work would have been chosen afresh without the subsidy.

## Why the Six Criteria Conflict

If the six criteria always agreed, ranking would be clerical work. They do not agree, because they measure genuinely different goods, and the conflicts between them are not noise in the system but information about what a given list values. Innovation and durability pull against each other whenever a radical style needs two generations to become playable common property. Craft and reach pull apart whenever the most accomplished practitioners work in an idiom whose pleasures require training to perceive. Body of work and influence pull apart at both extremes of a career, the meteoric and the monumental. Influence and craft part company whenever a limited technician teaches a generation more than a flawless one did, because what students can steal from a master is not always what the master did best.

| Criterion | What it measures | What it systematically favours | What it systematically misses | A figure who tops it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Innovation | The distance between practice before and after | Musicians at a tradition's bottleneck | Innovators who worked by deepening rather than rupture | Louis Armstrong |
| Influence | Named successors taking named elements | Teachers and recording pioneers with documented pupils | Masters inside oral traditions with unrecorded students | Johann Sebastian Bach |
| Craft | Command of the medium as practitioners judge it | Virtuosi whose peers left testimony | Accompanists, ensemble players and functional masters | Art Tatum |
| Body of work | The surviving shelf of durable material | Long careers inside archiving institutions | Improvisers and the carelessly preserved | Ludwig van Beethoven |
| Cultural reach | Travel across languages, classes and borders | Musicians served by film, broadcast and export industries | Masters of untranslated and unexported traditions | Elvis Presley |
| Durability | Continued function after the originating world is gone | Work that institutions teach and performers need | Work dependent on a vanished social setting | Johann Sebastian Bach |

Read down the fourth column of that table and the shape of every controversy in this article is already visible. Each criterion has a blind spot with a population living inside it, and the populations differ. The oral master, the accompanist, the improviser whose work evaporated, the provincial genius whose tradition nobody exported: a ranking built on one criterion erases one of those groups, and a ranking built on another erases a different one. This is why the familiar demand that a list simply be fair misses the mechanical reality. Fairness would require the evidence to exist in equal density for every candidate, and the evidence was never produced equally in the first place.

The conflicts also operate inside single careers, which is why the same musician can be the strongest exhibit for two opposite conclusions. Armstrong the innovator of the solo was also a craftsman whose control of tone and time professionals still describe with awe, yet his last decades of popular entertaining are counted against his body of work by critics who want the catalogue to match the revolution. Beethoven the titan of the catalogue was a poor candidate for reach in his lifetime, deaf, difficult and performed in a handful of German speaking cities, while musicians he overshadows in every conservatory outsold and outtravelled him for a century. Conflicting criteria are not a problem to be solved before ranking can begin. They are the ranking, and the only choice a list maker actually faces is whether to conduct the conflict in the open, with weights showing, or to conduct it in private and publish the winner.

## The Incommensurability Problem Across Traditions

Every comparison smuggles in a common measure, and the deepest problem in this article is that across musical traditions no common measure exists that is not itself a contestant. A Western classical composer is judged by works fixed in notation, designed to be re performed by strangers for centuries. A Hindustani master is judged by improvisations inside a raga, shaped for one evening, one audience and one devotional or courtly setting, in a transmission that runs through a teacher's household rather than a publisher's warehouse. A griot in West Africa carries genealogy, praise, history and entertainment in a single office that European categories split among five professions. To ask which of these people is the greater musician is to ask a question whose grammar promises a precision its terms cannot deliver.

### Can you compare a composer to a guitarist?

Only after changing the question. A composer leaves scores that strangers can perform for centuries, while a guitarist leaves performances, so the evidence, the unit of work and the community doing the judging all differ. Comparing what each did to the possibilities of a tradition is possible; comparing the artifacts directly is not.

Peter Manuel, whose Popular Musics of the Non-Western World mapped the terrain of this problem in 1988, spent much of his work on the way Western categories deform the musics they are applied to. His studies of Indian popular music showed a vast, sophisticated musical economy in which the composer as solitary author, the very unit European ranking assumes, barely exists: film songs emerged from teams of music directors, lyricists, arrangers and playback singers, inside an industry that credited them in combinations no Western list format can represent. When a ranking imports its unit from one tradition and applies it to another, it does not get a wrong answer. It gets the answer to a different question, printed under the original question's headline. The missing names in cross cultural lists are usually missing at the level of the category, long before any judge expresses a prejudice.

None of this makes comparison useless. It disciplines it. Comparison across traditions works at the level of function and transformation: what the music does for the community that keeps it, how far a musician extended the possibilities their tradition handed them, how completely they realised those possibilities by the standards the tradition itself keeps. A raga performance and a string quartet cannot be scored on one card, but the distance each musician travelled from their inheritance can be described in both cases, in prose, with the evidence shown. What cannot survive the crossing is the single number. The reader who accepts that limitation gains something better than a league table: a set of portraits in which excellence keeps its local meaning, and a clear view of the exact point where judgment becomes translation and translation admits its losses.

Time itself is organised differently inside these systems, and the difference defeats direct scoring from another direction. A tradition built on cyclical forms, the gamelan's great gong strokes returning like seasons, is pursuing a musical good, the deepening of a pattern until it becomes a place to live, that a tradition built on linear development, the symphony's argument toward a goal, is designed to avoid. The mbira music of Zimbabwe's Shona people makes the point in a single instrument: interlocking patterns played through the night at a ceremony whose purpose is to invite ancestral spirits, where the relevant excellence is endurance, accuracy of the inherited patterns and the capacity to sustain a community's rite until it works. Grading the mbira master against a symphonist on one card does not produce an unjust result so much as a category error with numbers attached, and the numbers lend the error a confidence it has not earned.

## What Recording Did to Who Is Remembered

Before the phonograph, musical reputation travelled at the speed of a touring performer and the printed page, and it died at roughly the speed of the witnesses. After the phonograph, a performance could be in two places, then in two thousand, then in every record shop on a continent, and reputation acquired a storage medium. The consequences for ranking were enormous and mostly unexamined. A musician's evidence for greatness was no longer the testimony of those who had been in the room. It was an object that any later listener could audit, which quietly disqualified nearly everyone who had lived before the audit became possible. The canon of the recording era is not the canon of the greatest musicians. It is the canon of the greatest musicians whose work happened to intersect with a microphone while the tape, wax or shellac was turning.

The medium also edited the music it preserved. The three minute side of the ten inch 78 rpm disc set a commercial length for popular song that radio and the jukebox enforced for decades, compressing introductions, solos and verses into a format that later generations mistook for a natural law of songwriting. Enrico Caruso's records, made from 1902 onward, helped sell the gramophone to households that had never entered an opera house, and his reputation was amplified by the machine he served: he was among the first musicians whose fame was partly manufactured by repeatable evidence. Musicians whose art depended on duration, volume, ceremony or the physical presence of a room full of dancers lost something in every transfer to disc, and no listener a century later can recover what the loss amounted to.

Elijah Wald's Escaping the Delta, published in 2004, is the sharpest study of the final stage of this process, the revival. Wald showed that Robert Johnson's towering modern reputation was built less by the small circle who bought his records in the nineteen thirties than by the reissue of his complete work on a Columbia LP in 1961, which landed in the hands of young British and American guitarists prepared by the folk revival to hear a solitary acoustic bluesman as the purest kind of artist. Johnson, a working professional who sang pop songs, novelties and whatever a paying audience requested, was recast as a lone prophet, and the recasting succeeded because the LP format delivered him as an album artist to an audience of album listeners. Harry Smith's Anthology of American Folk Music had performed the same alchemy a decade earlier, in 1952, rescuing commercial 78s by obscure string bands and blues singers and reframing them as a secret history of America for the folk musicians who studied it. In both cases the recording did not merely carry a reputation. It composed one, out of selection, sequencing and the tastes of the rescuers. Any ranking that draws on the recording era's evidence without pricing in the editors who curated that evidence is reading the anthology and crediting the artists alone.

## Language, Empire and Who Travels

Music crosses borders more easily than literature, since it asks for no translator, and this fact has misled many list makers into treating the world as a level field on which the best simply rose. The crossing was never free of charge. For most of the twentieth century the recording industry was headquartered in a small number of imperial and post imperial capitals, its scouts, studios and pressing plants distributed along the routes of empire, and its catalogues reflected the geography of capital far more faithfully than the geography of talent. A musician recorded because a company had a branch office, a local agent or a market it wished to open, and the musicians of regions the industry found unprofitable entered the global evidence pool decades later, if at all.

### Why are non Western musicians missing from these lists?

The lists are compiled inside institutions, magazines, record industries and academies, that grew up in Europe and North America, and their archives, languages and distribution networks decide who is visible before any judging begins. A master musician inside a tradition those institutions never recorded or translated starts the comparison unheard.

The early history of recording in India makes the mechanism concrete. In 1902 Fred Gaisberg, the Gramophone Company's pioneering recording engineer, set up a temporary studio in Calcutta and recorded Gauhar Jaan, a celebrated singer of the courtesan tradition, among the first artists captured in the country. The sessions were an imperial company's search for a market, not an act of documentation for its own sake, yet those discs became the foundation of an archive: the musicians Gaisberg's employers could sell are the musicians whose names the history books received. Peter Manuel's Cassette Culture, published in 1993, traced the sequel. When cheap cassettes broke the near monopoly of the film industry and the HMV label over Indian popular music in the nineteen eighties, regional, devotional and folk musicians flooded a market the gatekeepers had kept narrow, and the geography of who counted as a significant Indian musician changed within a decade. The talent had been there. The distribution had not.

The career that best measures the distortion is the one examined in [the life and career of Lata Mangeshkar](/2015/08/07/lata-mangeshkar-life-and-career/). As the dominant playback singer of Hindi cinema for half a century, she recorded on a scale that Western industry categories cannot process; a Guinness Book listing long credited her with more recorded songs than any other singer in the world, a superlative her admirers and the partisans of Mohammed Rafi argued over for years, and the argument itself is instructive. Inside India the question of who stood first among playback singers was a live national debate conducted in a dozen languages. In the Anglophone list making world, neither singer reliably appeared at all, because the institutions compiling the lists did not buy, broadcast or review the records. Language did the quiet work. A vocal tradition in Hindi, Urdu or Bengali could command audiences in the hundreds of millions and remain, to the magazines' electorate, a rumour from elsewhere.

None of this requires a conspiracy, and it is more damning without one. Empire built the roads: shipping routes, branch studios, radio networks modelled on the BBC, conservatory curricula exported to colonial capitals. Musicians travelled those roads in the direction of the market, and their music arrived in the metropolis classified as exotic, regional or world music, a shelving category that functioned as a ranking before any ranking began. The musician at the road's other end, writing in the metropolis's language and forms, was shelved simply as music. A list compiled from those shelves inherits the road system. Correcting for it means going looking for the musicians the roads bypassed, in archives the empires did not bother to keep, and that labour, not an adjustment of attitude, is what genuine correction costs.

## Survivorship Bias in the Historical Record

Survivorship bias is the error of judging a population by the members that happened to survive, and music history commits it at industrial scale. The musicians available for ranking are the residue of a series of filters, each with its own preferences: who was taught, who was employed by an institution that kept records, who was published, who was recorded, whose papers a family preserved, whose work later generations found useful for their own arguments. At every filter the same kinds of candidates pass more easily. The literate pass over the oral. The institutionally employed pass over the itinerant. Men pass over women, whose work was published under other names or not published at all. The musicians of colonising nations pass over the musicians of the colonised. By the time a modern list maker opens the archive, the contest has been rigged for two centuries, and the most scrupulous judge in the world can only rank the survivors the filters delivered.

The written tradition's losses show how indiscriminate the filters were even to their favourites. Bach, employed by churches and a town council that archived his manuscripts, still lost about a third of his cantatas, along with Passions, concertos and chamber works whose existence is known only from references and payment records. If the best archived composer of the eighteenth century reaches us with that much missing, the survival rate for a dance musician in the same century, or a court singer in Mughal India, or a praise poet in West Africa, approaches zero. Their greatness left no residue in the only currencies the archive accepts. A ranking of all time is therefore, at its most honest, a ranking of the documented, and the size of the undocumented remainder is not a footnote to the exercise. It is the exercise's largest single fact.

The treatment of women composers inside the literate tradition shows the filter operating where documents did survive. Fanny Mendelssohn Hensel wrote songs of a quality her family's circle recognised, and several of them reached print under her brother Felix's name in his published collections, because a woman of her class did not publish under her own. Clara Schumann, one of the century's most celebrated pianists, composed a piano concerto performed in Leipzig in 1835 with Mendelssohn conducting, and then watched the composing half of her identity dissolve from the record while the performing half, which left no objects, dissolved further. The archive does not merely undercount such musicians. It misfiles their work under other people's names, so that later rankings built on the archive reproduce the misfiling with clean consciences.

Recording changed the filters without removing them. The musicians who survived the twentieth century's cull are those the industry recorded in quantity, in the genres it favoured, during the years it operated in their region, on media that did not perish. Early jazz survives richly from Chicago and New York and patchily from the territories where the music was actually made nightly. The blues that record companies considered unsaleable stayed unrecorded, and the blueswomen of the classic era, who sold well for a few years, were allowed to lapse from the catalogues while their male successors were reissued. Every canon is a photograph of its archive. Survivorship bias does not make ranking impossible, but it fixes the epistemology: the past's candidates arrive pre selected by processes that had nothing to do with greatness, and the method that forgets this will mistake the archive's preferences for history's verdict.

## The Critic's Role in Building Reputations

Between the musician and posterity stands a profession whose members insist they merely report what they hear. Critics do not only report. They select which musicians receive the oxygen of print, they supply the vocabulary in which reputations are argued, and over time their syllabus hardens into the curriculum that teaches the next generation whom to admire. Eduard Hanslick, the most powerful music critic in nineteenth century Vienna, championed Brahms and attacked Wagner's operas in print for decades, and the argument he staged between absolute music and music drama structured how Europe heard both men. Hanslick was not a neutral gauge that happened to be standing nearby. He was a participant with a theory, and the theory won enough of the argument that later listeners mistook its victory for the music's own properties.

The twentieth century democratised the office without changing its function. Down Beat, founded in 1934, built jazz reputations through its reviews and its critics poll, running since the early nineteen fifties, which told readers annually which living players their professional peers ranked highest. Robert Christgau's Consumer Guide, begun in the Village Voice in 1969, graded records in capsule reviews whose judgments a generation of rock listeners absorbed as the price of admission to seriousness about the music. Joseph Kerman, in his 1985 essay Contemplating Musicology, accused his own discipline of studying everything about music except whether it was any good, and the ensuing turn toward criticism inside the academy changed which repertories universities taught as central. The deeper machinery, the way reviews, polls, syllabi and reissue programmes compound into a settled reputation, is traced in the series' own study of [how critics shaped reputations](/2026/07/21/how-critics-shaped-reputations/). For this article the point is narrower. A ranking never meets the musicians directly. It meets them through layers of advocacy deposited by critics with tastes, theories and deadlines.

Taruskin's double career is the cleanest modern illustration. As a historian he argues that the classical canon was built by institutions serving their own needs, and as a working critic for the New York Times he spent years participating in exactly that building, championing and demoting performers in prose that moved real careers. The two roles do not contradict each other in his case; they complete the picture of how reputations are actually manufactured, by informed people arguing in public under the pressure of their own commitments. The lesson for ranking is not that critics should be ignored, since they are often the best listeners a musician ever had. It is that their verdicts arrive with interests attached, and a method that quotes critical consensus as though it were a thermometer reading has failed to notice that the thermometer helped heat the room.

## The Popular Vote and Its Distortions

The magazine list is the form in which most readers meet the ranking question, and its mechanics deserve the same scrutiny as its results. A list begins with an electorate, and the electorate is the result. Rolling Stone's list of the five hundred greatest albums, published in 2003 with the Beatles' Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band in first place, was assembled from ballots cast by musicians, critics and industry figures the magazine selected, which means the outcome describes the taste of a particular professional generation at a particular moment, weighted toward the music that generation grew up defending. Its guitarists list of the same year placed Jimi Hendrix first, a verdict few working guitarists would dispute, and filled the places below him in an order that readers disputed furiously, because an aggregated ballot cannot tell any individual reader which criterion the aggregate used on any individual name.

### How are greatest musician lists actually made?

Most are made by assembling a panel, a readership ballot or an editorial shortlist, then aggregating whatever each voter happens to value, technique, memory, loyalty or sales, into one column of names. The method is rarely published, the criteria are almost never weighted, and the result reflects the electorate as faithfully as the musicians.

Ballot design decides outcomes in ways voters never see. A panel asked to rank ten names will produce a winner who offends nobody, because polarising candidates collect first place votes and last place votes in equal measure and sink to the middle of the aggregate. A panel asked to rate each name independently produces a different winner from the same opinions. A readership poll measures the organisation of fan communities as much as the standing of musicians, and the history of radio countdown polls is full of fan clubs who treated a vote as a shift to be worked. None of this is fraud. It is arithmetic, and it means the same musical world, interrogated by three honest ballot designs, returns three different greatest musicians. A teacher walking a class through a famous list, slide by slide, can make the mechanics visible by opening the pages in [a browser-based presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) and asking the students to find the electorate before they argue with the order, an exercise that permanently changes how the list reads.

The popular vote has one more distortion, the kindest and the most corrosive: it measures the voter's autobiography. People defend the music of their adolescence with a loyalty they grant to almost nothing else in adult life, because that music is soldered to first freedoms, first loves and the discovery of a self. A poll of any large population is therefore a census of remembered youth as much as a judgment of musicians, and its results tilt toward whichever generation's youth the electorate happens to be reliving. The distortion cannot be removed by sincerity. It can only be discounted, openly, by treating every popular ranking as a document about its voters first and about its subjects second.

## Chart and Sales Figures as Evidence

Sales figures enter these arguments carrying a false passport, stamped with the authority of arithmetic. The numbers feel like the one solid thing in a swamp of opinion, and list makers reach for them when they want a dispute to end. What the numbers actually record needs stating plainly, because once it is stated, their proper use and their limits are the same fact.

### Does record sales data prove greatness?

No. Sales data proves that a recording was manufactured, distributed, priced and bought inside a particular market at a particular moment, which is evidence about reach and about the machinery behind an artist. It says nothing directly about innovation, craft or durability, and across eras it compares different economies as much as different musicians.

The machinery behind the numbers changed so often that a sales figure is meaningless without its regime attached. Billboard published its first album chart in 1945 and launched the Hot 100 in 1958, and for decades the charts were compiled from reports telephoned in by selected retailers and radio stations, a system in which promotion departments worked the reporting panel as energetically as they worked the public. The arrival of SoundScan in 1991, counting actual point of sale transactions, detonated the industry's assumptions within months: country and hip hop records that the reporting system had systematically undersold appeared in their true commercial weight, and several reputations quietly revised themselves in both directions. The RIAA's gold and platinum certifications, meanwhile, are awarded on shipments reported by the labels themselves, on application, which makes a wall of gold discs partly a census and partly a press release. The IFPI, the industry's international federation, publishes global figures that are the best available and still rest on returns from the industry they describe.

None of this makes sales useless. It assigns them to their criterion. A verified sales figure is evidence about reach inside a stated market and period, full stop, and inside that boundary it is better evidence than anecdote. The corruptions begin when the figure is asked to testify about craft, innovation or durability, subjects on which a cash register keeps no opinion. Cross era comparisons fail hardest. A million selling record in 1930, when the American population was smaller, sets cost more and radio gave the music away, represents a different share of a different economy than a million selling record in 1990, and the two figures cannot be laid side by side as measurements of the same thing. The list maker who understands this uses sales the way a historian uses tax records: gratefully, narrowly, and never as a substitute for the judgment the figures were supposed to replace.

## The Strongest Classical Case

The classical tradition's case for supplying the greatest musicians rests on a property no other tradition can match: its evidence is cumulative and public. Because the tradition fixed its works in notation, a musician's achievement can be re examined by any competent stranger at any later date, and the comparisons inside the tradition have been running, with full access to the exhibits, for centuries. The long argument of [the history of classical composers](/2010/03/07/history-of-classical-composers/) is the story of that accumulation. When the strongest version of the case is stated, it runs as follows. Composers in this tradition built structures of a complexity that no improvising tradition attempts, because notation lets a single mind coordinate hundreds of decisions across an hour of music and lets later minds study the coordination. Bach's fugues, Beethoven's late quartets and Mozart's operatic ensembles are achievements whose difficulty can be verified at a desk, and the musicians who made them were playing a longer game against a more exacting record than any other tradition offered its masters.

The case gains strength from the tradition's own severity about its past. Classical institutions are conservative, and their conservatism functions as a filter with a long memory: works stay in the repertory only while generations of performers, who have every incentive to promote their contemporaries and themselves, keep choosing the dead masters over the living competition. Beethoven's quartets have survived two centuries of that choosing. Mozart's operas survived a nineteenth century that preferred its own products and shelved him as a decorative classic before the twentieth revived the dramas whole. A tradition that keeps re electing its ancestors against its vanity is supplying evidence of a kind no poll can manufacture.

Taruskin's presence in this argument is double, and keeping his characterisation straight matters. He is the historian who demonstrated that this canon was assembled by institutions with national and commercial interests, that its claim to be a natural order was a nineteenth century construction. In his own account that demonstration does not empty the canon; it explains the machinery by which music of extraordinary density came to be taught, published and preserved where other music was allowed to scatter. The strongest classical case therefore concedes the construction and stands on the contents. Grant that the syllabus was built by interested parties. The works the syllabus preserved still reward, under direct inspection, a density of craft and a depth of influence on every later Western musician that no rival tradition documents as completely. The case is at its most persuasive when it stops claiming the crown was discovered in nature and argues instead that the exhibits, open to anyone, justify the verdict they keep receiving.

The tradition's internal hierarchy supplies a further exhibit that rankings built on composition miss entirely. Joseph Haydn effectively invented the string quartet as a serious medium across some sixty eight examples, refining its conversational balance over decades while employed at a provincial court, and his London symphonies of the seventeen nineties were commercial triumphs in the largest music market in Europe. His nickname in the textbooks, father of the symphony and father of the quartet, is a rare case of the profession's verdict surviving intact into the handbooks, yet popular lists habitually place him below his own pupil's pupil, because his virtues are architectural rather than dramatic. A tradition severe enough to keep Haydn's innovations examinable for two centuries, and honest enough to record that its own judges rank him above the romantics who displaced him in public affection, is a tradition whose evidence deserves the weight this case claims for it.

## The Strongest Jazz and Blues Case

The jazz and blues case begins from an observation about parentage. Almost every popular music of the twentieth century descends from the encounter between African rhythmic and melodic practice and European harmony that produced the blues, and then jazz, in the cities of the American South and Midwest. The survey of [the history of jazz and blues music](/2010/03/21/history-of-jazz-and-blues-music/) traces that descent genre by genre. Stated at its strongest, the case runs like this. The musicians of these traditions did not merely excel inside an existing language. They built the language in which most subsequent popular music, on every continent, would be written: the twelve bar form, the blue note's negotiation between pitch systems, the rhythm section as an engine, the improvised solo as the centre of musical meaning, and a rhythmic feel, swing and its descendants, that retrained the bodies of dancers and listeners worldwide.

Ted Gioia's The History of Jazz organises this story around the innovators who forced each stage of it, and his method is itself part of the case. Where the classical tradition's greatness is documented in scores, jazz greatness is documented in recordings of performances, each one unrepeatable, and Gioia's narrative treats those performances as texts as exacting as any score. Armstrong's sides of the nineteen twenties, Charlie Parker's alto solos of the nineteen forties, John Coltrane's sheets of sound a decade later: the tradition's own practitioners cite these the way composition students cite Beethoven's sketchbooks, as the places where the art's possibilities visibly widened. The blues underwrites the whole structure. Its harmonic skeleton carried country, rock, soul and rhythm and blues, and its singers developed a vocal rhetoric of testimony and irony that every later popular singer inherited whether they knew the source or not.

The honest version of this case also carries Wald's corrective inside it, because the case is stronger for surviving him. Wald demonstrated that the blues canon beloved of revivalists, solitary guitarists singing of crossroads and deals, was a selection made by later collectors from a working music that had been broader, more professional and more commercial than the myth allows. Concede the selection, and the core claim stands untouched, because it does not depend on the purity of any individual bluesman. It depends on the documented fact that the musical grammar built by these traditions, pure or impure, commercial or obscure, became the grammar of the world's popular music. No other tradition can show a comparable transfer. The classical case claims the deepest archive. The jazz and blues case claims the largest living descendant population, and on the criteria of innovation and influence combined, that claim has never been answered.

Institutional recognition arrived late to this music, and the lateness is itself evidence about the criteria outsiders were applying. Universities that had taught Bach for centuries admitted jazz to the curriculum only in the nineteen forties and fifties, when North Texas State established the first degree programme in jazz studies in 1947, and the academy's tardiness is usually read as snobbery. Read more carefully, it marks the moment the tradition's own archive, its recordings, became extensive enough to teach from, and it underlines this case's central asset. Jazz built a complete, examinable literature of performances in under fifty years, in the open, against commercial indifference and racial obstruction, and stocked it with innovators whose claims can be audited by any listener with a record player. Few traditions in history have left so much evidence of their own greatness lying around in so accessible a form.

## The Strongest Rock and Pop Case

Rock and pop's case rests on a transformation of scale and function. In the three decades after 1955, popular music became the central art form of the world's youth, the medium in which successive generations conducted their arguments about freedom, race, sex, war and the shape of a life, and the musicians who led that transformation did work whose cultural consequences exceed anything a concert tradition could attempt in the same span. The detailed map belongs to [the history of rock and pop music](/2010/04/07/history-of-rock-and-pop-music/). The case at its strongest grants the opposition its premises. Grant that the individual works are shorter, harmonically simpler and less densely crafted than the classical masterworks. The question is whether greatness is only a property of objects, or also of what objects set in motion in millions of lives at once, and rock's advocates choose the second ground and defend it there.

Greil Marcus built the most ambitious version of that defence. In Mystery Train, published in 1975, he read Elvis Presley and his successors as carriers of a democratic promise native to American vernacular music, the promise that a nobody from nowhere could speak in a voice the whole country had to answer. In Lipstick Traces, twenty years later, he traced punk back through the avant garde movements of the European century and argued that its crude musical means were a deliberate assault on the idea that art belongs to the trained. Marcus's characterisation of this music is consistent across his work: the stakes of popular song are civic and mythic, not merely entertainment, and a criticism that grades it by conservatory standards has misunderstood the assignment. A ranking that wants to defeat the rock case has to defeat Marcus on that ground, by showing that cultural consequence on that scale should count for nothing in a judgment of musicians, and most rankings that dismiss rock simply decline the fight.

Wald supplies the case's useful complication from inside the tent. His How the Beatles Destroyed Rock 'n' Roll, published in 2009, argues that the Beatles, for all their gifts, helped shift rock from a dance music made by working bands for moving bodies into a listening music made for albums and headphones, narrowing the tradition's social function even as they widened its harmonic vocabulary. The argument matters here because it shows the rock case cannot be a simple story of ascent. The Beatles were innovators whose innovation partly consisted of retiring the conditions that had produced their predecessors, and any honest application of the innovation criterion to rock has to weigh what was opened against what was closed. With that complication admitted, the case stands: no body of musicians in the century reached more people, changed more lives' daily texture, or built a larger shared repertoire of songs known by heart across languages that share nothing else.

## The Strongest Soul and Hip Hop Case

Soul and hip hop make their case on two different centuries of the same argument: that the most consequential musical intelligence of the modern era worked inside Black American forms that the ranking institutions were built to overlook. Soul's claim is a claim about synthesis and command. Sam Cooke carried the melisma and testimony of gospel into secular song without surrendering either, and then did something rarer than singing: he founded his own label and publishing company at a time when Black performers were routinely separated from their earnings, asserting that the musician could own the machinery. James Brown rebuilt popular rhythm from the inside, subordinating melody and harmony to interlocking percussive parts until the band functioned as a drum ensemble with voices, and in doing so wrote the source code that funk, disco and hip hop would all compile. The line runs through [the history of soul funk and hip hop](/2010/04/21/history-of-soul-funk-and-hip-hop/), and its musicians score on innovation and influence by mechanisms anyone can audit in the recordings.

Hip hop's case requires the ranking to confront its own unit problem, which is why the case is valuable even to readers who resist it. The tradition's central creators include DJs and producers whose instrument is other people's records. Grandmaster Flash turned the turntable into a precision instrument for extending a drum break, and his 1981 recording The Adventures of Grandmaster Flash on the Wheels of Steel demonstrated a virtuosity of selection, timing and touch that pianists recognised as technique even when institutions did not. The Bomb Squad's production for Public Enemy at the end of the nineteen eighties layered dozens of fragments into a composed wall of sound whose complexity is orchestral by any functional definition. If a ranking's craft criterion cannot see this work, the flaw is in the criterion's definition of an instrument, and the soul and hip hop case says so, with exhibits.

The case also contains the appropriation ledger that any honest accounting has to keep visible, because the traffic between these traditions and the mainstream ran downhill along the gradient of power. Black innovators built forms; whiter, better capitalised performers repeatedly carried diluted versions of those forms to the largest audiences and the largest rewards, a pattern visible from the cover versions of the nineteen fifties to the marketing categories that separated race records from pop. Any ranking that credits the carriers over the builders has not made an aesthetic judgment. It has ratified a distribution outcome. The strongest soul and hip hop case therefore ends as a methodological demand: apply the criteria at the point of invention, with the documents open, and the names these traditions supply do not need charity to compete. They need the counting done where the music was made.

Craft inside these traditions had its own rigorous institutions, whatever the outside rankings assumed. The Black church trained singers in a discipline of testimony, breath and congregational command that functioned as a conservatory for musicians the actual conservatories excluded, and soul's vocal standards were set there, by peers, long before any critic arrived. Motown industrialised the examination: Berry Gordy's Friday product evaluation meetings in Detroit subjected finished recordings to a vote framed by a deliberately brutal question about whether a record deserved to exist beside the company's own hits, and the system that soul's detractors dismissed as an assembly line was, among other things, a quality tribunal with commercial teeth. Hip hop's cipher and battle traditions continued the same peer examination in public, where a producer's or an MC's standing was regraded nightly by the only electorate that could not be lobbied, the other practitioners in the circle.

## The Strongest World Traditions Case

The world traditions case is the only one that begins by questioning the courtroom. Its advocates point out that the ranking exercise itself, a single ladder with individual authors on its rungs, is a local custom of the literate West, and that most of the music humans have made was produced under other arrangements: hereditary lineages in which mastery is a family trust, devotional offices in which the musician is a servant of a rite, court and village ensembles in which authorship dissolves into a tradition that improves its works communally across centuries. The survey of [world music traditions explained](/2010/05/07/world-music-traditions-explained/) maps those arrangements region by region. Stated at its strongest, the case does not ask for seats to be reserved on the ladder. It asks what the ladder cannot see, and answers: most of the evidence, most of the musicians, and several entire definitions of what musical greatness is.

Consider what mastery means where the examination lasts a lifetime and the examiners are a hereditary audience. A Hindustani vocalist spends decades inside a guru's household before the tradition grants full standing, and improvises within a raga whose grammar is stricter than sonata form and older than the symphony. The Javanese gamelan distributes its intelligence across an ensemble tuned to itself alone, producing structures of cyclical time that Western composers, Debussy among them after he heard the gamelan at the Paris Exposition of 1889, recognised as a rebuke to their own habits. Fela Kuti built Afrobeat in Lagos into a music of genuine orchestral scale and used it as a political instrument against a military government, at a personal cost no conservatory ranking prices in. In each case the local criteria are exacting, the training is longer than a Western doctorate, and the resulting masters are invisible to lists compiled from Western evidence for one reason only: their traditions kept their records in memory, lineage and sound rather than in the print and paperwork the list makers search.

Then there is the fact that rearranges the reach argument from inside. When Umm Kulthum died in Cairo in 1975, the crowd that joined her funeral procession was estimated at four million people, in a city whose entire population was not many times that figure. She had sung, for half a century, in Arabic, in long improvisatory forms that made no concession to export markets, broadcast monthly concerts that emptied streets across the Arab world, and achieved inside her language a saturation and a civic standing that no Anglophone star has matched inside theirs. Manuel's work on popular musics outside the West generalises the lesson: the world's musical centre of gravity, measured by the intensity with which communities organise their lives around their musicians, does not sit where the recording industry's headquarters sat. The world traditions case, taken seriously, does not add a few exotic names to an unchanged list. It reveals that the list was a regional document all along, and it sets the terms on which any genuinely global ranking would have to be rebuilt.

## The Strongest Folk and Country Case

Folk and country music make the quietest case and, on the criterion of durability, possibly the strongest. Their unit of achievement is not the performer or even the composition but the song, and a song in these traditions is not finished when its maker stops. It is handed onward to be re cut, re harmonised, re worded and re inhabited by singers the maker never met, in a process that continues until the song either fails in other mouths or becomes indistinguishable from the culture itself. The terrain is mapped in [the history of folk and country music](/2010/05/21/history-of-folk-and-country-music/). A musician whose work survives that handing on has passed a durability examination far more brutal than the concert hall's, because the examiners are amateurs with no institutional duty to keep anything alive, and they keep only what they can use.

The Bristol sessions of 1927, where Ralph Peer recorded the Carter Family and Jimmie Rodgers for the Victor label in a Tennessee warehouse town, mark the moment this oral economy first met the recording industry on something like its own terms. The Carter Family's repertoire, gathered from the mountains of Virginia and arranged by A. P. Carter with a collector's instinct, became a seed bank for American song: hundreds of titles that later singers across country, folk, rock and gospel would draw on as though from a public commons, which is functionally what Carter made of them. Hank Williams then demonstrated what the tradition could produce at its commercial peak, writing songs of such compressed emotional exactness, eleven of them reaching number one on the country charts inside a recording career of six years, that they have never left the working repertoire of American singers in any genre. Woody Guthrie carried the same economy into politics and plain speech, and his influence is documented in the most literal way a folk musician can manage: Bob Dylan arrived in New York in 1961 and went, before almost anything else, to sit at the hospital bedside of the dying Guthrie, whose songs he had memorised as a syllabus.

The case these traditions make against the standard ranking is that the standard ranking grades the wrong artifact. It grades voices, careers and catalogues, where folk greatness lives in transmission: in a melody that survives its author by a century, in a chord progression worn smooth by ten thousand hands, in the fact that nobody needs to know who wrote Wildwood Flower to be changed by learning it. A criterion set that cannot register anonymous and communal durability will systematically undervalue the oldest music making arrangement humans have, and the folk and country case insists, with the repertoire as its witness, that an arrangement which has outlasted every institution in this article deserves better than a patronising paragraph in somebody else's ranking.

## Figures Who Top One Criterion and Fail Others

The cleanest way to see the criteria working is to watch them separate inside single careers. Franz Liszt tops the nineteenth century's influence table among pianists almost without argument. He invented the solo recital as a format, transformed piano technique so thoroughly that every later virtuoso works in his building, and taught, directly and through his Weimar masterclasses, the pianists who taught the twentieth century. His cultural reach in his own lifetime was a phenomenon the press named Lisztomania, a word coined by Heinrich Heine in 1844 for the hysteria of his audiences. Yet the durability of his own compositions has been argued over ever since, with critics dividing between the symphonic poems and operatic paraphrases they consider inflated and the late piano works, spare and harmonically prophetic, that pianists have steadily promoted. A ranking that places Liszt by his influence writes one article. A ranking that places him by the settled durability of his catalogue writes another, cooler one, and both rankings are working correctly.

Niccolò Paganini shows the separation running the other way. His craft, by the testimony of everyone who heard him, redefined what the violin could be made to do, and his twenty four Caprices, published in 1820, remain a proving ground violinists must pass through. But his body of published work is narrow beside the legend, his innovations were guarded as trade secrets during his life, which throttled the influence a teacher would have had, and his fame was substantially a fame of the platform, of events that only witnesses could audit. He tops craft as his contemporaries measured it and fades on body of work and transmissible influence, not because the legend lied about his playing but because greatness on one criterion buys nothing on the others.

Robert Johnson completes the triangle, and his case has already appeared in these sections from two directions. On body of work he is the smallest major figure in the modern canon: twenty nine songs, two sessions, no writings, no students, no band. On influence per surviving minute he may be the largest, because the guitarists who founded rock's vocabulary named his records as their textbooks and built styles on his turnarounds and falsetto cries. Wald's revision enters here as a discipline rather than a dismissal. If Johnson's repertoire was broader than the twenty nine songs, as the testimony about his working life suggests, then the influence we measure belongs partly to the selection the reissue made, and the criterion is grading the archive again. The three careers together make the article's central mechanical point impossible to unsee. There is no general greatness fluid that Liszt, Paganini and Johnson contain in different amounts. There are six different measurements, three men who each broke one of the instruments, and a reading public that insists on being given a single number anyway.

Franz Schubert separates the criteria along the timeline rather than across them. In his lifetime he was a Viennese schoolteacher's son whose songs circulated in manuscript among friends at evening gatherings, whose symphonies went unperformed by any major orchestra, and whose reach barely extended beyond one city's drawing rooms. Within decades of his death the same man stood revealed as the composer of over six hundred songs that reset the art of songwriting, of symphonies and chamber works that the institutions adopted entire, and of a body of work whose consistency professionals rank with anyone's. Lifetime influence near zero; posthumous influence, mediated by editors, publishers and champions such as Schumann, who retrieved the Great C major Symphony's score in Vienna in 1838 and carried it to Mendelssohn for performance, immense. Schubert proves that the criteria do not merely conflict between musicians. They can conflict between two halves of one reputation, divided by the accident of when the evidence was allowed to gather.

## Technically Great, Culturally Narrow

Art Tatum has haunted this article's account of craft, and he belongs here in full because his career is the purest available study of technical supremacy failing to convert into cultural reach. Pianists who heard him, including Vladimir Horowitz by persistent report and Oscar Peterson by his own repeated testimony, treated his command as a standard against which their own was measured and found wanting. His harmonic substitutions, his two handed runs at tempos that should have smeared into noise, and his ability to re compose a standard tune in performance placed him at a distance from other jazz pianists that the profession itself acknowledged with something like awe. Yet Tatum's name, outside musicians and committed jazz listeners, carries a fraction of the recognition given to contemporaries he could outplay without effort. His art made its maximal effect at the distance of a piano stool. It did not compress into anthems, it did not lend itself to myth, and the culture, which ranks what it can repeat at parties, filed him under appreciation for specialists.

Conlon Nancarrow pushed the same shape to its limit. From the late nineteen forties, living in Mexico City, he composed studies for player piano that exploited the instrument's mechanical precision to build rhythmic structures no human ensemble could perform: canons at irrational tempo ratios, accelerations layered against decelerations. For decades almost nobody heard them, because the medium barely circulated and the man had withdrawn from the musical capitals. Recognition arrived late, a MacArthur Fellowship in 1982 among its instruments, by which time the work stood as a completed world that the century's musical life had simply routed around. Charles Ives had rehearsed the pattern earlier in the century, composing in near total obscurity while running an insurance agency, his scores unperformed for decades, his Third Symphony receiving the Pulitzer Prize in 1947 for music written forty years before. Three careers, three different centuries of technique, one identical structure.

The structure is the lesson. Craft without transmission machinery stays local, because craft is demonstrated in rooms and admired by the qualified, and rooms hold dozens while machinery holds millions. What carries a technician's reputation outward is a student who becomes famous, a recording that circulates at the right moment, a scandal, a film, a critic with a column and a grievance. None of those carriers is a musical property. A ranking dominated by reach will never find Tatum, Nancarrow or Ives, and a ranking dominated by craft will never explain the world to itself. The conflict among the criteria is not an abstraction; it is the biography of every musician in this section, written in the gap between what they could do and what the world was equipped to notice.

## Culturally Vast, Technically Modest

The mirror image of the previous section is the musician whose technical means were modest and whose cultural effect was continental. Punk is the laboratory case because it made the modesty a doctrine. The Ramones built a catalogue on a handful of chords played at relentless speed, and the Sex Pistols released one studio album in 1977 before disintegrating, yet the movement those bands ignited re founded popular music's social contract in country after country: permission for the untrained to begin, for regional accents to stay unpolished, for a band to be a gang with instruments rather than a guild with certificates. Greil Marcus, in Lipstick Traces, argued that this was not an accident of marketing but a late eruption of a century long avant garde impulse to abolish the distance between art and life, and his argument, whatever a reader makes of its lineage, correctly identifies the mechanism. The technical modesty was the transmission device. A music anyone could start playing by Friday was a music that could be everywhere by the following year, carried by its own new practitioners rather than by an industry.

The pattern is older than punk and wider than rock. Lead Belly's twelve string guitar work was formidable by the standards of his peers, but the folk revival that carried his songs across the world prized the figure of the untutored singer, and musicians with conservatory training learned to simplify their delivery in homage to an authenticity they had purchased with lessons. Early rock and roll's three chord economy, mocked by jazz musicians of the day as primitivism, was also a distribution technology: it let every garage in the industrialised world reproduce the hit parade with household tools. Even Presley, whose voice was a genuine instrument of unusual flexibility, was sold to the world substantially on repertoire he had not written and arrangements built by others, which is precisely why his case separates reach from compositional craft so cleanly in the method section below.

What this section forbids is the reflex that treats modest means as fraud. The test is the same one applied to the virtuosi: what did the musician do with the possibilities in front of them, and what entered the common stock because they existed? By that test a three chord song that taught a million teenagers that music was theirs to make is an innovation of the first order, whatever its harmonic poverty, and the rankings that exclude such figures on craft grounds are not defending standards. They are defending a definition of music in which making is reserved for the certified, and the history of every tradition in this article is a long refutation of that definition by the uncertified.

## The Systematically Underrated

Underrating, to mean anything, must be argued against a stated criterion, and the three cases in this section are argued against influence, the criterion with the best paperwork. Sister Rosetta Tharpe recorded gospel with an electric guitar attack, distorted, rhythmic and assertive, from the late nineteen thirties onward, and her 1944 recording Strange Things Happening Every Day crossed from the gospel market into the upper reaches of Billboard's race records chart, a route almost no sacred record had travelled. Little Richard said plainly and repeatedly that she was his favourite singer and an early model, and the famous story of her inviting the teenage Penniman to perform at her Macon concert in 1947 is the kind of transmission the influence criterion exists to register. Chuck Berry's guitar style, the intros, the double stops, the stagecraft of playing while moving through a crowd, developed in a world where Tharpe had already done all of it on a bigger instrument of publicity than any bluesman of her decade commanded. Yet the standard genealogies of rock and roll ran for decades through male blues singers almost exclusively. Against the influence criterion, with named successors and datable recordings, Tharpe's customary position in the story is not a judgment. It is an omission with a pattern.

Florence Price supplies the case where the omission can be dated and the recovery audited. Her Symphony in E minor was performed by the Chicago Symphony Orchestra under Frederick Stock in June 1933, the first symphony by an African American woman to be played by a major American orchestra, and Price wrote hundreds of works across every classical form. The profession then lost her: publishers lapsed, performances thinned, and the manuscripts themselves survived by accident until a cache of her papers surfaced in 2009 in her former summer home in St. Anne, Illinois, found by the couple who had bought the house. A body of work criterion applied to the archive as it stood in, say, 1990, would have graded Price as a minor figure with one premiere. The archive was the problem. Her case is survivorship bias with a face, and it demonstrates that underrated is sometimes not an opinion about music but a report about paperwork.

Big Mama Thornton's case completes the set because hers is the appropriation structure in its textbook form. She recorded Hound Dog in 1952, in a performance built on her growl and swagger, and the record topped the rhythm and blues chart for seven weeks in 1953, while the song's writers, Jerry Leiber and Mike Stoller, were properly credited and the singer, by her own later account, received a single cheque for five hundred dollars from the whole success. Four years later Elvis Presley's version, adapted through a Las Vegas lounge act's arrangement, carried the song to a vastly larger audience and a permanent place in the culture. What was taken, the song and its attitude; what was added, a faster tempo and a national television apparatus; what was credited, the writers in both cases; what was paid, almost everything to the second carrier and almost nothing to the first. A ranking compiled from reach and sales will place Presley's Hound Dog in the story and Thornton's at its margins. A ranking compiled from the documents, as any honest method requires, records that the tradition's most famous performance of the song was its second one. Underrated, in all three cases, names a gap between the documented influence and the assigned place, and the gap, not a taste for obscurity, is the finding.

## The Systematically Overrated

Overrated is the most abused word in this territory, usually meaning only that the speaker dislikes what others love, and a method article has to rescue it or abandon it. Rescued, it names a measurable gap: a reputation larger than the documented performance on the criterion that reputation claims. So defined, overrating is rarely a property of a musician. It is a property of the machinery around a musician, and its mechanisms are as systematic as underrating's. The first mechanism is scarcity. A small surviving catalogue invites each item to be loaded with more meaning than any single work can comfortably carry, and the musician becomes a screen for the audience's theories about purity, authenticity and the roads not taken by a commercialised art.

Wald's study of Johnson is the model prosecution. The Johnson of modern legend, the solitary genius who sold his soul and invented the blues alone, is in Wald's account a construction of nineteen sixties revivalists who needed a pure origin for the music they loved, and who selected, from a working professional's varied repertoire, the songs that fit the portrait. Johnson's talent is not the issue in Wald's argument; the ratio between the evidence and the edifice is. Twenty nine songs, however remarkable, have been asked to support a mythology of an entire genre's birth, a weight they were never recorded to bear. When the edifice is mistaken for the musician, every rival of Johnson's generation with a larger documented footprint is made to look smaller by comparison with a figure who is partly a work of criticism. That is overrating as a mechanical process, with names, dates and a paper trail, and it requires no insult to Johnson to describe it.

The second mechanism is biographical romance substituting for listening. A famous letter in Mozart's name describes whole compositions arriving complete in his head, effortless as dreams, and for two centuries the letter did more for his reputation than any symphony, because it gave the public a genius it could picture. Maynard Solomon, in his 1995 biography, treated that letter, published by Friedrich Rochlitz years after Mozart's death, as unreliable evidence, one of several Rochlitz era documents that manufactured the Mozart the nineteenth century wanted. The music loses nothing when the letter goes. The reputation loses a load bearing myth, and the gap between the two is precisely the quantity this section exists to measure. The third mechanism is institutional inertia, Taruskin's territory: a name installed in syllabi and subscription series acquires defenders whose livelihoods are entangled with its standing, and the standing thereafter reproduces itself each season without anyone re examining the exhibits. In all three mechanisms the musician is almost innocent. The overrating is done by audiences, editors and institutions, for reasons that made sense to them, and a method that wants to correct for it corrects the machinery's output, not the dead.

National investment is a fourth mechanism, quieter than the others and harder to discount because it is often sincere. States and cities adopt composers as patrimony, fund their editions, name their halls and airports for them, and teach them to schoolchildren as evidence of the national genius, and the resulting standing abroad reflects diplomatic and educational budgets as much as listening. Finland's cultivation of Sibelius and Norway's of Grieg were deliberate cultural statecraft by young nations, and the music rewarded the investment often enough to make the arrangement look natural in retrospect. The mechanism matters for method because it is invisible from inside the beneficiary country, where the composer's eminence feels like a simple fact of the landscape. Every ranking panel sits inside somebody's landscape.

## Instrument Specific Rankings and Their Limits

Rankings by instrument look like the solution to every problem raised so far. Within a single instrument the evidence is comparable, the practitioners can judge each other, and the tradition supplies its own examinations. The greatest guitarist list, the greatest pianist list: these promise a contest with agreed rules. The promise survives about as long as it takes to read the entries. Rolling Stone's 2003 guitarists list placed Hendrix first, a verdict ratified by almost every practising guitarist who has been asked since, and immediately beneath him arranged bluesmen, metal technicians, folk fingerpickers and jazz chordal masters in a single column, as if the instrument were one job rather than a dozen. Charlie Christian, who made the amplified guitar a solo voice in Benny Goodman's band from 1939, was solving the problem of being heard over a big band. Andrés Segovia was solving the problem of making a parlour instrument hold a concert hall with Bach. Django Reinhardt was solving the problem of playing at all with a ruined hand. A column that ranks the solutions against each other has quietly decided that these were the same problem.

The instrument list does have a real virtue, and it should be kept: it confines the electorate to people who can play. Guitarists ranking guitarists produce results that track craft and influence inside the tradition with reasonable fidelity, because the voters can audit the evidence with their hands. The distortion arrives with the instrument's own hierarchy of visibility. Lead players outrank rhythm players whatever their relative importance to the music, because the solo is the unit the list format can picture. Drummers' lists marginalise the timekeepers in favour of the soloists, although every working musician knows the timekeeper is the band. Accompanists, the pianists behind singers, the bassists whose lines hold entire genres together, are structurally ineligible for an honour organised around individual display. The instrument list, that is, inherits the soloist bias of the recording era and mistakes it for the instrument's own values.

Used with those warnings posted, the instrument ranking is the most honest of the popular forms, and it teaches the general lesson of this article in miniature. Even inside one instrument, inside one century, with expert voters and perfect evidence, the ranking dissolves into sub traditions whose standards do not convert. If comparison fails at that scale, in the friendliest possible conditions, its failure at the scale of all music and all time is not a surprise to be lamented. It is a boundary to be designed around, and the method sections that follow are that design.

Some instruments expose the format's limits faster than others. The pipe organ's greatest practitioners built their reputations inside churches, on instruments each unique to its building, in a repertory where improvisation on a given theme was the supreme test and left no trace whatsoever; an organists' ranking is nearly pure testimony all the way down. Singers present the opposite problem, an embarrassment of evidence in the recording era and almost none before it, so that lists of the greatest voices silently restrict themselves to the century of the microphone while claiming the whole of history. Even the piano, blessed with two centuries of comparable repertory, splits between the composers who played and the players who composed nothing, between the recital athletes of the competition circuit and the recording artists whose edited takes no concert audience ever heard. The format keeps promising a contest and delivering a census of categories.

## Ranking Across Eras

Comparing musicians across eras means comparing the evidence regimes that produced them, and the first discipline is normalisation by opportunity. Ask not only what a musician achieved but what achievement was available to be recorded in their world. Josquin des Prez became the most famous composer in Europe at the opening of the sixteenth century, and the machinery of his fame survives for inspection: from 1501 onward, Ottaviano Petrucci's Venetian printing house issued his masses and motets in handsome editions that travelled the trade routes, making Josquin the first composer whose reputation was substantially a print phenomenon. His contemporaries judged him the master, his works were copied into choirbooks across the continent, and the testimony is dense enough to rank him inside his era with real confidence. Across eras the confidence must decay, because the comparison is between a reputation carried by print and testimony and a reputation carried by millions of identical discs, and no conversion rate between those currencies exists.

The eras also differ in what they asked music to be. A Kapellmeister of 1720 was a municipal craftsman supplying functional music weekly, judged by employers on reliability as much as inspiration, and Bach's prodigious output is partly the shape of that job. A recording artist of 1960 was a studio construction as much as a performer, the product of producers, engineers and arrangers whose contributions the ranking format assigns to the name on the sleeve. Comparing the two on craft means comparing a man examined weekly by a town council with a brand examined quarterly by a label, and the honest method compares the distance each travelled within a system before it compares across systems. Era adjusted, the pattern that emerges is not a league table but a series of local maxima, each as real as the evidence allows, joined by the transmission chains, teachers, prints, records, that let later musicians stand on earlier ones.

What ranking across eras can legitimately produce is a genealogy with judgments attached. The line from Bach through the conservatory curriculum to every trained musician since, the line from Armstrong through the swing bands to every improviser since, the line from the Carter Family through the folk revival to every songwriter with an acoustic guitar: those lines are documents, and a figure's position on several of them is the closest thing to a cross era measurement that exists. The method that follows is built on exactly that humility. It does not promise to settle the centuries against each other. It promises to state, for any proposed ranking, what was measured, what was assumed, and what evidence would change the result, which is the most that any honest instrument in this subject has ever delivered.

Push the comparison further back and the evidence thins to rumour with footnotes. Léonin and Pérotin, the masters of Notre Dame polyphony in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, are known essentially through one anonymous English student's treatise, written decades later, which praises their works and names a handful of pieces; on that single document two of the most consequential reputations in Western music rest. Nothing they made survives that a jury could grade against a modern rival's catalogue, yet no honest history can omit them, because the transmission chain runs through their schoolroom. Era ranking at that depth stops being measurement and becomes an act of historical justice, the deliberate seating of figures whose documents perished at the table their influence earned. A method should say when it has crossed that line, and this one does: beyond the reach of comparable evidence, the ranking is a memorial, and it should be written like one.

## What Durability Actually Requires

Durability was defined earlier as continued function after the originating world is gone, and it is time to specify the machinery, because a ranking that invokes durability without its mechanisms is invoking luck. Four requirements recur across every long survival in the record. The work must exist in a form strangers can re enter without the maker's presence: a score, a recording, a song simple enough to be carried in memory. An institution must profit from transmitting it, a church, a school, a publishing house, a repertory company, an examination board. Each generation of performers must find in it problems worth their ambition, difficulties that advertise their powers rather than merely taxing them. And the work must tolerate changes of function, surviving translation from dance floor to concert hall, from ritual to national symbol, from working music to heritage, without its essential effect depending on the lost context.

The revival of Bach is the worked example, and its details are in [the life and career of Johann Sebastian Bach](/2010/08/07/johann-sebastian-bach-life-and-career/). When the twenty year old Felix Mendelssohn conducted the St Matthew Passion in Berlin in March 1829, with the Sing Akademie, eighty years after its composer's death, every requirement was being assembled in real time. The score existed, preserved by the institutions Bach served. A new institution, the choral society movement, needed monumental repertory and found it. Performers discovered that the work displayed them as no salon music could. And the Passion translated functions, from Lutheran devotion to the concert platform of a nation building its cultural identity, gathering a German nationalist investment that Taruskin's account of the period refuses to separate from the music's merits. The revival was not the past being recognised. It was the past being rehired, for new jobs, by people who needed it. Durability, seen up close, is a series of such rehirings, and the works that fail to be rehired are not always the lesser works. They are the works for which no employer appeared.

Handel's Messiah shows the same machinery choosing a different survivor. Of Handel's vast output, one oratorio colonised the amateur choral societies of the English speaking world so completely that for two centuries it functioned as the public's idea of the man, while operas of equal craft waited for a later revival to find their employers. The lesson for ranking is that durability measures a relationship between a work and its future institutions as much as a property of the work. A method that reports durability must therefore report it as evidence about transmission, impressive and necessary, and resist converting it into a pure quality score. Bach's durability is the strongest single exhibit any musician offers this article, and even it is a story about choirs, publishers and nation builders as well as about fugues.

Publishing machinery performed for Bach the same office it performs for every durable name. The Bach Gesellschaft, founded in 1850, spent half a century issuing the first complete edition of his works, and that edition, more than any single performance, is what conservatories taught from and what conductors programmed: durability typeset, bound and sold by subscription across Europe. Handel had been served earlier by the same means, his works kept in print through the nineteenth century's choral festivals while equally gifted contemporaries whose music existed only in scattered manuscripts went dark. A candidate whose works lack a complete edition, a performing tradition and a teaching syllabus is not competing against a candidate who has all three. The second candidate's durability has been partly manufactured, honourably and openly, by identifiable people spending identifiable money, and the method that reports durability without those names attached is laundering an institutional history into a personal quality.

## A Defensible Method Stated

Everything in this article converges on a procedure, and it can be stated as a sequence of disciplines that any reader can apply and any list maker can be held to. Declare the criteria before the names, all of them, with the ranking's unit specified: the person, the catalogue or the performances. Declare the weights, because an unweighted list is a weighted list whose author hid the arithmetic, usually from themselves. Fix the evidence base for each criterion in advance, stating what counts as proof of influence, of craft, of durability, and refuse evidence gathered after the names were chosen. Discount every score for the density of its archive, so that a musician is not rewarded for having been born near a printing press or a record company, nor punished beyond what the missing evidence strictly requires. Audit the appropriation ledger wherever a tradition's builders and carriers differ, crediting invention at the point of invention. State what would change the result: the weight that would have to move, the document that would have to surface, the revival that would have to happen. And publish the sensitivity of the outcome alongside the outcome, so that the ranking reads as what it is, a function of declared choices, rather than as a tablet delivered from a mountain.

Two pieces of housekeeping make the procedure usable in practice. The first is the working file. A ranking built this way generates criteria, weights, scores and dissenting notes that no memory holds reliably across a long listening project, and readers building their own can keep the whole apparatus in one private document; the offline encrypted notes app available through the [VaultBook download page](https://vaultbook.net/download/) suits that kind of file, since a ranking argued honestly is a document its maker will revise for years. The second is the reading discipline. Scores assigned in advance of listening are hypotheses, and the method requires a pass through the actual music, the strongest works of every serious candidate heard whole and in good performances, before any score is allowed to harden. A ranking of musicians made without that pass is a ranking of reputations, and this article has spent thirty sections explaining why reputations are the one currency that cannot be trusted at face value.

The method does not abolish disagreement. It relocates disagreement to the places where it belongs: the choice of criteria, the setting of weights, the reading of documents. Two readers applying it in good faith can reach different winners, and the method counts that as success rather than failure, because their dispute will be about stated premises that evidence can genuinely address. What the method abolishes is the counterfeit dispute, the bar argument between a man measuring reach and a woman measuring craft who believe they are disagreeing about a musician. Under the method they are disagreeing about weights, they can say so in one sentence, and the conversation can proceed to the only question worth the evening: what music is for.

One more discipline deserves explicit statement, because panels and individual judges alike fail at it. Near ties must be reported as near ties. A method that returns scores of the same order for two musicians and then prints them in a numbered order has manufactured a distinction its own evidence does not contain, and the reader who trusts the numbering has been robbed of the most interesting finding in the table, which is that the contest was even. The application in the next section therefore speaks in standings with stated margins and stated reversal conditions rather than in false precision, and a personal ranking kept under this method will usually end as a small group of musicians separated by commitments the maker can name. That shape, a summit rather than a spike, is not a failure of nerve. It is what the evidence in this subject actually looks like when it is not being trimmed for a magazine cover.

## The Method Applied to Four Figures

Application requires the weights on the table first. This application weights influence and durability highest, on the argument built through the earlier sections that greatness in a musician is most defensibly the property of changing what later musicians can do and of continuing to function when the originating world has gone. Innovation follows, then craft, then body of work, with cultural reach weighted last, not because reach is worthless but because it is the criterion most corrupted by distribution machinery and therefore the least safe to let decide. The four figures are Johann Sebastian Bach, Louis Armstrong, Umm Kulthum and Elvis Presley, chosen because each leads the field on at least one criterion and because together they span the notated, recorded, non Western and mass media worlds. The full Armstrong record is in [the life and career of Louis Armstrong](/2011/08/21/louis-armstrong-life-and-career/); the argument here uses only what the criteria need.

Bach scores at or near the maximum on influence, since the conservatory curriculum of the entire Western tradition runs through his exercises and his harmonic practice is the grammar school of its composers; on durability, where his case is the strongest documented in this article; on body of work, some two hundred surviving cantatas, the Passions, the keyboard and chamber works constituting a shelf few musicians approach; and on craft, where three centuries of practitioners have left their testimony. He scores low on reach within his lifetime and modestly on innovation as rupture, since his method was deepening. Armstrong scores at the maximum on innovation, the solo's architecture and the rhythmic language of the swing century originating in his records, and near it on influence, since every improvising musician and most popular singers after 1930 worked in his settlement of the music's terms; high on craft by professional testimony; lower on body of work, since his catalogue is front loaded into one incandescent decade and diluted by later entertaining; high on durability within the recording era, with the reservation that his evidence is only a century old.

Umm Kulthum scores at the maximum on reach inside her language world, a saturation no other candidate matches at home, and high on craft and durability inside a tradition whose institutions, the monthly broadcast concert, the state honours, the conservatory of Arabic music, were built partly around her. Her scores fall on the cross tradition criteria through no defect of the music: her innovation operated inside a system Western evidence describes poorly, and her influence, immense across the Arab world, travelled weakly across the language border her art refused to cross. Presley inverts the pattern. He scores at the maximum on reach, high on a performer's innovation in fusing country, blues and gospel delivery into a new popular idiom, and low on craft as a composer, since he wrote almost none of his material, on body of work as an authored catalogue, and on influence of the documented, nameable kind, since what musicians took from him was permission and posture more often than technique.

With those weights declared, the method returns an order and states its price. Bach stands first, because the two heaviest criteria are his home ground and his deficits sit on the lightest. Armstrong stands second, and the sensitivity is exact: weight innovation above durability, or restrict the contest to the recording era, and Armstrong passes him. Umm Kulthum stands third, a placing that reverses with Presley the moment reach is weighted first, since a reach first method crowns Presley and demotes Bach to a specialists' answer. The defensible result is the whole sentence, order, weights and reversals together. A reader who accepts the weights has a winner. A reader who rejects them has been shown precisely which commitment the winner costs, and can recompute rather than merely protest. That is what a ranking owes its readers, and it is everything the magazine list, with its silent electorate and its single column of names, declines to provide.

## What the Method Still Cannot Settle

Honesty about the residue is the method's final discipline, and the residue is not small. The weights themselves are the largest unsolved item. No evidence can establish that durability should count for more than reach, because that is a judgment about what music is for, prior to all measurement, and this article's own weighting is an argued preference rather than a finding. A civilisation that experiences music mainly as communal celebration is entitled to weight reach and function first, and its method, faithfully applied, will crown its festival masters and its singer of the national soul over every fugue writer in Europe. The method cannot call that civilisation wrong. It can only refuse to let either party disguise a premise as a measurement.

The archive sets a second hard limit. The discount for missing evidence is an estimate, and for the oral traditions of most of human history it is an estimate of something close to total loss. Somewhere in the unrecorded centuries there may have stood musicians whose command, invention and effect on their contemporaries matched any figure in this article, and the method has no honest way to seat them. It can mark the empty chairs. It cannot fill them, and a ranking that forgets the empty chairs have occupants will mistake its own evidence base for the world. The third limit is the unit problem, which the method manages but does not dissolve. Where music is made by lineages, ensembles, production teams and traditions rather than by solitary authors, the method can rank traditions and functions, but the question readers actually ask, who was the greatest musician, presupposes the solitary author, and no procedure can grant that presupposition where the music itself refuses it.

What would have to be true for a single ranking of all musicians to be a finding rather than an argument is therefore statable, and stating it closes the article's business. A measure of musical value would have to exist that every tradition recognises as its own, so that a raga, a fugue and a three minute single could be scored on one card without translation loss. The archives of the world's traditions would have to survive in comparable density, so that the candidates stood on equal evidence. And the functions of music, devotion, dance, story, statecraft, consolation, would have to be reducible to one function, so that excellence at any of them was excellence at all of them. None of those conditions holds, and none is close to holding. What holds instead is a practice: criteria declared, evidence audited, traditions heard at their strongest, weights published with their reversals, and the argument conducted in the open where a reader can take hold of it. The greatest musicians of all time will go on being argued about, in magazines and classrooms and the small hours, and they should be. Under this method, at least, the argument is about the music.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What makes a musician great?

Greatness in a musician is a compound, not a single gift, and it separates into parts that can each be examined. One part is craft, the command of voice, instrument or compositional technique that other practitioners recognise and envy. Another is consequence: the musician changed what later musicians could do, by inventing a device, founding a style or proving that an audience existed for something new. A third part is the surviving work itself, its quantity, consistency and power to function outside the world that produced it. A fourth is reach, the distance the music travelled across languages and classes. Disagreements about the greatest musicians are nearly always disagreements about how to weight these parts, which is why honest rankings declare their weights before naming their winners.

### Q: Is there an objective answer to who is the greatest?

Part of the question has objective answers and part never will. Facts underneath the argument are checkable: dates, catalogues, sales inside a stated market, who taught whom, which recordings exist. Judgments built on those facts depend on criteria, and criteria depend on what a listener believes music is for, which is a value judgment that evidence can inform but cannot settle. A ranking becomes more objective as its method becomes more visible, with declared criteria, fixed evidence rules and published sensitivity to changed weights, but the final step, choosing the weights, stays an act of judgment. Anyone claiming a complete and final answer is selling certainty the subject does not contain. The most a method can honestly deliver is a ranking whose premises a reader can inspect, dispute and recompute.

### Q: How does influence get measured?

Influence is measured by tracing transmission, not by counting admirers. The strongest evidence is documentary: a musician names a teacher or a record that changed them, a pupil's style shows the transfer, a technique appears in one pair of hands and then, after contact, in everyone's. Composers leave letters, dedications and sketchbooks that let the transfer be watched closely. Recording artists leave borrowings that can be compared side by side, and in sample based music the transfer is literal, a drum break or a horn figure lifted from one record into hundreds of others. Where a claim of influence rests only on resemblance, it should be held loosely, because two musicians often sound alike through a shared source. Measured rigorously, influence asks a plain question with a paper trail: who took what from whom, and when.

### Q: Why do critics and the public disagree so often?

Critics and the public are answering related questions with different evidence and different time horizons. A critic listens comparatively, across a whole field, alert to technique, precedents and the difference between novelty and repetition, and is professionally rewarded for finding the significant early. The public listens biographically, through the music that accompanied its own life, and is rewarded with belonging and memory rather than accuracy. Critics also work on deadlines inside institutions with tastes to defend, which bends their judgments toward schools and against rivals. Neither side is simply right. The critic sees the map and can miss the territory of what a song does at a wedding or a graveside. The public feels the territory and can mistake familiarity for quality. Rankings that quote either verdict alone inherit its blind spot.

### Q: Is Mozart greater than Beethoven?

Under most declared criteria the contest is closer than the mythology of either camp suggests, and the answer changes with the weights. Mozart leads on fluency and on the operatic stage, where the ensembles of his mature comedies coordinate character and plot with a precision Beethoven, whose single opera cost him famous labour, never attempted again. Beethoven leads on documented transformation of the forms he inherited, on the sketchbook evidence of a mind remaking music's ambitions in public, and on influence over the following century, which organised itself around his example. Mozart's reputation also carries a surcharge of legend, including a famous letter about composing whole works in his head that Maynard Solomon and other scholars treat as unreliable. Weight durability and consequence heavily and Beethoven edges ahead; weight theatrical craft and melodic invention first and Mozart does. The criteria decide, as they always do.

### Q: Does innovation matter more than craft?

Neither outranks the other in the abstract, because they answer different questions about a career. Innovation asks what changed because this musician existed, and it can be enormous in a musician of modest technique whose one device reorganised a genre. Craft asks how completely the musician commanded the medium, and it can be supreme in a musician who changed nothing but played or wrote at a level peers still study. Ranking systems go wrong by forcing a conversion rate between them. A defensible method scores both separately and publishes the weights it used to combine them, admitting that a craft first ranking and an innovation first ranking will crown different names from the same evidence. The argument worth having is about the weights, conducted openly, not about which virtue is real. Both are real. They simply refuse to be exchanged at par.

### Q: Why do older musicians dominate these lists?

Several filters stack in their favour. Time is the first: durability, the heaviest criterion in most serious rankings, can only be certified by surviving, so a musician needs decades or centuries of aftermath before the strongest available evidence exists at all. Institutional teaching is the second: conservatories, examination boards and school curricula enshrine the musicians they were built around, and each new generation meets the enshrined names first, as the standard against which everything else is compared. Survival of evidence is the third: older Western musicians left scores, letters and published editions, dense archives that make their cases easy to argue in detail, while many of their contemporaries and nearly all of their counterparts in oral traditions left almost nothing. The dominance is therefore partly earned, partly inherited and partly an artifact of paperwork, and honest rankings say which part is which.

### Q: How did recording decide who is remembered?

Recording replaced testimony with evidence, and in doing so it rebuilt the canon around whoever intersected with a microphone. Before records, a reputation lived in the memories of witnesses and died with them, and the musicians we can still audit from earlier centuries are those who left scores. After records, a performance became an object that any later listener could judge, which promoted improvisers and singers whose art had previously evaporated, and froze their standing at the moment of the take. The industry then curated the evidence: which artists were signed, which sessions kept, which back catalogues reissued for each new format. Robert Johnson's modern stature rests heavily on a 1961 LP reissue that delivered him to the folk revival. Buddy Bolden, who made no records, survives as a rumour inside other men's memoirs. The microphone did not find the canon. It drew the map the canon was chosen from.

### Q: Are sales figures a fair measure of importance?

Sales figures are a fair measure of one thing, distribution inside a stated market and period, and importance is a larger word than that. A verified sales total shows that a record was manufactured, promoted, stocked and bought at a scale, which is genuine evidence about cultural reach and about the machinery behind an artist. It is silent about innovation, craft and durability, and it distorts when carried across eras, because markets, prices, formats and populations all change underneath the numbers. Chart data carries the same warning in concentrated form: before point of sale counting began in 1991, Billboard's charts rested on reports from selected stores and stations, a system promotion departments worked hard to influence. Use sales as reach evidence, dated and sourced, and they serve a ranking well. Ask them to certify greatness and they will certify the best distributed musician instead.

### Q: Which musicians are most underrated?

The most underrated musicians are usually those whose influence is documented but whose place in the story was assigned by someone else's archive. Sister Rosetta Tharpe shaped rock guitar and taught Little Richard by example, yet standard genealogies long routed rock's descent through male blues singers. Florence Price had a symphony premiered by the Chicago Symphony Orchestra in 1933 and then vanished from the record so thoroughly that her manuscripts surfaced by accident in 2009. Big Mama Thornton's recording of Hound Dog topped the rhythm and blues chart in 1953, and the fame settled on the later, bigger selling version. In each case the gap between the documented influence and the assigned reputation can be shown with names and dates, which is what separates underrating, a finding, from a taste for obscurity, a mood.

### Q: How do I build my own ranking?

Begin with criteria, not names. Write down the tests you intend to apply, such as innovation, influence, craft, body of work, reach and durability, and assign each a weight that reflects what you believe music is for. Fix your evidence rules next: influence needs named transmission, craft needs the testimony of practitioners, sales count only as reach inside a stated market. Then gather the strongest works of every serious candidate and listen to them whole before scoring anything, because a ranking built on reputations alone will reproduce the distortions of every list before it. Score each candidate on each criterion, discount for thin archives, and write down what would change your result: the weight that would have to move, the document that would have to surface. A ranking with its workings shown invites argument at the premises, where argument does some good.

### Q: How do I weigh two musicians from different centuries?

Normalise by opportunity before comparing achievement. Ask what evidence each century allowed a musician to leave, and what the job of musician consisted of in each world: a town employed Kapellmeister supplying weekly church music was examined by different standards than a studio artist assembled by producers and engineers. Compare first inside the era, where testimony, pupils and rivals supply a local verdict, then compare the distance each musician travelled from the inheritance their tradition handed them. Give weight to transmission chains, the documented lines of teachers, prints and recordings by which earlier musicians equipped later ones, since position on several of those chains is the nearest thing to a cross century measurement. Finally, report your confidence with the verdict. A comparison between two recording artists can be fairly exact. A comparison across five centuries is an argued judgment, and it should sound like one.

### Q: What does durability mean for a musician?

Durability means the work continues to function after the world that produced it has disappeared. Singers still need the songs, players still set the pieces before students as standards, audiences who share nothing with the maker's century still choose the music for their own occasions. Its machinery is specific: the work survives in a form strangers can re enter, a score, a recording, a memorable song; institutions profit from transmitting it; each generation of performers finds problems in it worth their ambition; and the work tolerates new functions, moving from dance floor to concert platform or from ritual to national symbol without breaking. Durability is therefore evidence about transmission as well as about quality, and it can only be certified late. A living musician's durability is a prediction. A musician dead for two centuries has submitted results.

### Q: Should I trust magazine greatest-of lists?

Trust them as documents and distrust them as verdicts. A magazine list reliably records what a chosen electorate, at a chosen moment, could agree to say in public, and that is real information about taste, institutions and the state of an argument. Rolling Stone's lists of the two thousands, for instance, tell a reader a great deal about the professional generation that compiled them and the canon it grew up defending. What a list cannot do is show its criteria, because the aggregation of hundreds of private weightings produces an order that nobody individually believes. Read the list for the arguments it starts rather than the answers it gives, check which traditions its electorate could not see, and notice which names recur across many different electorates and decades. Recurrence under varied judges is evidence. A single list's ordering is weather.

### Q: What is survivorship bias in music history?

Survivorship bias is judging the past by the fraction of it that happened to survive. In music the filters are relentless: who was taught, who was employed by an institution that kept records, who was published, who was recorded, whose papers a family preserved. The literate pass more easily than the oral, the institutionally employed more easily than the itinerant, men more easily than women, the colonising nations more easily than the colonised. Bach, favoured by church and council archives, still lost about a third of his cantatas. If the best documented composer of his century reaches us that incomplete, the survival rate for the dance musicians, court singers and praise poets of the same years is effectively zero. Rankings built on the survivors inherit the filters' preferences, so the honest method states plainly that it is ranking the documented, and treats the size of the undocumented remainder as its largest single fact.

### Q: How did empire shape which music travelled?

Empire built the roads and charged the tolls. The recording industry grew up in imperial capitals, planted studios and branch offices along shipping and colonial routes, and recorded what it could sell along them: in 1902 the Gramophone Company's engineer Fred Gaisberg was recording Gauhar Jaan in Calcutta because India was a market to open, and the archive of Indian music begins inside that commercial decision. Radio networks modelled on metropolitan systems, conservatory curricula exported to colonial capitals and the shelving category of world music all sorted travelling music into the central and the exotic before any listener judged a note. Musicians also travelled the roads toward the money, reshaping local styles for export as they went. The result is a global evidence pool whose geography maps capital and conquest more faithfully than talent, and every world ranking compiled from it inherits the distortion.

### Q: Does a short career disqualify a musician?

A short career disqualifies a musician on one criterion only, body of work, and even there the verdict needs care, because a small catalogue can be complete in a way a long one is not. Robert Johnson left twenty nine recorded songs and altered the technique and self image of generations of guitarists; by influence he is a giant wearing a miniature catalogue. The countervailing risk is romantic: early death freezes a reputation before decline, reinvention or embarrassment can complicate it, and the frozen image can be more marketable than any living career. The method used in this article scores the short career honestly on each axis, low on volume, unbounded on influence, unproven on durability until the work has survived circumstances its maker never faced. Length of career is evidence about opportunity and output. It is not, by itself, evidence about stature.

### Q: Where should I start if I want to hear the canon?

Start with the load bearing works rather than the longest ones, and let each tradition introduce itself through the recording or score its own practitioners treat as a doorway. In the classical line, Bach's Goldberg Variations and Brandenburg Concertos, a Mozart piano concerto and a Beethoven quartet will show the system's range in a week of evenings. For jazz, Armstrong's Hot Five and Hot Seven sides, then Parker and Coltrane, give the tradition's spine in three sittings. For the blues and its descendants, Johnson's complete recordings take under two hours and explain a century. Add Umm Kulthum in long form, a Hindustani raga heard whole rather than sampled, and the Carter Family's Bristol era sides. Listen to each more than once, because the canon is not a set of trophies to collect but a set of standards against which your later hearing will be calibrated.

### Q: How do I listen across unfamiliar traditions?

Slow down the demand for the familiar pleasures. A tradition you do not know will withhold its melodies' logic, its rhythmic centre and its emotional vocabulary on first hearing, and the boredom or irritation that follows is information about your training, not about the music. Give the work its full duration, since forms built on accumulation, a raga's slow exposition, a qawwali's building intensity, a gamelan cycle's layered return, cannot be sampled fairly in excerpts. Find the tradition's own account of what excellence is, from its practitioners and scholars, and listen for those virtues rather than importing your home criteria. Peter Manuel's studies of popular musics beyond the West model the posture: describe the music in its own terms first, compare only afterward. Repetition does the rest. The third hearing of a masterpiece in an unfamiliar grammar is usually the first one that counts.

### Q: What happens to a musician's reputation after death?

Death closes the catalogue and opens the reputation to management by other hands. Estates, publishers and record companies decide what is reissued, in what order and with what story attached, and those decisions, taken for commercial reasons, become the evidence base for the next generation's judgment. Institutions then recruit the dead for their own purposes: conservatories build syllabi, nations build heritage, movements build ancestries, and each recruitment edits the figure toward its needs. Myths rush into the gaps the documents leave, because a finished life can be narrated as destiny in a way a living career resists. Some reputations collapse when the publicity machine stops; others, Bach's being the grandest case, are built almost entirely after death by people who needed the music for new work. The process is not a corruption of an otherwise pure record. For most musicians in this article, the posthumous construction is most of the record there is.
