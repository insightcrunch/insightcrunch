The 2014 Indian general election produced the verdict that a generation of Indian electoral commentary had been trained to regard as impossible. The Election Commission of India, in its Statistical Report on the General Election of 2014, recorded 282 Lok Sabha seats for the Bharatiya Janata Party, the first single-party majority since the Congress victory of 1984, and 44 seats for the Indian National Congress, the lowest total the Congress had returned in any general election since independence. Narendra Modi, the Gujarat chief minister whom the BJP had named its prime ministerial candidate on 13 September 2013, took office on 26 May 2014 at the head of a government his party could have formed alone. The standard framing of that verdict is well known: the coalition era that began in 1989 ended in May 2014, and the BJP's long electoral arrival was confirmed at the head of a majority. This article accepts every figure in that framing and disputes its sufficiency. The claim defended here is more exact. The 2014 verdict's structural analogue is 1971, not 1998 or 2004. It was a single-party majority assembled on a three-component base, personal authority at the centre, organisational reach beneath it, and the principal rival's organisational collapse beside it, and that combination is the combination of the Garibi Hatao verdict of March 1971, not of the coalition mandates that bookend the coalition era.

## The Standard Framing: A Result Described, a Structure Missed

The standard framing begins from the count, and the count deserves its prominence. Voting in the 2014 election ran in nine phases from 7 April to 12 May 2014, and the counting of 16 May 2014 delivered a Lok Sabha unlike any since 1984. The Election Commission's statistical report records a turnout of 66.38 percent, the highest in the history of Indian general elections to that point, across an electorate of roughly 834 million. The BJP won 282 of the 543 elected seats on a national vote share of 31.0 percent for the party standing alone, a strike rate built on concentrated strength rather than uniform national spread. The wider National Democratic Alliance reached 336 seats. The Congress won 44 seats on 19.3 percent of the national vote, a fall of 162 seats from its 2009 total of 206, and failed to reach the one-tenth threshold that would have given its leader the recognised office of Leader of the Opposition. The United Progressive Alliance as a formation shrank to a rump of about sixty seats. Every element of the standard framing is present in those numbers: a majority party, a diminished principal rival, an alliance system demoted from necessity to decoration.

What the standard framing does with those numbers is narrate an ending. In its telling, the story of 2014 is the story of the coalition era closing, and the analytical work consists of marking the date. The telling is accurate and thin. It cannot explain why the arithmetic that had constrained every government from 1989 to 2014 failed in this particular election rather than merely weakening further, as it had weakened and recovered across 1996, 1998, 1999, 2004 and 2009. It cannot explain why the majority was assembled by the BJP rather than reassembled by the Congress, which had approached the threshold in 1991 with 244 seats and again in 2009 with 206. And it cannot explain what kind of mandate the 2014 majority was, which is the question that decides what the mandate could afterwards do. A verdict is a structure before it is a date. The parties that contested the 2014 election understood this. The BJP's 2014 manifesto, released on 7 April 2014, was written as the programme of a party expecting to govern on its own strength, and the campaign that carried it was built around a single named leader to a degree no Indian campaign had attempted since the Congress campaigns of Indira Gandhi. The reading defended here takes the structure seriously. It treats 2014 as an inflection verdict, a verdict that changes the operating rules of the system that produced it, and it asks which earlier verdict shares the 2014 structure closely enough to serve as its analogue.

## The Count Itself: Electorate, Machinery and the Shape of the Field

The election that produced the inflection was itself a record of scale, and the Election Commission's report supplies the dimensions. The electoral roll for 2014 carried roughly 834 million names, an increase of about 100 million over 2009, and some 554 million votes were polled across the nine phases, at 66.38 percent turnout the highest participation rate in the history of the republic's general elections to that point. Women voted at 65.63 percent, within a point of the male rate, closing a gap that had structured turnout analysis for decades. The field the voters faced was the coalition era's field at full extension: 8,251 candidates contested the 543 seats, the great majority of them independents or nominees of state parties, and the deposit-forfeiture columns of the report record the long tail of a fragmented system in which most candidacies were local facts rather than national ones. Two features of the machinery deserve record because they mark the election in the republic's administrative history. The none-of-the-above option, introduced on the electronic voting machine by the Supreme Court's judgment of September 2013 in the People's Union for Civil Liberties case, was available in a general election for the first time and drew about six million votes, 1.1 percent of the total, exceeding the winning margin in a scattering of constituencies without altering a single result's ownership. And the Commission's expenditure and affidavit regime required every candidate to file a sworn statement of assets, liabilities and criminal antecedents, the documentary residue of two decades of disclosure litigation, which the campaigns then mined against each other constituency by constituency.

The shape of the field matters to the argument because it disciplines the meaning of the headline shares. The BJP's 31.0 percent was won against a field in which the second national party held 19.3 percent and no other formation reached 5 percent nationally, while state parties held commanding positions inside their own territories: the All India Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam took 3.3 percent of the national vote and 37 seats, nearly all of Tamil Nadu; the Trinamool Congress took 3.8 percent and 34 seats in West Bengal; the Biju Janata Dal took 20 of Odisha's 21 seats. The national percentages therefore describe two elections layered on one ballot, a set of state verdicts delivered by state parties in their strongholds, and a national verdict contested between the BJP and the Congress across the rest of the map. The BJP won the second of those elections so completely that the first could not rescue the arithmetic. It led the count in state after state where the post-Mandal system had taught observers to expect a divided outcome, and its 282 seats included results in 23 states and union territories. The Commission's report, read this way, shows the inflection in its rawest form: a single party converting a layered field into a unitary result, because in the layer where the national question was asked, one campaign had posed a question the other national party could not answer.

The House the count produced deserves a paragraph of its own, because a majority is also a body of people. The sixteenth Lok Sabha seated the largest cohort of first-time members in the republic's history, more than three hundred of the 543 arriving without previous service in the chamber, and its composition, as analysed by the Association for Democratic Reforms from the members' own sworn affidavits, recorded 186 members declaring criminal cases against themselves and a large majority declaring assets above one crore rupees. The figures are cited here for a structural reason rather than a moral one. A parliament of newcomers, returned on a leader's name in constituencies many of them could not have won on their own standing, stands in a different relation to its government from a parliament of established notables. The whip in such a House is not a discipline imposed on independent power; it is the terms on which the members hold their seats at all. The 1971 House had the same property after the split's candidate selections, and in both cases the property helps explain the legislative velocity that followed. Institutions respond to the composition of the majorities inside them, and the composition of 2014 announced, before a single bill was moved, that the Lok Sabha's centre of gravity had moved from the floor to the leader's office.

## The Arithmetic That Ruled from 1989: Post-Mandal Fragmentation as a System

To see what broke in 2014, the arithmetic that preceded it has to be stated in its own numbers. The general election of 1989 closed the era of single-party majorities that the Congress had sustained, with interruptions, from 1952. The Election Commission's statistical reports for the quarter century after 1989 record a Lok Sabha that no party could command alone. In 1989 the Congress fell to 197 seats and V.P. Singh formed a government from a Janata Dal holding 143. In 1991 the Congress returned 244 seats, close to the line and short of it. In 1996 the BJP became the largest party with 161 seats and Atal Bihari Vajpayee's first government fell in thirteen days for want of numbers. In 1998 the BJP rose to 182 and governed through an alliance of more than twenty parties. In 1999 the same party held 182 again inside a National Democratic Alliance of 299. In 2004 the Congress recovered the plurality with 145 seats and governed through the United Progressive Alliance with outside support from the Left Front's sixty-one members. In 2009 the Congress reached 206, its best result since 1991, and still stood sixty-six seats short of a majority. Across seven general elections, the system's central fact was stable: the largest party's ceiling sat somewhere between 145 and 244, and government formation was an act of coalition architecture in every single case.

### What was the post-Mandal arithmetic in Indian elections?

The post-Mandal arithmetic was the electoral mathematics created on 7 August 1990, when the V.P. Singh government implemented the Mandal Commission's recommendation of 27 percent reservation for Other Backward Classes. Caste parties consolidated Hindi-belt vote banks, regional parties entrenched themselves in the south and east, and the fragmentation held every party below a Lok Sabha majority from 1989 to 2009.

The fragmentation had a sociology before it had a seat count. The Mandal decision of August 1990, and the agitation that answered it through the autumn of that year, converted caste from a background variable of Indian voting into the organising grammar of party competition across the northern states. The Samajwadi Party, founded by Mulayam Singh Yadav in 1992, built a durable Yadav and Muslim coalition in Uttar Pradesh. The Bahujan Samaj Party, founded by Kanshi Ram in 1984, converted Dalit assertion in the same state into a party that could win a state majority on its own, as it did under Mayawati in 2007 with 206 of 403 assembly seats. In Bihar, Lalu Prasad Yadav's Janata Dal and its successor, the Rashtriya Janata Dal formed in 1997, held the state through fifteen years of Yadav and Muslim consolidation. South of the Vindhyas the fragmentation ran on regional rather than caste lines, but its effect on Lok Sabha arithmetic was identical: the Telugu Desam in Andhra Pradesh, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam and the All India Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam in Tamil Nadu, the Biju Janata Dal in Odisha after 1997, and the Trinamool Congress in West Bengal after 1998 each held blocs of seats that Delhi governments had to rent rather than win. The post-Mandal system therefore rested on three props: caste parties durable in the Hindi belt, regional parties durable in the south and east, and two national parties that competed for the remainder while assuming that the remainder could never be a majority.

That assumption was the arithmetic's load-bearing wall, and commentary mistook it for a law. The coalition era generated a literature of permanence, in which India's social diversity was said to have made single-party majorities structurally impossible and coalition government the country's settled constitutional condition. The assumption survived contrary evidence because each election seemed to renew it. Even the strongest single-party performance of the period, the Congress result of 1991, was produced under the exceptional conditions of a campaign interrupted by the assassination of Rajiv Gandhi on 21 May 1991, and it still fell short. The BJP's rise from 2 seats in 1984 to 182 in 1998 looked, within this reading, like a party exchanging one ceiling for another: it had become the largest party and would remain a coalition-dependent one, because the social coalitions of the Mandal era capped every formation at roughly a third of the House. What the reading missed was that ceilings of this kind are made of voter behaviour, and voter behaviour can be reorganised by a campaign that changes what the election is about. The 2014 verdict did exactly that, and it did so on terrain the arithmetic itself had prepared, because a fragmented field lowers the vote share a majority requires. The BJP's 282 seats were won on 31.0 percent of the national vote, a lower share than the Congress had needed for its majorities of 1971 or 1980, precisely because the opposition vote was divided among the durable fragments the post-Mandal system had created. The arithmetic did not merely fail in 2014. It supplied the conditions of its own breaking.

## The Assembly of the Majority: A State-by-State Construction

A single-party majority in a House of 543 requires 272 seats, and no assembly of Indian parties had reached that figure unaided in thirty years. The BJP's 282 were not gathered evenly. They were built in blocs, and the bloc structure is the first piece of evidence for the reading defended here, because the blocs correspond to the three different mechanisms that produced them. The Election Commission's state-level results in the 2014 statistical report show the foundation laid across the Hindi belt. In Uttar Pradesh, which returns eighty members, the BJP won 71 seats on 42.6 percent of the state's vote, a sweep of a state where the party had won 10 seats in 2009. In Bihar the BJP won 22 of forty, and its allies the Lok Janshakti Party and the Rashtriya Lok Samata Party added six and three, giving the alliance 31. In Madhya Pradesh the BJP won 27 of 29. In Rajasthan it won all 25. In Gujarat, Narendra Modi's own state, it won all 26. In Chhattisgarh it won 10 of 11, in Jharkhand 12 of 14, in Uttarakhand all 5, in Himachal Pradesh all 4, and in Delhi all 7. Summed, the northern and western belt delivered a base within reach of a majority before a single southern or eastern result was counted, and it did so in states where caste parties had been expected, under the post-Mandal arithmetic, to hold the BJP to a fraction of the seats.

### How many seats did the BJP win in Uttar Pradesh in 2014?

The BJP won 71 of Uttar Pradesh's 80 Lok Sabha seats in 2014, on 42.6 percent of the state's vote as recorded in the Election Commission of India's statistical report, with its ally Apna Dal taking two more. The Samajwadi Party, which governed the state, fell to five seats, and the Bahujan Samaj Party won none at all.

The second mechanism operated beyond the party's traditional map. In Karnataka the BJP won 17 of 28 seats in a state the Congress had taken in the assembly election of May 2013. In Maharashtra the BJP won 23 of 48 while contesting only twenty-six seats in alliance with the Shiv Sena, which won 18, and the alliance's combined 41 made Maharashtra the second largest single contributor to the majority. In Assam the BJP won 7 of 14, the party's strongest result in the northeast to that date, in a state where its organisation had been thin a decade earlier. In Haryana it won 7 of 10. In Jammu and Kashmir it won 3. In West Bengal it won 2 seats and 17 percent of the vote in a state where it had been electorally negligible through the entire coalition era. These were not the deposits of state-level caste arithmetic. They were the gains of a national campaign wave, and their geography, scattered across states with unrelated local party systems, is the signature of a verdict driven from the centre of the campaign rather than assembled from state bargains. The third mechanism requires no map at all, because it is visible in the Congress column of the same report: the principal rival failed to defend even its strongholds. The Congress won no seat in seven states, was reduced to two seats in Uttar Pradesh, and in Andhra Pradesh, where it had won 21 seats in 2009 before the decision to bifurcate the state, its position collapsed so completely that the party of government at the centre for a decade became a minor presence across the south. A majority is a relation between a winner's strength and a rival's weakness. The state results show both sides of the relation at full stretch.

The southern and eastern columns of the same report show the wave's limits and, in showing them, confirm its character. In Tamil Nadu the BJP, contesting inside a small alliance, won a single seat, Kanyakumari, while the All India Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam swept the state; the national campaign's saturation could not substitute for organisation where a regional incumbent's machinery was intact and the national question had a local answer. In Kerala the BJP won no seat, though its vote share rose to about a tenth of the state's total and its candidate in Thiruvananthapuram, O. Rajagopal, finished a close second, the party's best Kerala result to that date. In Odisha it won one seat against the Biju Janata Dal's twenty. In West Bengal its two seats came with a vote share that announced arrival without delivering position. In the Telugu-speaking states it won two seats in harness with the Telugu Desam and one in the new Telangana. These results are the control group of the 2014 experiment. Where a durable state organisation, regional or caste-based, stood in working order, the wave broke against it and the old arithmetic operated. Where the state's party system was already hollowed, in the Hindi belt above all, the wave passed through. The majority was therefore built neither on uniform national conversion nor on the old arithmetic's survival, but on the differential condition of the organisations in its path, which is precisely what a three-component account predicts and a wave account cannot explain.

## The Rest of the Field: The Regional Parties and the Unfilled Space

The third mechanism named in the assembly of the majority, the emptiness of the anti-BJP space, has a state-party dimension that a Congress-centred account understates. The post-Mandal arithmetic had never depended on the Congress alone. Its insurance policy was the regional party: a formation rooted in one state's language, caste structure or regional pride, capable of defeating the national parties inside its territory and of selling its seats in Delhi at the price of policy. In 2014 the regional parties did not collapse. The All India Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam under J. Jayalalithaa won 37 of Tamil Nadu's 39 seats, the finest result in its history. The Trinamool Congress under Mamata Banerjee won 34 of West Bengal's 42. The Biju Janata Dal under Naveen Patnaik won 20 of Odisha's 21 and a fourth consecutive assembly term alongside. The Telugu Desam, the Telangana Rashtra Samithi in the state created on 2 June 2014, the Shiv Sena inside the Maharashtra alliance: the regional map of 2014 is a map of state parties at or near their peaks. What failed was not the regional party but the regional parties' collective function. Across the years from 1989 to 2009 their seats had pooled, in one formation or another, behind or against a national anchor, and the pooling had set the ceiling on every national ambition. In 2014 the pool had no national buyer. The Congress, the anchor of one pool since 2004, was too weak to bid. No third front survived the campaign as a plausible alternative government; the announced federal front of the counting season dissolved into its constituents' separate state campaigns. The regional parties therefore kept their states and lost their system. They returned 160 or more members between them, and for the first time since 1989 those members were decoration on a majority rather than its authors.

The contrast with the Hindi belt completes the point, because there the state-party function itself failed. The Samajwadi Party and the Bahujan Samaj Party in Uttar Pradesh, the Rashtriya Janata Dal and the Janata Dal (United) in Bihar, between them the very parties whose durability after Mandal had defined the arithmetic, won eleven seats in the two states combined. Nitish Kumar's Janata Dal (United), which had governed Bihar in alliance with the BJP until the rupture of June 2013 over the Modi candidacy, contested separately and won two seats, a result that measures the cost of leaving the winning side of a consolidating field. The space these parties vacated did not transfer to the Congress, which won two seats in Uttar Pradesh and four in Bihar between its own name and its allies. It transferred to the BJP, whose caste coalition in the two states, built by explicitly courting the non-dominant backward castes and the non-Jatav Dalit communities that the Mandal parties' dominant groups had kept at the margin of their own coalitions, is the subject of the Chhibber and Verma analysis taken up in the adjudication. The post-Mandal arithmetic broke, in other words, at both ends at once. In the south and east the regional parties survived and were orphaned. In the north the caste parties were routed on their own ground by a national party that had learned to speak the caste language more inclusively than its owners. A system ends not when its parties die but when its parties' behaviour stops determining the result, and by that measure the regional results of 2014 are the inflection's clearest documentary evidence.

The electorate the campaign addressed had itself changed composition since the arithmetic's heyday, and the change is part of the explanation. The Commission's roll revisions added roughly one hundred million electors between 2009 and 2014, a cohort larger than the population of most democracies, drawn overwhelmingly from the young: the Commission's statements through the election season placed the number of electors aged eighteen and nineteen alone in the tens of millions. A voter casting a first ballot in 2014 had been born around 1996, after the Mandal settlement, after liberalisation, into an India of mobile telephones, private television in every language and an economy whose commanding stories were of enterprise and migration rather than of the licence and the queue. The campaign's offer was addressed to that cohort with deliberation: employment, enterprise, the dignity of aspiration, and a leader presented as the self-made product of the same forces. The manifesto's economic chapters promised the machinery of that offer in the language of governance reform, minimum government and maximum governance in the campaign's compressed formula. The post-Mandal parties, whose coalitions had been assembled around the distribution of state goods to enumerated communities, found their grammar answered by a candidate who spoke of the neo-middle class and the aspirational voter as categories that cut across the enumerations. Whether the young cohort voted distinctly is a question the survey record treats with care, and the National Election Study's findings counsel against any single-bloc story. What the roll's arithmetic establishes without surveys is simpler and sufficient: by 2014 a majority of the electorate had no adult memory of a single-party majority government, and the campaign that promised one was addressing, in large part, voters for whom the coalition era was not a settlement to be honoured but a condition to be escaped.

## The Campaign as Artefact: Slogan, Screen and Saturation

The 2014 campaign left a material record distinct from any before it, and the record belongs in the structural account because the campaign's form was the personal-authority component made visible. Its slogan, Abki Baar Modi Sarkar, this time a Modi government, named a man where Indian national slogans had named parties, programmes or promises, and the party's own accounting after the victory credited its candidate with 437 rallies and public programmes and some three hundred thousand kilometres of travel across the nine months. Its signature technology, the three-dimensional hologram appearance, had been pioneered in the Gujarat campaign of 2012 and was deployed through 2014 to place the candidate's life-size image before gatherings in constituencies he could not physically reach, multiplying the single body the campaign was selling into parallel presences across several states on a single evening. Its media economy was saturation: the candidate's interviews, roadshows and nomination at Varanasi on 24 April 2014 were carried as national spectacles, and the campaign's command of the news cycle was such that even its collisions with the referee became coverage, as on 30 April 2014, when the Election Commission directed the registration of a case over the candidate's display of the party symbol and address to the media within the prohibited vicinity of a polling station in Gandhinagar after voting. The manifesto, released on 7 April 2014, the morning the first votes were cast, drew criticism for its lateness from the Congress, a criticism the BJP answered by treating the document as a formality the campaign had already superseded: the programme, in effect, was the candidate.

None of this was decoration on a conventional campaign. It was the mechanism by which a party whose organisation reached unevenly across the map achieved uniform national presence. In states where the BJP's unit was strong, the campaign's saturation reinforced the machinery. In states where the unit was weak, the saturation substituted for it, giving the local candidate a national meaning to sell in the constituency. The Congress, fighting without a named candidate, could not answer in kind, because a party cannot saturate a country with an institution. The asymmetry is the campaign-level expression of the article's central claim. Elections in the coalition era had been contests among organisations, and their results had the fragmented shape of the organisations contesting them. The 2014 campaign was a contest between an organisation that had made itself into a person and a person-shaped vacancy where the rival's answer should have stood, and its result has the shape of that asymmetry: concentrated, plebiscitary, and indifferent to the boundaries the arithmetic had drawn.

## Component One: Personal Authority as the Campaign's Load-Bearing Wall

The first component of the 2014 base was the personal authority of the candidate, and it was constructed deliberately over a decade. Narendra Modi entered national politics carrying the heaviest contested inheritance of any Indian politician of his generation. As chief minister of Gujarat from October 2001, he presided over the state during the violence of February and March 2002, in which more than a thousand people, most of them Muslims, were killed. The characterisation of his responsibility for that violence remains disputed at the strongest level on both sides: critics, including the Concerned Citizens Tribunal of 2002, attributed complicity to his administration, while Modi and the BJP attributed the charge to political prosecution, and the Supreme Court-appointed Special Investigation Team's closure report of 2012 found no prosecutable evidence against him, a finding that stood through the proceedings that followed it. This article takes no side in that attribution beyond recording its structure, because the analytical point is what Modi and his party built on the contested ground: a governance positioning, marketed from the mid-2000s under the formula of development and Gujarati pride, ratified by three consecutive assembly victories in 2002, 2007 and 2012. The Gujarat elections made him the only BJP chief minister of his era with a repeatedly renewed personal mandate, and the Vibrant Gujarat investment summits from 2003 onward gave corporate India a standing venue in which to treat him as a prime minister in waiting years before his party did.

### When was Narendra Modi named the BJP prime ministerial candidate?

The BJP Parliamentary Board announced Narendra Modi as the party's prime ministerial candidate on 13 September 2013, after the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh had made its preference unmistakable through that summer and L.K. Advani had registered his dissent by absenting himself from the Goa national executive of June 2013, where Modi had been made chairman of the campaign committee.

The nomination of September 2013 was an organisational act before it was a campaign act, and its significance lies in what it committed. The 2013 coordination between the Sangh and the BJP, in which the Sangh's leadership settled the question of the party's face and the party's machinery accepted the settlement, converted the entire apparatus described in the next section into an instrument of one man's candidacy. The campaign that followed, running roughly nine months to May 2014, was the most leader-centred operation in Indian electoral history to that point. Modi addressed hundreds of public meetings across the country. The campaign's Chai pe Charcha programme of February 2014 staged satellite-linked conversations at tea stalls, turning his biography into an organising device. The constituency of Varanasi, from which he contested alongside Vadodara, was made a national stage. The point for the structural argument is not the scale but the direction of accountability. In the coalition-era campaigns of 1998, 1999, 2004 and 2009, the voter was asked to endorse a party, an alliance, or a record. In 2014 the BJP asked the voter to endorse Narendra Modi, and its own candidates repeated the request in constituency after constituency, in many cases over the claims of their own local standing. Christophe Jaffrelot, in Modi's India: Hindu Nationalism and the Rise of Ethnic Democracy (2021), reads this centralisation as the electoral expression of a populist style in which the leader embodies the nation directly. The reading defended here is narrower and structural: whatever its ideological content, the personalisation performed a mechanical function that the arithmetic required. It gave voters in states with unrelated local contests a single common reason to vote for the same party, which is precisely the input a fragmented, first-past-the-post field rewards with seats.

The decade that built the component has its own documentary trail, and its milestones mark the conversion of a state leader into a national candidacy by stages. The Gujarat assembly election of December 2002, fought in the shadow of the violence of that year, returned the BJP with 127 of 182 seats and made Modi's hold on the state a fact his party's national leadership had to price into every calculation. The elections of December 2007 and December 2012 renewed the mandate twice, with 117 and 115 seats, on campaigns in which the state's development record and the chief minister's person were deliberately fused. Between those renewals stood the obstacles that were supposed to cap his ascent: the United States' refusal of a visa in March 2005 under its religious-freedom law, which Modi and the BJP converted into evidence of foreign interference; the Special Investigation Team's closure report of February 2012, which removed the immediate legal jeopardy; and the gradual rehabilitation in Western capitals, marked by the European Union ambassadors' lunch meeting of January 2013 and the British high commissioner's return to Gujarat in October 2012. Inside the BJP the ascent required the displacement of the generation of L.K. Advani, and its public marker was the Goa national executive of June 2013, where Modi was made chairman of the campaign committee and Advani absented himself in protest before being reconciled. By the time the Parliamentary Board acted on 13 September 2013, the question before it was not whether the party had an alternative candidate but whether it would ratify the authority its Gujarat unit, its Sangh parent and its prospective voters had already conferred. Personal authority of this kind is an institutional fact disguised as a biographical one, and the 2014 campaign simply operated at national scale the machine that Gujarat's three elections had assembled and tested.

## Component Two: Organisational Reach Beneath the Wave

Personal authority explains the direction of the 2014 vote. It does not explain its conversion into seats in constituency after constituency, which is an organisational achievement, and the BJP's organisation in 2014 was the product of a quarter century of construction and a specific decade of maintenance under adverse conditions. The expansion phase belongs to the 1990s: the party that won 85 seats in 1989 and 120 in 1991 reached 182 in 1998 and built state governments across the north and west, so that by the time of the 2004 defeat it held a governing presence in states that had been Congress territory within living memory. The maintenance phase is the less visible and more decisive one. Between 2004 and 2014 the BJP spent ten years in opposition at the centre, lost the general election of 2009 with 116 seats, and changed presidents repeatedly, yet the cadre relationship with the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh was renewed rather than allowed to lapse. The coordination machinery between the Sangh and the party, strained at points during the Vajpayee years, was operated deliberately through the United Progressive Alliance decade, and the Sangh's network of full-time workers remained available to the party's campaigns in states where the party itself was weak. An organisation of this kind does not show in a wave election's headlines. It shows in the unglamorous statistics of a campaign: candidates fielded in every winnable seat, polling agents present at booths, and a voter-mobilisation operation that reaches the last settlement in a constituency.

The maintenance had survived stresses that a weaker arrangement would not have. In June 2005 L.K. Advani's remarks on Muhammad Ali Jinnah during a visit to Pakistan brought the Sangh's displeasure into the open and ended his tenure as party president within the year, a reminder that the coordination machinery had terms and could enforce them against the party's most senior figure. The party's defeat of 2009, in a campaign led by Advani as its prime ministerial candidate, then posed the opposite question, whether the machinery would hold to a party that kept losing. It held, and the holding was itself a decision renewed at each Sangh and party conclave of the period: the movement would treat the opposition decade as an investment period rather than a verdict on its political vehicle. The contrast with the Congress's use of its own better decade could not be sharper. Between 2004 and 2014 the incumbent party consumed organisational capital while the opposition party accumulated it, and the 2014 result is in part simply the balance sheet of those opposite habits presented for payment on a single day.

The rebuilding after 2009 had named authors and dates. Nitin Gadkari, made party president in December 2009, set about reconstructing state units that the 2009 defeat had exposed as hollow, with particular attention to the party's absence in the east and the northeast. Rajnath Singh, who succeeded Gadkari in January 2013, presided over the transition to the Modi candidacy. In Uttar Pradesh, the state that would deliver 71 seats, the campaign was placed in June 2013 under Amit Shah, Modi's Gujarat associate, whose appointment as the general secretary in charge of the state was itself a statement that the party's most valuable organisational resource would be spent where the seats were. The Uttar Pradesh operation built committees down to the polling booth across a state with more than a hundred thousand booths, and it managed the alliance arithmetic with small caste-based parties such as Apna Dal rather than ceding the state's fragmented vote to the Samajwadi Party and the Bahujan Samaj Party in separate pools. The documentary habit matters because the BJP's 2013 and 2014 National Council resolutions, preserved in the party's archives, record the formal side of the same commitment the booth committees record in practice: a party deciding, at every level it possessed, to make one candidacy the vehicle of its whole strength. Those proceedings, the nomination's paper trail through the Parliamentary Board and the national council's ratifications, are the under-used primary record of the component, and they settle a question the memoir literature argues in circles: the commitment to the personal-authority campaign was not a drift ratified afterwards but a sequence of dated organisational decisions, each taken by the body entitled to take it, beginning a full eight months before the first vote. The machine knew what it was building while it built it. Organisation alone does not produce a majority, as the BJP's own 2009 result proves. A wave alone dissipates without it, as the Janata experiment of 1977 had proved a generation earlier. The 2014 base required both, and its third component required something the BJP could not build at all: the collapse of the other side.

The Sangh side of the machinery carried a scale the party's own membership figures do not convey. The Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh's annual reporting through the early 2010s placed its network of daily shakhas above fifty thousand across the country, each shakha a standing cell of trained volunteers in a locality, and the Sangh's practice of seconding full-time pracharaks to its affiliated organisations supplied the BJP with organisers whose careers belonged to the movement rather than to the electoral cycle. In a campaign of nine months this depth performs tasks that money hires badly: the verification of voter lists at the mohalla level, the staffing of the candidate's local office in constituencies where the party's own unit was a shell, and the carriage of the campaign's message into settlements no rally reaches. The 2014 operation also added a professional layer the movement had not previously fielded at scale, in the volunteer networks organised around the candidate's name, which enrolled supporters through missed-call registration and deployed them for turnout work in the cities. The two layers, cadre and volunteer, movement and campaign, are the organisational form the personal-authority component required: a structure deep enough to make a single candidacy present everywhere at once, yet disciplined enough to keep the candidacy single. Where the Congress of the same years could not fill its own district offices, the contrast is not one of enthusiasm but of stock. The BJP entered 2014 holding a decade's accumulated organisational capital, and it spent the whole of it on one election.

## Component Three: The Congress Collapse as a Structural Fact

The Congress that contested the 2014 election was the weakest version of the party to face the electorate since independence, and its weakness was organisational before it was reputational. The party had won 206 seats in 2009 and a second consecutive term for the United Progressive Alliance, and the five years that followed consumed that inheritance in sequence. The Commonwealth Games of October 2010 in Delhi closed amid corruption prosecutions of the organising committee's leadership. In November 2010 the Comptroller and Auditor General's report on the allocation of 2G spectrum quantified a presumptive loss to the exchequer that fixed the phrase spectrum scam in the political vocabulary, and the telecommunications minister, A. Raja, resigned that month. In April 2011 the India Against Corruption movement led by Anna Hazare began its fasts at Jantar Mantar in Delhi, and its August 2011 mobilisation filled the Ramlila Maidan and forced the government to negotiate with a street movement over the Lokpal legislation. In December 2012, protests in Delhi after a crime of exceptional brutality widened the indictment from corruption to governance itself. Each episode had its own facts and its own disputed attributions, and the government's defenders, including Manmohan Singh himself, argued at the time and afterwards that the presumptive-loss figure was methodologically contestable and the atmosphere of scandal disproportionate. The argument's merits are separate from its political effect, and the effect is recorded in the state results of the period: in the assembly elections of late 2013 the Congress lost Rajasthan heavily, lost Delhi to the Aam Aadmi Party after fifteen years of Congress government, and failed to recover Madhya Pradesh or Chhattisgarh.

### What did the Comptroller and Auditor General report on spectrum allocation in 2010?

The Comptroller and Auditor General's report, tabled in Parliament on 16 November 2010, examined the allocation of 2G spectrum licences in 2008 and quantified a presumptive loss to the exchequer of 1.76 lakh crore rupees, a figure the government contested while the report's findings drove the resignation of the telecommunications minister and the prosecutions that followed.

The spectrum case was not the cycle's only furnace. In August 2012 a further Comptroller and Auditor General report, on the allocation of coal blocks between 2004 and 2009, quantified a presumptive benefit to private allottees of 1.86 lakh crore rupees and fixed a second number in the public indictment, the more damaging because the coal portfolio had been held for much of the period by the Prime Minister himself. The government's defenders disputed the methodology of both reports, and the Central Bureau of Investigation's prosecutions moved at the pace of prosecutions, which is to say too slowly to clear the air and fast enough to keep it foul. The economic weather compounded the political. Inflation in food prices ran in double digits through stretches of 2010 to 2013, the growth rate fell from the highs of the first United Progressive Alliance term to below 5 percent in the years 2012 and 2013 by the series then in use, and in the taper shock of August 2013 the rupee touched 68.85 to the dollar, a record low that the Reserve Bank's emergency measures under its new governor, Raghuram Rajan, from September 2013 only gradually steadied. The phrase policy paralysis, applied to a government whose second-term legislative programme had stalled against its own alliance arithmetic and the street movements of 2011, entered the vocabulary from the government's own business constituency. When the assembly elections of November and December 2013 returned their verdicts, the Congress won 21 of Rajasthan's 200 seats against the BJP's 163, held eight of Delhi's seventy, and watched the Aam Aadmi Party, a party founded in November 2012 out of the Hazare movement, form the government of the capital. The 2014 campaign opened with the incumbent party already counted out in the states that vote first in every analyst's model, and a challenger who could campaign against a record rather than against a rival.

The decay had been financed, so to speak, by the victory of 2009, and the terms of that victory explain the speed of the fall. The Congress result of 2009, 206 seats won against expectation in the middle of a global financial crisis, was read inside the party as the electorate's endorsement of its governing arrangement: a prime minister drawn from outside the family, a party president drawn from within it, and a National Advisory Council under the president's chairmanship drafting the government's welfare programme from outside the cabinet. The reading converted an expedient into a constitution. Rahul Gandhi declined cabinet office and the organisational secretaryship alike and pursued a programme of internal party elections through the Youth Congress and the National Students' Union, a long-horizon project whose results, whatever their merits, did not include the reconstruction of the district units in the states where the party was dying. The state governments the party held became its principal remaining assets, and they were spent as such: Andhra Pradesh, its largest contributor of seats in 2004 and 2009, was destabilised from 2009 by the Telangana question, mishandled through four years until the bifurcation decision of 2013 destroyed the party in both successor regions, one of which it had created. Maharashtra, the other great contributor, was held in an alliance whose terms kept the party's own organisation in tutelage to the Nationalist Congress Party. A party that holds the centre and lets the states go is living on reserves, and the 2014 election was called when the reserves were spent. The collapse, measured this way, was a decade's organisational choice arriving at its audit, and it is why the third component of the 2014 base should be dated not from the scandal cycle of 2010 but from the settlement of 2009 that made the cycle fatal.

Beneath the scandal cycle lay the older organisational decay that the coalition era had masked. The Congress had not rebuilt its state units after the defeats of the 1990s in Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, where its vote had migrated to the caste parties and never returned. Its district and block structures in much of the country functioned as patronage queues for state governments rather than as recruitment machines, and where the party held no state government the structures thinned to letterheads. The leadership question compounded the decay. Sonia Gandhi, party president since 1998, had built the 2004 and 2009 victories, and Rahul Gandhi, made vice president in January 2013, was presented as the party's future without being named its prime ministerial candidate in 2014, a hesitation read across the country as an admission. The party fought the 2014 election behind a sitting prime minister who was not its campaign's face, a president who was its past, and a vice president who was its unannounced future. Pradeep Chhibber and Rahul Verma, in Ideology and Identity: The Changing Party Systems of India (2018), describe the Congress of this period as a party whose ideological offer had thinned to a default, against a BJP that was reorganising the axes of political competition itself. When the votes were counted, the collapse was total in form: 44 seats, no state in which the party led the tally except Kerala and Karnataka in weakened form, and a national vote share below one fifth for the first time in the party's history. For the structural argument, the Congress collapse is not background colour. It is a component of the majority, because the anti-BJP space that the post-Mandal arithmetic had always filled with regional and caste parties stood, in state after state in 2014, without a national occupant, and votes that a functioning Congress would have pooled against the BJP scattered or stayed home.

## The 1971 Mirror: The Analogue Stated on Evidence

The claim that 2014 resembles 1971 is not a flourish about landslides. It is a claim that the same three components, visible in the same configuration, produced both majorities. The 1971 election followed the Congress split of November 1969, in which the party's organisational old guard, the Syndicate, had expelled Indira Gandhi and she had answered by building a parallel party, Congress(R), on the authority of her office. [The 1971 mandate as structural analogue](/2019/04/12/1971-election-bangladesh-war-india/) was constructed across the sixteen months between the split and the dissolution of the Lok Sabha in December 1970. Its personal-authority component was Indira Gandhi herself, campaigning on the Garibi Hatao slogan against an opposition alliance whose slogan was her removal, and converting a parliamentary crisis into a plebiscite on her leadership. Its organisational component was the rebuilt Congress(R) machinery, assembled under her control after the old party apparatus had gone with the Syndicate, and tested in the state tours and candidate selections of 1970. Its rival-collapse component was the condition of Congress(O), the Syndicate's party, which retained eminent names and state machines from an earlier era and discovered in the campaign that the machines answered to habits that had died with the undivided party. When the Election Commission counted the votes of March 1971, Congress(R) held 352 of 518 seats on 43.7 percent of the vote, and the Grand Alliance of Congress(O), the Jana Sangh, Swatantra and the Samyukta Socialist Party had been routed as a combination. The mechanism matches 2014 part for part: a leader who makes the election a referendum on herself, a party rebuilt as the instrument of that leader, and a principal rival whose organisation collapses at the moment of testing.

### How many seats did Congress(R) win in the 1971 election?

Congress(R) won 352 of the 518 seats in the Lok Sabha election of March 1971, on 43.7 percent of the national vote recorded in the Election Commission of India's statistical report, while Congress(O), the party of the Syndicate that had expelled Indira Gandhi in November 1969, was reduced to 16 seats and the Jana Sangh held 22.

The framework that makes the comparison legible across the whole series is [the personality-as-institution framework](/2019/03/21/indira-gandhi-personality-institution/): the condition in which a leader's authority performs the functions of an institution, supplying the coordination, the discipline and the public meaning that a party apparatus would otherwise have to supply. Indira Gandhi's authority after 1969 was of that kind, and the post-1980 career of her office, [the post-1980 office-rebuilding completed](/2019/05/04/1980-indira-return-power/) in a second plebiscitary mandate, extended the pattern. The baseline against which both cases stand is [the Nehruvian-era institutional baseline](/2019/01/25/nehruvian-era-settlement-three-tensions/), in which authority was distributed across institutions and the leader's person was one element among several. What 1971 and 2014 share, against that baseline, is the inversion: the institution thins, the person carries the load, and the election becomes the ratification of a pre-existing concentration. The resemblance is structural rather than biographical. Indira Gandhi in 1971 led a party of the left-populist centre with a programme of bank nationalisation behind her and state-led development ahead of her. Narendra Modi in 2014 led a party of the Hindu nationalist right with a programme of market-led development and cultural assertion. The ideologies point in opposite directions. The base structure is the same, and it is the base structure, not the ideology, that determines what a mandate can do to the system that elected it.

The 1971 campaign's own record, preserved in the Election Commission's report on the fifth general election, fills in the organisational cell of the comparison with a precision the summary treatment rarely attempts. The election was a midterm election, the first in the republic's history: Indira Gandhi advised the dissolution of the fourth Lok Sabha on 27 December 1970, a year before its term expired, precisely because her government, reduced to a minority by the split and sustained by outside support from the Communist Party of India and the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, could not carry its programme through the House it had. The campaign she then fought was organised as a national plebiscite from the first day. The Congress(R) manifesto centred the removal of poverty, and the slogan Garibi Hatao, abolish poverty, answered the Grand Alliance's Indira Hatao with an asymmetry the alliance never repaired: one side campaigned against a person, the other for a programme attached to a person, and the electorate was asked to choose between a government and a removal. The alliance's arithmetic looked respectable on paper, four parties pooling the anti-Congress vote of 1967 in seat adjustments across the northern states. It failed in the manner the 2014 field would repeat, because pooling arrangements among parties cannot substitute for a single answer to the question the campaign is asking, and the question of 1971, like the question of 2014, was whom the voter wished to install. The results by party tell the collapse story in the Commission's own columns: Congress(O) 16 seats on 10.4 percent of the vote, the Swatantra Party 8, the Jana Sangh 22, the Samyukta Socialist Party 3, against Congress(R)'s 352. The Syndicate's leaders lost in their own strongholds, K. Kamaraj's organisation in Tamil Nadu surviving only through the Dravidian parties' separate arithmetic. A party system had been weighed against a plebiscite and found to be the lighter object.

The era's founding demonstration, the election of 1996, belongs in the assembly account because it taught every participant the rules that 2014 broke. That verdict returned the BJP as the largest party with 161 seats and no path to a majority: Vajpayee's government of May 1996 resigned in thirteen days without facing a vote it could only lose, and the United Front governments that followed, examined in their own right earlier in this pillar, governed at the pleasure of a Congress supporting them from outside. The lesson the political class drew was that the system's centre of gravity had moved permanently from the largest party to the post-election negotiation, and party strategies for eighteen years were built on the drawing: cultivate allies, hoard state governments, keep every commitment negotiable. The BJP's own conduct through 1998 and 1999 conformed, shelving its founding programme for a common agenda. What changed by 2014 was not the system's rules but one party's refusal to keep strategising for negotiation. The Modi candidacy was, in organisational terms, a bet that the negotiation stage could be skipped, that a campaign run for a majority rather than for a plurality would produce the majority it assumed. Bets of that kind are vindicated or buried by the count, and the count of 16 May 2014 vindicated it so completely that the eighteen years of negotiation strategy came to look, in retrospect, like a convention the system's participants had imposed on themselves. Conventions of that sort are exactly what inflection verdicts are made of.

## Why 1998 and 2004 Fail as Analogues

The rival analogues fail on the same evidence. The 1998 verdict, in which the BJP first led a government at the centre for a full season, was a coalition-mandate verdict in its construction and its operation. The party won 182 seats, and the distance between 182 and 272 was filled by the National Democratic Alliance, a formation whose members extracted prices that are a matter of record: a National Agenda for Governance that shelved the BJP's three core commitments, coalition partners holding portfolios as fiefs, and a government that fell in April 1999 by a single vote when the All India Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam withdrew. [The 1998 NDA's strategic paradox](/2019/06/11/1998-nda-formation-vajpayee-era/) is that the alliance made government possible and majority politics impossible, and the paradox held through the fuller 1999 mandate, in which the BJP again won 182 inside an alliance of 299 and governed, creditably and by common consent transformatively in foreign and economic policy, as a coalition manager rather than as a majority. Nothing in 1998 or 1999 resembles the 2014 structure: the leader, Atal Bihari Vajpayee, was a consensus figure whose authority operated by accommodation, the party's organisation was one claimant among allies for the government's direction, and the principal rival, the Congress of 1998 and 1999, was diminished but intact, holding 141 and 114 seats and the governments of major states.

The verdict of 2009, the coalition era's strongest, fails the test most instructively of all, because it came nearest and shows exactly where the ceiling stood. The Congress won 206 seats that year, the best single-party result since 1991, on the strength of an incumbent government's record, a divided opposition and the personal standing of a prime minister no one described as a plebiscitary leader. It still governed as a renter. The United Progressive Alliance's combined strength stood below the majority mark, the government's second term was made possible by outside assurances, and within thirty months the arithmetic of dependence had produced the paralysis the previous sections recorded: allies vetoing policy, the street vetoing legislation, and a majority partner unable to convert 206 seats into command of its own government. The distance between 206 and 272 is sixty-six seats on paper. In the structure of the coalition era it was the distance between two kinds of power, and no verdict between 1989 and 2009 crossed it, including the one that came closest. The crossing, when it came in 2014, was therefore not a matter of degree. A party that had watched 206 seats fail to purchase command learned the lesson the BJP's strategists had also learned, that in the post-Mandal field only the majority itself, held in one hand, ended the renting.

The 2004 verdict fails the test from the other side. It was the verdict that removed the BJP-led alliance and installed the Congress-led United Progressive Alliance, and its structure was sectoral and negative: a rejection of the incumbent alliance's record by particular constituencies, most sharply in Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu, rather than the construction of a new majority. [The 2004 UPA verdict's sectoral-specific reading](/2019/06/22/2004-upa-shock-verdict-explained/) captures the pattern: the Congress won 145 seats, fewer than the BJP's losing 138 by a margin of seven, and governed for a decade through an alliance whose outside support cost it the nuclear agreement crisis of 2008, when [the 2008 rupture's coalition-architecture costs](/2019/06/28/2008-indo-us-nuclear-deal-rupture/) came due and the government survived a confidence vote on 22 July 2008 by nineteen votes with its Left support gone. A verdict that produces a decade of that arithmetic is a coalition-era verdict whatever its surprises. The 2014 verdict produced a government that did not need a confidence strategy. The difference between a plurality that rents its majority and a majority that owns its numbers is the difference between the coalition era and what ended it, and neither 1998 nor 2004 stands on the majority side of that line. To call 2014 a replay of either is to classify by decade and party colour. To call it a replay of 1971 is to classify by mechanism, and mechanism is what the rest of this article, and the era that follows, has to explain.

What the 1971 majority did with its first year is the analogue's sharpest evidence, because it shows the form before the Emergency coloured every judgement of it. Within nine months of the count, the government had carried the Twenty-Fourth Amendment, restoring Parliament's power to amend the fundamental rights, and the Twenty-Fifth, subordinating property claims to the directive principles in the matters it specified; had enacted the Maintenance of Internal Security Act of 1971; and had abolished the privy purses and privileges of the former princes through the Twenty-Sixth Amendment of December 1971, closing by constitutional amendment a question it had failed to carry in 1970 and lost in the courts. The December war against Pakistan and the creation of Bangladesh then converted the electoral mandate into a national one, and the state elections of 1972 renewed it across the provinces. Each item on that list is a measure of the same quantity: the range of actions available to a government whose numbers are its own. Two of the list's items, the amendments on property and the purses, settled questions the founding generation had left open and the coalition-free Congress of the 1950s had not pressed. A majority of the plebiscitary kind does not merely govern the system it inherits. It reopens the system's settled questions in sequence, because nothing in its arithmetic requires it to leave them closed, and the sequence's direction is set by the programme the leader carried into the plebiscite. That is the precise sense in which the 2014 mandate's opening moves, the judicial appointments amendment of its first months and the fiscal reconstruction of its third year, belong to the pattern rather than to improvisation.

## The Seat-Vote Mechanics: How 31 Percent Became 282

Between the vote share and the seat count stands the translation machinery of the first-past-the-post system, and the 2014 translation was the most efficient ever performed by an Indian party. The BJP's 31.0 percent yielded 51.9 percent of the seats. The mechanics of the conversion are visible in the state columns already examined: in Gujarat the party took all 26 seats on 60.1 percent of the state's vote; in Rajasthan all 25 on about 55 percent; in Madhya Pradesh 27 of 29 on about 54 percent. In each case the opposing vote was divided between a Congress in collapse and state or caste parties in decline, so that pluralities in the low forties sufficed for sweeps. Uttar Pradesh is the extreme case and the most instructive: 42.6 percent of the vote, divided opposition in nearly every constituency between the Samajwadi Party, the Bahujan Samaj Party and the Congress, produced 71 seats from 80, a strike rate approaching nine in ten. Post-poll analyses of the constituency data recorded the same pattern in the direct contests: in the seats where the BJP and the Congress finished first and second between them, the BJP won the overwhelming majority, because the Congress's vote in such seats was a wasting asset, too large to transfer and too small to win. The Bahujan Samaj Party's experience is the translation's mirror image: about a fifth of Uttar Pradesh's vote, spread with near uniformity across the state, returned no seats at all, the system's maximum penalty for even distribution.

The mechanics carry an implication the structural argument needs. A majority assembled by translation efficiency is not thereby a fragile majority. Its fragility would depend on the opposition's vote reconsolidating, which is a political event requiring the very organisational reconstruction whose absence the verdict recorded. The 1971 translation had the same property: Congress(R)'s 43.7 percent yielded 68 percent of the seats because the Grand Alliance's pooled vote distributed itself across four party labels and lost constituency by constituency. In both cases the seat surplus over the vote share is the arithmetic signature of a fragmented opposition facing a consolidated plurality, and in both cases the surplus was then available, in the House, as though it were a two-thirds mandate of conviction. Governments so seated do not experience their majorities as artefacts, and neither do the institutions that face them. The translation machinery is part of the structure, and it is one more reason the coalition-era readings err when they discount the 2014 majority by its vote share: the same discount, applied in 1971, would have dissolved a mandate whose consequences fill the rest of this series.

## The Strong Counter: Conditions the Analogue Cannot Carry

The strongest counter to the 1971 reading deserves its full force, because if it stands, the analogue misleads more than it reveals. The counter is that 1971 and 2014 occurred inside different Indias. The election of 1971 was fought in a political economy of state-led development, in which the commanding heights were public, the private sector operated under industrial licensing, and the electorate's economic expectations ran through the state. The election of 2014 was fought twenty-three years after the liberalisation of 1991, in a market economy with a vast private sector, an aspirational electorate addressed in the language of enterprise, and a media environment of continuous television and social media that no campaign of 1971 could have imagined. The social system differed as deeply. The 1971 verdict preceded the Mandal transformation: the caste parties that structure the 2014 field did not exist, the Congress of 1971 still held much of its old coalition of upper castes, Dalits, Adivasis and Muslims, and the politics of reservation had not yet reorganised the Hindi belt. The 2014 verdict was fought through the post-Mandal field and partly by means of it, with the BJP assembling an expanded caste coalition of its own. A mechanism that operates through unlike materials, the counter concludes, is a metaphor wearing the dress of an explanation, and the prudent reading of 2014 is the modest one: a strong campaign, a divided opposition, an unpopular incumbent government, and a first-past-the-post system generous to the largest party.

A second counter, quieter and in some ways harder, accepts the mechanism and disputes its novelty. On this reading, personal authority over a party is a recurring Indian condition rather than a structural signature of two verdicts: Jawaharlal Nehru's authority over the Congress of the 1950s, on this view, was of the same kind, and the verdicts of 1952, 1957 and 1962 were plebiscites before the word was applied to 1971. The objection usefully forces a distinction. Nehru's authority operated inside a party that was itself the system's central institution, staffed by leaders with independent bases, state governments of their own, and a collective claim on the national movement's inheritance; the settlement of his era, the baseline this series has used throughout, distributed authority across those institutions and survived its maker by design. The authority of 1971 and of 2014 operated in the opposite condition: the party as independent institution had thinned, by expulsion and reconstruction in the one case, by the movement's deliberate subordination of the party's collective leadership to the candidate in the other, and the leader's person carried functions the institution no longer performed. The recurrence the counter identifies is therefore real but incomplete. Personal leadership is a constant of Indian politics. Personal authority substituting for institutional depth, and being ratified by a majority in that substitution, is the rarer configuration, and it is the configuration the analogue names.

One further difference between the two verdicts runs through the federal structure, and the analogue must carry it honestly. The Congress(R) of 1971 was also the governing party in most of the states, heir to a quarter century in which the same formation held the centre and the provinces, so that the mandate's centralisation met no organised federal resistance of consequence. The BJP of May 2014 held a minority of state governments: Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Rajasthan, Gujarat and Goa were its principal holdings on counting day, while the Congress, the regional parties and the Left held the rest, including the whole of the south except Karnataka's recent loss. A mandate of the 2014 kind therefore faced a federal field its 1971 counterpart never faced, and its centralising form had to work through negotiation with state governments it did not own, through the goods and services tax council's structured bargaining and through the electoral calendar of the states. The sequel is part of the record: in the assembly elections that followed, from Maharashtra and Haryana in October 2014 through the contests of the following four years, the BJP converted its national majority into state governments across most of the map, so that the federal condition of 1971, one formation astride both levels, was reconstructed by instalments. The difference between the verdicts is real. It is a difference in the mandate's starting conditions, not in the mechanism that assembled it, and the instalment reconstruction is itself evidence for the structural reading: the majority behaved, federally, as a majority of the personal-authority kind behaves, treating the states as territory to be brought under the same command rather than as partners in a settled division.

The counter is right about the conditions and wrong about what the analogue claims. An analogue of mechanism is not an identity of conditions, and the three-component base is specified at the level where the two elections genuinely are alike: the configuration by which a majority was assembled in a system that had spent years, in 1971 four years since the Congress dominance cracked in 1967, in 2014 twenty-five years since 1989, demonstrating that such assembly was impossible. The counter's own evidence strengthens the point it attacks. Precisely because the 2014 materials were unlike those of 1971, the recurrence of the configuration carries information: the same relational pattern, leader-centred authority, a rebuilt party instrument, a collapsing rival, assembled a majority out of a market economy and a caste-fragmented field just as it had assembled one out of a state-led economy and a Congress-system field. Mechanisms earn their standing by surviving changes of material. What the analogue cannot do is predict policy content, and this article claims no such thing for it. Indira Gandhi's majority moved toward bank nationalisation's consolidation, the Forty-Second Amendment of 1976 and the Emergency of 1975 to 1977. The ideological direction of the 2014 majority is set by its own party's commitments, not by 1971. What the analogue predicts is form: that a mandate built on personal authority over a thinned institutional field will govern by centralisation, will treat intermediate institutions as obstacles or instruments, and will test the constitutional furniture harder than a coalition mandate can. On that prediction the evidence since May 2014 is the subject of the adjudication and the closing sections.

## The Adjudication: What the Scholarship Settles and What It Leaves Open

The named disagreement this article adjudicates is between the coalition-era readings of 2014, which treat the verdict as the last and largest event of the post-1989 system, and the inflection reading, which treats it as the first event of a successor system whose mechanism repeats 1971. The coalition-era readings have distinguished holders. Suhas Palshikar, writing in the Economic and Political Weekly in the weeks after the counting of May 2014, cautioned against reading the result as a settled ideological transformation and stressed the contingency of the mandate, its dependence on the incumbent government's discredit and on the opposition's fragmentation, a caution continuous with the 2004 tradition in which verdicts are sectoral judgements on incumbents. The caution is the strongest form of the 1998-or-2004 family of readings: it holds that a 31 percent majority is a coalition-era artefact, assembled by the arithmetic it appears to abolish, and liable to dissolve back into that arithmetic when the contingencies pass. Chhibber and Verma's Ideology and Identity (2018) complicates the picture from within the evidence, arguing on survey data that Indian politics had been reorganising around two ideological axes, the politics of statism and the politics of recognition, and that the BJP's rise expressed a durable shift in the second axis rather than a passing anti-incumbency wave. Jaffrelot's Modi's India (2021) presses further, reading the post-2014 order as an ethnic democracy in construction, in which the majoritarian content of the mandate is the substance and the majority is its instrument.

Measured against the primary evidence assembled in this article, the readings divide by what they explain. The contingency reading explains the vote share and cannot explain the seat structure's consequences, because a government holding 282 seats does not govern contingently whatever the share that produced it, and the first term's record of centralised decision, from the demonetisation of 8 November 2016 to the goods and services tax of 1 July 2017, is not the record of an artefact awaiting dissolution. The ideological readings explain the mandate's content and direction, and the evidence of the campaign supports them on the second axis of recognition. The inflection reading defended here explains the mechanism that both families of rivals leave unexamined: why this electorate, in this fragmented field, delivered a single-party majority at all, and why the majority it delivered behaves structurally as the 1971 majority behaved, concentrating decision in the leader's office regardless of the ideology the office serves. The verdict of this article is that the 1971 analogue stands, with the discipline the counter imposed: it is an analogue of assembly and of governmental form, not of programme. The 2014 verdict's structural analogue is 1971, not 1998 or 2004, and the post-Mandal arithmetic broke on a personal-authority-plus-rival-collapse base.

Palshikar's wider body of work on the period adds a state-level refinement that the inflection reading should absorb rather than resist. His studies of Maharashtra politics across these years traced the BJP's construction of an expanded social coalition, in which the party reached beyond its upper-caste base to recruit leadership and votes among the Other Backward Classes and the smaller caste clusters that the dominant-caste parties of the Mandal era had treated as clients. Read at state level, the mechanism generalises the national claim: the post-Mandal arithmetic broke not because caste ceased to organise voting but because the BJP reorganised the caste field under a national sign, outbidding the caste parties for the allegiance of the groups those parties had left at their coalitions' edges. The point matters for durability. A majority assembled by defeating the caste parties at their own grammar is not exposed to the simple restoration of those parties in the way a majority assembled by a passing mood would be, and the 2019 result, examined in the next section, is consistent with the stronger claim.

The survey evidence behind the ideological readings deserves its weight in the adjudication, because it disciplines both sides. The National Election Study conducted by the Centre for the Study of Developing Societies around the 2014 counting recorded a substantial bloc of BJP voters naming the party's prime ministerial candidate as their reason for the choice, a finding the study's analysts treated as the first clear measurement of a leader effect at national scale in the survey series running back to the 1990s. Chhibber and Verma's interpretation of the same electoral moment, that the axes of competition were themselves moving, rests on the finding that voters' positions on the role of the state and on the recognition of religious and caste identities sorted the parties more cleanly in 2014 than in 2009. Neither finding assists the contingency reading. A mandate produced by a measurable leader effect riding a measurable ideological sorting is many things, but it is not an accident of incumbency fatigue; and the Palshikar caution, properly understood, is aimed at the durability of the arrangement rather than at its reality, which relocates the disagreement to the ground the closing section occupies, the question of what such a mandate does with the years it commands.

## The First Test: The Analogue Meets the 2019 Verdict

An inflection reading makes a falsifiable promise: if 2014 changed the operating rules, the next general election should be fought under the new rules rather than the old. The election of April and May 2019, counted on 23 May 2019, is the first test, and it falls inside this article's horizon as a matter of chronology, though the full anatomy of that verdict belongs to its own treatment in the pillar that follows. The Election Commission's statistical report on the 2019 election records the BJP at 303 seats on 37.4 percent of the national vote, an increase on both measures over 2014, with the Congress at 52 seats. The structural features the analogue predicts are the features the count displays. The campaign was again a referendum on the incumbent leader, fought by the BJP as a presidential contest in a parliamentary system and by an opposition whose pooling arrangements, concluded in some states and absent in others, never produced a single counter-answer to the question the campaign asked. The Hindi-belt consolidation held, the gains in the east extended, and the rival's organisation, five years after the collapse this article has documented, had not rebuilt to the point of contesting the translation machinery's operation. A coalition-era system would have punished a government that had carried out the demonetisation of 2016 and the tax reconstruction of 2017 with the restoration of arithmetic. The 2019 count restored nothing. The majority grew. For the purposes of the present argument the result functions as the analogue's corroboration rather than as a new subject: the operating rules visible in 2014 were still the operating rules five years later, which is what calling 2014 an inflection, rather than an event, asserts.

The geography of the 2019 count adds the detail the summary conceals. The Election Commission's report records the BJP rising to 18 of West Bengal's 42 seats, the state whose regional incumbent had broken the wave in 2014, and to 8 of Odisha's 21, while holding Karnataka at 25 of 28 and returning 62 of Uttar Pradesh's 80 on a higher vote share than the sweep of 2014 had required. The translation machinery, in other words, operated in 2019 on a wider map with the same differential logic: it advanced where the opposing organisation had decayed and was checked where a state incumbent's machinery stood intact, as in Tamil Nadu and Kerala, where the BJP's tally remained marginal. A wave in the journalistic sense, a mood, would have receded uniformly or not at all. A structure behaves as the 2019 map behaved, extending along the lines of organisational weakness the 2014 verdict had mapped. The distinction is the one this article has pressed throughout, and the second verdict's service to the argument is that it was delivered by an electorate that had experienced five years of the majority's governance, including its most contested decisions, and answered the referendum question in the same terms as the first.

## What the Inflection Sets in Motion

A closing pillar article owes its reader the forward account, and [the coalition-era renegotiation framework](/2019/05/15/coalition-era-1989-2014-explained/) supplies the terms: the quarter century from 1989 to 2014 was the period in which the founding settlement's tensions were managed by arithmetic, deferred by every government that coalition discipline produced. A majority of the 2014 kind is under no such discipline, and the first term's record reads as the analogue predicts. The demonetisation of November 2016, announced by the Prime Minister on national television on the evening of 8 November, was a decision of the centre taken in a circle so small that the cabinet was informed as it was executed. The demonetisation's own record illustrates the form's operation and its costs. The decision withdrew legal-tender status from the 500 and 1000 rupee notes that constituted about 86 percent of the currency in circulation by value, and the Reserve Bank of India's annual report for 2017 and 2018 eventually recorded that 99.3 percent of the withdrawn value had returned to the banking system, a figure the government's critics read as the measure of the exercise's failure against its stated aims and its defenders read as the cost of formalising the economy. The adjudication of aims belongs to the decision's own record. The form is what concerns this article: a decision of that magnitude, taken by surprise, defended afterwards as a test of national endurance, and carried politically without the parliamentary negotiation a coalition cabinet would have required before the announcement rather than after it. The goods and services tax, launched on 1 July 2017 under the Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act of 2016, rewrote the fiscal federalism of the founding settlement through a council in which the centre's weight is structural. The pattern extends, on the directing brief's own evidence scope, to the decisions that stand at the head of the succeeding pillar's record: the Article 370 decision of 5 August 2019 and the citizenship legislation of December 2019, which are treated in their own right in the articles that follow this one and are named here only as the trajectory's continuation, the point at which the renegotiation the 2014 mandate enabled reaches the religion and state tension directly. Each of these acts was contested at the strongest level by its critics and defended at the strongest level by the government, and the characterisations of motive on both sides belong to the record of those disputes. The structural point, which is this article's, is prior to the disputes: none of these decisions was available to a government of the coalition kind, because each required a parliamentary position that arithmetic could not veto. The first term's early institutional skirmish makes the same point in miniature and adds the necessary complication. The Constitution (Ninety-Ninth Amendment) Act and the National Judicial Appointments Commission Act, passed by Parliament in August 2014 with near-unanimous support, attempted to rewrite the appointments architecture of the higher judiciary, the most consequential institutional inheritance of the post-Emergency settlement. A Constitution bench of the Supreme Court struck the arrangement down on 16 October 2015 by a majority of four to one. The episode shows the freedom and its boundary in a single sequence: a majority of the 2014 construction reached, within months, for an institutional settlement that no coalition government could have attempted, and the one institution the majority did not control answered with the Constitution. The analogue predicts the reaching. It does not predict that every reach succeeds, and the post-2014 era's history is written in the alternation.

The 1971 majority had used the same freedom to amend the Constitution twenty-four times in five years and to carry the republic into the Emergency. The majority's institutional signature appeared in the House itself within weeks. The Congress, at 44 seats, fell below the one-tenth of the House that the governing directions require for the recognition of a Leader of the Opposition, and the Speaker, Sumitra Mahajan, declined to recognise any holder of the office for the sixteenth Lok Sabha, leaving the chamber without the office for the first time since the coalition era's conventions had made it a fixture. The vacancy was not a technicality. The Leader of the Opposition sits by statute on the selection committees for the Central Vigilance Commissioner, the Central Bureau of Investigation's director and the Lokpal, and the government's legislative response, amending statutes to substitute the leader of the largest opposition party where no recognised holder existed, recorded in law the new condition: an opposition present as parties and absent as an institution. The 1971 House had known the same vacancy in substance after the Grand Alliance's rout, when no opposition formation approached the threshold of an alternative government. A system in which the opposition cannot constitute itself as an institution is a system in which the majority's internal discipline becomes the principal check on the majority, and the history of both eras is in part the history of that check's strength.

The freedom is the inheritance. What a majority does with it is the history of the era that the next pillar records, and the analogue's last service is to warn the reader of that era against the coalition era's habits of expectation. Majorities of this construction do not manage tensions. They settle them, or try to, and the settlement attempt is the story from 2014 onward. The pillar that follows takes up that story decision by decision, with the verdict of May 2014 as its premise and its explanation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the 2014 Indian general election?

The 2014 Indian general election was the sixteenth general election to the Lok Sabha, held in nine phases from 7 April to 12 May 2014, with counting on 16 May 2014. The Election Commission of India administered voting across an electorate of roughly 834 million, the largest recorded to that date, and turnout reached 66.38 percent, the highest in Indian general election history at the time. The election removed the Congress-led United Progressive Alliance after two terms and returned the Bharatiya Janata Party with 282 seats, the first single-party majority since 1984, installing Narendra Modi as Prime Minister on 26 May 2014.

### Q: How did Narendra Modi win in 2014?

Narendra Modi won in 2014 by converting a party election into a referendum on his candidacy. Named the BJP's prime ministerial candidate on 13 September 2013 after the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh and the party settled the leadership question, he campaigned for nine months as the election's single subject, addressing hundreds of public meetings and using devices such as the Chai pe Charcha satellite conversations of February 2014. Behind the candidacy stood the BJP and Sangh cadre organisation, maintained through the opposition decade after 2004 and rebuilt after 2009, which converted the personal wave into constituency-level seat gains, above all in Uttar Pradesh under Amit Shah's management.

### Q: Why did the Congress lose so badly in 2014?

The Congress lost in 2014 because organisational decay met a scandal cycle at the moment of electoral testing. The party had never rebuilt its state units in Uttar Pradesh and Bihar after the 1990s, its district structures had thinned into patronage queues, and its leadership offer in 2014 combined a retiring prime minister, a president identified with its past, and a vice president, Rahul Gandhi, who was not named its prime ministerial candidate. Over this base fell the Commonwealth Games prosecutions, the Comptroller and Auditor General's 2G spectrum report of November 2010, the Anna Hazare mobilisation of 2011, and the state defeats of late 2013. The count recorded the result: 44 seats.

### Q: What was the 2014 BJP majority?

The 2014 BJP majority was 282 seats in a Lok Sabha of 543 elected members, ten seats above the 272 required to form a government alone, as recorded in the Election Commission of India's Statistical Report on the General Election of 2014. The party achieved it on 31.0 percent of the national vote, a share made sufficient by the fragmentation of the opposition vote among regional, caste-based and national parties. The wider National Democratic Alliance held 336 seats, but the defining fact of the verdict is the single-party figure: no party had crossed the majority line on its own since the Congress victory of 1984.

### Q: How is the 2014 verdict different from 1998 or 2004?

The 2014 verdict differs from 1998 and 2004 in the structure of the mandate it produced. The 1998 verdict gave the BJP 182 seats and a government rented from the National Democratic Alliance, whose National Agenda for Governance shelved the party's core commitments and whose withdrawal politics ended the government in April 1999 by one vote. The 2004 verdict gave the Congress 145 seats and a decade of coalition management under the United Progressive Alliance, including outside support from the Left Front until the rupture of 2008. The 2014 verdict gave one party 282 seats and a government that owed its numbers to no ally, which is the property it shares with 1971 rather than with either coalition-era verdict.

### Q: Did the coalition era end in 2014?

The coalition era ended in 2014 in the strict arithmetical sense that defines it. From 1989 to 2009, seven consecutive general elections left every party short of a Lok Sabha majority, and government formation was in every case an act of coalition architecture. The 2014 verdict broke the sequence by returning the BJP with 282 seats, a majority independent of allies. Whether coalition habits ended with the arithmetic is a separate question, since the BJP continued to govern within the National Democratic Alliance's form and coalition politics persisted in the states. The era defined by the impossibility of a single-party majority, however, closed on 16 May 2014.

### Q: Is the 2014 mandate similar to the 1971 mandate?

The 2014 mandate is similar to the 1971 mandate in its base structure, which is the comparison this article defends. In 1971 Indira Gandhi's Congress(R) won 352 of 518 seats on a campaign that made the election a referendum on her leadership, using a party organisation rebuilt under her control after the split of November 1969, against a Congress(O) whose organisation collapsed at the test. In 2014 the same three components recur: personal authority in Narendra Modi's candidacy, organisational reach in the BJP and Sangh machinery, and rival collapse in the Congress fall to 44 seats. The ideologies and the historical conditions differ profoundly. The mechanism of assembly is the same.

### Q: What share of the national vote did the BJP win in 2014?

The BJP won 31.0 percent of the national vote in 2014, as recorded in the Election Commission of India's statistical report, the highest vote share the party had achieved in a general election to that date and a comparatively low share for a majority government. The Congress, in its majorities of 1971 and 1980, had polled above 40 percent. The 2014 figure illustrates the mechanics of the post-Mandal field: with the remaining vote divided among the Congress at 19.3 percent and a field of regional and caste-based parties, the first-past-the-post system converted a concentrated plurality into 282 seats.

### Q: How many seats did the National Democratic Alliance win in 2014?

The National Democratic Alliance won 336 seats in 2014, of which the BJP itself held 282 and its allies 54, including 18 for the Shiv Sena in Maharashtra, 16 for the Telugu Desam Party in Andhra Pradesh, six for the Lok Janshakti Party and three for the Rashtriya Lok Samata Party in Bihar, and two for Apna Dal in Uttar Pradesh. The alliance total mattered less than its composition. In 1998 and 1999 the allies' seats had been the government's condition of existence. In 2014 the BJP could have formed a government without a single allied seat, which reversed the power relation between the majority party and its alliance for the first time in the coalition era's history.

### Q: Who was India's Prime Minister when the 2014 election was held?

Manmohan Singh was India's Prime Minister when the 2014 election was held, completing the second term of the Congress-led United Progressive Alliance government that had taken office in May 2009. Singh, an economist who had served as finance minister in the government of P.V. Narasimha Rao from 1991 to 1996, led the Congress to its 2009 result of 206 seats and then through the scandal cycle of 2010 to 2013, including the 2G spectrum prosecutions and the Commonwealth Games cases. He did not lead the Congress campaign of 2014 as its prime ministerial candidate, and he left office on 26 May 2014, the day Narendra Modi was sworn in.

### Q: What role did Amit Shah play in the BJP campaign of 2014?

Amit Shah, a Gujarat politician and close associate of Narendra Modi, was appointed the BJP general secretary in charge of Uttar Pradesh in June 2013 and managed the party's campaign in the state that returned eighty Lok Sabha members. His operation built party committees down to the polling booth across the state, handled caste arithmetic constituency by constituency, and managed the alliance with Apna Dal that added two seats to the party's own 71. Uttar Pradesh supplied the largest single bloc of the 282-seat majority, and the result made Shah's reputation as the party's principal election manager. He became BJP national president in July 2014.

### Q: When were the results of the 2014 general election declared?

The results of the 2014 general election were declared on 16 May 2014, when counting took place across the country after the ninth and final phase of voting on 12 May. The scale of the BJP victory was clear by the afternoon of counting day: the party led in over 280 constituencies, the Congress fell below fifty, and the incumbent United Progressive Alliance conceded defeat. Narendra Modi, who had won both Vadodara and Varanasi, addressed supporters in Gujarat that evening and in Varanasi the following day. He resigned the Vadodara seat, was elected leader of the BJP parliamentary party on 20 May, and was sworn in as Prime Minister on 26 May 2014.

### Q: What happened to the Bahujan Samaj Party in the 2014 election?

The Bahujan Samaj Party won no Lok Sabha seats in 2014, the most striking single result in Uttar Pradesh after the BJP's 71. The party of Mayawati, founded by Kanshi Ram in 1984 to organise Dalit political assertion, had won 20 of the state's 80 seats in 2009 and a majority in the Uttar Pradesh assembly in 2007, and it retained a substantial vote share in 2014 while converting none of it into seats under the first-past-the-post system. The result demonstrated the post-Mandal arithmetic's reversal: a durable caste-based vote bank, which for two decades had guaranteed the party a bloc of seats, delivered none when the opposing vote consolidated behind a single rival.

### Q: Which state gave the BJP its largest bloc of seats in 2014?

Uttar Pradesh gave the BJP its largest bloc of seats in 2014, returning 71 of its 80 members for the party, with two more for its ally Apna Dal. The scale of the reversal is visible against the state's recent record: the BJP had won 10 seats in Uttar Pradesh in 2009, the Samajwadi Party had won the state assembly election of 2012, and the Bahujan Samaj Party had held 20 Lok Sabha seats from the state. The 2014 campaign in Uttar Pradesh, managed by Amit Shah from June 2013, combined the national Modi candidacy with booth-level organisation and caste-by-caste constituency management, and it converted 42.6 percent of the state's vote into nearly nine tenths of its seats.

### Q: What was the Chai pe Charcha programme of 2014?

Chai pe Charcha, discussion over tea, was a campaign programme run by the BJP in February 2014 in which Narendra Modi addressed gatherings at tea stalls across hundreds of locations simultaneously through satellite and video links, beginning with an address from Ahmedabad on 12 February 2014. The programme converted Modi's biography, including his family's tea stall at Vadnagar railway station, into an organising device against the Congress's characterisation of him, and it carried the campaign into the smallest commercial spaces of Indian towns. It is remembered as an emblem of the 2014 campaign's method: a single leader's persona distributed by technology into localities the party organisation alone could not have saturated.

### Q: From which constituencies did Narendra Modi contest the 2014 election?

Narendra Modi contested the 2014 election from two constituencies, Vadodara in Gujarat and Varanasi in Uttar Pradesh, and won both. In Vadodara he defeated the Congress candidate Madhusudan Mistry by a margin of about 570,000 votes, among the largest of the election. In Varanasi he defeated Arvind Kejriwal of the Aam Aadmi Party by about 371,000 votes, in a contest that drew national attention because Varanasi placed the BJP's candidate at the symbolic centre of the Hindi belt whose consolidation the campaign required. Under the law permitting a candidate to retain only one seat, Modi resigned Vadodara after the results and represented Varanasi in the sixteenth Lok Sabha.

### Q: What was the Samajwadi Party's result in Uttar Pradesh in 2014?

The Samajwadi Party won five seats in Uttar Pradesh in 2014, a collapse for the party that governed the state under Akhilesh Yadav, who had become chief minister after the assembly election of 2012. All five of its winners came from the extended family network of its founder Mulayam Singh Yadav, who himself won from both Mainpuri and Azamgarh. The party's Yadav and Muslim coalition, the foundation of its strength since its founding in 1992, could not withstand the consolidation of the opposing vote behind the BJP across the state. The result, read beside the Bahujan Samaj Party's failure to win any seat, marked the breaking of the post-Mandal party system in its home state.

### Q: What did Christophe Jaffrelot argue about the 2014 verdict in Modi's India?

Christophe Jaffrelot, in Modi's India: Hindu Nationalism and the Rise of Ethnic Democracy, published in 2021, argued that the 2014 verdict installed a populist leadership style in which the leader claims to embody the people directly, and that the governments formed after 2014 set about converting India toward an ethnic democracy in which the Hindu majority's precedence is asserted across institutions and citizenship. His reading treats the majoritarian content of the mandate as its substance and the personalisation of the campaign as its method. The structural reading defended in this article accepts his account of personalisation's mechanics while locating the verdict's distinctiveness in its three-component base, the configuration it shares with 1971.

### Q: What was the result of the 2014 election in Delhi?

The BJP won all seven of Delhi's Lok Sabha seats in 2014, completing a sweep of the capital in a result that measured the Congress collapse in the city it had governed for fifteen years under Sheila Dikshit until December 2013. The Aam Aadmi Party, contesting its first general election after winning 28 seats in the Delhi assembly election of December 2013, finished second in all seven constituencies without winning one, and the Congress finished third across the capital, losing deposits in several seats. Delhi's verdict arrived five months after the assembly result had already ended the Congress government, and it confirmed that the capital's anti-incumbent energy had transferred to the national challenger rather than to the city's own new party.

### Q: Why did the Congress not name a prime ministerial candidate in 2014?

The Congress did not name a prime ministerial candidate in 2014 because its leadership structure made the announcement impossible to make cleanly. Sonia Gandhi had led the party since 1998 and had declined the prime ministership in 2004. Rahul Gandhi, made vice president in January 2013, was the acknowledged successor and led the campaign in practice, but the party declined to project him formally against Narendra Modi, a hesitation its opponents read as protection against a personalised defeat. Manmohan Singh, the incumbent, announced in January 2014 that he would not serve a third term. The party therefore fought a presidential-style campaign, of the kind the BJP was running openly, without a named president of its own.
