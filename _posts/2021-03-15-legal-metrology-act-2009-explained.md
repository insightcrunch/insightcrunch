The Legal Metrology Act 2009 is the statute behind the most common consumer grievance in Indian retail: the feeling of having been charged too much, measured too little, or told too late what a package contained. It is also one of the most misunderstood statutes on the book. A shopper who finds a bottle of oil priced higher than an identical bottle in another shop tends to blame this Act, and a shopkeeper who is caught selling above the printed price tends to blame it too, and both are usually pointing at the wrong target. The statute does something narrower and, in its way, more demanding. It fixes the units in which India measures, it polices the instruments that do the measuring, and it prescribes what a package must say about itself before it may be sold. What it does not do, at any point, is decide what a lawful price for a kilogram of anything ought to be.

![A retail package carrying the printed maximum retail price and net quantity declarations required by the Legal Metrology Act 2009 - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The Act carries the short title of 2009 and the number of a later year, and the distinction matters for citation. Parliament enacted it as Act 1 of 2010, the first statute of that year, and it received assent on 13 January 2010. Its provisions were brought into force on 1 April 2011, and on that date it repealed and replaced the two statutes that had divided this field between them: the Standards of Weights and Measures Act, 1976, which was Act 60 of 1976, and the Standards of Weights and Measures (Enforcement) Act, 1985, which was Act 54 of 1985. In their place stands a single code of fifty-seven sections in five chapters, running from the units of measurement at one end to a long chapter of offences and penalties at the other. The distance between those two ends is the whole argument of the statute, because the offences exist almost entirely to protect the integrity of the units and of the declarations that the units make possible.

Read as a whole, the Act answers three questions that reach every household. What must a package declare before it can lawfully be sold? May a seller charge more than the price printed on that package, and if the answer is sometimes yes, in exactly which settings? And who checks, with what powers, and with what consequences for the person caught short? The wider field in which these questions sit, including the consumer commissions and the remedies that run alongside this statute, is mapped in the [complete guide to consumer law in India](/2020/11/01/consumer-law-india-complete-guide/), and this article stays inside the metrology statute itself. Its central claim can be stated in one sentence and defended through every chapter: the statute governs the declaration, not the price.

The tour that follows keeps to the statute's own order before it turns to the controversies. The standards come first, because every later rule borrows their authority; then the officers and the verification of instruments; then the package and its declarations, which is where the statute meets the citizen; then the price ceiling and the case law that bounds it; then the modern amendments, the electronic marketplace, the end of standard pack sizes and the arrival of the unit price; and finally the offences, in both of their settlements, the one Parliament enacted in 2009 and the one it substituted in part in 2023. A reader pressed for time can take the article's argument from the declarations table and the answers on the printed price. A reader who stays for the whole road will see why those answers are what they are, and why a statute about the honesty of measurement ended up deciding what a hotel may charge for a bottle of water.

## What the Legal Metrology Act 2009 governs, and what it leaves alone

The long title of the Act announces a statute to establish and enforce standards of weights and measures, to regulate trade and commerce in weights, measures and other goods which are sold or distributed by weight, measure or number, and to provide for matters connected with those subjects. Every operative provision hangs off one of those three pegs. Chapter II builds the standards themselves. Chapter III appoints the officers who guard them. Chapter IV requires the instruments of trade to be verified and stamped before use. Chapter V punishes the conduct that corrupts measurement or misdescribes a package. Section 3 gives the Act overriding effect, so that its provisions prevail over anything inconsistent in another law, in a contract, or in any instrument or document, a clause that explains why a trade usage or a shop's own printed terms cannot displace the declarations the Act requires.

The first thing the Act governs, therefore, is quantity. Section 10 requires every transaction, dealing or contract, so far as it concerns weight, measure or number, to be made in accordance with the prescribed standards, and section 30 punishes the person who delivers less than the contracted quantity or receives more than the contracted quantity through a false measure. The second thing it governs is language: the vocabulary in which quantity and price may be expressed at all. The third thing it governs is the package. Section 18 forbids the manufacture, packing, sale, import, distribution, delivery, offer, exposure or possession for sale of any pre-packaged commodity unless it is packed in standard quantities and bears the declarations the rules prescribe. Between them, quantity, language and the package exhaust the statute's ambition. Price, as a number chosen by a seller, is not in the list.

That omission is deliberate, and it is the point on which public discussion of the Act most often goes wrong. Indian law does control prices, but it does so in other statutes built for that purpose. The Constitution itself separates the subjects: price control is Entry 34 of the Concurrent List, a distinct entry from the weights and measures entries, and the statute that works that entry for essential goods is the Essential Commodities Act, 1955, whose scheme of control orders and notified prices is examined in the [guide to the Essential Commodities Act 1955](/2014/03/01/essential-commodities-act-1955-guide/). A price fixed under a control order binds because that order says so. A maximum retail price printed on a packet binds for a different reason: because this Act requires the packet to declare a maximum, and forbids a sale above the declaration. The ceiling is real, but it is a ceiling the manufacturer printed, not one the State computed. A seller who prints a modest maximum and sells at it has complied with the Act however the market judges the bargain, and a seller who prints a generous maximum has equally complied. The complaint that a product is simply expensive is a complaint this statute cannot hear, because nothing in it asks whether the declared maximum was a fair figure when it was chosen.

What the Act hears instead is a family of precise complaints. The quantity delivered was less than the quantity declared. The instrument that weighed the goods had never been verified, or had been altered. The package omitted a declaration, or carried two different maximum prices, or was sold above the one maximum it carried. The advertisement quoted a price without stating the net quantity the price bought. Each of these is a failure of measurement or of declaration, and each maps onto a section. The discipline of the statute is that it refuses to convert a grievance about value into a grievance about measure, and insists that only the second kind is its business. That refusal is not a gap in the law. It is the design, and understanding it is the difference between using the Act well and invoking it to no purpose.

Section 2 supplies the definitions on which the design rests, and two of them do most of the work. A pre-packaged commodity is a commodity which, without the purchaser being present, is placed in a package of whatever nature, whether sealed or not, so that the product contained in it has a pre-determined quantity. The definition turns on the sequence of events: packing first, in the buyer's absence, with the quantity settled before the buyer arrives. A sale, correspondingly, is defined by the transfer of property in goods for a price, and the boundary of that definition, tested in the hotel and restaurant litigation examined later in this article, marks the outer edge of the price ceiling. Around those two definitions the Act arranges its cast: the manufacturer who makes, the packer who packs a commodity made by another, the importer who brings goods or instruments into India, the dealer who trades, and the legal metrology officers who inspect them all.

## The vocabulary of the Act: definitions that do the work

A statute of this kind lives in its definitions section, and section 2 repays the reader who lingers. Beyond the pre-packaged commodity and the sale, four definitions organise the obligations of the whole Act. A manufacturer is a person who makes or manufactures any weight, measure or commodity, including the person who makes a part of it. A packer is a person who packs a commodity made by another, and the separate existence of the word in the Act is a small history of Indian manufacturing, in which the firm whose name is on the packet very often did not make what the packet holds; the Act therefore names both, and makes both answerable for the declarations, so that a brand cannot hide behind its contractor nor a contractor behind the brand. An importer is the person who brings goods or instruments into India from outside, and stands in the manufacturer's shoes for everything the Act requires, which is why the country of origin declaration and the registration of instrument importers fall where they do. A dealer is the person who buys or sells weights, measures or commodities for trade, and the word carries the licensing and selling offences that attach to the trade counter.

Two further definitions shape the enforcement chapters. A legal metrology officer is the officer appointed under the Director's or the Controller's chain, the person on whom the inspection, verification and seizure powers converge. And the Act's use of the word protection, in the requirement that an instrument used in a transaction or for protection be verified, extends the verification net beyond buying and selling to the instruments used to safeguard persons and property, the weighs and gauges whose truth matters even where no sale takes place. The breadth is characteristic. The Act is not drafted as a shopkeepers' statute with a few technical sections attached; it is drafted as the law of measurement in trade and protection generally, of which the packaged commodity, for all its public prominence, is one regulated instance.

One more reach of the Act is habitually overlooked: services. The statute speaks of goods sold or distributed by weight, measure or number, and its offence in section 35 punishes the rendering of services by non-standard weight, measure or number. The laundry weighed by the kilogram, the consignment charged by its weight, the cloth measured for the tailor and the hire charged by the measured distance all stand inside the Act's world when they are quantified, because the mischief is identical whether the measure sells a thing or prices a service: the customer pays by a number the customer cannot check. Section 34 and section 35 are drafted as twins for exactly this reason, the one for commodities delivered short, the other for services rendered short, and the parity explains why the Jan Vishwas ladders substituted into the two sections in 2023 are identical. A reader who grasps that the Act governs quantified exchange, and not merely packaged goods, has the measure of its full territory.

## A constitutional split: national standards, shared enforcement

The architecture of the Act, with a Director appointed by the Central Government and Controllers appointed by the State Governments, is not an administrative preference. It reproduces a division written into the Seventh Schedule of the Constitution. Entry 50 of the Union List gives Parliament the establishment of standards of weight and measure, and it gives that subject to the Union alone. Entry 33A of the Concurrent List gives Parliament and the State Legislatures together the subject of weights and measures except the establishment of standards. The framers separated the unit from its policing. What a kilogram is, no State may decide for itself, because a kilogram that changed at a State border would dissolve the single market the Constitution elsewhere builds. How the kilogram is enforced in the markets of a State, by contrast, is a subject on which both levels of government may legislate, and in practice the daily work of enforcement has settled on the States.

The Act honours the split in its own structure. The standards chapters are central: the base units, the manner of numeration and the hierarchy of reference standards are set by or under the authority of the Central Government, and the rule-making power in section 52, which carries the packaged commodities regime, is a Central power. Enforcement is deliberately two-tiered. Under section 13, the Central Government appoints a Director of Legal Metrology for the enforcement of the Act in relation to inter-State trade and commerce, and under section 14 each State Government appoints a Controller for intra-State trade and commerce, with legal metrology officers working under each. A consignment moving between States answers to the Centre's chain; the weighing scale in a neighbourhood shop answers to the State's. The same split appears in the appellate structure of section 50 and in the compounding allocations of section 48, where the Director and the Controller are given overlapping but distinct lists of offences each may settle.

Section 53 completes the federal picture by empowering State Governments, after consultation with the Central Government, to make rules for the matters closest to the ground: the time within which an instrument may be got verified after seizure, the registers to be maintained, the form and fees of licences, and the fees for verification and stamping. This is why the practical experience of legal metrology varies by State even though the kilogram does not. The intervals at which a trader's weights fall due for re-verification, the fees charged at the verification camp, and the licence conditions for the repairer who services the scale are State-made law under a Central statute, and a business trading in several States holds several slightly different compliance calendars under one unchanging system of units. The constitutional design accepts that untidiness as the price of the arrangement: uniformity where uniformity is the substance, local administration where administration is the substance.

There is a further consequence of the Concurrent List placement that litigants sometimes miss. Because weights and measures, short of the establishment of standards, is a concurrent subject, a State law on enforcement is not displaced merely because Parliament has legislated; the two operate together unless a direct conflict engages the repugnancy rule of Article 254. The 2009 Act was built on that assumption. It repealed a Central standards statute and a Central enforcement statute and replaced them with one code that assigns the States their officers, their rule-making field and their appellate authorities within its own four corners, rather than leaving enforcement to whatever each State chose to enact. The result is a rare thing in Indian consumer law: a single statute whose text, read carefully, tells the reader which level of government answers for each function, from the definition of the metre to the fee for stamping a balance.

The split can be watched at a single counter. At a fair price shop distributing grain under the public distribution system, two different statutes supervise two different aspects of the same transaction. The price at which the grain is issued is a controlled price, fixed under the essential commodities regime that Entry 34 supports, and a complaint about that price belongs to that world. The scale on which the grain is weighed, its verification stamp, the quantity actually delivered against the quantity charged for, and the declarations on any pre-packed commodity sold across the same counter belong to legal metrology, and a complaint about them belongs to the Controller. Traders who work under both regimes sometimes experience them as one inspectorate of the State; legally they are distinct systems that happen to meet at the till, and the complainant who separates the price grievance from the measure grievance has taken the first step toward the remedy each system can actually give. That separation, repeated across every market in the country, is the constitutional architecture of the Seventh Schedule translated into administrative routine.

## From two statutes to one: the road to the 2009 Act

India adopted a uniform system of weights and measures founded on the metric system in 1956, and the legislative expression of that choice matured in two stages. The Standards of Weights and Measures Act, 1976 restated the standards themselves on the basis of the international system of units and built the central machinery of standardisation. A decade later, the Standards of Weights and Measures (Enforcement) Act, 1985 supplied the enforcement half: the State-level apparatus of Controllers and inspectors, verification and stamping, and the offences through which the standards were to be protected in daily trade. The Packaged Commodities Rules of 1977, made under the earlier dispensation, carried the labelling regime for goods sold in packed form. For a generation, anyone advising on a weighing dispute or a defective label had to work across both statutes and the rules under each, and to know which of the two Acts a given power or penalty lived in.

The division had a logic when it was made, mirroring the constitutional split between establishing standards and enforcing them, but it exacted a steady price in duplication and doubt. Definitions sat in one Act and consequences in the other. The packaged commodity, which by the turn of the century had become the form in which most urban Indians bought most manufactured goods, was regulated largely through rules under a statute whose main text was about weights and measures in the abstract. Amendments had to be carried in pairs. The case for consolidation, pressed through the years in which India's packaged goods market expanded beyond anything the 1976 Parliament had pictured, was that measurement law should read as one code: standards, officers, instruments, packages and penalties in a single sequence that a trader, an inspector and a magistrate could each follow from beginning to end.

### Why were the Acts of 1976 and 1985 replaced by a single statute?

The two Acts split one subject into a standards code and an enforcement code, so a single dispute could require both books, and the packaged goods regime that mattered most to consumers lived mainly in rules. The 2009 Act fused standards, officers, verification, package declarations and penalties into one sequence of fifty-seven sections in a single text.

The Legal Metrology Act, 2009 is that fusion. It took over the standards chapters of the 1976 Act, the enforcement machinery of the 1985 Act, and the concerns of the packaged commodities rules, and it added what the older code had lacked: an express model approval regime for instruments, a registration regime for importers of weights and measures, a statutory institute for training the service, and a nominated-person mechanism for offences by companies. Section 57 performs the repeal in terms, naming the Standards of Weights and Measures Act, 1976 (60 of 1976) and the Standards of Weights and Measures (Enforcement) Act, 1985 (54 of 1985), and the sections around it manage the transition with care. Section 57 itself preserves, through the General Clauses Act, 1897 (10 of 1897) and its own sub-sections, the notifications, rules, licences, registrations and approvals in force under the repealed Acts, which continue as if made under the corresponding provisions of the new Act. Section 56 protects the incumbents: every Director, Controller and legal metrology officer appointed before the new rules commenced is deemed appointed under sections 13 and 14 notwithstanding any new qualification prescribed, and the rules a State had made under the 1985 Act remain in force until that State replaces them. A repeal, in other words, that changed the book without emptying the offices or cancelling the licences issued under the old one.

Commencement was by appointment, as section 1(3) permits, and the appointed day for the Act was 1 April 2011. From that date the two predecessor statutes ceased to govern, and the single code, together with the fresh rulebooks made under it, of which the Legal Metrology (Packaged Commodities) Rules, 2011 are the most consequential for consumers, became the complete statement of the subject. The point is worth holding onto when reading older case law: decisions under the 1976 and 1985 Acts remain instructive where the language carried over, as the Supreme Court's treatment of the two codes together in the hotel litigation shows, but the provisions to be applied are the provisions of the 2009 Act.

The prehistory explains why uniformity had to be legislated at all. Before the metric turn, India measured in a profusion of local units: the seer and the maund for weight, the tola for precious metals, the gaz for length, varying in magnitude from district to district and sometimes from market to market, so that the same word named different quantities on opposite sides of a river. Such a vocabulary served local trade and defeated national trade, and it made systematic fraud difficult to prove because the unit itself was contestable. The uniform metric system adopted in 1956 was therefore as much a commercial reform as a scientific one, and the legislative sequence that followed, standards in 1976, enforcement in 1985, consolidation in 2009, is the long tail of that single decision. Section 12's voiding of contrary customs is the 2009 Act closing the door the 1956 reform opened: the old units survive as folklore and as the names of quantities in speech, but no transaction, contract or record may be built on them.

Nor should the 1985 half of the inheritance be undervalued. The standards of 1976 would have remained a text in search of a machinery had the Enforcement Act not created the State apparatus of Controllers and inspectors, the verification and stamping cycle, and the offences that gave the standards a presence in the market. The 2009 Act's enforcement chapters are best read as the 1985 machinery retained and refined, with the packaged commodity offences deepened around it. When the single code is criticised for the length of its offences chapter, the history supplies the answer: the chapter is long because it absorbed an entire second statute, and the alternative tried for three decades was a law of measurement in two books that practitioners had to hold open at once.

The declarations regime has its own lineage within that history. The duty to print the sale price on the packet entered Indian law through the Standards of Weights and Measures (Packaged Commodities) Rules, 1977, made under the 1976 Act, which first obliged the packer to declare the identity of the commodity, its quantity and its sale price on the label, and the definition of a pre-packed commodity litigated in Whirlpool was a definition of those Rules. When the 2009 Act carried the subject into its section 18 and the Rules of 2011 rebuilt the panel, the concept that travelled intact across all three generations was the printed price as the buyer's protection: the notion that a packet must announce, before it is bought, the most that may be charged for it. The maximum retail price is therefore not an invention of the 2009 Act but its oldest inheritance, older than the enforcement machinery, and the litigation and reform that surround it are arguments about a device Indian law has maintained, in one vocabulary or another, for well over four decades.

## The standards architecture: sections 4 to 12

Chapter II is the foundation on which everything else in the Act stands, and it repays slow reading, because the offences in Chapter V are unintelligible without it. Section 4 declares that every unit of weight, measure or numeration shall be in accordance with the metric system based on the international system of units, the system known worldwide by its French initials as the SI. Section 5 then names the base units from which all measurement in Indian trade derives: the metre for length, the kilogram for mass, the second for time, the ampere for electric current, the kelvin for thermodynamic temperature, the candela for luminous intensity and the mole for amount of substance. Derived units and special units follow from these, prescribed under the Act, so that everything from the litre in which milk is sold to the carat by which a jeweller weighs is an authorised descendant of the same seven foundations.

Two quieter sections do work that is easy to underestimate. Section 6 requires numeration itself to follow the international form of Indian numerals and the decimal system, which is the provision that makes a declared quantity of 1.5 kilograms mean the same thing on a packet in any State and forbids the survival of local notations in trade documents. Section 12 voids any custom, usage, practice or method of whatever nature that contradicts the standards the Act prescribes, a clause aimed at the stubborn afterlife of older measures in particular trades and regions: the seer and the maund may survive in speech, but a transaction conducted in them is not a transaction the law recognises, and section 28 punishes dealing in contravention of the prescribed standards.

Section 7 empowers the prescription of the standard units of weight and measure themselves, and section 9 builds the physical hierarchy through which the paper standards become brass, steel and glass in the field. At the top stand the reference standards, kept at the national level. Below them, secondary standards are calibrated against the reference standards, and below those, the working standards against which the instruments of trade are actually tested are calibrated against the secondary standards. The chain is the legal form of a scientific idea called traceability: every verified scale in a vegetable market can, in principle, be followed up a documented chain of comparisons to the national reference, and the reference itself is maintained in alignment with the international prototypes and definitions administered through the International Organisation of Legal Metrology, the intergovernmental body through which national metrology laws are harmonised. India's participation in that system is what allows a pattern of weighing instrument approved abroad to be considered for approval in India under the proviso to section 22, and it is why the units in an Indian statute read identically to the units in the metrology statutes of other member countries.

### How are India's national standards of weight and measure maintained?

The Central Government prescribes the standard units under section 7 and keeps reference standards at the national level. Secondary standards are verified against the reference standards, and the working standards used to test traders' instruments are verified against the secondary standards, so every stamped scale traces through an unbroken chain to the national reference.

The conformity obligations bite in section 8. Every weight or measure, and every numeration, manufactured or imported for trade must conform to the standards prescribed under the Act, and section 8(4) bars the manufacture or import of a non-conforming weight or measure altogether, with exceptions for instruments made for export and for scientific research. Section 10 states the transactional rule: every transaction, dealing or contract in respect of goods, things or services by weight, measure or number must follow the prescribed standards. It is section 11, though, that reaches furthest into daily commercial life, and it deserves its reputation as the Act's most quietly radical provision.

Section 11 forbids any person from quoting or announcing a price or charge, issuing a price list, invoice, cash memo or other document, publishing an advertisement, indicating the net quantity of a pre-packaged commodity, or expressing any quantity or dimension, otherwise than in accordance with the standard units of weight, measure or numeration. An exporter is excepted, since foreign markets may demand foreign units. For everyone else, the provision abolishes the dual vocabulary of Indian commerce as a matter of law: a price may not be quoted per pound, a plot may not be measured for sale in a unit the Act does not recognise, and an advertisement may not describe a quantity in terms outside the standard system. The offence in section 29, punishing quotation or publication in non-standard units, is the teeth of the rule. Read together, sections 10 and 11 make the metric system not merely the official system but the only lawful language of quantity in Indian trade, and the declaration regime for packages, examined next in the enforcement and labelling chapters, is simply that language given a printed form.

Two features of the architecture deserve emphasis before the machinery is examined. The first is that the standards are maintained physically, not merely proclaimed. The hierarchy of section 9 is serviced by laboratories: reference standards are kept under the Central Government, and regional reference standard laboratories carry the comparison work across the country, so that the State working standards against which a trader's balance is tested are themselves periodically verified upward through the chain. A failure anywhere in that chain is a failure of the whole system's claim to truth, which is why wrongful verification by an officer is an offence in its own right under section 43 and why the Test Centres that share the verification work are brought within section 37. The second feature is the place of the derived units. The litre, the hectare, the tonne and the carat by which trade actually speaks are not base units; they are authorised descendants, prescribed under the Act with exact relations to the base units, and the jeweller's carat weight and the fuel retailer's litre sit inside the same system as the physicist's kilogram. The man who sells petrol by the litre and the laboratory that certifies the reference standard are using one language, and sections 4 to 12 are the grammar of it.

Within that grammar, precision is rationed by purpose. The General Rules prescribe denominations of weights and measures and classes of accuracy, because the tolerance that serves a grain merchant's platform scale would disgrace a bullion dealer's balance, and the tolerance a bullion dealer needs would price an ordinary shop scale out of the market. Instruments are accordingly made, approved and verified to the class their trade requires, and the maximum permissible error that decides a verification, and later a prosecution, is the error allowed to that class. The architecture thus refuses a single standard of exactness in favour of a ladder of exactness, each rung honestly labelled: what the Act demands everywhere is not perfection but truth within the tolerance the instrument claims, tested against a standard several rungs more accurate than itself. That is the scientific settlement on which the legal one rests, and it is why a prosecution for a false measure is, at bottom, a dispute about which rung of the ladder the instrument stood on and whether it kept its footing there.

## The enforcement chain: Director, Controllers and legal metrology officers

Chapter III appoints the human machinery. At its head stand two officers whose jurisdictions interlock along the line the Constitution drew. Section 13 provides for a Director of Legal Metrology, appointed by the Central Government, who carries the Act in relation to inter-State trade and commerce: the standards of units, the registration of importers, the approval of models, and the offences that arise in trade crossing State lines. Section 14 provides for a Controller in every State, appointed by the State Government, who carries the Act in relation to intra-State trade and commerce, supported by legal metrology officers appointed under the same scheme. The inspectors of marketplaces and high streets, the officers who test the trader's balance and examine the declarations on the shelf, belong overwhelmingly to the State chain, and it is to the Controller that most traders, and most complainants, will ever speak.

The powers these officers wield are set out principally in section 15. A legal metrology officer may enter and search premises where weights, measures or packaged commodities are made, kept or sold, inspect the instruments and packages found there, examine and seize records and documents, and seize the weights, measures and packages that offend against the Act. Search and seizure are governed by the corresponding provisions of the Code of Criminal Procedure, which imports the familiar safeguards about witnesses and procedure. Section 16 deals with the fate of what is seized: a weight or measure that has not been verified and stamped is liable to forfeiture, but the proviso is humane and practical, since the instrument is not forfeited if its owner gets it verified within the time the rules prescribe. The legislative instinct is consistent throughout the chapter. The object is a verified instrument in lawful use, not a confiscated one, and the seizure power exists to interrupt unlawful trade rather than to punish by confiscation.

### What may a legal metrology officer seize during an inspection?

A legal metrology officer may enter and inspect trade premises, inspect records, and seize any weight or measure that is unverified or non-standard, along with any package that does not conform to the declaration rules. Seizures follow the safeguards of the criminal procedure code, and an unverified instrument escapes forfeiture if it is got verified within the prescribed time.

Section 17 adds the paperwork of compliance, obliging the persons the rules specify, including manufacturers, repairers and dealers in weights and measures, to maintain the records and registers prescribed and to produce them on demand; the failure to produce documents, returns or records is itself an offence under section 31. The enforcement design that emerges is inspection-led rather than complaint-led. Officers verify instruments on a cycle, inspect premises on information or in the ordinary course, test packages for net quantity against the maximum permissible errors the rules allow, and check declarations against the labelling rules. Prosecution is the end of the road, not the beginning, and the compounding power in section 48, examined with the offences below, means that a great many detections end in a settled payment rather than a courtroom. For a consumer, the practical consequence is that a grievance about a short measure or a false declaration is addressed to an administrative machine that exists in every State: the district legal metrology office and, above it, the Controller.

The texture of the work is worth a paragraph, because it explains the Act's priorities better than any preamble. The officer's year is built around the verification cycle: traders bring instruments to the department's offices and camps, or the officer attends the premises where a weighbridge or a dispensing pump cannot be moved, and each instrument that passes leaves with a stamp and a due date. Around that cycle run the inspections: the market visit in which shelf packages are drawn and weighed against their declarations, the check at the fuel station in which the dispensing pump's delivery is measured against the standard capacity measure, the examination of a manufacturer's packing line records against section 17. The offences the system is designed to catch are, by the same token, mostly documentary and physical rather than testimonial. Either the stamp is present and in date or it is not; either the sample's average net quantity meets the declaration within the permitted error or it does not; either the panel carries the declarations or it does not. A statute that can be enforced by measurement is a statute whose enforcement can be routine, and the modesty of most penalties in the original scheme reflects exactly that routineness: these are offences meant to be detected in bulk, settled in bulk, and prevented by the knowledge that the officer and the working standard will come round again.

### What does the Indian Institute of Legal Metrology do?

The Institute is the training arm of the service. Deemed established under section 21, it runs the courses through which legal metrology officers of the Centre and the States are trained in the science and law of measurement, with its management, teaching staff, curricula and admission qualifications prescribed by rules under the Act.

Section 21 is easy to pass over and worth pausing on, because it explains how a technical service reproduces itself. The Indian Institute of Legal Metrology, which existed before the Act, is deemed to have been established under it, and the Central Government's rule-making power extends to its management and control, its teaching staff, the courses and curricula taught there, and the qualifications a person must hold to be admitted. Legal metrology is an unusual profession, half physics and half law: the officer must understand the error characteristics of a spring balance and the burden of proof in a prosecution for using it. A statute that created offences of this technicality without creating the institution that trains its officers would have been incomplete, and section 21 is the Act's acknowledgement that standards are kept by people before they are kept by instruments.

## Verification, stamping and licensing: sections 23 and 24

If the standards chapters state what measurement is, Chapter IV states how a measuring instrument earns the right to be used in trade. Section 24 is the operative rule: no weight or measure shall be used in any transaction, or for protection, unless it has been verified and stamped in the manner the Act and rules prescribe. Verification is the comparison of the instrument against the working standards of the department; stamping is the officer's mark, applied when the instrument passes, which tells every later inspector and every customer that the instrument was true when tested. The unverified scale is not merely an administrative irregularity. Its use is the offence in section 33, and goods sold by it engage section 34, so the stamp is the hinge between the standards architecture and the offences chapter.

The verification system is built to be usable. Verification is done by the legal metrology officers of the State, on payment of the prescribed fee, at the offices and camps the Controller arranges, and the Act also admits a second channel: Government approved Test Centres, notified under section 24, which may verify specified kinds of weights and measures on the terms and fee the rules prescribe. The Centre that verifies falsely is itself within the offences chapter, under section 37. Re-verification falls due at the intervals State rules prescribe for each class of instrument, so the stamp is a licence with an expiry, and the trader's obligation is cyclical rather than once-for-all.

The Test Centre device deserves its sentence of explanation, since it is the Act's one deliberate experiment in sharing a sovereign function. Certain instruments, the weighbridges and storage tanks and high-capacity measures of industry among the kinds that strain a district office, are more conveniently verified where they stand by a centre equipped for them, and section 24 therefore lets the Central Government notify Government approved Test Centres for prescribed kinds of weights and measures, on prescribed terms, with the qualifications and experience of the persons engaged and the fee to be charged themselves matters for the rulebook. The delegation is fenced: the Centre verifies under the same standards and the same stamp discipline as the department, its wrongful verification is an offence under section 37, and the Controller's authority over the instruments in the State is not displaced. What the device purchases is capacity, the scarcest resource in any inspection state, without selling the standard.

Around verification stands the licensing regime of section 23. No person may manufacture, repair or sell a weight or measure without a licence from the Controller, issued for the area and on the conditions the State rules prescribe. The licence offences mirror the requirement: manufacturing without a licence is punished under section 45, repairing or selling without one under section 46, and tampering with a licence, or quoting the licence number of another person, under section 47. The policy is visible: because a doctored instrument can falsify thousands of transactions before it is caught, the Act controls entry into the trades that make and mend instruments, and not merely the instruments themselves.

Section 55 marks the boundary of the verification chapter with three sensible carve-outs. The provisions on verification and stamping do not apply to a weight or measure used in a factory exclusively engaged in manufacturing arms or ammunition for the Armed Forces of the Union, to one used for scientific investigation or research, or to one manufactured exclusively for export. The laboratory balance and the export line stand outside the trade regime because the mischief the chapter addresses, deception in domestic trade, is absent from both. The carve-outs also explain an apparent puzzle in section 8, which permits the manufacture of non-standard weights and measures for export and for scientific research: the Act governs the instruments of Indian commerce, and leaves the instruments of science and of foreign commerce to their own disciplines.

What verification involves, mechanically, deserves a sentence of demystification. The officer tests the instrument against the working standard: standard weights are placed on the scale pan, a standard capacity measure is filled from the dispensing pump, a standard length is laid against the measure, and the instrument passes if its indication stays within the maximum permissible error allowed for its class. Instruments are also examined for the frauds of construction: the false bottom, the shortened arm, the magnet's friendliness to the pan. An instrument that fails is refused the stamp, and until it is adjusted by a licensed repairer and presented again, its use in trade is the use of an unverified instrument. The stamp itself, with the officer's mark and the period of validity, is designed to be read by the customer as readily as by the next inspector, which is why the counterfeiting of the seal in section 44 is treated as an attack on the currency of the whole system rather than as a petty forgery.

## Measurement at the counter of daily life

The Act is usually discussed through its packages, but its instruments govern transactions that never see a packet. The dispensing pump at a fuel station is a measure within the Act, verified against the department's standard capacity measures and stamped, and the five litres the meter announces are a legal representation before they are a physical fact. The liquefied petroleum gas cylinder refilled and sealed for domestic use is sold by its net weight of gas, and the checking of cylinders for short filling is a standing part of the inspectorate's work, since the buyer cannot weigh the gas without the cylinder and cannot separate the two at the kitchen door. The weighbridge that weighs a truck of grain or scrap, the platform scale at the agricultural market where a farmer's produce is converted into a price, the jeweller's balance with its carat weights, the metre rod of the cloth merchant and the meter by which a hired vehicle charges its fare belong to the same legal family: each is an instrument whose indication settles what one party pays the other, and each is held by the Act to the discipline of verification, stamping and the standard units.

This breadth explains two otherwise puzzling features of the statute. The first is the place of protection in the verification duty: instruments matter to the Act not only when they sell but when they safeguard, because a false gauge endangers in the same way whether the falsity is read by a buyer or by an operator trusting it for safety. The second is the severity reserved for the instrument offences. A false declaration cheats one buyer of one packet; a false balance cheats every buyer who steps up to it, in quantities too small to notice and too steady to forgive, which is why the manufacture of the non-standard instrument drew the chapter's heaviest first-principles sentence and why the alteration of an instrument to deceive sits in the band of offences that neither compounding nor the decriminalisation of 2023 was allowed to touch. The packet is the Act's most visible citizen, but the instrument is its first concern, and the offences chapter is unintelligible unless that order is kept in mind.

## Imports and model approval: sections 19, 20 and 22

Trade in instruments is policed at the border as carefully as in the bazaar. Section 19 requires every importer of a weight or measure to register with the Director, so that the Central authority holds a roll of the persons bringing measuring instruments into the country, and section 38 punishes the unregistered importer. Section 20 states the substantive bar in the shortest of forms: a weight or measure that does not conform to the standards prescribed under the Act shall not be imported, and section 39 punishes its import, with graduated severity for a repeat offence. The combined effect is that the conformity inquiry happens before the instrument reaches a shop. An imported balance that cannot measure in the standard units, or that is built to tolerances the Act does not accept, is stopped as an import rather than chased through the markets after sale.

Section 22 adds the most technically interesting control in the Act: approval of the model. Before a weight or measure of a kind the rules specify is manufactured or imported, its model must be approved by the prescribed authority after testing, so that the design itself is certified true before any individual instrument is made. Model approval is the point at which Indian law meets the international pattern-approval practice of legal metrology, and the section's provisos are carefully drawn. Certain humble instruments are exempted from the requirement, including cast iron, brass, bullion and carat weights, beam scales, the length measures used in retail trade in textiles and timber, and capacity measures not exceeding twenty litres of the kind used for kerosene, milk and potable liquors. A model that has already been approved in a country outside India may be approved with or without further test, which is the statutory doorway through which international approvals enter the Indian system. Section 32 punishes the manufacture or sale of a weight or measure whose model has not been approved, completing a regime in which an instrument can be stopped at four points: the model before production, the import at registration and at the border, the licence of its maker or repairer, and the verification stamp before its first use in trade.

Approval testing is pattern evaluation in the proper sense. The authority examines not one instrument but the type: whether the design measures accurately across its range, whether its indication stays true under the disturbances of ordinary use, whether its construction invites or resists fraud, and whether its sealing arrangements let an officer secure the parts whose adjustment would falsify it. An instrument that passes as a type may still fail as an individual, which is why approval never replaces verification; the two controls answer different questions, the one about the honesty of the design, the other about the truth of the specimen on the counter. The exemption list in the provisos marks the boundary of the exercise with a craftsman's sense of proportion: the beam scale and the brass weight have no pattern to evaluate in the modern sense, their truth being a matter of their metal and their making, and the law spends its laboratory time where electronics and mechanism have multiplied the ways of being wrong.

## The rulebooks beneath the Act

A pillar statute of this kind states the scheme and leaves the working numbers to rules, and the Legal Metrology Act is no exception. Section 52 empowers the Central Government to make rules across the Act's whole field, and its catalogue of heads is a map of where the operative detail lives: the denominations and tolerances of standard weights and measures, the physical characteristics of the standards, the manner of verification, the standard quantities and declarations for packages under section 18, the registration of importers under section 19, the management of the Institute under section 21, the manner and fee of model approval under section 22, the notification and conditions of Government approved Test Centres under section 24, the error in net quantity that section 36(2) punishes, and the fee for compounding under section 48. Section 53 gives the State Governments the complementary field listed earlier: licences, verification fees, registers and the machinery of the camps. A breach of a Central rule carries, where the Act does not itself punish the conduct, a fine of up to Rs 5,000 under section 52(3), and State rules carry the same ceiling under section 53(3), a modest tariff that marks the boundary between the offences Parliament chose to grade in the Act and the housekeeping of the rulebooks.

Two consequences follow for anyone reading the law of this subject. The first is that the Act cannot be applied from its own text alone at the points where the citizen's life touches it: the declarations on a packet are in the Packaged Commodities Rules, the procedure of model approval and the denominations of standards are in the Legal Metrology (General) Rules, 2011, and the licence in a repairer's drawer is a creature of State rules. The second is that amendment travels through the rulebooks faster than through the statute. Every reform examined in the second half of this article, the e-commerce declarations, the end of dual pricing, the unit sale price, the abolition of standard pack sizes, was made by amending the Rules of 2011, not by amending the Act; the Act's sections 18 and 52 supplied the authority, and the Gazette notifications supplied the law. The decriminalisation of 2023 is the exception that proves the pattern, since penalties live in the Act itself and only Parliament could move them.

## What must be on the pack: section 18 and the Packaged Commodities Rules

For most consumers, the Act is not the standards chapters or the verification camps. It is the printed panel on the back of a packet. The gateway is section 18. Sub-section (1) provides that no person shall manufacture, pack, sell, import, distribute, deliver, offer, expose or possess for sale any pre-packaged commodity unless it is in standard quantities and bears the declarations prescribed by the rules. The breadth of the verb list is the point: the obligation attaches at every hand the package passes through, so that no one in the chain can answer a missing declaration by pointing to the hand before. Sub-section (2) extends the discipline to advertising, requiring every advertisement that quotes the retail sale price of a package to declare the net quantity contained in it, so that a price in an advertisement cannot float free of the quantity it buys.

The declarations themselves are prescribed by the Legal Metrology (Packaged Commodities) Rules, 2011, made by the Central Government under section 52 of the Act. Rule 6 lists what every package meant for retail sale must bear, and the list is best understood as the buyer's substitute for the inspection the buyer cannot perform. Because the commodity was packed in the purchaser's absence, the package must speak for it: whose it is, what it is, how much of it there is, when it was made, what at most may be charged for it, what that works out to per unit, and where to complain. The declarations belong on the principal display panel, the face of the package presented to the buyer at the point of sale, and the rules prescribe the size and manner of the lettering so that the panel is legible rather than decorative. Particular categories carry additional or harmonised labelling of their own: food products follow declarations harmonised with the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006, medical devices declared as drugs were brought within the declarations regime by the amendments of 2017, and medicines themselves are labelled under the regime examined in the [guide to the Drugs and Cosmetics Act 1940](/2015/06/15/drugs-and-cosmetics-act-1940-explained/), with this Act's declarations operating alongside.

The dealer's side of the regime is in Rule 18 of the same Rules, the provision that gives the declarations their daily force. Rule 18(1) forbids any wholesale dealer, retail dealer or importer from selling, distributing, delivering, displaying or storing for sale any commodity in packaged form unless the package complies in all respects with the Act and the Rules. The retailer, in other words, is not a passive conduit for the packer's label. A shelf of packages that lack the prescribed declarations is the retailer's contravention as much as the packer's, which is why inspection drives so often end at the shop counter: the package on the shelf is the evidence, and the person who offers it for sale answers for it.

The regime has boundaries, and they are part of its sense. Chapter II of the Rules is directed at packages intended for retail sale, and the heaviest packages stand outside the Rule 6 declaration list: a package containing a quantity more than 25 kilograms or 25 litres is not required to carry the retail declarations, since a sack of that size is bought on terms closer to bulk trade than to the packaged-goods mischief. Packages meant for industrial or institutional consumers, bought by enterprises and institutions rather than by households, follow a distinct and lighter declaration pattern, and wholesale packages, which pass between traders rather than to a final buyer, carry their own set of particulars under the Rules. The dividing idea throughout is the one in the definition of a pre-packaged commodity: the declarations exist because the buyer was absent when the goods were packed and cannot test what the packet contains. Where the buyer is a trade buyer equipped to test, weigh and bargain, the law relaxes the script the packet must recite.

Three details of the panel reward a closer look, because they are where prosecutions are actually won and lost. The first is grouping and legibility: the declarations are to appear together on the principal display panel in characters whose size the Rules prescribe by reference to the size of the package, and the amendment of 2017 enlarged the minimum sizes of letters and numerals precisely because a declaration printed to be missed is a declaration withheld. The second is the form of the quantity declaration itself, which must state the net quantity in the standard unit, by weight, by volume, by length or by number as the commodity requires, without the padding of qualifying phrases that soften a number into an estimate; the word approximately has no lawful place beside a net quantity. The third is the advertisement rule of section 18(2), which follows the package into print and screen: a seller who advertises a package by its price must state the net quantity the price buys, so the discipline of the panel governs the hoarding and the listing as well as the shelf.

## Testing the quantity: samples and maximum permissible errors

The net quantity declaration would be a pious hope without a testing regime behind it, and the Packaged Commodities Rules supply one of recognisable statistical shape. Officers draw samples of packages at the premises of the manufacturer, packer or dealer, in the numbers the Rules prescribe for the size of the lot, and weigh or measure the contents. Two questions are then asked of the sample. The average net quantity of the packages in the sample must not fall below the quantity declared, so that a packer cannot defend a systematically light line by pointing to the heavy packets that balance it in somebody else's basket. And the number of packages whose error exceeds the maximum permissible error for that quantity must stay within the allowance the Rules make, with a still stricter outer limit beyond which no individual package may fall. The maximum permissible errors themselves are prescribed in the First Schedule to the Rules as a sliding scale: a percentage tolerance for the smallest quantities, shading into fixed gram and millilitre allowances as the declared quantity grows, since a gram lost in a ten-gram sachet and a gram lost in a ten-kilogram sack are different orders of carelessness.

Section 36(2) is where the testing regime acquires its teeth, punishing the error in net quantity contained in a package where the error exceeds the limits the Rules prescribe. The layering with section 36(1) should be noticed. A package that omits a declaration, or prints it defectively, offends sub-section (1) as a non-conforming package, and the whole chain that made, packed, imported, sold or distributed it is exposed. A package that declares honestly and simply contains too little offends sub-section (2), the quantity offence, whose proof is the officer's sample rather than the consumer's scales. The consumer who suspects a light packet is therefore not expected to prove the shortage at home; the complaint triggers the sampling machinery, and the machinery produces the evidence. It is a fitting emblem of the whole Act: a grievance that begins as a feeling in the hand is converted, by standards and samples, into a measurement that a court can act on.

## The maximum retail price: a ceiling the seller prints

Every thread in the declarations regime runs into the letters MRP. The maximum retail price is defined by the Rules as the maximum price at which the commodity in packaged form may be sold to the ultimate consumer, inclusive of all taxes, and Rule 6(1)(e) requires it to be declared on the package in Indian currency. Two features of the declaration repay attention. First, it is a maximum, not a price: the packer fixes the highest figure at which the packet may be sold, and the market below that ceiling is free. Second, it is inclusive of all taxes, which forecloses the familiar addition at the till. What the packet states is the most the buyer can be asked to pay for that packet, taxes already inside the number.

The enforcement rule is Rule 18(2) of the Packaged Commodities Rules: no retail dealer or other person, including a manufacturer, packer, importer or wholesale dealer, shall make any sale of a commodity in packed form at a price exceeding the retail sale price declared for it. The offence is complete on the sale itself, and it needs no proof that the buyer complained or that the excess was large. A rupee above the printed maximum on an ordinary retail sale is a contravention, detectable by comparing the bill with the packet, which is why the excess-price complaint is the most straightforward complaint in the whole of legal metrology and why the guidance on [how to file a consumer complaint in India](/2021/01/01/file-consumer-complaint-india-guide/) treats the preserved packet and the bill as the heart of the evidence. The same rulebook protects the integrity of the printed figure: the declared price may not be altered, obliterated or tampered with once printed, so a sticker raising the price on an existing packet is itself a contravention, whatever the seller's costs have done.

The subtlest abuse the rules had to meet was the printing of two ceilings. A manufacturer supplying both ordinary shops and premium venues had every incentive to print a higher maximum on the packets destined for airports, cinemas and malls, so that the excess charged at the captive counter was, on paper, a sale at the printed price. The Amendment Rules of 2017 closed the device by inserting Rule 18(2A), in force from 1 January 2018: unless another law specifically provides otherwise, no manufacturer, packer or importer shall declare different maximum retail prices on an identical pre-packaged commodity. One product, one maximum. The declaration that remains is the single figure the whole country may read, which is what makes the ceiling meaningful in the first place.

The ceiling's limits are as instructive as its force, and they appear the moment the commodity is not in a packet. Loose produce weighed at the counter, grain scooped from an open sack, sweets sold by weight from a tray: none of these carries a printed maximum, because there is no package to print it on, and the seller's price for them is the seller's own, restrained by competition and by nothing in this Act. The consumer's protection in the loose trade is of the older kind: the verified and stamped scale, the standard unit, and sections 30 and 34 against the short delivery and the false measure. Much public confusion about the maximum retail price dissolves once the two trades are separated in the mind. The printed ceiling is an incident of the packet, invented for goods the buyer cannot inspect; where the goods lie open and the measure is applied in the buyer's presence, the Act protects the measurement and leaves the price, as it leaves every price it does not print, to the market.

### When a tax rises after a package is printed, which price may the retailer charge?

The revised price may be charged, even above the printed price, where the manufacturer or packer has communicated the revision as the rules prescribe: newspaper advertisements and notices to dealers, the Director and the State Controllers. The increase may not exceed the tax rise, and the route covers packages packed in the month of revision or the month after.

Rule 18(3) builds that route because the alternative would be absurd. The maximum is declared inclusive of taxes; if a tax on the commodity is revised upward after packing, a frozen printed figure would force the trade to absorb a levy the price was designed to carry, or to break the ceiling rule in order to collect the State's own tax. The route is hedged accordingly. The revised price must be communicated by the manufacturer or packer, not improvised by the retailer; it must be publicised by at least two advertisements in newspapers and by notices to dealers and to the Director and the State Controllers; the difference between the printed price and the revised price may not exceed the extent of the tax increase or the fresh tax imposed; and the route is open only for packages packed in the month of the revision or the month immediately following. Where the revision runs the other way and the revised price is lower than the printed price, the lower figure binds at once, for every package, whenever it was packed. The asymmetry is the consumer policy of the whole chapter in miniature: an increase must justify itself step by step, a decrease needs no justification at all.

Enforcement practice adds a rider that shoppers meet constantly. The ceiling is on the total price of the packet as sold, and enforcement treats an added charge at the counter, of which the cooling charge demanded for a chilled bottle is the stock example, as part of the price charged for the commodity, so that a sale at the printed maximum plus a rupee for the fridge is a sale above the maximum. The same logic disposes of the argument that the excess was for a bag, a straw or the privilege of the shop's location; the rule asks what the buyer paid for the commodity in packed form, and compares that sum, and no other, with the figure on the packet. The narrowness of the comparison is deliberate and is what makes the offence provable without a trial about value: two numbers, both printed or billed, and a subtraction.

## Hotels, restaurants and the composite service holding

The single most litigated question in this territory asks whether the ceiling binds where the packet is not so much sold as served: the bottle of mineral water opened at a restaurant table, the can of aerated water handed over in a hotel room, priced in each case above the maximum printed on the container. The Federation of Hotel and Restaurant Associations of India carried the question to the Delhi High Court against the enforcement of the predecessor statutes, and the litigation travelled for fourteen years before the Supreme Court settled it. The answer the courts gave is the most consequential construction ever placed on this branch of the law, and it must be stated with its limits attached, because in the retelling the limits are usually the first casualty.

A single judge of the Delhi High Court, Vikramajit Sen J., decided the writ petition in the Federation's favour on 5 March 2007, in a judgment that reasoned from the nature of the transaction. The customer who enters a hotel or restaurant, the Court held, does not enter to make a simple purchase of a bottle of water; the direct purpose is to enjoy the ambience and the service of the establishment, and the supply of food and drink within it is one indivisible contract of service to which the sale of any item is incidental. The reasoning rested on two Supreme Court authorities on the cognate question in sales tax law: State of Punjab v. Associated Hotels of India Ltd., (1972) 1 SCC 472, and Northern India Caterers (India) Ltd. v. Lt. Governor of Delhi, reported at [1979] 1 SCR 557, with its review at (1980) 2 SCC 167, which together establish that a composite supply of service and goods cannot be split into a taxable sale of the goods alone. The single judge added the comparison that has followed the case ever since: there is no justifiable reason to interdict the price of a bottle of water while ignoring the relatively exorbitant charge for a cup of tea or coffee, when neither is bought as a commodity off a shelf.

The procedural path then took a turn that kept the question alive for the successor statute. In letters patent appeals, a Division Bench of the Delhi High Court disposed of the matter on 11 February 2015 by consent, recording the Federation's agreement that the single judge's judgment would not stand in the way of the authorities enforcing the new Act, the Legal Metrology Act, 2009, and that whether hotels and restaurants could charge above the printed price under the new Act would be left to be adjudicated in proceedings brought under it. The Supreme Court took the question up directly and answered it on the merits. By its judgment of 12 December 2017, delivered by R.F. Nariman J. in Civil Appeal Nos. 21790 and 21791 of 2017, the Court allowed the Federation's appeals, set aside the Division Bench judgments of 11 February 2015 and 15 May 2015, and held that the definition of sale in both the 1976 Act and the 2009 Act leaves composite, indivisible agreements for the supply of services with food and drink outside the reach of either statute. The operative sentence repays quotation: neither the Standards of Weights and Measures Act, 1976 read with the enactment of 1985, nor the Legal Metrology Act, 2009, would apply so as to interdict the sale of mineral water in hotels and restaurants at prices which are above the MRP.

Both sides of the question deserve to be heard at full strength, because each identifies something real. The case for the holding, as the Federation argued it and the courts accepted it, is that a restaurant bill is the price of a composite experience: premises, service, preparation, ambience and the item together, charged as one sum for one indivisible supply. The printed maximum on the bottle governs the bottle sold as a package across a counter; it does not purport to price a table, a waiter and an evening, and the statute, which regulates packages sold as packages, contains no words that convert a service establishment into a retail dealer for everything on its menu. The tea and coffee comparison does genuine work in this argument, since no one supposes the price of a brewed cup could be capped by the price of the leaves, and the water bottle differs from the cup only in arriving pre-packed.

The case against, pressed by consumer organisations and by the enforcement authorities who had prosecuted the hotels, is that the packet on the table is physically the same packet that sits on the shop shelf, bearing the same printed maximum that the consumer has been taught to read as a promise. The printed figure says maximum, and a diner charged double for the identical sealed bottle experiences the difference as overcharging, not as ambience. The objection sharpens in the captive settings that cluster around the holding: the cinema counter, the airport outlet, the railway premises, where the consumer cannot walk to a competing shelf and where service, in any rich sense, is thin. An exemption discovered by construction, the critics say, operates most profitably exactly where the consumer's choice is least real.

The verdict the text supports is narrower than either side's slogan. The Supreme Court did not write an exemption for hotels into the Act; it construed the word sale, and held that the composite supply is not the kind of transaction the Act's price ceiling fastens on. Nothing in the judgment touches the ordinary retail sale, where the ceiling binds with full force, and nothing in it licenses a hotel to sell a sealed packet across a counter for takeaway above the printed price as a simple sale of goods. The holding is a boundary marker on the statute's central definition, and its lesson runs both ways: the Act reaches less than aggrieved consumers suppose in service settings, and exactly as far as it says in the marketplace, where its declarations and its ceiling remain the governing law of the packet.

The argument has not rested there, and the contested ground has shifted to the venues at the edge of the ratio. Consumer bodies continue to press the case of the cinema counter and the airport kiosk, where the sealed packet is sold across a counter in premises the customer cannot leave to find a competitor, and where the service element, they argue, is nominal beside the sale of the branded packet itself. The establishments answer with the Supreme Court's own logic: the ticket buys an experience within premises maintained for it, the refreshment is supplied within that composite, and the definition of sale does not change because the room has a screen instead of tablecloths. That exchange is not a failure of the judgment; it is the ordinary working of a definition at its border, and it will be settled, if it is settled, by the same method the Federation case used, by asking of each setting whether what is supplied is in substance a sale of the package or a service of which the package is an incident. What the judgment forecloses is only the shortcut: no court can reach the answer for a new setting without performing that inquiry, and no seller outside a genuine service setting can borrow the hotel's answer at all.

## Selling online: the declarations move to the listing

A package bought on a screen presents the problem the Act was designed to solve in its purest form: the buyer is absent not only from the packing but from the package itself, and must decide on the declarations alone. The Amendment Rules of 2017, in force from 1 January 2018, answered by carrying the label onto the listing. Goods displayed by a seller on an electronic platform must carry the declarations the Rules require, displayed before the purchase is made: the name and address of the manufacturer, packer and importer, the name of the commodity, the net content, the retail sale price, the consumer care details and the rest of the Rule 6 script, excepting only the month and year of manufacture or packing, which belongs to the physical packet. The listing is, for legal metrology purposes, the display panel.

The marketplace model required a further answer about responsibility. On a platform that merely provides access to a communication system over which information supplied by the manufacturer, seller, dealer or importer is transmitted or temporarily stored, the correctness of the declarations rests with that manufacturer, seller or importer, while the platform remains bound to the due-diligence obligations of an intermediary under the Information Technology Act, 2000. The platform cannot launder a non-compliant packet into compliance by describing it badly, and the seller behind the listing answers for the label exactly as if the packet stood on a physical shelf. Alongside this statute runs the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, whose own regime for electronic commerce, explained in the [guide to the Consumer Protection Act 2019](/2020/12/01/consumer-protection-act-2019-explained/), governs platform conduct from the consumer-law side; a non-compliant listing can engage both.

The practical test the 2017 amendments impose is sequence. On a shelf, the buyer reads the panel and then pays; the listing must reproduce that order in a medium where the seller controls the page. A listing that shows the price prominently and the net quantity nowhere has failed the statute's central requirement even if the quantity appears in a specification tab the buyer never opens, because the declaration discipline is about what is displayed, and displayed means presented to the purchaser in the ordinary course of the purchase. The delivered packet, of course, must carry the full panel including the month and year of manufacture, and a discrepancy between the listing's declarations and the packet's is evidence against the seller under both regimes: the listing promised what the label does not perform. For marketplaces, the settlement of 2017 is a workable division of labour: the platform builds the fields and displays them, the seller warrants their truth, and the department polices both ends of the transaction with the same sections it uses in the bazaar.

## Standard pack sizes and the unit sale price

For most of the life of the 2011 Rules, a manufacturer could not choose pack sizes freely. Rule 5, read with the Second Schedule, prescribed the quantities in which nineteen specified commodities, including several sold in value-based packages, were to be packed, and a packet of an unlisted size was non-compliant. The Amendment Rules of 2021, issued as G.S.R. 779(E) on 2 November 2021, omitted Rule 5, and the Amendment Rules of 2022, issued as G.S.R. 226(E) on 28 March 2022, omitted the Second Schedule. The changes were first appointed to take effect on 1 April 2022, and after staged deferrals they took effect in the closing months of that year. From that point, a manufacturer may pack in any quantity, and the standard sizes survive only as the sizes manufacturers choose rather than the sizes the law commands.

The liberalisation had two readings from the start, and both deserve a hearing. The case for it is innovation and access. Fixed sizes forced a manufacturer who wanted a smaller price point to seek an exception rather than to pack a smaller packet; abolishing the list lets trade respond to household budgets with a twenty-rupee sachet or a half-litre bottle without asking permission, and it ended a regime under which the same commodity could be compliant in one size and an offence in another for no reason a consumer could see. The case against is comparability. Standard sizes did the consumer's arithmetic silently: a shopper comparing two brands of the same commodity knew the quantities were equal and could compare prices directly. With sizes free, the comparison requires the buyer to divide price by quantity for every packet on the shelf, a computation the market knows most buyers will not perform and that rewards the packet whose size flatters its price.

The 2021 amendments answered that second objection with the companion reform they are remembered for. Rule 6(11) introduced the declaration of the unit sale price: the price per gram where the net quantity is below a kilogram and per kilogram above it, per millilitre below a litre and per litre above it, per centimetre below a metre and per metre above it, and per number or unit where the commodity is sold by number, rounded to the nearest two decimal places, with an exception where the retail sale price is itself the unit price. The declaration does by print what standard sizes did by law. The shopper comparing two packets of different sizes compares two unit prices instead of dividing two totals, and the freedom to choose sizes is matched by the information that makes the choice informed. Together, the removal of the schedule and the arrival of the unit price mark the statute's second settlement on the old question, standardising the information rather than the packet.

The debate that followed the change has a name in the trade press: shrinkflation, the practice of holding the price of a familiar packet steady while reducing the quantity inside it. Under the standard sizes, the practice was constrained by the list; a quantity could not quietly migrate downward because the lawful sizes were enumerated. Under the free regime, the quantity may lawfully shrink, and the entire burden of detection shifts to the declarations: the net quantity must state the smaller truth in the prescribed size of type, and the unit sale price must worsen visibly even where the headline price stands still. Whether that informational answer is enough is the live policy question of the chapter, and honest opinion divides on it, since the consumer who does not read panels gains nothing from a panel that tells the truth. What cannot be denied is the coherence of the design: a statute whose claim is that it governs declarations rather than quantities or prices was bound, in the end, to trust the declaration to do the work, and to spend its enforcement effort on making the declaration accurate and legible rather than on fixing the size of the packet by law.

A textual loose end deserves notice, because practitioners meet it. Section 18(1) of the Act still speaks of a pre-packaged commodity being in standard quantities as a condition of lawful sale, and the words were written when the Rules prescribed those quantities size by size. The omission of Rule 5 did not amend the Act, and the phrase survives. Its working meaning in the amended regime is supplied by the Rules that remain: the quantity in the package must be a quantity expressed in the standard units of the Act, declared in the prescribed form, and proved by the testing regime to be the quantity the package contains, within the permissible error. The standard, in other words, migrated from the size to the statement, which is the same migration the unit sale price performs for price comparison. A prosecutor who charged a packet merely for being an unusual size would find no rule left to point to; a prosecutor who charges it for declaring a quantity it does not hold stands on section 36(2) as firmly as ever. The episode is a small lesson in how this statute is amended: the Act's grand phrases hold their place for decades, and their content is renegotiated in the rulebook beneath them.

## What counts as a pre-packaged commodity, and who answers for the label

The definition of a pre-packaged commodity has generated the statute's sharpest boundary disputes, because it decides who must print everything and who need print nothing. The leading decision was rendered on the predecessor statute but on language the 2009 Act kept. In Whirlpool of India Ltd. v. Union of India, (2007) 14 SCC 468, a three-judge bench of the Supreme Court decided on 2 November 2007 whether a refrigerator, which dealers necessarily unpack for display and which customers inspect and test before buying, was a commodity in packaged form and a pre-packed commodity for the purposes of the Standards of Weights and Measures Act and the rules. The Court held that it was. What mattered was the state of the commodity when it left the manufacturer without the purchaser present: packed in polythene, thermocol and hardboard cartons, in a quantity that was predetermined and could not be altered without opening the package. The later unpacking for display or testing changed nothing, because the quantity and value of the commodity were settled at packing, and the refrigerator had to bear the maximum retail price declaration like any other packed commodity. The ratio is the definition's ratio: pre-packaged is a description of how the commodity entered trade, not of how the dealer displays it.

The boundary the other way was drawn in State of Maharashtra v. Subhash Arjundas Kataria, where the Supreme Court held that sunglasses, which customers habitually open, try on and have adjusted at the counter, do not fall within the definition. The distinction the Court drew with Whirlpool was functional: where the opening of the package leads to the commodity being handled, fitted and modified rather than merely inspected, the packet has not fixed the commodity's value in the way the definition requires. Read together, the two decisions make the definition a test of the packet's role. If the packet settles what the buyer gets, the declarations attach; if the packet merely carries what the buyer will choose, inspect or shape at the counter, they do not. Packets that must be opened for the buyer to examine the goods at all, like the electric bulb illustrated in the old rules' explanation, stay on the Whirlpool side of the line, since opening to inspect does not alter what was packed.

The second question the brief requires, the liability of the retailer as distinct from the packer, is answered by the Act's own structure rather than by any leniency the courts have read into it. Section 36(1) punishes the person who manufactures, packs, sells, distributes or delivers a pre-packaged commodity that does not conform to the declarations, and section 36(2) punishes the error in net quantity beyond the maximum permissible errors the rules allow. The verb lists are deliberate. The offence attaches to the sale itself, not to the printing of the label, and Rule 18(1) reinforces the point by forbidding any dealer to sell a package that does not comply in all respects with the Act and the rules. A retailer who sells a packet that lacks a required declaration has committed the offence whether or not the retailer printed the label, and the Act provides no general due-diligence defence for the retail sale of a non-conforming package. The nominated-person machinery of section 49, examined with the offences below, mitigates this only for companies, by channelling the personal liability to the person the company names; it does not move the liability from the seller to the packer. The statute's allocation is therefore severe by design: the packer answers for what the packet says, the importer answers for what it brings, and the retailer answers for every packet it offers, because the consumer's contract is with the shelf.

## Offences and penalties as enacted

Chapter V, sections 25 to 57, is the longest chapter in the Act, and its structure is disciplined: nearly every offence is graded, with a moderate penalty for the first contravention and imprisonment entering on repetition. The centre of gravity is the honesty of measurement. Section 25 punishes the use of a non-standard weight or measure with a fine that may extend to Rs 25,000, and with imprisonment of up to six months and fine for a second or subsequent offence. Section 26 addresses the worse fault of deliberately altering a weight or measure in a manner likely to make it appear to indicate more or less than the true quantity, punishable with a fine up to Rs 50,000 and, on repetition, imprisonment between six months and one year or fine or both. Section 27 punishes the manufacture or sale of a non-standard weight or measure with a fine up to Rs 20,000, and with imprisonment of up to three years or fine or both for a repeat offence, the steepest first-principles penalty in the chapter, since the maker of a false instrument manufactures every false transaction the instrument will ever perform.

The transaction offences protect the dealing itself. Section 28 punishes a transaction, deal or contract made in contravention of the prescribed standards, with a fine up to Rs 10,000 and up to a year of imprisonment on repetition. Section 29 does the same for quoting or publishing prices, charges or quantities in non-standard units, the offence that enforces section 11. Section 30 is the consumer's classic wrong: the person who, in selling or delivering goods or rendering services, delivers less or renders less than the quantity contracted for, or who in buying receives more, faces a fine up to Rs 10,000 and up to a year of imprisonment on a second offence. Sections 34 and 35 reach sale or delivery of commodities, and the rendering of services, by non-standard weight, measure or number, each with a fine of Rs 2,000 to Rs 5,000 and imprisonment of three months to one year on repetition.

The verification offences guard the stamp. Section 33 punishes the use of an unverified weight or measure with a fine of Rs 2,000 to Rs 10,000, and up to a year of imprisonment with fine for a repeat. Section 37 punishes a Government approved Test Centre that contravenes its conditions with a fine up to one lakh rupees, and the wilful verification, stamping or approval of an instrument against the Act with imprisonment up to a year, or a fine up to Rs 10,000, or both. Sections 42 and 43 discipline the guardians themselves: an officer who carries out a vexatious search, and an officer who wilfully verifies or stamps an instrument against the Act, each faces up to a year of imprisonment, or a fine up to Rs 10,000, or both. The offences against the machinery of trust are completed by section 44, punishing the counterfeiting of seals and stamps with imprisonment of six months to one year, rising on repetition to between six months and five years, and by section 40, which punishes obstruction of a legal metrology officer with imprisonment up to two years, and up to five years on repetition, the longest terms in the chapter, reflecting how much of the Act depends on the inspector being allowed to inspect.

The package offences sit in section 36, the section consumers actually live under. Section 36(1) punishes whoever manufactures, packs, imports, sells or distributes a pre-packaged commodity that does not conform to the prescribed declarations, with a fine up to Rs 25,000 for a first offence, up to Rs 50,000 for a second, and for a third and subsequent offence a fine of Rs 50,000 to one lakh rupees or imprisonment of up to one year or both. Section 36(2) punishes the quantity error itself, where the net quantity contained in a package errs beyond the prescribed limits, with a fine of Rs 10,000 to Rs 50,000, rising on repetition to a fine that may extend to one lakh rupees or imprisonment up to one year or both. The remaining offences fill the perimeter: section 31, failing to produce documents, records or returns, fine up to Rs 5,000; section 32, making or selling an instrument whose model is not approved, fine up to Rs 20,000; section 38, importing weights or measures without registration, fine up to Rs 25,000; section 39, importing non-standard weights or measures, fine up to Rs 50,000; section 41, giving false information or a false return, fine up to Rs 5,000; and sections 45 to 47, the licensing offences of manufacturing, repairing or selling without a licence, and tampering with a licence, with fines of Rs 20,000, Rs 5,000 and Rs 20,000 respectively, imprisonment entering each on repetition. Two general provisions complete the chapter. Section 51 provides that nothing in the Indian Penal Code, and section 153 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, shall apply to offences regarding weight or measure punishable under this Act, keeping metrology offences a self-contained code. Section 50 gives appeals: an appeal lies from the officer's decision within sixty days, extendable by a further sixty for sufficient cause, running to the Director and then to the Central Government for the Centre's officers, and to the Controller and then to the State Government for the State's, with the governments empowered to call for the record of a proceeding.

Stepping back, the grading of the chapter discloses its own theory of blame. The offences cluster into three moral bands. At the bottom stand the defaults of paperwork and routine: the unproduced register, the unapproved model, the unregistered import, the unverified instrument on its first detection, punished with money alone at first instance. In the middle stand the offences against the transaction: the non-standard dealing, the short delivery, the false quotation, the non-conforming package, where imprisonment waits for the repeat offender who has shown that a fine did not teach the lesson. At the top stand the offences against trust itself: the deliberately altered instrument, the counterfeit seal, the obstructed inspector, the vexatious search, where imprisonment is present from early in the scale because the conduct attacks the machinery by which every other offence is detected. A reader who sees the three bands can navigate the chapter without memorising it, and can also see, in advance, exactly where the decriminalisation of 2023 was bound to fall: on the boundary between the first band and the second, where default ends and dishonesty begins.

## Compounding and offences by companies: sections 48 and 49

Most contraventions of the Act never see a courtroom, and section 48 is why. It makes the principal offences compoundable, by the Director or the Controller, either before or after a prosecution is instituted, on payment of the prescribed sum, which the section caps at the maximum amount of fine that could be imposed for the offence. The allocation follows the federal split. The Director may compound offences under section 25 and sections 27 to 39. The Controller may compound offences under section 25, sections 27 to 31, sections 33 to 37, and sections 45 to 47. Compounding is barred for an offence of the same or a similar nature committed within three years of a previous compounding; once three years have run, the later offence is treated as a first offence. Where an offence is compounded before prosecution, no prosecution lies for it, and where it is compounded after, the composition has the effect of an acquittal of the accused in custody. The design is frank: the department trades the theatre of prosecution for prompt payment and a corrected trade practice, reserving the courts for repeat and aggravated conduct. The offences that carry the chapter's real menace, deliberate alteration under section 26, obstruction under section 40 and counterfeiting under section 44, are conspicuously outside the compoundable lists, which is the clearest signal in the Act of the line between error and fraud.

Section 49 then answers the question every company asks of a strict regulatory statute: who inside the firm is the accused? Its primary rule makes the person in charge of, and responsible to the company for, the conduct of its business liable for an offence committed by the company, and then it builds the mechanism that gives the section its fame. Under sub-section (2), a company may, by order in writing, nominate one of its directors to be the person responsible for compliance, with that director's written consent, and give notice of the nomination to the Director, the Controller or the authorised officer; different persons may be nominated for different establishments, branches or units of the same company. Where a nomination stands, the nominated person is the one proceeded against as the person in charge, and the nomination that appears in the compliance papers of every serious manufacturer and retail chain descends from this sub-section. The nomination is not an absolution machine. Any other director, manager, secretary or officer remains liable under sub-section (4) where the offence was committed with that person's consent or connivance, or is attributable to that person's neglect, and the person in charge may escape under the proviso to sub-section (1) by proving the offence was committed without that person's knowledge or that all due diligence was exercised to prevent it. Finally, sub-section (5) arms the court, after conviction of a company, to order publication of the company's name, place of business and the nature of the contravention at the company's own expense, though the order may not issue until the time for appeal has run or the appeal is disposed of: a reputational penalty calibrated to a field where the consumer's trust is the asset at stake.

In practice, the nomination is where the section meets the filing cabinet. A manufacturer or a retail chain that takes the Act seriously files its written nominations with the Director or the Controller for each establishment, keeps the consent of each nominated director with the nomination, and revises both when directors change, because a prosecution that finds the nomination stale or unsigned will proceed against the person in charge by default and the company will have lost the channel it built. The section also disciplines internal design: the nominated person must be a director with real authority over packing, labelling and sourcing, since the due diligence proviso asks what the accused did to prevent the offence, and a nominee without power is a confession that nobody held it. For the department, the mechanism converts the metaphysical question of corporate guilt into a correspondence with a named human being, which is why section 49 is, in the daily administration of this Act, among its most used provisions.

The appellate ladder of section 50 completes the machinery, and its design is of a piece with the rest: appeals in this Act run inside the executive, not to the courts. A person aggrieved by a decision of a legal metrology officer in the Centre's chain appeals to the Director, and from the Director to the Central Government; in a State's chain, the appeal runs to the Controller and then to the State Government. The limitation is sixty days, with a further sixty available where the appellant shows sufficient cause for the delay, and the governments hold in reserve the power to call for and examine the record of a proceeding, the revisional safety valve of sub-section (5). The arrangement suits a technical code: the appellate authorities are the officers who keep the standards, and a dispute about a tolerance or a stamp is decided, at each rung, by a superior of the same service that made the decision, with the ordinary jurisdiction of the courts on questions of law and jurisdiction standing behind the ladder rather than forming part of it.

## Decriminalisation: the Jan Vishwas amendments of 2023

The penalty structure just described is the Act as enacted, and it is no longer the whole position. The Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act, 2023, Act 18 of 2023, which received the President's assent on 11 August 2023, amended 183 provisions across 42 central statutes in a single exercise of decriminalisation for minor offences, and the Legal Metrology Act was one of the statutes it reached. The amendments to this Act were brought into force on 1 October 2023, by notification S.O. 4128(E) dated 19 September 2023. Their method was substitution: in the sections they touched, the old fine, and the imprisonment that attended repetition, were replaced by a ladder of higher monetary penalties alone.

The substitutions can be stated precisely. In section 25, use of a non-standard weight or measure, the penalty became a fine of one lakh rupees for a first offence, two lakh rupees for a second, and five lakh rupees for a third and subsequent offence. In section 27, manufacture or sale of a non-standard weight or measure, the ladder became one lakh, two lakh and four lakh rupees. In section 28, transacting against the prescribed standards, and in section 29, quoting or publishing in non-standard units, the ladders became fifty thousand rupees, one lakh rupees and two lakh rupees. In section 31, failure to produce documents, records or returns, the ladder became twenty-five thousand, fifty thousand and one lakh rupees. In sections 34 and 35, sale or delivery of commodities, and rendering of services, by non-standard weight or measure, the ladders became twenty-five thousand, fifty thousand and one lakh rupees. In each of these seven sections, imprisonment disappeared entirely; there is nothing left to be prosecuted for but money. The same statute amended section 48 to add the offence of giving false information or a false return under section 41 to the compoundable lists in sub-sections (1), (2) and (3), so that the false return joined the offences that the Director and the Controller may settle without a prosecution.

Equally important is what the 2023 Act did not touch, because the survival is the story. It left standing the imprisonment in section 26 for deliberate alteration of an instrument on repetition, in section 30 for short delivery on repetition, in sections 32 and 33 for unapproved models and unverified instruments on repetition, in section 36 for the non-conforming package and the net quantity error, in sections 38 and 39 for unregistered and non-standard imports on repetition, and throughout the offences against the enforcement machinery itself: obstruction under section 40, vexatious search under section 42, wrongful verification under section 43 and counterfeiting of seals under section 44, together with the licensing offences in sections 45 to 47 on repetition. The decriminalisation of this statute was therefore surgical rather than general. The imprisonment that Parliament withdrew was the imprisonment that hung over the routine instrument and paperwork offences of ordinary trade; the imprisonment that protects the stamp, the honest measure and the inspector's authority remains exactly where the 2009 Parliament put it. Any account of the Act that recites the old penalties for sections 25, 27, 28, 29, 31, 34 or 35 without the 2023 substitutions is stating a law that has ceased to exist for those sections, and any account that announces the Act decriminalised without qualification is overstating a reform that deliberately stopped at the fraud line.

Both cases on the reform deserve their strength. The case for it is proportionality and formalisation. A labelling defect or a first use of an unverified scale, produced by a small trader's ignorance of a verification date, attracted the machinery of criminal process: arrest was never likely, but prosecution, court appearances and the threat of imprisonment on repetition were real, and they bore hardest on the smallest businesses least able to keep compliance calendars. Criminal liability for paperwork errors also deterred formalisation, pushing marginal traders to stay outside the licensed economy the Act tries to universalise. The 2023 settlement answers this by making the consequence of the routine contravention a substantial, escalating fine, imposed without the stigma and delay of a criminal trial, in line with the amending Act's stated purpose of trust-based governance for ease of living and doing business.

The case against is deterrence arithmetic. A fine, however large, is a price, and a large seller can price it. Where imprisonment stood behind the second offence, the repeat player faced a consequence that money could not settle; under the substituted ladders, the same player compares the fine against the margin earned by the short measure or the non-standard instrument across thousands of transactions, and may rationally absorb the penalty as a cost of business. The burden of the new regime also lands unevenly: one lakh rupees is a rounding figure to a national chain and an existential sum to a single-shop trader, so the reform that relieves the small trader of the criminal court exposes that trader to a fine scaled for the large one. The composition of the settlement, in the end, is the answer the Act itself gives: Parliament kept imprisonment for every offence that involves deception of the measure or defiance of the officer, and removed it only where the contravention is capable of being pure default. Whether that line holds in practice will be decided by how the compounding lists and the surviving prosecutions are used, and it is the line on which any honest account of the 2023 reform must rest.

One quiet amendment in the same exercise shows the draftsman's intent as clearly as the headline substitutions. Before 2023, the offence of giving false information or a false return under section 41 stood outside the compounding lists, with the odd result that a trader who filed a false return faced the choice between a criminal prosecution and nothing, while the trader who used a false measure could settle on the spot. The addition of section 41 to sub-sections (1), (2) and (3) of section 48 removed that anomaly and completed the pattern: after the Jan Vishwas amendments, every offence in the Act that is capable of being a mere default, of money, measure or paper, can be closed by payment to the Director or the Controller, and every offence that remains outside the lists is outside for a reason the chapter itself supplies. The reform is best understood not as leniency but as sorting, the administrative state reserving its criminal process for the conduct that corrupts measurement, and pricing the rest.

## How a complaint works, and what it can win

The practical route into this statute runs through the State. A grievance about a short measure, an unverified instrument, a missing declaration or a sale above the printed price is made to the legal metrology officer of the district or to the Controller of Legal Metrology of the State, whose department maintains the inspectorate. The officer's response is administrative before it is criminal: inspection of the premises, testing of the instrument or the package, seizure where the Act permits, and then a fork between compounding the offence on payment under section 48 and prosecuting it. The complainant's contribution to that process is evidence, and in metrology cases the evidence is unusually tangible. The packet with its declarations, the bill with the price charged, and a note of the date and the asking price are usually the whole case, which is why they should be preserved exactly as received. The same documentary discipline decides disputes far beyond this statute, and accounts of [how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) describe, in another field, the same habit of keeping the record intact that wins a metrology complaint.

The trader's side of the same encounter is worth stating, since compliance is the cheapest outcome the Act offers. The trader who keeps instruments verified within their intervals, buys only from licensed manufacturers and repairers, checks incoming stock for the declarations before it reaches the shelf, and trains the counter staff that the printed figure is the ceiling and not a suggestion, has answered in advance nearly every charge the chapter contains. Where a contravention is detected, the compounding route rewards speed: most first defaults can be closed by payment without prosecution, and the three-year shadow that a compounding casts over a repeat offence is itself an incentive to fix the system rather than the instance. The Act, read as a whole, is considerably less interested in punishing traders than in making the next transaction honest, and its machinery, from the verification camp to the compounding desk, is pointed that way.

Two limits on the remedy should be understood before the complaint is filed. The first is that legal metrology proceedings punish the trader and correct the trade; the fine or the compounded sum is paid to the State, not to the complainant. A consumer who wants the excess refunded, or compensation for the loss, takes that claim to the consumer commissions under the consumer protection statute, where charging above the declared price and selling a deficient quantity can be pursued as the consumer wrongs they also are. The second limit is the theme of this article: the complaint must be framed in the statute's own language. A complaint that a packet was expensive will fail, because expense is not an offence the Act knows. A complaint that the same packet was sold above its printed maximum, or contained less than its declared quantity, or lacked a required declaration, states an offence the officer can act on the same day. This article is legal information about the statute, offered so that such complaints can be framed accurately; it is not legal advice on any individual dispute.

The two tracks are cumulative, not alternatives to be elected between. A single act of overcharging can draw a metrology prosecution or compounding for the offence against the declaration regime and a consumer complaint for the unfair trade practice, and neither proceeding answers the other, because each vindicates a different interest: the first, the integrity of the system of declared quantities and prices on which all buyers rely; the second, the individual buyer's money. Traders occasionally protest that this is double punishment for one act, and the answer is the one the structure gives: the metrology penalty is not compensation and the consumer award is not a fine, and a legal system that separates the policing of a market from the redress of a purchaser will routinely run both for a single dishonest transaction. The complainant who understands the separation will file in both places without embarrassment, and the trader who understands it will see why a compounded offence does not close the file.

## The declaration, not the price

The series of which this article is a part has returned repeatedly to a single finding: a statute is most often misused by being invoked for a purpose it does not serve, and the Legal Metrology Act, 2009 may be the purest instance in the whole field of consumer law. It is invoked daily as a price law. It is not one. It is a law about the trustworthiness of quantity: about whether the kilogram is a kilogram, whether the instrument that weighed the goods was tested against a standard that traces to the national reference, whether the packet tells the buyer, in a prescribed and legible form, what is inside it, when it was made, whose it is, and the most that may be charged for it. The maximum retail price, which attracts nearly all the argument, is in the statute's own scheme only one declaration among several, distinguished from the others by being the one a seller may not exceed in the act of sale.

Held to that scheme, the Act is coherent and its controversies fall into place. The hotel litigation was never about whether hotels may charge for service; it was about whether a composite service is a sale, and the Supreme Court answered the question the statute asks. The decriminalisation of 2023 was never a repeal of the offences; it was a repricing of default, which left the imprisonment for fraud and defiance intact. The abolition of standard pack sizes was never deregulation of the packet; it was a transfer of the standardising effort from the size of the packet to the information on it, completed by the unit sale price. A reader who holds the namable claim, that the statute governs the declaration and not the price, can predict the outcome of most disputes in this territory before reaching the section that decides them, and can say with precision which grievances the Act will remedy and which it will politely decline, because they belong to another statute, another entry of the Concurrent List, and another idea of what law is for.

The three readers this article set out to serve can each take a working rule away. The consumer's rule is to read the packet as the statute writes it: the printed maximum is the ceiling of an ordinary purchase, the net quantity is a promise enforceable through the samples of the inspectorate, and the complaint that succeeds is the one framed in quantities and declarations rather than in prices. The trader's rule is that compliance under this Act is a calendar and a shelf, not an argument: instruments verified in their intervals, licences and nominations in their files, incoming packets checked for their declarations before they are displayed, and the printed figure treated at the counter as the end of the negotiation. The student's rule is structural: whenever a metrology problem appears, ask first which of the statute's three machines it belongs to, the standards that define the unit, the officers who verify against it, or the offences that punish its corruption, and the section that answers will rarely be far away. A statute that began as two books and became one has, in the end, a single lesson to teach, and it is printed on every packet it governs: say what the thing is, say how much of it there is, say the most that may be charged for it, and let the market do the rest.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Legal Metrology Act regulate?

The Act regulates the standards of weights and measures and everything that depends on them. It adopts the metric system based on the international system of units, requires transactions to be made in the prescribed standard units, requires every weight or measure used in trade to be verified and stamped, and governs the declarations that a pre-packaged commodity must carry, including the maximum retail price. It appoints a Director for inter-State trade and Controllers and legal metrology officers in the States to enforce it, and it punishes false measures, non-standard quotations, defective packages and kindred defaults. It does not fix prices or judge whether a price is fair; it regulates the measure, the instrument and the statement on the packet.

### Q: What must a packaged commodity declare?

Under Rule 6 of the Legal Metrology (Packaged Commodities) Rules, 2011, every package meant for retail sale must declare the name and complete address of the manufacturer and, where different, of the packer, or of the importer for imported goods; the common or generic name of the commodity; the net quantity in the standard unit of weight, measure or number; the month and year of manufacture; the maximum retail price inclusive of all taxes; the unit sale price; and the name, address, telephone number and email address for consumer complaints. Imported packages must also declare the country of origin, and a commodity that becomes unfit for consumption with time must carry a best before or use by date. The declarations appear on the principal display panel of the package, in the prescribed size and manner.

### Q: Can a seller charge more than the maximum retail price?

On an ordinary retail sale, no. Rule 18(2) of the Packaged Commodities Rules forbids any retail dealer or other person, including a manufacturer, packer, importer or wholesale dealer, from selling a commodity in packed form at a price exceeding the retail sale price declared on the package. The rule binds the whole chain, the declared price may not be altered once printed, and the excess is an offence however small it is. Two qualifications belong to other rules and settings rather than to this prohibition: the Supreme Court has held that the ceiling does not govern a composite service in a hotel or restaurant, and the rules provide a narrow route by which a revised price may be charged where a tax change intervenes after packing.

### Q: Does the maximum retail price apply in hotels and restaurants?

Within limits settled by the Supreme Court, it does not govern the bill. In Federation of Hotel and Restaurant Associations of India v. Union of India, decided on 12 December 2017, the Court held that neither the predecessor statutes nor the Legal Metrology Act, 2009 applies so as to interdict the sale of mineral water in hotels and restaurants at prices above the printed maximum, because what is supplied in such an establishment is a composite and indivisible service, to which the supply of the commodity is incidental, and not a sale of the package as such within the Act's definition. The holding construes the word sale; it is not an exemption written into the Act. It does not affect the ordinary retail sale across a counter, where the ceiling binds in full, and consumer organisations continue to contest its reach in captive settings such as cinemas and airports.

### Q: What legal metrology declarations apply to goods sold online?

Since the amendments of 2017 took effect on 1 January 2018, goods displayed for sale on an electronic commerce platform must carry the declarations the Packaged Commodities Rules require, displayed on the platform before the purchase is made. The listing must show the name and address of the manufacturer, packer and importer, the name of the commodity, the net content, the retail sale price and the consumer care details, among the other required particulars; the month and year of manufacture or packing is the one declaration that belongs only to the physical package. Where the platform operates a marketplace and merely provides access to a communication system, responsibility for the correctness of the declarations rests with the manufacturer, seller or importer whose goods are displayed.

### Q: Who enforces legal metrology and how?

Enforcement is divided between the Union and the States along the line the Constitution draws. A Director of Legal Metrology, appointed by the Central Government under section 13, carries the Act for inter-State trade and commerce, importer registration and model approval. A Controller, appointed by each State Government under section 14, carries it for intra-State trade, and the legal metrology officers under the Controller do the field work: verifying and stamping instruments, inspecting premises, examining records, testing packages for net quantity, seizing non-standard or unverified weights and measures and packages that do not conform, and initiating prosecutions. Detected offences are frequently compounded under section 48 rather than prosecuted, and appeals run to the Director or Controller and then to the Central or State Government under section 50.

### Q: What legal metrology penalties apply and were they decriminalised?

As enacted, the offences in Chapter V carried graded fines, with imprisonment entering on repetition: a non-conforming package attracted a fine up to Rs 25,000 under section 36, rising on repetition, and an unverified instrument, a short delivery or a non-standard quotation each had its own graduated scale. The Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act, 2023 partly decriminalised the statute with effect from 1 October 2023, substituting higher fines alone for imprisonment in seven sections: 25, 27, 28, 29, 31, 34 and 35. It did not touch section 36, and imprisonment survives for deliberate alteration of an instrument, obstruction of an officer, counterfeiting of seals, repeat short delivery and the other offences on the fraud side of the Act. The penalties for the seven substituted sections must be read in their amended form.

### Q: What happened to standard pack sizes for packaged commodities?

They were abolished. Under the original Packaged Commodities Rules, 2011, Rule 5 and the Second Schedule prescribed the quantities in which nineteen specified commodities had to be packed, and any other size was non-compliant. The Amendment Rules of 2021 omitted Rule 5 and the Amendment Rules of 2022 omitted the Second Schedule; after staged deferrals from the appointed date of 1 April 2022, the changes took effect in the closing months of 2022. A manufacturer may therefore pack a commodity in any quantity, and the standard sizes survive only as commercial practice. The declaration requirements were not relaxed in exchange; instead, the unit sale price was introduced so that packets of differing sizes could be compared by their price per gram, litre or unit.

### Q: What is a pre-packaged commodity under the Legal Metrology Act?

Section 2(l) defines it as a commodity which, without the purchaser being present, is placed in a package of whatever nature, whether sealed or not, so that the product contained in it has a pre-determined quantity. The test is the sequence: the goods are packed first, in the buyer's absence, with the quantity settled before any buyer appears. In Whirlpool of India Ltd. v. Union of India, (2007) 14 SCC 468, the Supreme Court held that a refrigerator packed by the manufacturer is a pre-packaged commodity even though dealers unpack it for display and testing, because the quantity was fixed at packing. Sunglasses that are opened, tried and fitted at the counter were held in the Kataria case to fall the other side of the line, since the handling forms part of what the buyer is choosing.

### Q: What does verification and stamping of a weight or measure involve?

Section 24 forbids the use of any weight or measure in a transaction, or for protection, unless it has been verified and stamped. Verification is the physical comparison of the instrument against the working standards of the legal metrology department, carried out by legal metrology officers or by a Government approved Test Centre for the kinds of instruments notified. If the instrument measures true within its tolerance, the officer stamps it, and the stamp is the public warrant that the instrument was tested and passed. Re-verification falls due at the intervals the State rules prescribe for each class of instrument, and an unverified instrument used in trade is an offence under section 33 and liable to seizure and forfeiture under section 16, subject to the owner's right to get it verified within the prescribed time.

### Q: What is the unit sale price that a package must declare?

Rule 6(11) of the Packaged Commodities Rules requires every pre-packaged commodity to declare its unit sale price: the price per gram where the net quantity is less than a kilogram and per kilogram where it is more, per millilitre below a litre and per litre above it, per centimetre below a metre and per metre above it, and per number or unit where the commodity is sold by number, rounded to the nearest two decimal places. The declaration is not required where the retail sale price is itself the unit price. It was introduced by the amendments of 2021 alongside the removal of standard pack sizes, and its purpose is comparison: with pack sizes free to vary, the unit price is the figure that tells the buyer which of two differently sized packets is cheaper for the same quantity.

### Q: Can two different maximum retail prices be declared on the same commodity?

No, subject to one reservation. Rule 18(2A) of the Packaged Commodities Rules, inserted by the Amendment Rules of 2017 and in force from 1 January 2018, provides that unless some other law specifically provides otherwise, no manufacturer, packer or importer shall declare different maximum retail prices on an identical pre-packaged commodity. The practice it abolished, called dual MRP, printed a higher ceiling on packets destined for premium or captive venues such as airports, cinemas and malls, so that the higher charge at the counter appeared to be a sale at the printed price. With one product carrying one maximum, the ceiling printed on the packet is the ceiling everywhere, and any venue-specific price above it must be defended, if at all, on the composite service ground decided by the Supreme Court, not on the packet.

### Q: Who must register as an importer of weights or measures?

Every person who imports a weight or measure into India must register with the Director of Legal Metrology under section 19 of the Act, so that the Central Government maintains a roll of those who bring measuring instruments into the country. Registration stands alongside the substantive bar in section 20, under which a weight or measure that does not conform to the standards prescribed under the Act may not be imported at all. Importing a weight or measure without registration is an offence under section 38, punishable with a fine that may extend to Rs 25,000 for a first offence and with imprisonment of up to six months, or fine, or both, on repetition. The registration is of the importer of the instrument, and is separate from the declarations regime that governs the labels on imported packaged goods.

### Q: What is model approval for a weight or measure, and when is it required?

Model approval under section 22 is the certification of the design of a weight or measure before instruments of that kind are made or brought into the country. A weight or measure of a prescribed kind may not be manufactured or imported unless its model has been approved by the prescribed authority after the prescribed tests, so that accuracy is established at the level of the pattern rather than left to the inspection of each instrument. The section exempts certain simple instruments, including cast iron, brass, bullion and carat weights, beam scales, the length measures used in retail textile and timber trade, and capacity measures up to twenty litres used for kerosene, milk and potable liquors. A model approved in a country outside India may be approved with or without further test, which is how international pattern approvals enter the Indian system.

### Q: What happens if the net quantity in a package is less than the quantity declared?

A shortfall is judged first against the maximum permissible error the Packaged Commodities Rules prescribe for the quantity declared, since no packing process is exact and the rules allow a small tolerance. An error beyond that limit is an offence under section 36(2) of the Act: the person who manufactures, packs, imports, sells or distributes the package is punishable with a fine of Rs 10,000 to Rs 50,000 for a first offence, and on a second or subsequent offence with a fine that may extend to one lakh rupees or imprisonment of up to one year or both. Legal metrology officers test for quantity by drawing and weighing samples of packages in inspection, and a consumer who finds a packet materially light can complain to the Controller of the State with the packet and the bill as evidence.

### Q: Can an offence under the Legal Metrology Act be compounded instead of prosecuted?

Yes, for most of the offences that arise in ordinary trade. Section 48 empowers the Director to compound offences under section 25 and sections 27 to 39, and the Controller to compound offences under section 25, sections 27 to 31, sections 33 to 37 and sections 45 to 47, either before or after a prosecution is instituted, on payment of a prescribed sum not exceeding the maximum fine for the offence; the offence under section 41 was added to the lists in 2023. Compounding is refused where the same person has committed the same or a similar offence within three years of an earlier compounding. A compounded offence cannot then be prosecuted. The offences of deliberate alteration, obstruction and counterfeiting of seals stand outside the lists and are never compoundable.

### Q: Who is liable when a company commits an offence under the Legal Metrology Act?

Under section 49, the person who was in charge of, and responsible to, the company for the conduct of its business when the offence was committed is liable, as is the company itself. A company may instead nominate one of its directors, by order in writing with that director's written consent, as the person responsible for compliance, giving notice of the nomination to the Director, the Controller or the authorised officer; different persons may be nominated for different establishments, branches or units. The person proceeded against may escape by proving the offence was committed without that person's knowledge or that all due diligence was exercised to prevent it. Other directors and officers remain liable where the offence was committed with their consent or connivance or is attributable to their neglect, and a convicted company may be ordered to publish its conviction at its own expense.

### Q: Does the Legal Metrology Act fix or control the prices of goods?

It does not. The Act never prescribes what the price of any commodity should be, and it establishes no machinery for fixing prices. What it does is require the manufacturer, packer or importer to declare a maximum retail price on the package and then forbid any sale of that package above the figure declared. The ceiling is the seller's own number, printed under the Act's discipline; a high declared maximum offends nothing in this statute, and a complaint that a product is overpriced, in the sense of priced too high by its maker, is outside the Act altogether. Actual price control in Indian law belongs to a different entry of the Concurrent List and to statutes such as the Essential Commodities Act, 1955, which authorise the government to fix or control prices by order.

### Q: Can a packaged commodity be sold for less than its maximum retail price?

Yes. The declared figure is a maximum and not a price, so a sale at any figure at or below it is lawful, and discounts, offers and competition below the ceiling are exactly what the form of the declaration invites. Nothing in the Act or the Packaged Commodities Rules obliges a seller to charge the full maximum, and the rules speak throughout of a ceiling rather than a tariff. The offences gather at the other side of the line: selling above the declared maximum on an ordinary retail sale, altering the printed figure, and declaring two different maxima on the same commodity. A consumer who is quoted a price below the printed maximum needs no provision to rely on; a consumer quoted a price above it has the whole of Rule 18(2) to rely on.

### Q: Which packages are exempt from the packaged commodity declaration rules?

The declaration regime of Chapter II of the Packaged Commodities Rules is built for packages intended for retail sale, and its edges follow that purpose. A retail package containing a quantity of more than 25 kilograms or 25 litres is not required to carry the Rule 6 declarations, since packages of that size are traded on terms closer to bulk sale than to the packaged-goods mischief the declarations address. Packages meant for industrial or institutional consumers, such as factories, hotels buying as institutions and other enterprises, follow a distinct and lighter pattern of declarations rather than the retail panel. Wholesale packages, which pass between traders and are not sold to a final consumer in that form, carry their own set of particulars under the Rules instead of the retail declarations.
