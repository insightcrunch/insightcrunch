French criticism of Romeo and Juliet begins with a paradox that no other national tradition matches in quite the same form. France was the first foreign country to argue about Shakespeare in print, the country that produced the most systematic case against his kind of drama, and the country that eventually carried the play into opera, symphony, and its national library. Between those poles lies a reception unlike the English one, in which admiration was early and continuous, and unlike the German one, in which a translator's triumph settled the question for a century. The French record is a negotiation that lasted two hundred years, conducted in prefaces, pamphlets, adaptation workshops, concert halls, and editorial committees, and the play was its favourite test case because it committed, in their most concentrated form, every offence the French classical stage had defined and offered, in the same five acts, the beauties that made those offences impossible to dismiss.

![Harriet Smithson as Juliet in the Paris Odéon season, when French criticism met the play on stage - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-106.webp)

The verdict argued through the whole of this article is that France did not, in the end, surrender to the play. France naturalized it by rewriting it, generation after generation, and the famous Romantic embrace changed the rhetoric of French criticism far faster than it changed the French text of the play. Voltaire's objections were never answered on their own ground; they were outlasted, routed around, and quietly absorbed into the practice of adaptation, which kept one hand on the scissors long after the manifestos had declared unconditional love. The documentary proof is a sequence no survey of opinions can explain away: the first French anthology of Shakespeare left the play out entirely; the first French staging cut the brawl and the balcony; the two great French musical settings both borrowed an ending altered in London by David Garrick; and the Comédie-Française, the house of Molière and Racine, waited until 1952 to admit a version its own historian calls faithful. French criticism's long ambivalence is not a failure of taste to be regretted. It is the most instructive reception the play has anywhere, because France kept the accounts of what it was doing: every stage of the negotiation left a dated document, and the documents, read in order, show a national culture deciding what it could afford to admire.

## What Did French Taste Object to in Romeo and Juliet?

The neoclassical charge sheet was not a vague distaste. It was a codified system, descending from Aristotle through the French Academy's judgment on Corneille's Le Cid in 1637 and Boileau's Art poétique of 1674, and it specified exactly what a tragedy owed its audience: unity of action, unity of time compressed toward a single revolution of the sun, unity of place, a separation of genres so strict that comedy and tragedy might not share a stage, the decorum that kept violence offstage and reported it in noble verse, and a hierarchy of persons in which kings and princes spoke and servants, when they appeared at all, kept to the antechamber. Judged by that system, the Veronese tragedy was not a flawed tragedy. It was a compendium of every prohibited thing, assembled with a perverse completeness, as if the playwright had worked from the rule book in reverse.

Begin with the opening minutes. The Prologue, a sonnet spoken by a Chorus, promises "the fearful passage" of a "death-marked love" (Prologue, 9), and the play then opens not on princes but on two serving men trading bawdy puns about maidenheads before biting their thumbs at the opposing household: "Do you bite your thumb at us, sir?" (1.1.42). For a French classical audience, the offence was triple and immediate. Low persons opened a tragedy; they opened it with obscenity; and the obscenity was not ornament but mechanism, since the thumb-biting ignites the first of the play's public brawls, fought in full view with swords, clubs, and citizens crying "Down with the Capulets! Down with the Montagues!" (1.1.84). Onstage violence, mixed ranks, prose where verse should be: the French objection was not prudery about a rude gesture. It was that the play's engine ran on materials tragedy was forbidden to touch.

The mixture never lets up. The ball scene alternates a formal masque with the Nurse's garrulous prose and lets the lovers meet inside a perfectly regular sonnet, "If I profane with my unworthiest hand" (1.5.92), whose closing joke, "You kiss by th' book" (1.5.109), turns a liturgy of pilgrims and saints into a flirtation. Mercutio's Queen Mab speech, "O, then I see Queen Mab hath been with you" (1.4.53), is a detachable fairy fantasia of ninety lines that advances no action and obeys no decorum of scale, while the same Mercutio dies punning, "Ask for me tomorrow, and you shall find me a grave man" (3.1.96-97), and cursing both households, "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1.99). A wit cracking jokes on his own death wound broke the classical law twice over, because the death of a principal figure belonged to the highest register of pathos, and because wit itself was the property of comedy. The Friar's plot machinery offended differently: a cleric who marries children in secret, "For this alliance may so happy prove, / To turn your households' rancour to pure love" (2.3.91-92), and then supplies a sleeping potion whose forty-two hours of counterfeit death drive the last two acts, imports the devices of romance and farce into tragedy's centre. Add the apothecary, a starving tradesman bribed to sell poison in defiance of Mantua's law, the heroine's age of not quite fourteen announced in the first act, corpses accumulating onstage until five bodies crowd the tomb, and a timescale of four or five days scattered across streets, orchards, cells, and two cities, and the French verdict wrote itself. The play had no unity to violate because it had never heard of one.

What saved it, in French eyes, was equally specific, and the specificity matters because it shaped everything that followed. The balcony scene's exchange, opening "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks?" (2.2.2) and rising through the rose speech, "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other word would smell as sweet" (2.2.43-44), offered beauties of a kind French verse tragedy itself prized: passion analysed in balanced periods, metaphor sustained and resolved, a heroine who reasons about language while she falls in love. The parting at dawn, "Parting is such sweet sorrow" (2.2.184), and Juliet's solitary terrors before the potion, and the last scene's concentrated grief, "O happy dagger, / This is thy sheath" (5.3.169-170), could be detached, translated, and admired as pearls. The French word that governs the whole reception was coined in this act of salvage. Again and again, from Voltaire onward, French critics praised the play's beauties and deplored its form, as if the beauties were contraband smuggled inside an illegal structure. The history that follows is the history of a culture trying to keep the pearls and refuse the setting, and discovering, across two centuries, that the setting was the price of the pearls.

The heroine herself stood in the indictment. Juliet is not quite fourteen, a fact the play insists on; she receives a suitor's visit in her mother's presence, exchanges vows with a stranger the same night in her orchard, and it is she who converts the vow into a contract, directing the marriage with a practicality that overturns every French decorum of the jeune fille: "If that thy bent of love be honourable, / Thy purpose marriage, send me word tomorrow" (2.2.142-143). Racinian heroines burn, confess, and die, but they do not manage. A girl of thirteen who proposes, schedules, and stage-manages her own wedding, drinks a drug to escape a second one, and stabs herself awake in a tomb broke the French law of pudeur as thoroughly as the brawls broke the law of kinds, and French adaptation treated her accordingly for a century, aging her in effect, softening her initiative, and surrounding her with the pathos of victimhood. The objection matters to the reception's later course, because the Romantics' Juliet, Smithson's Juliet of 1827, triumphed precisely by restoring what the decorum had removed: a heroine whose desire acts.

Two further counts in the indictment deserve their own paragraphs, because they governed French practice long after the general theory was forgotten. The first is probability, the vraisemblance on which French tragedy staked its claim to rationality. The catastrophe of the Veronese play turns on a sequence its French judges found mechanically unworthy of tragedy: a friar's potion that simulates death for forty-two hours, a letter that never arrives because its bearer is quarantined in a plague house, a bridegroom who reaches the tomb minutes too early and a bride who wakes minutes too late. In the classical economy, catastrophe was to unfold from character and passion with the necessity of a syllogism; here it unfolds from a pharmacist's vial and a failed postal delivery. French critics returned to the machinery for a century, because it crystallized their deepest suspicion, that the play's effects were purchased by contrivance rather than earned by design. The second count is the clergy. A Franciscan friar who is the plot's engineer, who hears confessions, celebrates a clandestine marriage in his cell, dispenses drugs, and presides over the final explanation, placed on the tragic stage a figure the French theatre, formed in a Catholic monarchy yet bound by a decorum that kept sacred persons and rites at the margin of tragedy, found doubly foreign: too sacred for farce, too busy for reverence. When the Prince closes the play over five corpses and distributes blame, "See what a scourge is laid upon your hate" (5.3.292), French taste heard a chronicle's confusion where English audiences heard a city's judgment on itself.

## Voltaire and Shakespeare: The Patron Who Turned Prosecutor

Every account of Shakespeare in France begins with Voltaire because Voltaire arranged that it should. Exiled in England from 1726 to 1728, he attended the London theatres, read the dramatists in the original, and came home determined to import English freedoms into a French literature he judged magnificent and cramped. His Lettres philosophiques, published in French in 1734 after an English edition the year before, contained the eighteenth letter, on tragedy, which performed the founding act of French Shakespeare criticism: it introduced the playwright as a force of nature and condemned him in the same breath. Shakespeare, Voltaire told his readers, was England's Corneille, a genius of force and fecundity, natural and sublime, who nevertheless possessed not a spark of good taste and knew not one rule of the drama. His plays were monstrous farces to which the name of tragedy had been given, yet they contained beautiful, noble, and terrifying scenes that explained their success. English poetic genius resembled a tufted tree planted by nature, throwing out a thousand branches at random; it would die if forced into the trimmed shapes of the gardens of Marly. The metaphors did double work for two centuries. France would repeat the concession, genius, and the sentence, no taste, as if they were a single indissoluble judgment, and Romeo and Juliet, which concentrates the sublimity and the irregularity in one action, became the judgment's standing illustration.

The brief's verification flag concerns attribution, and the record supports a precise statement of what Voltaire did and did not say about this play. He wrote no essay on Romeo and Juliet, conducted no scene-by-scene prosecution of it, and singled it out for neither praise nor blame beyond the general verdicts. His judgment of the play was delivered by classification. Everything his 1734 letter condemned in Shakespeare's theatre, the mixed tones, the low persons, the violated unities, the deaths exhibited to the eye, this play exhibited in full; everything he praised, the strong scenes of passion and nature, the balcony and the tomb supplied in full. When later French critics attacked or defended the Veronese tragedy in Voltaire's vocabulary, they were applying a system whose author had aimed it at the whole English stage, and the play's French fate was decided for decades by critics who rarely needed to open it. Voltaire's own borrowings underline the doubleness of the patronage. His Zaïre of 1732, the most successful French tragedy of the century, rebuilt Othello's jealousy plot under classical discipline, proof that he wanted Shakespeare's energy at French conditions: the ore imported, the minting domestic. He spent his career as that mint's master, and his tragedy of the 1730s and 1740s admitted ghosts, tombs, and onstage deaths into French practice while his criticism policed the border he had crossed.

### Did Voltaire ever write about Romeo and Juliet directly?

No sustained treatment of the play exists in Voltaire's work: no essay, no commentary, no scene-by-scene verdict on the Veronese tragedy. His judgment reached the play by classification, through the general theory of Shakespeare's theatre announced in the Lettres philosophiques in 1734 and sharpened in the Académie letter of 1776.

The early Voltaire, indeed, went further in practice than his letter's formula suggests, and the record of his conduct belongs in any fair account of his criticism. The discourse on tragedy he prefixed to his own Brutus in 1731, addressed to his English patron Lord Bolingbroke, praised the English stage's licence to show action, blood, and the tumult of history, and held that licence up as a reproach to French timidity; his own early tragedies experimented with ghosts, daggers, and scaffold scenes that the Paris stage had not admitted. This was the Voltaire who functioned as Shakespeare's French patron in deed, enlarging the national theatre's permissions from inside its most prestigious genre, and his authority with the actors and the Academy made the enlargement stick. The later prosecution cannot be understood without the earlier patronage, because the bitterness of 1776 is the bitterness of a sponsor disowned: the young men who deified Shakespeare in the 1770s were spending capital Voltaire had raised, and his letter to the Académie reads, in its private asides, like a founder's protest against his own foundation. French criticism inherited the doubleness entire. For two centuries its characteristic gesture toward this play would be Voltaire's own: to enlarge the permission and resent the beneficiary.

The turn came with age and with rivals. From the 1760s, as translations multiplied and younger writers began to speak of Shakespeare as a model rather than a mine, Voltaire's tone hardened from magisterial ambivalence to something nearer alarm. He had opened the border; he had not intended an invasion. The climax is documented exactly. Pierre Le Tourneur's new complete translation, appearing from 1776, hailed its author in its preface as the god of the theatre, and Voltaire answered with a letter to the Académie française, read aloud on 25 August 1776, the feast of Saint Louis, protesting the deification and repeating, in embittered form, the figure of his youth: Shakespeare remained a barbarian whose works were a huge dunghill in which a few pearls lay hidden. The image completed a circle. In 1734 the pearls had justified the introduction; in 1776 the dunghill justified the resistance; and Voltaire confessed in his correspondence the wound beneath the doctrine, complaining that he had been the first to show the French the few pearls he had found in that enormous heap, as if the discoverer were being robbed by his own discovery. John Pemble, whose Shakespeare Goes to Paris of 2005 is the standard modern history of this long affair, reads Voltaire's reversal as the first instance of the pattern his book traces throughout: in France, Shakespeare was never only a playwright. He was a measure of French cultural confidence, praised when France felt secure enough to borrow and denounced when borrowing began to look like subordination. The adjudication offered here accepts Pemble's reading and extends it to this play. Romeo and Juliet was the test case because it was the hardest to borrow safely: a tragedy whose beauties were inseparable from the irregularity that made borrowing it an act of cultural surrender.

The afterlife of Voltaire's formula inside the eighteenth century confirms that it functioned as law. His lieutenants, Jean-François Marmontel in his criticism and Jean-François de La Harpe in his lectures, administered the estate, and Marie-Joseph Chénier, the Revolution's own dramatist, inclined to the master's view even while the political world overturned; the Dictionary of National Biography's old survey of the question judges that Paul Duport's Essais littéraires sur Shakespeare of 1828 was the last work by a French critic of repute to repeat Voltaire's censure unreservedly. The date is the point. Voltaire's sentence on Shakespeare was pronounced in 1734, sharpened in 1776, and still required, in 1828, a critic of reputation to defend it in full, one year after the Odéon season had begun its repeal by experience. For this play the sentence's long administration had a specific consequence that the documents make visible: there was no French Romeo and Juliet to defend. Until Ducis, the play existed in France only as an entry in a foreign catalogue, condemned in advance by its author's classification, excerpted nowhere, staged never. When French writers of the 1820s took up the play's cause, they were not correcting a French tradition of reading it. They were introducing it, for the second time, against the resistance of the man who had introduced it first.

## How Did France First Read the Play? La Place, Le Tourneur, and the Missing Lovers

Print reached the play before the stage did, and the print record begins with an omission that speaks louder than most inclusions. Pierre-Antoine de La Place's Le Théâtre anglois, published between 1745 and 1749, was the first French attempt to present English drama whole: eight volumes, opening with a discourse on the English theatre and a life of Shakespeare, and devoting its Shakespeare volumes to a generous selection of the plays in prose renderings and summaries. French readers met Othello there, and Richard III, Hamlet, Macbeth, Cymbeline, Julius Caesar, Antony and Cleopatra, Timon of Athens, and The Merry Wives of Windsor. They did not meet Romeo and Juliet. La Place, a translator of real courage who softened what he judged untransportable and cut what he judged coarse, simply passed the Veronese tragedy by, and the omission is evidence of the exact weight of the neoclassical objection in the 1740s. A translator building France's first English theatre chose the histories, the Roman plays, and the tragedies of state and ambition, and left on the shelf the one play whose subject was private passion, whose machinery was a friar's potion, and whose stage was crowded with servants, nurses, and street fighters. What France could first read of Shakespeare was Shakespeare disciplined by selection before he was disciplined by rewriting.

### What did La Place's anthology give French readers, and what did it withhold?

La Place's Théâtre anglois of 1745 to 1749 gave France its first English theatre in French: a discourse, a life of Shakespeare, and prose renderings or summaries of nine of the plays, from Othello and Hamlet to Cymbeline and The Merry Wives of Windsor. It withheld Romeo and Juliet entirely, and France's acquaintance with the play waited a generation.

Le Tourneur's Shakespeare traduit de l'anglois changed the scale of everything. Published in Paris between 1776 and 1783 in twenty volumes, dedicated to Louis XVI and presented to the royal family with ceremony, it was the first attempt at a complete Shakespeare in French prose claiming fidelity as its programme, and this play stood in it entire, among the tragedies, for any reader to possess. The preface's enthusiasm detonated the quarrel described in the previous section: Le Tourneur saluted Shakespeare as the god of the theatre, Voltaire protested to the Académie, and the exchange fixed the poles of French debate for fifty years. Yet fidelity in Le Tourneur was a relative term. His prose was stately, his Shakespeare decently dressed; allusions of low register and passages of lewdness were suppressed or thinned, so that French readers received the whole design of the Veronese tragedy, its five acts, its brawls and balcony and tomb, wrapped in a diction that quietly repaired the improprieties Voltaire had catalogued. The pattern that governs the entire French reception is already present in these two publications. In print, France advanced by selection and softening; on paper, the play could be complete before it could be literal. The revision of Le Tourneur by François Guizot and Amédée Pichot in 1821, fronted by Guizot's substantial essay on Shakespeare's life and works, carried the process into the Romantic generation with better scholarship and the same instinct for the decent paraphrase, and Guizot's essay, reissued as Shakespeare et son temps in 1852, became the respectable Frenchman's Shakespeare for decades.

The two inaugurations of 1776, Le Tourneur's volumes and Voltaire's letter, deserve a final joint reading, because together they define the channel through which the play flowed into French culture for the next fifty years. Le Tourneur's Shakespeare was a reader's Shakespeare, bought by subscription, blessed by the crown, and shelved in libraries; his Romeo and Juliet could be studied entire, its plot weighed, its famous speeches savoured in dignified prose, by exactly the class of French reader who would never have been permitted to see its like upon a stage. Voltaire's protest, meanwhile, policed the border between that library and the theatre: the god of the theatre might be read, but the theatre itself, the national institution with its committee, its pensionnaires, and its memory of Racine, was to be defended against him. The division of labour persisted. Across the Revolutionary decades the play circulated in France primarily as print, in Le Tourneur's stately periods and then in Guizot's revision, while the stage made do with Ducis. Criticism of the play in this long interval is therefore largely a criticism of a reading experience, conducted by men judging a prose narrative of the tragedy, and it helps explain the peculiar abstraction of so much French Shakespeare commentary before 1827, its readiness to debate systems, genius against taste, north against south, while the stage event that might have settled the argument waited for an English company to provide it.

## Ducis and the Classicized Stage: A Romeo and Juliet With No Brawling and No Balcony

What print omitted, the stage corrected, in the French sense of correction that means confinement within the rules. Jean-François Ducis, who knew no English and worked through French intermediaries, made the first sustained effort to put Shakespeare's tragedies before a French audience, beginning with Hamlet in 1769, the first Shakespeare play performed on a French stage. His Roméo et Juliette followed at the Comédie-Française on 27 July 1772, announced by its genre label as a tragedy imitated from the English, and it held the stage for generations. It was not the play. Michèle Willems, in her study Classicizing Shakespeare, published in the Arden Shakespeare series, gives the process its exact name: Ducis classicized his originals, remodelling plot, persons, and scenic arrangement until the English matter could pass the French customs house of the unities and decorum. For the Veronese tragedy the remodelling was radical. Pemble's summary is brutal and accurate: Ducis gave France a Romeo and Juliet with no brawling and no balcony. The opening street war, the engine of the feud's public madness, disappeared; the orchard scene, the play's most famous concentration of lyric passion, disappeared with it; and the Comédie-Française's own archival history records the substitution at the adaptation's centre, since Ducis made old Montaigu, the father, the true hero of his version, drawing the figure partly from Dante, who had named the Montecchi and Cappelletti among the feuding houses of Italy six centuries before. The tragedy of the lovers became, in French dress, a tragedy of the fathers, in which repentance and paternal grief carried the weight that Shakespeare had given to desire.

The speed of the parody tells the same story from below. Within a year, in 1773, Paris had a burlesque, Roméo et Paquette, attributed to a woman writing as Mademoiselle Carrière-Doisin, which replaced the Capulets with a family called the Cascarets, Mercutio with a figure called Gargaric, and the double suicide with a garden party at which everyone drinks wine and celebrates the lovers, its title page brazenly claiming a Verona imprint for a book printed in Paris. A parody is a reception document of the first order: it proves the original adaptation was familiar enough to be mocked, and it reveals what the culture had actually absorbed, a famous story of feuding houses and young love, detached already from Shakespeare's structure and available for farce. Ducis himself should not be mocked as a vandal. He worked honestly within an institution whose reading committee enforced the rules as conditions of performance, his adaptations carried Shakespeare's plots across Europe in retranslations, and he succeeded Voltaire in his chair at the Académie in 1778, a succession rich in irony, since the man who had raged against Shakespeare's barbarous histrionics was replaced by the man who had tamed them. The adjudication on Ducis belongs to Willems's account, which this article adopts: classicizing was not a failure of nerve but a technology of import, the only one the French stage possessed in 1772, and its cost is measurable in this play above all, because the elements it removed, the brawl and the balcony, are precisely the elements in which Shakespeare's mixture of public violence and private lyricism consists. France's first Romeo and Juliet was the tragedy with its thesis deleted.

The classicized text, meanwhile, had a European career of its own that measures how completely France mediated the continent's first Shakespeare. Ducis's adaptations, reprinted and revered at home, were translated onward into Italian and the languages of eastern Europe, carrying his regularized versions to audiences who believed they were receiving the English original; a German company in 1776 performed the lovers' tragedy from a Swedish translation that rested on Ducis's French; and Russia's first Romeo and Juliet, translated in 1772, the very year of the Paris premiere, arrived through the same French mediation that governed the continent's supply. The irony deserves emphasis because it reverses the usual direction of the story. The France that prosecuted Shakespeare for barbarism became, through the industry of its adapters, barbarism's exporter in a tailored suit: half of Europe first met the Veronese lovers in Ducis's cut, with Montaigu grieving at the centre and the balcony unbuilt. When the nineteenth century's translators in those countries returned to the English text, they were not discovering a new play so much as discovering how much of the play France had withheld from them, and the French mediation of Shakespeare, begun by Voltaire's letter, completed by Ducis's stage, stands as the precondition of the Romantic reaction's violence. The young men of 1827 were not only rejecting their grandfathers' taste. They were reclaiming a text their grandfathers' culture had spent sixty years keeping from them in plain sight.

## Madame de Staël and the Northern Turn: Shakespeare Reclassified

The reversal of the French verdict began as a work of criticism, not of the theatre, and its author was the most formidable French intelligence of the Revolutionary generation. Madame de Staël's De la littérature of 1800 rebuilt the map on which Shakespeare had been condemned. Where the classical tradition arranged literatures by their obedience to rules perfected in antiquity, Staël arranged them by climate, history, and national spirit, dividing Europe's poetry into a literature of the south, Homeric, pagan, and serene, and a literature of the north, melancholy, philosophical, and Christian in its depths, whose genius was Shakespeare. The move dissolved the charge sheet by changing the court. Irregularity in a northern poet was no longer a failure to be French; it was fidelity to a different and equally legitimate order of feeling, and the melancholy of the northern literatures, their taste for tombs, spectres, and inward passion, was precisely the element French classical tragedy had excluded and modern readers craved. For the Veronese play the reclassification mattered in detail. A tragedy of young love conducted through gardens, night scenes, a churchyard vault, and two suicides was, by Staël's scheme, not a barbarous accident but a characteristic product of the northern imagination, to be judged by the intensity of its feeling rather than the regularity of its frame.

Staël's second act of mediation carried the argument into the heart of the German achievement and delivered it to France in French. Her De l'Allemagne, printed in Paris in 1810 and destroyed on Napoleon's orders as an offence against French supremacy, then published in London in 1813, introduced French readers to the German critical revolution, above all to the Vienna lectures of August Wilhelm Schlegel, in which the opposition of classical and romantic drama had been given systematic form and this very play had been read as the apotheosis and the funeral rite of love, a sigh sustained across five acts. The lectures in which [the German Romantics and Schlegel's reading of the play](/2026/10/30/schlegel-german-romantics-reading/) had converted Shakespeare's irregularity into a rival poetics reached France, therefore, through a Frenchwoman's prose and under the colours of national resistance to an emperor. The comparative point deserves one sentence and no more: Germany had its verdict delivered by a translator's finished work, while France received the same doctrine as contraband philosophy, and the difference in the manner of arrival explains much of the violence of the French quarrel that followed. Staël did not stage the play, translate it, or analyse its scenes at length. Her gift to its French history was jurisdiction. After De la littérature and De l'Allemagne, a French critic could admire the balcony scene without pleading guilty to barbarism, because admiration had acquired a theory.

Staël's own relation to the play itself illustrates the period's division between theory and text with almost comic precision. The woman who did more than anyone to acquit Shakespeare before French opinion never translated him, never staged him, and engaged his scenes hardly at all; her Shakespeare is a climate and a civilization, a proof in a philosophy of literature, and her famous pages defend the northern genius in terms that would fit the Veronese tragedy perfectly while never pausing on its balcony. The Coppet circle around her, the exiles and visitors who gathered at her Swiss château, made the defence a European salon doctrine, and the young French writers who frequented her orbit in the Restoration years received Shakespeare as an article of the liberal creed, allied to constitutional politics and Protestant seriousness against the throne-and-altar classicism of the returned Bourbons. That political colouring, which Staël's work made unavoidable, explains a feature of the French reception that purely literary histories miss: for a generation, to praise this play in Paris was to declare a party. The classicists understood the declaration perfectly, which is why Auger's manifesto of 1824 was delivered not in a journal but at the Institut, the state's own academy, and why the quarrel over a Veronese love story could draw the police attention that had already destroyed De l'Allemagne. French criticism of Romeo and Juliet was never, in these decades, only about Romeo and Juliet.

## Stendhal's Racine et Shakespeare and the Romantic Brief Against the Rules

Stendhal turned Staël's jurisdiction into a fighting programme. His paired pamphlets Racine et Shakespeare, published in 1823 and 1825, the second written to answer an anti-Romantic manifesto pronounced by Louis-Simon Auger at the Institut, are the founding documents of French Romantic theatre criticism, and their method is confiscation: they seize the enemy's central term and redefine it. Romanticism, Stendhal argued, is the art of presenting a people with the literary works that, in the present state of its habits and beliefs, are capable of giving it the greatest possible pleasure; classicism is the literature that gave the greatest possible pleasure to that people's great-grandfathers. By that definition Racine had been a romantic for the court of Louis XIV and was a classic for the Restoration, and Shakespeare, who wrote for the strong souls of a rougher England, was the standing proof that a national drama lives by pleasing its own age rather than obeying a dead one. The pamphlets demanded a French theatre of energy and history, prose where prose served the illusion, and an end to the unities as anything but conveniences. Stendhal even preferred prose tragedy and distrusted the coming Romantic verse drama, a fastidiousness history overruled, but his central reversal held: after 1825, regularity was no longer self-evidently a virtue in French criticism. It was a position, and positions can be defeated.

For this play the pamphlets worked indirectly and decisively. Stendhal's Shakespeare is a general banner, Hamlet and Othello his usual exhibits, and the Veronese tragedy gains from his campaign not an analysis but a climate: a young generation of Parisian writers learned from him to treat the rules as a party programme and the English stage as the arsenal of the opposition. The scholarly infrastructure moved in parallel. Guizot's 1821 revision of Le Tourneur, with its long prefatory essay weighing Shakespeare's genius historically rather than judicially, taught the respectable classes to read the plays as documents of an age; Abel-François Villemain's lectures and essays of the 1820s performed the same office in the university; and the young men who would storm the theatres at the decade's end, Hugo, Vigny, Dumas, Gautier, and the composer Berlioz, absorbed from pamphlet, edition, and lecture a single conviction: that Shakespeare was the future of French drama wearing an English face. What none of this yet supplied was the experience of the play itself, unmediated, in a theatre. Print had carried the controversy for ninety years. In September 1827, performance took it over, and French criticism of Romeo and Juliet acquired the thing it had always lacked: an event.

Stendhal's criticism repays one more moment of attention, because his account of theatrical pleasure contains the seed of the French verdict on this play's most condemned device. His test for any drama was the moment of illusion, the instant in which the spectator forgets the theatre entirely, and he argued that such moments are rare, brief, and destroyed by whatever recalls the machinery, including verse declaimed for its own music. Judged by that test, the Veronese tragedy's notorious contrivances change their character. The potion, the letter, and the tomb are not syllogisms and were never offered as such; they are instruments for manufacturing the instant of illusion at maximum pressure, the waking that comes a minute late, and the French audiences of 1827, weeping at Smithson's terror in the tomb scene, experienced exactly the surrender Stendhal had theorized and the classical critics had declared impossible by such means. His pamphlets also carried an Italianate authority the Parisian debate valued: Stendhal wrote as a man formed by Milan, where Shakespeare had been naturalized without a Voltaire to prosecute him, and his cosmopolitan impatience with Parisian rules presented the French quarrel as provincial, a village's defence of its customs against the literature of Europe. The young Romantics took the hint with gratitude. If the rules were provincial, then breaking them was not barbarism but emancipation, and the play that broke them all while breaking hearts became, in the later 1820s, the opposition's most useful exhibit.

## September 1827: The Odéon Season and Harriet Smithson's Juliet

The English company's season at the Odéon in the autumn of 1827 is the best-documented theatrical shock in French cultural history, and the play stood near its centre. The company, under the management associated with William Abbot and led on stage by Charles Kemble, opened its Shakespeare with Hamlet on 11 September 1827, Kemble in the title role and the Anglo-Irish actress Harriet Smithson as Ophelia. Her mad scene, played with a naturalism of gesture and mime that French declamation had never attempted, convulsed the house; among the spectators were Alexandre Dumas, who recorded that the performance revealed to him what drama could be, and Hector Berlioz, who wrote in his Memoirs that Shakespeare, falling on him unawares, struck like lightning and opened the heaven of art to its farthest depths. Four days later, on 15 September, the company gave Romeo and Juliet in David Garrick's acting version, with Kemble as Romeo and Smithson as Juliet, and the effect repeated itself in a different register. Smithson's Juliet made the heroine, in the French phrase the season earned her, the equal of her partner: where the classical stage had treated the woman in tragedy as an occasion for noble lament, Smithson's physical, impulsive, terrified, and resolute Juliet showed a generation of French artists a tragic heroine who acted. She became, for a season and more, the figurehead of the French Romantic movement, her Ophelia and her Juliet reproduced in prints, poems, and paintings, and Berlioz's pursuit of her, through the Symphonie fantastique of 1830 to their marriage in 1833, bound the play to the biography of French Romanticism itself.

The national house supplied the counterpoint in the same season, and its own archives preserve the embarrassment. The Comédie-Française's history of the play records that a new production mounted there in 1827 closed after three performances, even as the Odéon, a boulevard house playing in a foreign tongue, was turning Parisians away. The juxtaposition needs no commentary, but it deserves one: in the autumn of 1827 Paris contained two Romeo and Juliets, a French one in the temple of the national drama, correct, adapted, and dying in three nights, and an English one in Garrick's mangled text, unintelligible in its words to half its audience, and received as a revelation. Whatever French criticism decided afterward, it decided in the knowledge of that experiment, in which the variable was not the story, not the language, and not the audience, but the manner of embodying the play. The rules had been weighed against the event, and the event had won.

Two cautions belong to the record, and both strengthen rather than weaken the event's importance. First, the text the Odéon audience applauded was not Shakespeare's. Garrick's version, standard on the London stage since the middle of the previous century, cut and rearranged freely and altered the ending so that Juliet wakes in the tomb before Romeo dies, giving the lovers a final interview Shakespeare had denied them. The French Romantics' founding experience of the play was thus mediated by an English classicizing of exactly the kind Ducis had practised, a fact the celebrants did not know and the historian must not forget. Second, the shock belonged to acting as much as to writing: what the French witnesses describe, in memoir after memoir, is a manner of performance, bodies in emotion, silence used as speech, madness and love rendered without the mediation of declaimed verse. The season's critical consequence was therefore double. It armed the Romantic party with an experience no classicist could argue away, and it fixed in French minds an image of the play, Garrick's tomb, Smithson's terror and tenderness, that French versions would reproduce for decades. When Victor Hugo published the preface to Cromwell in December 1827, weeks after the season, and enthroned Shakespeare as the supreme name of the modern drama, the manifesto had an audience that had seen, or heard at one remove, exactly what the theory meant. The preface's doctrine, that the modern muse joins the grotesque to the sublime and refuses the separation of kinds, is the Odéon season translated into a system.

The season's wider repertory confirms that the shock was systematic rather than accidental. Othello followed on 18 September, with Smithson as Desdemona, and the company went on to its greatest commercial success in Rowe's Jane Shore, a tragedy of exactly the pathetic heroine the French stage had trained its audiences to weep for, now played in a manner that made the French manner look embalmed. Paris responded with the full apparatus of a craze: Smithson's portrait circulated, her gestures were imitated, her name attached itself to fashions, and the young men of the Romantic cénacles, Gautier and Nerval among the witnesses whose generation would staff the movement, treated the Odéon evenings as a conversion experience to be dated like a birth. The critical writing that followed in the journals, much of it by men who had been in the house, abandoned the old judicial vocabulary altogether; nobody who had seen the tomb scene attempted to discuss the play as a problem in the unities. That silence is itself the season's most eloquent document. Voltaire's categories were not refuted in September 1827. They were made unusable, in public, by an actress, and French criticism took a generation to build the new vocabulary, part Staël, part Stendhal, part Hugo, with which to say what the audience at the Odéon had already decided.

## The Romantic Romeo: Deschamps, Vigny, Hugo, and the Battle for the Repertoire

The Romantics understood that manifestos do not naturalize a foreign classic; repertoire does. The test came at once. In 1828 Émile Deschamps and Alfred de Vigny, the two poets then most identified with the new school, produced a verse Romeo and Juliet together, Deschamps the more practiced translator of the pair, Vigny already at work on the Othello that would reach the Théâtre-Français in 1829 as Le More de Venise. Hugo, who saw the stakes exactly, hoped their version would do for the French stage what the Odéon season had done for French imaginations and spark the Romantic revolution in the national theatre itself. It did not. The actors of the Comédie-Française admired the young men's version, the institution hesitated, and in 1832 the version that entered the Repertoire was Frédéric Soulié's, which the house's own history characterizes as staunchly classicist. The sequence is the French nineteenth century in miniature: enthusiasm outside the walls, caution at the committee table, and a compromise text on the stage. Hugo's Hernani had its famous battle and victory at the same theatre in February 1830, but Hernani was a French original imitating Shakespearean freedoms. The Veronese play itself, in French, in the national house, remained a negotiated text, and the Deschamps and Vigny version waited for print and for posterity rather than for performance.

Vigny's case is worth a paragraph because it shows the embrace's sincerity and its limits in a single career. He knew English well, had lived in England, and translated with a fidelity ambition far beyond Ducis; yet the 1911 Britannica's verdict on his Othello, a translation or rather a paraphrase, catches the gravitational pull of French verse decorum on even a willing translator. George Sand, unorthodox in everything else, softened Shakespeare's savage genius in her As You Like It for the Comédie-Française in 1856, as Pemble notes with relish. The pattern is not hypocrisy. It is the discovery, repeated by each Romantic in turn, that French theatrical French, the alexandrine, the audience's ear, and the institution's memory all taxed every act of importation, and that the tax was paid in the currency of alteration. Naturalization by rewriting was not the failure of the Romantic programme. It was the programme's operating cost, visible nowhere more plainly than in this play, whose beauties were lyric and whose lyric beauties invited recomposition.

The feuilleton critics did the embrace's daily work, and Théophile Gautier's name stands for the corps. Gautier, who had worn the famous red waistcoat at the battle of Hernani, became from 1836 the drama critic of La Presse and spent four decades reviewing the Paris stage with Shakespeare as his standing measure of what theatre could be. Week by week, for the newspaper public that never opened a Pléiade or a pamphlet, Gautier's notices normalized the once-barbarous repertory, treating the English plays as the classics of the modern stage and judging French novelties against them; and the Veronese tragedy, in its procession of French versions, operas, and revivals, passed repeatedly under his affectionate and exacting eye. Reception histories built on books and manifestos can miss this stratum, but a national verdict is finally delivered in the newspapers, and by the 1850s the Parisian press had delivered this one: the play was no longer a foreign case to be tried. It was part of the furnishings of French theatrical life, argued about as property, which is what naturalization means when it is complete everywhere except the text.

The embrace found its monument not on the stage but in the study. Between 1859 and 1866, François-Victor Hugo, the poet's son, published his complete prose translation of Shakespeare in eighteen volumes, undertaken in exile's long leisure and intent on a fidelity his predecessors had not attempted: this translation kept the puns, the coarseness, and the prose where earlier French versions had smoothed them, and it stands as the French equivalent of the Schlegel and Tieck achievement in German, arrived a generation later and in prose rather than verse. His father supplied the enterprise with its thunder. Victor Hugo's William Shakespeare, published in 1864 for the tercentenary of the playwright's birth, began as the introduction to the son's translation and swelled into a three-hundred-page meditation on genius itself, in which Shakespeare stands at the head of a procession running from Job and Homer through Dante and Rabelais to the nineteenth century. French critics of the day joked that the book should have been titled Myself, and the joke contains the verdict this article will defend in its place: Hugo's Shakespeare is the largest act of French homage and the least French act of reading, a genius worshipping genius across the Channel, valuable as the embrace's charter and useless as a guide to the play. Yet charters matter. The tercentenary year made Shakespeare a French civic event, with Alfred Mézières's Shakespeare, ses œuvres et ses critiques of 1860 preparing the scholarly ground and the jubilee of 1864, studied in Catherine Treilhou-Balaudé's landmark reception history of 1994, crowning the process by which France's former barbarian became an occasion for national self-congratulation. By the time Gounod's opera reached the stage in 1867, the critical war was over. What remained unsettled, as the opera itself proves, was the text.

The jubilee year of 1864 supplies the embrace's official portrait, and its details repay the reception historian. Shakespeare's tercentenary was celebrated in France as a state occasion of letters, with Alfred Mézières, whose Shakespeare, ses œuvres et ses critiques of 1860 had given the French academy its sane and documented appreciation, publishing his souvenirs of the Stratford pilgrimage, and with the London commemoration committee enrolling the exiled Hugo among its honorary vice-presidents while Stratford's own committee courted his translating son. Catherine Treilhou-Balaudé's study of the jubilee reads the festival as the moment the French conquest of Shakespeare became a French self-coronation: the nation that had prosecuted the barbarian now presented itself as his truest European heir, with Hugo, banned from France for a dozen years and refusing amnesty, cast by acclamation as the living proof. For the Veronese play the jubilee's meaning is gentler and sadder. The lovers had become, by 1864, the common property of French sentiment, quoted in albums, painted on salon walls, and set by every composer with a lyric gift; and precisely that familiarity completed the process the documents have traced throughout, in which France possessed the story so thoroughly that the text could be left unread. The play's French apotheosis and its French eclipse are the same event, and the scholars of the following century, from Jusserand onward, would have to excavate the tragedy from beneath its own monument.

## Berlioz's Roméo et Juliette: Criticism by Other Means

The most searching French criticism of the play in the nineteenth century was written in orchestral score. Berlioz never forgot the Odéon evening of 15 September 1827, and when Paganini's gift of twenty thousand francs, pressed on him after a concert in December 1838, freed him from journalism's treadmill, he spent it on the play. Roméo et Juliette, a dramatic symphony for soloists, choruses, and orchestra, to a libretto fashioned by Émile Deschamps, the same Deschamps who had translated the play with Vigny a decade before, was composed between the first weeks of 1839 and September of that year and first performed under the composer's direction at the Paris Conservatoire on 24 November 1839, with the score dedicated to Paganini. Its form is a critical argument. Berlioz distributes the drama between voices and instruments according to a theory of what each medium can bear: the feud and the public world go to the choral Prologue, which narrates the story in recitative like a Greek chorus naturalized in Verona; Mercutio's Queen Mab goes to a tenor scherzetto and then to the orchestra alone in the famous scherzo, wit rendered as pure velocity; the funeral procession and the tomb are given their ritual weight; Friar Laurence closes the work with a choral oath of reconciliation. The love scene itself, the symphony's still centre, is entrusted to the orchestra without a single sung word.

### Why did Berlioz keep the love scene wordless?

Berlioz argued the point in the preface printed with the score: the lovers' dialogue is of a sublimity that sung words would only diminish, while instrumental music, vaguer and therefore richer, could render the passion itself. The decision is the Romantic embrace made structural: fidelity to the balcony scene required translation out of language altogether.

The symphony also preserves, fossilized in glory, the mediation this article has tracked throughout. Berlioz's tomb scene follows Garrick: his Juliet wakes, as Smithson's had, to a final exchange with the dying Romeo, because the work memorializes the performance of 1827 rather than the text of 1597, which Berlioz, who had no English in 1827 and encountered the play first through Deschamps and the French versions, received at several removes. None of this diminishes the score, which conductors and critics have ranked among the supreme Shakespeare compositions; it defines it. Roméo et Juliette is the French reception's masterpiece precisely because it is not a reading but a transfiguration, the play converted into the medium in which French genius of that generation was freest. The neoclassical objection had complained that the play mixed incompatible kinds. Berlioz answered by unmixing them into a new kind of his own, choral symphony, in which feud, wit, love, and ritual each receive the medium fitted to them. It is Voltaire's charge sheet, answered by a composer who accepted its premise, that the kinds must be separated, and gloried in the separation.

The work's internal architecture deserves a closer inventory, because Berlioz builds the French reception's whole history into it without intending to. The Introduction renders the feud as an orchestral fugato, street violence translated into learned counterpoint, the brawl Ducis had deleted returning as the symphony's first sound. The Prologue then does openly what French criticism had done for a century: it summarizes the story in advance, in choral recitative, domesticating the irregular plot by announcing it as a fable, even retaining a version of the lovers' fate as a moral to be contemplated rather than a surprise to be suffered. The second part gives Romeo his solitude and the Capulet ball its glitter; the love scene follows, wordless, at the work's centre; the Queen Mab scherzo converts the speech Voltaire's tradition would have cut as a detachable fantasia into the symphony's most admired movement, proof that the French genius for the miniature had found in Mercutio's prose a subject after its own heart. Juliet's funeral procession and the tomb scene darken the design, and a second prologue, later withdrawn by the composer, once prepared the final sequence in which Friar Laurence, alone among the principals entrusted with extended solo song, swears the reconciled families to peace over the bodies. The young Richard Wagner, in Paris in the winter of 1839 and present at the Conservatoire performances, recorded that the work overwhelmed him, and the line from Berlioz's tomb to the mature music drama is a matter of his own testimony. France's Romeo had become, by 1839, a European influence in its own right, no longer an import under negotiation but an export of the first rank, and the exporter was a symphony that contained not one line of Shakespeare's verse.

## Gounod's Roméo et Juliette and the Lyric Settlement

Charles Gounod's opera, composed in 1865 to 1867 to a libretto by Jules Barbier and Michel Carré and first performed at the Théâtre Lyrique in Paris on 27 April 1867, completed the musical naturalization and exposed its terms with unusual clarity. Barbier and Carré, professionals of the French lyric stage who had already given Gounod his Faust, reduced the play to its lyric skeleton: four great duets for the lovers carry the structure, from the ball through the balcony and the wedding night to the tomb, and the supporting world, feud, friar, nurse, Mercutio with his Queen Mab ballad, survives as frame and colour. Juliette's waltz song in the first act, the famous "Je veux vivre", was an interpolation tailored to the creator of the role, Marie Miolan-Carvalho, who wanted brilliance where the libretto had planned declamation; the opera's most beloved number is thus an exhibit of the French system itself, the performer's legitimate demand reshaping the imported text. The tomb scene repeats the century's signature alteration: Gounod's Juliet wakes from the draught while Romeo's poison is still working, and the lovers sing their last duet together before she stabs herself as he dies. Garrick's ending, born on the London stage of 1748, naturalized at the Odéon in 1827, monumentalized by Berlioz in 1839, received its operatic passport in 1867, and the work travelled accordingly, to the Opéra-Comique in 1873 and to the Paris Opéra itself on 28 November 1888, the summit of French lyric consecration.

Set the opera beside the criticism and the settlement's shape becomes visible. French theory, from Staël through Stendhal to Hugo, had by 1867 entirely reversed Voltaire's verdict on the play's kind: the mixture was glory, the irregularity was nature, the lovers were the type of modern passion. French practice, in the same years, continued to adjust the play toward French lyric expectations whenever it was actually embodied: duets where Shakespeare wrote soliloquies and scenes, a waltz where he wrote a girl's reluctance, a waking Juliet where he wrote the tragedy's loneliest irony, that she sleeps through the one arrival that could have saved them both. The verdict on Gounod is not that he betrayed the play; his opera is, on its own terms, the most successful French Romeo ever made, and its survival in the international repertoire proves the success was not parochial. The verdict is that the opera demonstrates the rule of the whole French reception: France's embrace was real, passionate, and permanently conditional on recomposition. The play was welcomed as a quarry of situations and a licence for lyricism, and welcomed most warmly exactly where it had ceased to be itself.

The opera's subsequent career rounds off the musical evidence. Within the year of its premiere it was heard in London, in Italian, and in New York; it entered the Opéra-Comique in 1873 and the Opéra in 1888; and it settled into the international repertoire as the Romeo and Juliet the world sings, a French work whose libretto generations of foreign audiences have taken for the play itself. A reception history must note the completion of the circle with some dryness. The country that began by refusing the tragedy a place in its first Shakespeare anthology ended by supplying the version through which much of the world knows the tragedy best, and that version embodies, in perfected form, every adjustment the French tradition had practised since Ducis: the feud softened into atmosphere, the heroine given her showpiece, the tomb rearranged for a final duet, the whole irregular structure resolved into lyric number and ensemble. Gounod did not corrupt a French Romeo that existed before him. He distilled the only French Romeo that had ever existed, the one manufactured by a century of negotiation, and gave it the immortality its makers had always intended for it. The criticism and the opera are the same fact at different temperatures.

## What Did Modern French Criticism Make of the Play?

The twentieth century converted the quarrel into a discipline. Jean-Jacques Jusserand's Shakespeare en France sous l'ancien régime of 1898, first serialized in Cosmopolis, founded the scholarly study of the subject by treating the old French hostility as a historical problem rather than a scandal, and the institutional settlement followed in stages, each stage measurable by what the French text of the play had become. The Comédie-Française's own archive supplies the emblematic date: after the adaptations proposed by the dozen across the previous century, eight of them between 1852 and 1916 alone, and after André Rivoire's commissioned Juliette et Roméo of 1920, it was Jean Sarment's version that, in the house historian's words, definitively left free adaptation behind, simplified while remaining faithful, and brought the play into the Repertoire in 1952, where it was performed sixty-eight times in three seasons before the house let it sleep until 2015. Fidelity, in the national theatre, is a twentieth-century French achievement, and a fragile one.

The editorial settlement kept pace. Henri Fluchère, the Anglicist who directed the Pléiade Œuvres complètes of 1959 to 1963 with a foreword by André Gide, admitted the play to the leather-bound canon in which France keeps its classics, in the generation's poetic translation by Pierre Jean Jouve; his Shakespeare, dramaturge élisabéthain of 1966 then gave French readers a native account of the dramatist's craft informed by the Oxford debates of those years. The founding of a professional community followed: Jean-Marie Maguin's survey of Shakespeare studies in France since 1960, written for the Internet Shakespeare Editions, records a field organized around the Société française Shakespeare, working in close collaboration with the stage, and no longer asking whether Shakespeare may be admired but how he is to be edited, translated, and taught. In that field the play's French discussion joined the international conversation on equal terms; the quarrel of schools that fills the rest of this series reached France as method rather than as invasion. France also exported one of the century's commanding readings outright. René Girard, the French theorist whose account of desire as imitation made the feud's two households into doubles of a single wanting, built the most influential French interpretation of the play's violence in the modern period; the full adjudication of [René Girard's reading of mimetic desire and the feud](/2027/01/15/rene-girard-mimetic-desire-feud/) belongs to its own article, but its passport is part of this one's record, since the country that had once prosecuted the play for formlessness ended by supplying the theory that explained its form. French theory's other export travelled by adoption: the poststructuralist instruments forged in Paris were carried into the play's criticism by Anglophone readers, among them [Catherine Belsey's account of desire and the body in the play](/2027/01/22/catherine-belsey-desire-body/), whose Lacanian machinery is French theory naturalized abroad, the mirror image of Shakespeare naturalized in France.

Fluchère's critical book deserves a sentence of its own in this history, because it marks the point at which French criticism of the play stopped importing English verdicts and started exporting French ones. Shakespeare, dramaturge élisabéthain of 1966 reads the plays as the work of a professional dramatist writing for a known stage, a known company, and a known audience, against the Romantic inheritance that had made Shakespeare a force of nature beyond technique; applied to the Veronese tragedy, that method directs attention to construction, to the management of the double plot of love and feud, to entrances, exits, and the disposition of bodies in the tomb, the very matters the French classical tradition had been trained to notice and the French Romantic tradition had been trained to ignore. The wheel had come full circle with a difference: France was once again reading the play as a piece of dramatic engineering, as Voltaire's generation had demanded, but now with the engineer's respect for a design that worked. That reunion of the two French traditions, judicial craft and passionate homage, is the modern settlement's intellectual form, and the editions that embody it are its institutional form.

The settlement's capstone is editorial. In April 2002 Gallimard published the Tragédies volumes of the new bilingual Pléiade Œuvres complètes, edited under the direction of Jean-Michel Déprats with the collaboration of Gisèle Venet, with a preface by Anne Barton and new translations by a team that included Line Cottegnies, Robert Ellrodt, Richard Marienstras, Henri Suhamy, Catherine Treilhou-Balaudé, and Déprats himself, the most active translator of Shakespeare for the French stage of his generation. The format is the verdict in physical form: English and French on facing pages, the text established and annotated, nothing cut, nothing prettified, the play presented to French readers as France presents Molière. Beside it stands the reception scholarship that made such confidence possible, from Treilhou-Balaudé's history of the nineteenth-century conquest in 1994 to Pemble's of 2005 and Michèle Willems's study of Ducis. That the French recovery of this play belongs to the same broad movement as [the twentieth-century rehabilitation of the play](/2027/04/16/twentieth-century-rehabilitation/) in the English-speaking world is plain; what distinguishes the French case is that the rehabilitation had two full centuries of documented resistance to overcome, and kept the documents.

The facing-page format has a consequence beyond scholarship that completes the reception's logic. A bilingual edition assumes a reader who checks the translation against the original, line by line, and a French audience for the Veronese tragedy now does exactly that, in classrooms and studies, as a matter of course: the French text is no longer a substitute screened by doctrine but a companion held accountable to its English neighbour. Every previous settlement had asked French readers to trust an intermediary, La Place's taste, Ducis's rules, Le Tourneur's decency, Hugo's genius. The settlement of 2002 asks them to verify. In the history of this play in France, verification is the final form of respect, and it is the first settlement in two hundred and seventy years that required nothing to be cut, softened, or forgiven.

One technical thread runs beneath the whole modern settlement and explains why it took the form it did: the alexandrine. French verse tragedy spoke in the twelve-syllable line, rhymed in couplets, and every verse translation of Shakespeare into French before the twentieth century faced the choice of forcing the English pentameters, with their mixtures of verse and prose, high speech and low, into that single majestic metre or abandoning verse altogether. Ducis chose the alexandrine and paid with the play's variety; Le Tourneur and François-Victor Hugo chose prose and paid with its music; Jouve's poetic prose of the Pléiade generation tried to keep the images at the price of the metre; and the modern stage translations associated with Déprats and his contemporaries settled the account by matching form to form, verse with supple verse and prose with prose, so that the thumb-biting servants and the sonnet-sharing lovers could at last speak French in different registers, as they speak English. The mixture that Voltaire condemned as barbarism turned out to be, in the translator's workshop, a technical problem with a technical solution, and its solution is the material form of the French verdict's reversal. A criticism that had begun by judging the play's soul ended by solving its prosody.

Mercutio deserves separate notice in any account of the French difficulty, because he concentrates it. His great speech is a prose fantasia on a fairy midwife no larger than an agate stone, delivered at a run and detachable without loss to the plot; his death converts a pun into a prophecy; and his function, to embody the world's wit, sexuality, and violence in a single disposable figure, has no equivalent in the French tragic repertory, where such energies were assigned to comedy and kept there. French versions handled him as the rules required: reduced, refined, or removed, and with him went the play's internal critic, the voice that mocks the lovers' rhetoric while the rhetoric is still charming them. The loss is diagnostic. A culture that could not house Mercutio could not house the play's self-knowledge, its awareness that the language of love is also a performance, and French criticism's long preference for reading the tragedy as a pure poem of passion, undivided and unironic, begins in that amputation. When Berlioz set the Queen Mab speech as an orchestral scherzo of gossamer speed, he was solving, in music, the exact problem the translators had failed in words: how to give French form to a wit whose essence is that it cannot be paraphrased.

## Why This Play? The Veronese Tragedy as France's Test Case

A question remains that the chronicle, left to itself, does not answer: why did this play, among all of Shakespeare's, become the French quarrel's favourite ground? Hamlet had the earlier French stage history and the larger philosophical prestige; Othello gave Voltaire his Zaïre; Macbeth and Lear drew translations and imitations in plenty. Yet when the documents are counted, it is the Veronese tragedy that recurs at every station of the French reckoning: omitted by La Place, staged first among the tragedies after Hamlet by Ducis, parodied within a year, danced around by Staël's theory, carried into the Odéon triumph, claimed by Deschamps and Vigny, symphonized by Berlioz, operatized by Gounod, and installed by the Pléiade. The explanation lies in the peculiar relation between the play and France's own tragic inheritance. French classical tragedy was, at its greatest, a tragedy of love under law: Racine's heroines burn inside structures of duty, dynasty, and prohibition, and the French audience's competence in passionate analysis was the finest in Europe. Romeo and Juliet invaded that home ground. It offered French spectators a love tragedy whose lovers were not princes of the blood but children of merchants' houses, whose confidants were a nurse and a friar rather than the licensed confidants of the Racinian stage, whose passion was argued in sonnets and consummated in marriage within three days, and whose catastrophe was administered by a potion and a missed letter rather than by the gods, the state, or the heroine's divided soul. Every element either outraged or electrified the audience whose own tradition had perfected the analysis of love in confinement, and the play's French history is the record of that double response working itself out across two centuries.

The feud gave French readers a second, political purchase on the play that purely amorous readings understate. Verona's disorder is civic before it is domestic: an ancient grudge breaking to new mutiny in the streets, a prince whose edicts fail three times, citizens arming with clubs and bills, and a settlement purchased with the lives of both houses' heirs. France, whose own history supplied the Wars of Religion, the Fronde, and, living memory by the time Staël wrote, a revolution that had begun in fraternity and ended in proscription, was exceptionally equipped to read a tragedy of faction, and French commentary across the nineteenth century repeatedly treats the feud not as scaffolding but as the play's political substance: the portrait of a city that can be reconciled only over its children's bodies. The Prince's closing audit, apportioning punishment to all and pardon to some, "See what a scourge is laid upon your hate" (5.3.292), spoke to a nation that had conducted the same audit upon itself. This civic reading is one reason the play outlasted the quarrel that introduced it: generations of French readers who cared nothing for the unities found in Verona a mirror their own classics, with their courts and their camps, did not hold up to them.

The play also returned to France a form France believed it owned. The lovers' first exchange is a sonnet, built line by line between two voices, "If I profane with my unworthiest hand" (1.5.92), and the sonnet was the prestige instrument of French Renaissance lyric, the form of Ronsard and the Pléiade poets whose name the great French edition of Shakespeare would one day borrow. French readers who met the feast scene in Le Tourneur's prose or in François-Victor Hugo's rendering encountered a homecoming disguised as an import: the Italianate form their own poets had naturalized, returned as English drama, and made to do the work of seduction in real time. Something similar holds for the story itself, which had passed through French hands on its way to Shakespeare, in Pierre Boaistuau's Histoires tragiques, from which the English line of transmission descends. France's claim on the Veronese lovers was therefore older than its criticism of them, and the quarrel's intimacy, its quality of family dispute, follows from that buried kinship. Voltaire's France prosecuted the play as a foreign barbarian. The documents suggest a truer description: France prosecuted it as a relative whose manners disgraced the house, and spent two centuries teaching it to behave, before admitting, in the bilingual edition of 2002, that the manners had been the genius all along.

One more French monument belongs to the modern record, because it shows the play passing from criticism into mythography. Denis de Rougemont's L'Amour et l'Occident, published in 1939 and revised across the following decades, argued that the Western idea of passionate love descends from the Tristan legend, a love that secretly loves obstruction itself and consummates itself only in death. In that genealogy the Veronese lovers occupy a central chapter: the myth's Renaissance heirs, whose feuding houses supply the obstruction the passion requires and whose double suicide the West has never stopped retelling. Whatever the thesis's standing among medievalists, its French influence is a reception fact of the first order, since it taught two generations of French readers to approach the play not as a drama to be judged but as a symptom to be diagnosed, the West confessing its confusion of love with death. French criticism's journey is complete in that reversal. The country that began by prosecuting the lovers' tragedy for breaking the rules ended by indicting, through those same lovers, the civilization that produced the rules' makers.

## The Shape of the French Reception: A Verdict

The scholars of the reception divide their emphases, and the division should be registered before the main adjudication. Pemble's history stresses the national nerve: France's Shakespeare was a proxy in the long adjustment to lost cultural paramountcy. Willems's study of Ducis stresses the mechanism: classicizing as a technology with rules, costs, and a European market. Treilhou-Balaudé's account of the nineteenth century, anchored in the 1864 jubilee, stresses the festival politics by which homage became self-coronation. The three are compatible at different depths, and this article's verdict draws on each in turn: Pemble for the quarrel's ferocity, Willems for its method, Treilhou-Balaudé for its ceremonial completion. Where they might be thought to compete, on the question of what finally changed the French mind, the documentary sequence assembled in the timeline adjudicates: no single conversion occurred. Theory changed the jurisdiction in 1800, experience changed the imagination in 1827, music changed the affections in 1839 and 1867, and editors changed the text in 1952 and 2002. A reception with four turning points is not a conversion narrative at all. It is a negotiation with four settlements, and the play survived all of them.

The mandatory disagreement can now be adjudicated on the documents assembled. On one side stands Voltaire's sentence, the most durable formula in the play's French history: a genius of force and fecundity without a spark of good taste, pearls of beauty embedded in a dunghill of barbarous form, a judgment renewed in anger in 1776 when its author saw his own discovery turned into a cult. On the other stands the Romantic counter-sentence, codified by Staël's reclassification, armed by Stendhal's definition, and crowned by Hugo's tercentenary volume: the irregularity is the form of a northern genius, the rules are the provincial custom of one court and one century, and the play's mixture of brawl and sonnet, bawdy and sublimity, is not its defect but its life. Judged as criticism, the counter-sentence wins, and the victory is not close. The classical charge sheet survives inspection only as a description of difference: the play does violate the unities, mix the kinds, and exhibit its deaths, and two centuries of performance on every stage in Europe have established that these are sources of its power, the brawl making the feud a public fact rather than a reported one, the mixture letting desire speak in the same breath as the street, the tomb's crowded stage forcing the city to look at what its hatred cost. Voltaire's pearls metaphor refutes Voltaire's verdict, because the pearls cannot be extracted: remove the brawling and the balcony, as Ducis did, and what remains is a correct tragedy nobody has revived since.

### Did the Romantic embrace restore the play's text or replace it?

It replaced it, for a century. The manifestos reversed the verdict on Shakespeare's kind while practice went on adjusting the text whenever it was embodied: Garrick's tomb at the Odéon in 1827 and in Berlioz in 1839, Soulié's classicism in the Repertoire in 1832, Gounod's waking Juliet in 1867. The unaltered play reached the stage with Sarment in 1952.

The strongest objection to this article's thesis must be faced before the verdict can stand. Rewriting was not a French peculiarity. England classicized its own Shakespeare with an industry France never matched: Nahum Tate gave Lear a happy ending that held the London stage for a century and a half, Davenant and Dryden rebuilt The Tempest as an opera, and the Garrick version that conquered Paris in 1827 was itself an English correction of the tomb scene, the cuts, and the text. If every culture rewrote the play, the French negotiation loses its distinctiveness, and the thesis collapses into a commonplace about adaptation. The objection is sound about practice and wrong about the record. England rewrote its Shakespeare pragmatically, in the theatre, and apologized rarely; France rewrote its Shakespeare on principle, in the study, and published the principles. The French case is the only one in which the rewriting was preceded, accompanied, and justified by two centuries of first-rate critical argument about why the original was defective, from the Lettres philosophiques to the Académie's defence of the word romanticism, so that the adaptations survive as the executive arm of a criticism that can be read, dated, and answered. That is why the French reception, and not the equally inventive English one, is the instructive case: it kept its reasons.

Judged as history, however, Voltaire's side keeps a residue that the Romantic celebration cannot dissolve, and the adjudication must record it. The French record does not show a culture converted by an experience of the play. It shows a culture that converted the play, repeatedly and brilliantly, into the forms its own genius commanded: Ducis's tragedy of the fathers, Berlioz's symphony of separated kinds, Gounod's lyric of duets, Hugo's prose epic of genius. Pemble's thesis, that the long struggle over Shakespeare indexes France's painful adjustment to a world in which French culture was no longer paramount, explains the quarrel's ferocity better than any aesthetic account, and Willems's classicizing explains its mechanism; both are upheld here, with one qualification the timeline makes unavoidable. Whatever France was negotiating about itself, the negotiation ended in surrender of the only kind that matters to a text: the bilingual Pléiade of 2002 prints the play whole and lets the French face it. The shape of the French reception is therefore neither the resistance the classicists hoped for nor the liberation the Romantics advertised. It is a two-century negotiation in which the play kept its beauties hostage to its form, France paid the form's price in instalments of rewriting, and the final settlement, when it came, was signed not by a poet but by an editor. The cliche assumes universal acclaim. France's documents record something better worth having: the most fully documented account anywhere of what it costs a great culture to admit a foreign masterpiece that breaks its rules.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch French timeline, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch French timeline is the table in this article that sets the stations of the play's French reception in order, from Voltaire's letter of 1734 to the bilingual Pléiade edition of 2002, with the text each station made of the play. The pattern it exposes is that admiration and alteration rose together for two centuries: France withheld the play, classicized it, softened it, and recomposed it in music, while praising it ever more warmly, and fidelity arrived only at the end, carried by editors rather than poets. Each row names a dated document, a translation, an adaptation, a performance, or an edition, so the pattern can be checked against the record rather than taken on trust. It is the quickest available map of how a foreign classic was manufactured into a national possession.

### Q: What did Voltaire actually say about Shakespeare in the Lettres philosophiques of 1734?

In the eighteenth letter, on tragedy, Voltaire called Shakespeare a genius of force and fecundity, natural and sublime, who possessed not a spark of good taste and knew not one rule of the drama. He described the plays as monstrous farces to which the name of tragedy had been given, while conceding that they contained beautiful, noble, and terrifying scenes, and he compared English poetic genius to a tufted tree planted by nature that would die if trimmed like the gardens of Marly. The letter founded French Shakespeare criticism by joining the concession and the sentence in a single formula. Every later French judgment of the Veronese play, favourable or hostile, was delivered for two centuries in some arrangement of Voltaire's two terms: overwhelming genius, indefensible form.

### Q: Why did Voltaire turn against Shakespeare in his letter to the Académie in 1776?

Voltaire turned prosecutor in 1776 because Pierre Le Tourneur's new complete translation had hailed Shakespeare in its preface as the god of the theatre, and the deification threatened everything the old critic had spent his life administering. His letter, read to the Académie française on 25 August 1776, protested the cult and repeated his youthful figure in embittered form: the playwright was a barbarian whose works were a huge dunghill with a few pearls hidden in it. Beneath the doctrine lay a discoverer's wound, confessed in his correspondence: he had been the first to show France the few pearls he had found in that enormous heap, and the generation spending his discovery treated the mine as a temple. The letter is therefore both a critical document and a founder's protest against his own foundation.

### Q: Which Shakespeare plays did La Place's Théâtre anglois offer French readers, and which famous tragedy was missing?

La Place's Théâtre anglois of 1745 to 1749 offered French readers Othello, Richard III, Hamlet, Macbeth, Cymbeline, Julius Caesar, Antony and Cleopatra, Timon of Athens, and The Merry Wives of Windsor, in prose renderings and summaries preceded by a discourse on the English theatre and a life of the playwright. The famous tragedy missing was Romeo and Juliet. The omission was not an accident of space: La Place, who softened and cut what he translated, passed over the one major play whose subject was private passion, whose machinery was a friar's potion, and whose stage was crowded with servants and street fighters. France's first Shakespeare anthology thus disciplined its author by selection before any adapter disciplined him by rewriting, and the lovers waited a generation for print.

### Q: What did Le Tourneur's translation of 1776 to 1783 change for French readers of the play?

Le Tourneur's Shakespeare traduit de l'anglois changed the play's French existence from rumour to possession: across twenty volumes published between 1776 and 1783 and dedicated to Louis XVI, French readers received the whole tragedy in prose, all five acts, the brawls, the balcony, the potion, and the tomb, in the first French Shakespeare to make fidelity its programme. The change was real but qualified. Le Tourneur's diction was stately, and passages of low register and lewdness were suppressed or thinned, so the design arrived complete while the texture arrived decently dressed. His preface's salute to Shakespeare as the god of the theatre also detonated the quarrel of 1776 with Voltaire, fixing the poles of French debate for fifty years. The play could now be read entire in France, decades before it could be staged entire.

### Q: Who was Jean-François Ducis, and when did his Roméo et Juliette reach the stage?

Jean-François Ducis was the French dramatist who, knowing no English and working through French intermediaries, made the first sustained effort to bring Shakespeare's tragedies to the French stage, beginning with Hamlet in 1769. His Roméo et Juliette, announced as a tragedy imitated from the English, reached the stage of the Comédie-Française on 27 July 1772 and held the boards for generations, becoming the form in which France, and through retranslation much of Europe, knew the play. Ducis succeeded Voltaire in his chair at the Académie in 1778, an irony the reception never tires of: the man who had raged against Shakespeare's barbarous stagecraft was replaced by the man who had tamed it for the national theatre. His adaptations were honest work under an institution that enforced the classical rules as conditions of performance.

### Q: What did Ducis cut from the play, and who became his version's hero?

Ducis cut the opening brawling and the balcony scene, the two elements in which the play's mixture of public violence and private lyricism most consists, as John Pemble's summary memorably puts it: a Romeo and Juliet with no brawling and no balcony. The feud's street war, the servants' bawdy, and the orchard exchange all disappeared under the discipline of the unities and decorum, and the scenic arrangement, the persons, and the plot were remodelled until the English matter could pass the French customs house. The hero of the version, as the Comédie-Française's own archival history records, was old Montaigu, the father, a figure Ducis drew partly from Dante, who had named the Montecchi among Italy's feuding houses. The tragedy of the lovers became a tragedy of the fathers, with repentance and paternal grief carrying the weight Shakespeare had given to desire.

### Q: What was Roméo et Paquette, and what does its existence prove about the play's first French season?

Roméo et Paquette was a burlesque parody published in 1773, a year after Ducis's premiere, attributed to a woman writing as Mademoiselle Carrière-Doisin, which replaced the Capulets with a family called the Cascarets, Mercutio with a figure called Gargaric, and the double suicide with a garden party at which everyone drinks wine and celebrates the lovers, its title page brazenly claiming a Verona imprint for a Parisian book. Its existence proves two things about the first French season. The adaptation was familiar enough, quickly enough, to sustain a book-length mockery, which means Ducis's version had penetrated Parisian culture well beyond the theatre. And what the culture had absorbed was the story detached from Shakespeare's structure, a famous tale of feuding houses and young love already available for farce: the play's French life as a free-floating myth begins in 1773.

### Q: What did Madame de Staël argue in De la littérature that reversed Voltaire's judgment?

In De la littérature of 1800, Madame de Staël argued that literatures are formed by climate, history, and national spirit, and that Europe's poetry divides into a southern line descending from Homer, serene and pagan, and a northern line, melancholy, philosophical, and inward, whose genius was Shakespeare. The argument reversed Voltaire's judgment by changing the court that delivered it. Irregularity in a northern poet ceased to be a failure to obey the rules perfected by the ancients and the French; it became fidelity to a different and equally legitimate order of feeling, strong in tombs, spectres, and passion. Applied to the Veronese tragedy, the reclassification turned a barbarous compound of brawl and sonnet into a characteristic product of the northern imagination, to be judged by intensity of feeling rather than regularity of frame. Staël gave French admiration a theory, and her De l'Allemagne of 1813 then imported Schlegel's system to support it.

### Q: How did Stendhal define romanticism in Racine et Shakespeare?

In the pamphlets Racine et Shakespeare of 1823 and 1825, Stendhal defined romanticism as the art of presenting a people with the literary works that, in the present state of its habits and beliefs, are capable of giving it the greatest possible pleasure, and classicism as the literature that gave the greatest possible pleasure to that people's great-grandfathers. The definition confiscated the quarrel's central term: Racine had been a romantic for the court of Louis XIV and was a classic for the Restoration, so obedience to his example was not fidelity to art but loyalty to dead men's pleasures. Shakespeare stood in the argument as the standing proof that a national drama lives by pleasing its own age, and the pamphlets demanded a French theatre of energy and history freed from the unities as anything but conveniences. The Veronese play gained from Stendhal not an analysis but a climate of permission.

### Q: What happened at the Odéon theatre in Paris in September 1827?

In September 1827 an English company at the Odéon theatre gave Paris its first sustained experience of Shakespeare performed, opening with Hamlet on 11 September, Charles Kemble in the title role and Harriet Smithson as Ophelia, whose mad scene convulsed the house, and following with Romeo and Juliet on 15 September, Kemble as Romeo and Smithson as Juliet, in David Garrick's acting version. The witnesses included Alexandre Dumas, who recorded that the playing revealed to him what drama could be, and Hector Berlioz, who wrote that Shakespeare fell on him like lightning and opened the heaven of art. The Comédie-Française's own records add the counterpoint: a new production of the French Romeo mounted there that year closed after three performances. In one autumn Paris ran the experiment of the adapted text against the embodied one, and the adapted text lost.

### Q: Who was Harriet Smithson, and why did her Juliet matter to French criticism?

Harriet Smithson was the Anglo-Irish actress whose Ophelia and Juliet at the Odéon in September 1827 made her, for a season and more, the figurehead of the French Romantic movement, reproduced in prints, poems, and paintings, and whose marriage to Berlioz in 1833 bound her story to the movement's own biography. Her Juliet mattered to French criticism because she refuted the classical case by performance rather than by argument. Where French declamation had treated the tragic heroine as an occasion for noble lament, Smithson played a Juliet who acted: impulsive, terrified, resolute, physical, the equal of her partner in driving the action. Critics who had debated the play for ninety years as a problem of rules watched it become an event of feeling, and the vocabulary of judgment changed accordingly. After Smithson, defending the play in France no longer required a theory. It required only a memory.

### Q: Why did Hugo champion the Deschamps and Vigny Romeo of 1828, and what did the Comédie-Française choose instead?

Hugo championed the verse Romeo and Juliet prepared by Émile Deschamps and Alfred de Vigny in 1828 because he saw that manifestos do not naturalize a foreign classic; repertoire does, and a Romantic Romeo in the national theatre would have carried the revolution into the institution itself. The actors admired the young men's version, the committee hesitated, and in 1832 the text that entered the Repertoire was Frédéric Soulié's, which the house's own history characterizes as staunchly classicist. The sequence distils the French nineteenth century: enthusiasm outside the walls, caution at the table, compromise on the stage. Hugo's Hernani triumphed at the same theatre in 1830, but Hernani was a French original borrowing Shakespearean freedoms. The Veronese play itself, in French, remained a negotiated text, and the Deschamps and Vigny version waited for print and posterity rather than performance.

### Q: What did François-Victor Hugo's translation give French readers between 1859 and 1866?

Between 1859 and 1866 François-Victor Hugo's complete prose translation, published in eighteen volumes, gave French readers the first Shakespeare in their language that attempted fidelity whole: the puns kept rather than paraphrased away, the prose left as prose, the coarseness left standing, the plays presented entire in a version meant to stand beside the German achievement of Schlegel and Tieck, which it matched a generation later and in prose rather than verse. For the Veronese tragedy the gain was a French text in which the balcony's reasoning, the Nurse's garrulity, and the tomb's ironies could all be read at full strength, seasoned by the long introduction the enterprise eventually provoked from the translator's father. The translation remained a standard French Shakespeare for a century, and its existence marks the point at which France's quarrel with the play's form stopped mattering to its readers, whatever the stage went on doing.

### Q: Why is Victor Hugo's William Shakespeare of 1864 not really a book about the play?

Victor Hugo's William Shakespeare of 1864 is not really a book about this play, or about any single play, because it began as the introduction to his son François-Victor's complete translation and swelled into a three-hundred-page meditation on genius itself, in which Shakespeare stands at the head of a procession running from Job and Homer through Aeschylus, Dante, Rabelais, and Cervantes to the nineteenth century. The Veronese tragedy appears inside that pageant as one exhibit among the works of the supreme modern mind, not as an object of analysis. French critics of the day joked that the volume should have been titled Myself, and the joke identifies its value exactly: the book is the French embrace's charter, genius saluting genius across the Channel in the tercentenary year, priceless as a document of what France had decided Shakespeare meant, and useless as a guide to what happens in Verona.

### Q: How does Berlioz's Roméo et Juliette of 1839 depend on Garrick rather than on Shakespeare's ending?

Berlioz's Roméo et Juliette, first performed at the Paris Conservatoire on 24 November 1839 to Émile Deschamps's libretto, depends on Garrick because the performance that inspired it, the Odéon evening of 15 September 1827 with Harriet Smithson, used David Garrick's acting version, and the symphony memorializes that evening rather than the text of 1597. Garrick had altered the ending so that Juliet wakes in the tomb before Romeo dies, granting the lovers a final interview Shakespeare denied them, and Berlioz's tomb scene preserves the arrangement: his lovers meet awake, as Smithson's had, in the work's climactic sequence. The dependence defines the symphony's place in the reception. France's greatest Shakespearean composition is a monument to an English adaptation of the play, received by a composer who in 1827 had no English, at several removes from the words it transfigures. Its glory and its mediation are the same fact.

### Q: Why does Juliet wake before Romeo dies in Gounod's opera of 1867?

Juliet wakes before Romeo dies in Gounod's Roméo et Juliette, first performed at the Théâtre Lyrique on 27 April 1867, because the opera inherits the ending as the French tradition had received it: David Garrick's alteration, naturalized at the Odéon in 1827 and monumentalized in Berlioz's symphony, in which the heroine wakes in the tomb while the poison is still working. Barbier and Carré's libretto builds its fifth act on the arrangement so that the lovers can sing the last of the opera's four great duets together before Juliet stabs herself as Romeo dies. The alteration is the lyric settlement in miniature. Shakespeare's ending, in which Juliet wakes minutes too late and speaks her despair over a corpse, was the tragedy's loneliest irony; French lyric theatre, from Garrick's import onward, consistently traded that irony for a final communion, and audiences rewarded the trade for two centuries.

### Q: When did a faithful version of the play finally enter the Comédie-Française repertoire, and whose was it?

A faithful version of the play entered the Comédie-Française repertoire in 1952, in the version by Jean Sarment, which the house's own archival history describes as the point at which France definitively left free adaptation behind: Sarment simplified while remaining faithful to the text, and his Romeo was performed sixty-eight times between 1952 and 1954 before the house let the play sleep until 2015. The date deserves emphasis. The institution that had staged Ducis's classicized lovers in 1772, refused the Deschamps and Vigny version in the Romantic decades, and received eight further proposed adaptations between 1852 and 1916 admitted the substantially unaltered tragedy one hundred and eighty years after its French premiere. Fidelity, in the national theatre of Molière and Racine, is a twentieth-century achievement, and the Comédie-Française's own records date it.

### Q: What does John Pemble argue the French struggle over Shakespeare was really about?

In Shakespeare Goes to Paris of 2005, the standard modern history of the reception, John Pemble argues that the long French struggle over Shakespeare was an index of French self-esteem: Shakespeare was crucial to the painful adjustment of French consciousness to a world in which France and the French were no longer paramount. On that reading, Voltaire's championship and prosecution, the Romantics' idolatry, and the academy's resistance were never only literary events; each generation's verdict on the irregular Englishman measured its confidence in French culture's supremacy, praising him when borrowing felt safe and prosecuting him when borrowing looked like subordination. This article adopts Pemble's thesis with the qualification its timeline supplies: whatever the struggle was about, it ended only when editors, Sarment in the theatre and Déprats in the Pléiade, printed the play whole and closed the account.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch French timeline to argue that France naturalized the play by rewriting it?

An essay should use the InsightCrunch French timeline by reading its third column as a single argument: at every station from 1734 to 1867, the French advance toward the play is simultaneously a revision of it, by La Place's omission, Ducis's cuts, Le Tourneur's softened diction, Garrick's tomb at the Odéon and in Berlioz, and Gounod's waking Juliet, while the rhetoric of admiration climbs from Voltaire's grudging pearls to Hugo's deification. The timeline's last rows then supply the proof of the rule by their contrast, since fidelity arrives only with the editors, Sarment in 1952 and the bilingual Pléiade in 2002, after the quarrel that had made rewriting a doctrine was dead. Set against the objection that England rewrote the play too, the timeline shows the French difference: the rewriting was argued in print, on principle, for two centuries, and the documents survive to be cited.
