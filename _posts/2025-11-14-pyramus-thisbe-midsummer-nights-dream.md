Pyramus and Thisbe die twice in Shakespeare's work of the mid-1590s, once to tears and once to laughter, and the second death is the more instructive. In A Midsummer Night's Dream, a company of Athenian artisans performs the Ovidian tale of two lovers parted by a wall, misled by a bloodied garment, and united only in a double suicide, and the court that watches it cannot stop laughing. In Romeo and Juliet, built from the same narrative skeleton in the same theatrical seasons, the corresponding beats produce the most famous tomb scene in English drama. The pairing is not a curiosity. It is a controlled experiment run by a single playwright on a single plot, and its result settles a question that criticism of the tragedy keeps circling: whether the ending of Romeo and Juliet is tragic because of what happens, or because of how the happening is framed, timed, and received.

![The mechanicals performing Pyramus and Thisbe before the Athenian court - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-78.webp)

The verdict argued throughout this article is precise. The mechanicals' interlude is not a parody of Romeo and Juliet, because the chronology is too close and too uncertain to support a one-way act of mockery, and because its targets are the inherited love-death convention and the bombast of older stage tragedy rather than the companion play. The interlude is instead the tragedy's twin and its mirror: the same machine, assembled from the same parts, run with the frame reversed. Every device that makes the Verona tomb devastating is present in the Athens performance in a form that makes it funny, and the difference never lies in the plot. The difference lies in whether the fiction is allowed to be believed, whether the fatal token is read in silence or announced aloud, whether the deaths are witnessed by mourners or by connoisseurs, and whether the verse is permitted to persuade. Genre, the mirror demonstrates, is a contract between stage and audience about how a known machine will be received. Shakespeare wrote both contracts at once.

That claim requires the interlude to be read beat by beat against the tomb sequence it shadows, and that is the work of the sections that follow. The dating evidence comes first, because the whole argument depends on the two plays being near-simultaneous products of one imagination rather than an original and its later burlesque. Then the inherited tale, the artisan company, the rehearsal that systematically dismantles illusion, the court performance in its parts (Wall, Lion, Moonshine, the mantle, the two deaths), and the closing comparison with Verona, before the critics who have framed the pairing are heard and a verdict on their disagreement is given. ## Which Came First? Dating the Twin Plays of the Mid-1590s

The dating of both plays rests on a small set of documents and a larger body of stylistic inference, and the honest summary is that neither play can be proved to precede the other. Romeo and Juliet existed by 1597, when its first quarto appeared with a title page advertising performance by the Lord Chamberlain's Men. A Midsummer Night's Dream was not printed until 1600, but Francis Meres listed both plays among Shakespeare's works in Palladis Tamia, published in 1598, and listed them in different generic columns, the Dream among the comedies and Romeo and Juliet among the tragedies, which is the earliest surviving evidence that a contemporary reader filed the twins under opposite genres. Stylistic tests, including verse structure, rhyme density, and the handling of lyric set pieces, place both plays in the middle of the decade, most often in 1595 or 1596, in the company of Richard II and King John.

### Were the two plays written in the same seasons, and how is that known?

The two plays were written within a year or two of each other in the mid-1590s, on converging evidence rather than a single document. Romeo and Juliet was in print by 1597, the Dream was known to Francis Meres by 1598, and metrical analysis dates both to about 1595 or 1596, Shakespeare's most concentrated lyric period.

The closeness matters more than the order, and the closeness is not in dispute. Peter Holland, editing the Dream for the Oxford Shakespeare, dates the comedy to 1595 or 1596 and treats the two plays as products of a single phase of work. Sukanta Chaudhuri, in the Arden Third Series edition of the Dream published in 2017, likewise assigns the play to the middle of the decade and notes its entanglement with the Romeo and Juliet material. René Weis, editing Romeo and Juliet for the same Arden series, dates the tragedy to the same span and observes that the Pyramus and Thisbe story was plainly on the playwright's desk in these years, since the tragedy itself invokes it by structure if not by name. When three editors of two plays, working independently on different texts, converge on the same two or three theatrical seasons, the simultaneity can be treated as a fact of the career even where the sequence within those seasons cannot.

One persistent complication is the wedding hypothesis for the Dream. The comedy ends in a triple wedding and contains an epilogue suited to a festive occasion, and scholars have repeatedly proposed that it was composed for a specific aristocratic marriage, with candidates including the wedding of Elizabeth Carey in February 1596 and an earlier Stanley family wedding in 1595. The hypothesis would, if proved, pin the Dream to a date and perhaps to an order relative to the tragedy. It remains unproved. No external record connects the play to any wedding, the internal evidence is circumstantial, and Chaudhuri's edition treats the occasion theory with marked caution. The prudent handling, adopted here, is to let the wedding hypothesis stand as a possibility that colours the reading of the interlude (a tragedy performed at a wedding is itself a joke about occasion) without resting any argument on it.

What the dating will not support is the comfortable picture of a finished tragedy later sent up by its author. Parody in the strong sense requires a settled target, and nothing about the mid-1590s evidence offers one. If the Dream came first by some months, the interlude cannot be mocking a tragedy that did not yet exist, though it could still be mocking the convention both plays inherit. If the tragedy came first, the burlesque was composed while its supposed target was still new in the repertory, sharing actors, audiences, and the playwright's current habits of verse. Either way, the two treatments grew together, and the likelier model is not original and copy but a single fascination pursued in two registers at once: a young dramatist testing how much of the love-death plot's machinery was load-bearing and how much was convention that could be made to creak audibly when the frame around it changed.

## Ovid Twice Over: The Tale Both Plays Inherited

The story Shakespeare handled twice was already ancient and already doubled in tone. In the fourth book of Ovid's Metamorphoses, Pyramus and Thisbe are neighbours in Babylon whose parents forbid the match; the lovers whisper through a chink in the wall between their houses, agree to meet at night at the tomb of Ninus beneath a mulberry tree, and are destroyed by a sequence of misread signs. Thisbe arrives first, flees a lioness fresh from a kill, and drops her veil; the lioness mouths the veil and bloodies it; Pyramus finds the garment, concludes that his beloved is dead, and falls on his sword; Thisbe returns, finds the body, and kills herself with the same blade; the mulberry's white fruit is stained dark forever in memory of the deaths. Arthur Golding's English translation of the Metamorphoses, published in 1567, was the version in the grammar-school curriculum and the one Shakespeare certainly knew, though the tale also circulated in Chaucer's Legend of Good Women and Gower's Confessio Amantis, a pedigree traced in the study of [Ovid's Pyramus and Thisbe as Ancestor](/2025/11/07/ovid-pyramus-and-thisbe-source/).

Three features of the inherited tale deserve emphasis because both plays exploit them. The first is that the plot is a machine for generating a double suicide out of a single false inference: no villain acts, no malice operates, and the catastrophe is assembled entirely from a dropped cloth, an animal, and a lover who reads evidence too fast. The second is that the story is narrated from the start as an exemplum, told by one of the daughters of Minyas while she weaves, which means the tale arrives in Shakespeare's hands already framed as a performance for an audience that comments and judges. The third is that Ovid's own telling is poised oddly between pathos and a kind of elegant detachment, lingering over the blood that spurts from Pyramus's wound like water from a burst pipe, a simile whose grotesque concreteness English translators kept. The tale, in other words, already contains the seeds of both treatments: it can be wept over as a fable of faithful love or smiled at as an over-engineered accident, depending on the pressure a reteller applies.

Romeo and Juliet reaches the tale at several removes, through the Italian novella tradition and Arthur Brooke's poem, a line of descent mapped in [The Sources of Romeo and Juliet: A Family Tree](/2025/09/12/sources-of-romeo-and-juliet-family-tree/), and the tragedy's debt is structural rather than verbal: feuding houses replace the single forbidding wall of parents, a friar's failed letter replaces the lioness as the agent of misinformation, and a tomb in Verona replaces the tomb of Ninus. The Dream reaches the same tale directly and keeps its furniture intact: wall, lion, moon, tomb, veil become mantle, sword. One playwright, one source, two distances. The tragedy translates the story out of recognition and keeps its engine; the comedy keeps the story's furniture and dismantles its engine in public. Holding those two facts together is the whole method of the comparison that follows.

## Kept and Discarded: Two Adaptations of One Inheritance

The surest way to see the two plays as twins is to watch what each does with the same inheritance, item by item, because the choices are mirror opposites with a consistency that cannot be accidental. Ovid gives the lovers forbidding parents but no feud; the Dream keeps the parents offstage entirely, reducing prohibition to a premise so thoroughly assumed that Wall can stand in for the whole parental system, while the tragedy expands prohibition into a civic war between two households with a body count and a prince. Ovid gives the lovers a single tryst and a single night of catastrophe; the Dream compresses the entire story into a twenty-minute performance inside one wedding night, while the tragedy inflates the same outline into five acts and four days of Verona time, adding a secret marriage, a street brawl, a banishment, and a potion, so that the catastrophe arrives over-determined, earned by a dozen causes where Ovid needed one lioness and a dropped veil.

The mulberry tree is the most eloquent casualty. In Ovid the stained berries are the story's memorial, the permanent natural sign by which the deaths are remembered; the tale ends in metamorphosis, which is the Metamorphoses' whole business. Neither Shakespeare play keeps the tree. The Dream drops memorial altogether, because its lovers are fictional inside the fiction and their deaths are dissolved by a dance; memory would be an embarrassment to the joke. The tragedy translates the memorial out of nature and into culture: the mulberry becomes the golden statues, the fruit's stain becomes the fathers' competitive grief, and remembrance is a municipal expense rather than a botanical fact. Even here the mirror holds. What Ovid assigns to the landscape, comedy deletes and tragedy invoices.

The lioness shows the same division of labour in reverse. The Dream keeps her, defanged by disclaimer but structurally intact, the agent whose mouthing of the veil manufactures the false evidence. The tragedy abolishes her and distributes her function among human agents: the feud that makes night meetings necessary, the quarantine that stops the letter, the apothecary who sells the means, the watch that arrives late. Critics sometimes describe this as the tragedy naturalising its source, replacing marvel with mechanism. The interlude's evidence suggests the sharper formulation: the tragedy does not remove the lioness, it promotes her into society. Every Veronese who touches the lovers' secret performs a fragment of her office, carrying the false sign a stage further toward the tomb, and none of them has to roar. Athens keeps the beast and makes her fill in a form. Verona fires the beast and gives her work to the neighbours.

What neither play keeps is Ovid's cool. The Metamorphoses tells the tale as one story among hundreds, framed by a weaver's narration and closed by the next transformation, and its emotional temperature is deliberately moderate, a beautiful object rather than an occasion for grief. Shakespeare's two treatments are both, in opposite directions, refusals of that moderation. The tragedy heats the tale until it becomes the standard of romantic suffering for four centuries; the comedy heats it in the other direction, until the suffering curdles into a set piece that a wedding court can applaud. The twins agree in rejecting the source's detachment, and their agreement is the deepest evidence that they belong to one phase of one imagination: a dramatist who could no longer leave the old story at room temperature, and who spent the middle of the decade finding out how far it could be pushed toward tears and toward laughter before it broke. It never broke. That is the finding the rest of this article documents.

## Casting the Catastrophe: Quince's Company of Artisans

The interlude's first joke is its cast list, and the joke is more structural than it looks. Peter Quince, a carpenter, directs a company made of a joiner (Snug), a bellows-mender (Flute), a tailor (Starveling), a tinker (Snout), a cabinet-maker's colleague in Quince himself, and Nick Bottom the weaver, the most ambitious amateur in English drama. These are the men who build and mend the material world of Athens, and they have chosen to perform the most refined matter in the classical repertoire: a tragedy of aristocratic young love ending in suicide. The comedy begins in that mismatch, but the mismatch has a serious edge, because the mechanicals take the play's dangers with a literal-minded seriousness that the tragedy's own characters never apply to theirs. They worry about consequences. They ask what a sword on stage might do to the ladies, what a lion might do to the company's necks, whether the moon will shine on the night of performance. The tragedy of Romeo and Juliet happens because nobody in Verona performs this kind of anxious audit on the fiction unfolding around them; the farce of Pyramus and Thisbe is generated because the artisans perform the audit obsessively and solve every problem by explaining the illusion away.

Bottom dominates the casting scene in the first act, and his appetite is the company's problem in miniature. Assigned Pyramus, he immediately asks to play Thisbe as well, promising to speak "in a monstrous little voice" (1.2), and then the lion too, offering to roar so movingly that the Duke will call for an encore. Quince's refusals are grounded in theatrical physics: if Bottom roars too terribly, the Duchess and the ladies will shriek, "and that were enough to hang us all" (1.2). The exchange establishes the interlude's governing anxiety, which is that representation might work. A lion too well played becomes a real lion in its effects; a suicide too well played becomes an offence against the audience's nerves. The company's entire dramaturgy will be devoted to preventing that success, and the tragedies of the repertoire, Romeo and Juliet included, are built on the opposite devotion, on making representation work until an audience forgets to breathe. The mirror is already angled: Athens fears the power that Verona courts.

Flute's reluctance sharpens the point from the other side. Assigned Thisbe, the bellows-mender protests that he has a beard coming and cannot play a woman, and must be talked into the part by the promise of a mask and a falsetto. Snug, given the lion, confesses that he is "slow of study" and is relieved to learn that the part is "nothing but roaring" (1.2). Every member of the company is thus introduced through a gap between the worker and the role: the bearded Thisbe, the slow lion, the director who writes prologues he cannot punctuate, the weaver who would play all parts at once and thereby abolish the fiction of separate persons. In the tragedy, the gaps between person and role are where the catastrophe lives too, in a friar playing providence, a nurse playing fate's messenger, a prince playing judge too late. But the tragedy conceals its gaps under the pressure of verse and event, while the interlude exhibits its gaps as the entertainment itself. The audience of the Dream is never allowed to forget that Thisbe is Flute; the audience of the tragedy is never allowed to remember that Juliet is an actor. Same theatre, opposite discipline.

The mechanicals also supply something the tragedy structurally lacks: a chorus of makers who discuss the play as a made thing before performing it. Romeo and Juliet has its Chorus, a single voice that frames the action in sonnets and then withdraws. The Dream distributes the choral function among six tradesmen with hand tools, and the effect is to demystify in advance everything the performance will attempt. By the time the interlude begins in the fifth act, the audience has heard the wall designed, the moonlight budgeted, the lion's prologue drafted, and the deaths choreographed as problems of stage management. Nothing can surprise, and surprise is one of the tragedy's chief weapons, withheld even from an audience told the ending in advance by the Chorus and then made to hope against its knowledge. The interlude's audience, like the tragedy's, knows the ending from the start. The difference is that the interlude keeps reminding its watchers how the ending will be manufactured.

## The Audition Pieces: Bottom's Hercules and the Old Bombast

The interlude's verse has a target beyond the love plot, and Bottom advertises it in the casting scene. Asked what he can play, the weaver offers his party piece: a tyrant's rant in the manner of Hercules, "The raging rocks and shivering shocks shall break the locks of prison gates" (1.2), delivered, he explains, in "Ercles' vein, a tyrant's vein" (1.2). The lines are a parody of the ranting style of the older heroic drama, the Senecan bombast that still echoed through the popular repertory in the early 1590s, with its alliterative violence, its catalogue of cosmic breakages, and its confusion of volume with passion. When Quince casts Bottom as a lover instead, the joke sets up the performance's central stylistic event: Pyramus will die in Ercles' vein, in short rhymed lines crammed with apostrophes to nature, fate, and his own organs, while the matter he dies of is a misunderstanding about a cloak.

The burlesque verse of the death speeches repays close attention because it is doing two kinds of work at once. On the surface it mocks incompetence: the rhymes are thumped, the images inflated, the dying broken into announcements. Underneath, it is a recognisable miniature of the high tragic style of a generation earlier, the style of The Spanish Tragedy's imitators and the chronicle plays, in which heroes addressed the heavens in balanced quatrains and died by instalments of apostrophe. Romeo and Juliet is written in conscious reaction against that manner. Its lovers speak in sonnets, shared quatrains, and blank verse of unprecedented suppleness, and its deaths are swift and comparatively quiet: a draught, a dagger, a sentence each. The interlude shows the road not taken, the tragedy as the previous theatrical generation would have staged it, with every feeling notarised in rhyme before it is permitted to occur. The laughter therefore has a literary-historical object. Shakespeare is not only mirroring his own tragedy in the next play over; he is exhibiting the older machinery his tragedy replaced, and letting a weaver demonstrate why it had to go.

The point sharpens when the company's prose is set beside its verse. The mechanicals are fluent, funny, and precise in prose, in rehearsal negotiations of genuine practical intelligence, and they become helpless only when they put on verse. Verse, in the interlude's economy, is the technology that fails: it is the inherited form that the amateurs cannot operate and that keeps reversing their meanings, tangling their prologues, and stretching their deaths into instalments. The tragedy runs the same experiment with operators of genius. Romeo and Juliet is saturated with formal verse, sonnets inside dialogue, quatrains at partings, a chorus in sonnet form, and the forms hold, bend, and finally break under pressure in ways that register as feeling rather than failure. When Juliet's verse fractures into prose-like urgency in the tomb, the fracture signifies. When Pyramus's verse holds its jog-trot to the last "die, die, die," the regularity signifies the opposite: a form running unattended, like a mill wheel after the miller has fallen asleep. The twins share a prosody lesson. Form is tragic when it is mastered and comic when it masters the speaker, and the mid-1590s audience, hearing both plays in the same seasons, was being taught to hear the difference.

## The Rehearsal in the Wood: Engineering Disbelief

The rehearsal scene in the third act is the most quietly radical piece of theatre criticism in Shakespeare, because it consists of a company identifying, one by one, every convention on which tragic illusion depends, and voting to disable it. Bottom opens the session with a producer's note: "There are things in this comedy of Pyramus and Thisbe that will never please. First, Pyramus must draw a sword to kill himself; which the ladies cannot abide" (3.1). Starveling proposes the logical remedy of leaving the killing out altogether. Bottom's counter-proposal defines the interlude's method: "I have a device to make all well. Write me a prologue; and let the prologue seem to say we will do no harm with our swords, and that Pyramus is not killed indeed" (3.1). The device is disclosure. Whatever might be believed must be announced as false before it happens.

### Why do the mechanicals rehearse ways to keep the audience from believing the play?

The mechanicals dismantle illusion because they fear its consequences: a convincing lion might frighten the ladies, a convincing suicide might offend them, and either failure could cost the company its reward or its safety. Their solution is a prologue and a set of explanations that announce every fiction as fiction before it occurs, reversing the tragedy's craft of concealment.

The lion receives the same treatment. Bottom requires a second prologue to certify that the lion is not a lion, ideally with the actor's face visible through the costume and his name spoken aloud: half his face should be seen through the lion's neck, and Snug should tell the audience directly that he is a joiner and no beast (3.1). Moonshine is subjected to a budget meeting. Quince consults the almanac, confirms that the moon does shine on the appointed night, and proposes leaving a casement open so that real moonlight can serve; Bottom prefers a performer with a lantern, a thorn bush, and a dog, presenting the moon by inventory (3.1). Wall, whose absence from any stage inventory Quince laments ("you can never bring in a wall"), is solved by Bottom's plaster-and-loam proposal: a man coated in roughcast, holding his fingers apart so that the lovers can whisper through the cranny (3.1).

Each device is funny, and each is a precise inversion of a tragic technique. The tragedy of Romeo and Juliet depends on a wall that is never explained and never speaks, the Capulet orchard wall that Romeo scales "with love's light wings" (2.2.66), a boundary whose whole meaning is that it separates without comment. It depends on moonlight that is romantically unreliable, invoked by Romeo as an oath and rejected by Juliet as inconstant, never footnoted as a lighting effect. It depends on deaths that are not prefaced by any assurance that the actor is unharmed. The rehearsal scene reads like a list of the tragedy's load-bearing conventions drawn up by men determined to saw through every one. That the list is comic does not make it less exact. Shakespeare, in the same seasons, was writing the tragedy that trusts these conventions completely and the comedy that exhibits them as joinery, and the exhibition is so accurate that it functions as a diagram of the tragedy's stagecraft, labelled part by part.

There is a further turn. The rehearsal is interrupted by Puck, who transforms Bottom's head into an ass's, and the company flees in terror from a stage effect none of them designed. The one moment of genuine theatrical illusion in the mechanicals' plot is worked on them, not by them, and their response is the panic they had feared in their own audience. The joke completes the argument: illusion is not a property of plays but of the relation between stage and watcher, and the artisans are as helpless before it as any duchess. The tragedy knows this and exploits it. The interlude proves it on the bodies of its own performers.

## Very Tragical Mirth: Theseus Chooses the Entertainment

The fifth act opens with the court discussing love, madness, and imagination while the wedding feast waits for its entertainment, and Philostrate, the master of the revels, presents a list of offerings that reads like a catalogue of generic impossibilities. The available plays include a battle with the Centaurs sung by a eunuch, the tearing of Orpheus by Bacchanals, the Muses mourning the death of Learning, and, last and least promising, "a tedious brief scene of young Pyramus and his love Thisbe; very tragical mirth" (5.1.56-57). Theseus's response isolates the oxymoron with a connoisseur's pleasure: "Merry and tragical? Tedious and brief? That is hot ice and wondrous strange snow" (5.1.58-59). The court is being offered a menu of contradictions, and the item it selects is the contradiction that contains a double suicide.

Philostrate's review is the funniest piece of arts criticism in the period. He has seen the rehearsal and reports that the play "is nothing, nothing in the world," unless the court can find sport in the performers' intents, "extremely stretched and conned with cruel pain, to do you service" (5.1). Hippolyta declares that she does not want the poor men abused for their pains. Theseus overrules the warning with a doctrine that governs the whole scene: "I will hear that play; for never anything can be amiss when simpleness and duty tender it" (5.1.82-84). The choice is often read as gracious condescension, the great man indulging his inferiors, and it is that. It is also a critical programme. Theseus announces in advance that the performance will be judged by intention rather than achievement, which is to say that the court has agreed to supply from its own imagination whatever the stage cannot furnish.

Minutes earlier, Theseus has delivered the play's famous account of imagination, placing the lover beside the lunatic and the poet as creatures whose brains apprehend more than cool reason comprehends: "The lunatic, the lover and the poet are of imagination all compact" (5.1.7-8). The speech is usually detached from its context and quoted as a credo. In context it is the preface to the interlude, and it sets the terms on which the tragedy-within will be watched. Theseus is the tragedy's ideal bad audience: a man who has already decided that lovers are madmen whose stories are "more strange than true," and who therefore cannot receive a lovers' tragedy as anything but a curiosity. Verona has no Theseus; the tragedy there is watched by participants who are inside its logic and cannot step outside to annotate. Athens stations its annotator on the throne, and his commentary will run through the performance like a second, deflating text.

The court's selection also completes a structural rhyme with the tragedy. Romeo and Juliet is, by its own Chorus's account, the story of a pair of lovers whose deaths reconcile two households, and it was played by Shakespeare's company as commercial tragedy. The interlude is a lovers' tragedy selected as wedding entertainment by a duke who has just married his own conquered bride, Hippolyta, won "with his sword." A double suicide performed to celebrate a marriage is an outrageous programming decision that nobody in the fiction finds outrageous, and the outrageousness is the joke's foundation: the court treats the matter of tragedy as a garnish for the matter of comedy, exactly as a wedding's festivities treat the solemnity of the vows. Shakespeare's company, performing the Dream at actual festivities if the wedding hypothesis holds any truth, would have sharpened the joke to a point. Tragedy at a wedding is the wrong genre in the wrong room, and the play knows it, and the knowledge is the frame that keeps the deaths safe.

## Wall, Lion and Moonshine: Tragic Scenery with Faces

The performance begins with the Prologue, and the Prologue fails in the most instructive way available to language: Quince delivers his welcome with the punctuation misplaced, so that the sentences reverse their meanings and the company appears to announce that it has come to offend. Hippolyta's verdict is exact: "Indeed he hath played on his prologue like a child on a recorder; a sound, but not in government" (5.1). Theseus adds the image of "a tangled chain; nothing impaired, but all disordered" (5.1). The failure matters beyond its laugh because the tragedy also begins with a prologue, a sonnet that tells the audience the entire plot and the ending in advance, and asks to be believed. Quince's prologue is that sonnet's mirror image: the same device, framing a lovers' tragedy for an audience, executed so that the frame itself becomes the spectacle. Where the Verona Chorus disappears into its information, Quince's prologue advertises its own machinery and stumbles over it. The interlude's first lesson is that framing is a skill, and that the tragedy's invisible competence in framing is part of what the audience of Romeo and Juliet is paying for without noticing.

Then the scenery walks on and introduces itself. Snout presents Wall in a speech of perfect, helpful clarity: "In this same interlude it doth befall that I, one Snout by name, present a wall" (5.1.154-155). The wall of Babylon, which in Ovid is the mute condition of the lovers' separation and in Verona becomes the orchard wall that Romeo must scale in the dark, here has a name, a trade, and a speaking part. Pyramus addresses it in the high style: "O wall, full often hast thou heard my moans, for parting my fair Pyramus and me" (5.1), and thanks it for holding its stones so faithfully apart; Thisbe kisses its stones and speaks through the cranny of its raised fingers. The comedy is usually located in the absurdity of a man playing masonry. The sharper point is that Wall performs the tragedy's central function better than the tragedy can: the barrier between the lovers is the true third character of the love-death plot, the thing that converts desire into secrecy, secrecy into night meetings, and night meetings into death. Athens gives the barrier a voice and a curtain call. Verona keeps it silent and lets it kill six people. When Wall exits with the satisfied air of a job discharged ("Thus have I, Wall, my part discharged so"), the interlude has already stated the tragedy's thesis in the person of its least regarded element.

### What does Wall do in the performance that no wall in the tragedy is allowed to do?

Wall speaks, explains its own construction, holds its fingers apart to make the chink the lovers whisper through, and withdraws when its function is complete, receiving the court's thanks. The Capulet orchard wall never speaks, explains nothing, and cannot withdraw; it stands between the lovers and permission, which is why it can kill and Wall can only entertain.

Lion and Moonshine extend the demonstration to the tragedy's atmosphere. Snug's lion enters with the disclaimer drafted in rehearsal: "You, ladies, you, whose gentle hearts do fear the smallest monstrous mouse that creeps on floor, may now perchance both quake and tremble here, when lion rough in wildest rage doth roar. Then know that I, one Snug the joiner, am a lion fell, nor else no lion's dam" (5.1.220-225). The beast that in Ovid empties a glade with terror and sets the catastrophe moving here files a certificate of harmlessness before roaring. Moonshine, played by Starveling with lantern, thorn bush, and dog, announces the inventory ("This lanthorn doth the horned moon present; myself the man i' th' moon do seem to be"), submits to Theseus's pedantic correction that the man should be inside the lantern, and answers Hippolyta's weariness ("I am aweary of this moon: would he would change!") with the patience of a tradesman enduring a difficult client. Night, moonlight, and wild nature are the tragedy's great atmospheric powers, the forces Juliet invokes and Romeo swears by and the Friar reads like a pharmacist's chart. In the interlude they are employees. The moon is a man with a prop, the lion is a joiner with a disclaimer, and the wall is a tinker with plaster on his coat. Tragedy requires the world to conspire in silence; the burlesque makes the world clock in, state its name, and ask not to be blamed.

Thisbe's entrance and flight complete the sequence of disarmed dangers. She keeps her appointment at the tomb, meets the certified lion, and flees, dropping her mantle in a flight that the court's commentary treats as choreography. The mantle is then worried by the lion, who bloodies it in pantomime, and exits. Every element of Ovid's machinery has now been presented, labelled, and rendered safe, which is precisely the condition that tragedy cannot permit. Romeo's Verona keeps its dangers unlabelled: a letter that looks like delivery, a potion that looks like death, a kinsman's temper that looks like honour. The interlude's methodical labelling is the comedy, and it amounts to an argument that the difference between the genres is the presence or absence of honest signage.

## The Chink and the Balcony: Two Conversations Through a Barrier

At the centre of the performance stands the scene the whole comparison turns on, the lovers' conversation through the wall, and it deserves separate treatment because it is the one place where the interlude restages, almost line for line in function, the tragedy's most famous scene. Pyramus and Thisbe, forbidden to meet, speak through the chink in the party wall: in the Dream's staging, through the gap in Snout's raised fingers. Romeo and Juliet, forbidden to meet, speak across the vertical barrier of the orchard wall and the balcony, he below in the dark, she above at the window. Both scenes are built from the same grammar: a barrier that permits voice and forbids touch, a night setting, an exchange of vows conducted in the knowledge that the barrier's owners may interrupt at any moment. The balcony scene is the chink scene transfigured by competence, and the chink scene is the balcony scene with its poetry repossessed.

The verbal contrast is surgical. In the orchard, the barrier generates the play's greatest love poetry because the speakers rise to the discipline the barrier imposes: names are interrogated, oaths are audited, the exchange tightens into a shared sonnet's worth of invention distributed across a wall of air. At the chink, the same discipline collapses into synaesthetic confusion. Pyramus, listening at the gap, announces the interlude's most famous solecism: "I see a voice: now will I to the chink, to spy an I can hear my Thisbe's face" (5.1). The senses are filed under the wrong organs; sight claims the voice, hearing is sent to inspect a face. The line is a perfect miniature of the scene's meaning, because the wall's whole cruelty in the serious version is that it separates the senses, granting the lovers each other's voices and withholding everything else, and the tragedy makes that deprivation eloquent while the burlesque makes it grammatical. Romeo, straining in the dark, says "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks?" (2.2.2) and converts a window into a sunrise. Pyramus, straining at the chink, converts a voice into a view. The two lines are the same lover's effort at two levels of verbal skill, and the distance between them is the distance between the genres stated in a single figure.

The vows exchanged through the chink complete the parody of the balcony's oath-swearing. Juliet famously refuses Romeo's oath by the moon and makes him swear by himself, auditing the convention as she uses it. Pyramus and Thisbe swear by mangled classical precedents instead: "Not Shafalus to Procrus was so true," Pyramus vows, and Thisbe caps it, "As Shafalus to Procrus, I to you" (5.1), the names of Cephalus and Procris, another pair of lovers destroyed by a misread token and a jealous hunt, garbled in the tradesmen's mouths into nonsense syllables. The allusion is the interlude's most learned joke, and it is aimed with precision. Cephalus killed Procris by mistake in a thicket, having misheard and misjudged, exactly as Pyramus will misjudge the mantle within the hour; the lovers at the chink are citing, without knowing it, the classical warrant for their own error. Verona's lovers cite their precedents with full command, Romeo measuring himself against every lover in the catalogue and Juliet correcting the measurements. The difference between the couples is not feeling. It is literacy, and the interlude's bleak little proposition is that feeling without literacy dies in rhyme at a tomb, while feeling with it dies in blank verse at another. The tomb does not care which.

The scene's exit seals the comparison. The Babylonian lovers part through the wall with kisses blown at masonry ("My cherry lips have often kissed thy stones"), and arrange the fatal tryst. The Veronese lovers part across the balcony with the most famous goodnights in the language and arrange a marriage. A marriage is what the balcony scene produces and a tryst is what the chink produces, and that single divergence generates everything downstream: because Verona's lovers marry, their story acquires a friar, a potion, a letter, a double timetable, and the machinery of delay in which the catastrophe assembles itself; because Babylon's lovers merely tryst, their story stays naked, wall, lion, mantle, tomb, with no institution to slow it down. The interlude is in this sense the tragedy's control case stripped of the marriage plot, the love-death machine with the ecclesiastical attachments removed, running at full speed to the same end in a tenth of the time. Watching it, the court laughs at the speed. Watching the tragedy, the audience grieves at the delay. The machine reaches the tomb either way.

## The Mantle, the Swords and the Double Death

Pyramus enters to keep the tryst and finds the mantle. The moment is the tragedy's hinge exactly: a lover alone at a tomb at night, reading a bloodied garment as proof of a beloved's death, with no witness to correct the reading. Ovid's Pyramus draws the correct tragic inference from false evidence and acts on it within lines. Bottom's Pyramus does the same, in verse whose battered grandeur keeps collapsing into inventory: "Come, tears, confound! Out, sword, and wound the pap of Pyramus; ay, that left pap, where heart doth hop" (5.1). The specification of the left pap is the whole burlesque in one detail. Tragic dying in the period's high style gestures at the wound; Pyramus files a diagram. He then dies in instalments, announcing each stage ("Thus die I, thus, thus, thus"), expiring through a cascade of farewells to his own faculties ("Tongue, lose thy light; Moon, take thy flight") and the repeated "Now die, die, die, die, die," while the court scores the performance like judges at a contest: Demetrius caps the death with a dice joke ("No die, but an ace, for him; for he is but one"), and Theseus observes that with a surgeon's help the patient "might yet recover, and yet prove an ass" (5.1).

### Does the court audience laugh at the lovers' deaths or at the men playing them?

The laughter is aimed at the playing, not the dying, and the distinction is the scene's safeguard and its thesis. The court's jokes land on execution, on dice puns, surgical recoveries and missed cues, while the fiction of two lovers dead by misreading is handled gently, as if everyone understood that the story, competently told, would not be funny.

Thisbe's return and death repeat the structure at higher pressure. She finds the body, and Flute delivers the lament in the same battered music: "Asleep, my love? What, dead, my dove? O Pyramus, arise! Speak, speak. Quite dumb? Dead, dead? A tomb must cover thy sweet eyes" (5.1). The questions pile up unanswered, which is the exact rhetoric of Juliet over Romeo's body in the Verona tomb, where the discovery speech likewise proceeds by interrogatives addressed to a man who cannot answer. Thisbe then takes the sword: "Come, trusty sword; come, blade, my breast imbrue," and ends on the triple farewell, "Thus Thisbe ends: adieu, adieu, adieu" (5.1). Juliet's corresponding line, "O happy dagger! This is thy sheath; there rust, and let me die" (5.3.169-170), is the same action compressed into a single metaphor that the burlesque version spreads across a rhymed inventory. Set side by side, the two speeches measure the distance between the genres in purely verbal terms: identical event, identical weapon, identical posture over the corpse, and the difference is diction under discipline. Bottom and Flute speak the tragedy's lines as if the words were furniture to be counted; Romeo and Juliet speak theirs as if the words were the event itself.

The deaths also expose the role of duration. Pyramus takes so long to die that the dying becomes the joke; the tragedy kills Romeo in a long speech too, but the speech is sustained at a pitch that forbids the audience the leisure to count its stages. Thisbe dies briskly; Juliet dies mid-sentence against the sound of the watch approaching. In each case the tempo is a directorial decision about belief. Given time to inspect a death, an audience inspects the actor; denied that time, it inspects its own grief. The interlude grants inspection everywhere, and the tragedy denies it everywhere, and the two plays between them amount to a practical treatise on the management of an audience's attention at the moment of stage death.

The closing exchange returns the argument to the wall. Theseus, surveying two corpses and the surviving personifications, remarks that "Moonshine and Lion are left to bury the dead," Demetrius adds "Ay, and Wall too," and Bottom, rising from the corpse of Pyramus to answer for the company, delivers the interlude's most perfect line: "No, I assure you; the wall is down that parted their fathers" (5.1). The joke works because it is structurally true. The wall is down; the separation that generated the plot has been removed by the deaths it caused; the story is over because its engine has been dismantled. Verona ends on the same recognition in the opposite register, with the Prince distributing blame over two bodies and two fathers shaking hands across them. The burlesque states the shared moral in a tradesman's aside. The tragedy needs a prince, a friar's confession, and a pair of golden statues to say it.

## Two Stage Tombs: Ninus's Monument and the Capulet Vault

Both plays end in a tomb, and the tomb is the one piece of scenery the twins share without alteration of function. The rendezvous in Babylon is the tomb of Ninus, a founder-king's monument outside the city walls, chosen by the lovers because it is lonely, nocturnal, and outside the jurisdiction of the parents whose wall divides them. The rendezvous in Verona is the Capulet monument, the family's vault, where Juliet is laid in state and where Romeo forces the door with mattock and wrenching iron. In each case the tomb is the only address in the fiction where the lovers can be together unobserved, which is the tragedy's bleakest joke told in both registers: the houses that forbade the union in life provide, in their architecture of death, the couple's only private room. The mechanicals understand the assignment perfectly and state it in their casting call, a tomb to meet at, by moonshine. Verona understands it too and cannot say it, because saying it is the tragedy's whole action.

The contents of the tombs divide the genres one last time. The Capulet vault is crowded with the feud's prior harvest: Tybalt lies there in his bloody shroud, and Paris is added to the company in the course of the scene, killed at the door by the man who has come to die inside it. Romeo's great speech in the vault is an inventory conducted in earnest, a survey of death's menagerie in which he hails Tybalt, greets the "palace of dim night," and addresses Juliet's body as a beauty that death has not conquered. The Athens tomb, by contrast, receives its corpses one at a time and returns them on demand: Pyramus is buried by nobody, rises to discuss the scenery, and is available for a dance. Where Verona's tomb accumulates the dead until the living must be summoned to count them, Athens' tomb is a piece of furniture that the entertainment borrows and gives back. The monument of Ninus, mispronounced in rehearsal and never actually shown as more than a meeting cue, performs its office by being believed in just enough. The Capulet monument performs its office by being believed in completely, down to the last corpse's identity papers.

The discovery scenes staged in the two tombs are built from the same blocking, a survivor entering with a light, finding the arranged body, and speaking to it in the vocative while the audience watches knowledge arrive too late. The difference is the light. In Verona the light is carried by servants and the watch, torches that belong to authority arriving after the event, and by Paris's page, and by Romeo's own torch borne into the vault by Balthasar's master; illumination in the tragedy is always official or belated. In Athens the light is Moonshine himself, a man with a lantern who has been standing in the scene the whole time, introducing himself, enduring corrections, and providing, by his mere obstinate presence, the condition under which nothing can be hidden and therefore nothing can be tragic. A tomb scene with the lighting technician visible and talkative cannot accumulate dread. A tomb scene whose lights arrive with the police cannot accumulate anything else. The twins share a set, a plot, and a pair of corpses. They differ in whether the man holding the lantern is part of the show.

## Verona Read Backwards: The Tomb Scene in the Mirror

Read the interlude against Act 5 of Romeo and Juliet point by point, and the correspondence is close enough to be tabulated, as the twin-plays chart below does. A barrier divides two houses and forces the lovers into night speech: orchard wall and party wall are one device at two pressures. A meeting is fixed for a tomb: the Capulet monument and the tomb of Ninus are the same stage property with different paint. A token is misread in the dark: the bloodied mantle and the Friar's undelivered letter are functionally identical, a piece of evidence whose meaning a lover must guess, and guesses wrong. The machinery of that guess is worth pausing on, because the tragedy's token is even more fragile than the comedy's. Pyramus at least holds physical evidence, a garment with blood on it, and errs in interpretation. Romeo receives no token at all, only a servant's report, and converts hearsay into certainty with the same speed, crying "Then I defy you, stars!" (5.1.24) and riding to an apothecary. The feigned-death machinery behind the letter, the potion that counterfeits a corpse for forty-two hours, belongs to the folktale substratum traced in [The Folktale Root of the Death-Feigning Potion](/2025/12/05/death-feigning-potion-folktale-root/), and it is the tragedy's equivalent of the lion: an offstage agent whose entire function is to make the evidence lie.

The first death follows in both plots from the misreading, and the second from the discovery of the first. Romeo drinks poison beside a body he believes dead, Thisbe's counterpart wakes to find the corpse and repeats the suicide with the weapon at hand; the order of weapons is reversed between the plays (the man takes the sword in Babylon and Athens, the poison in Verona; the woman takes the blade in both), but the grammar is identical: corpse, lament in questions, weapon, farewell. Even the survivors' settlements rhyme. Verona closes with a prince apportioning punishment and two fathers contracting a joint memorial; Athens closes with a duke declining the epilogue, accepting a dance instead, and dismissing the corpses to be buried by the scenery. The settlements differ in everything except structure: in both, an authority figure surveys the finished machine and declares the account closed.

The mirror also runs in the other direction, and this is where it stops being a parlour correspondence. The tragedy contains, inside itself, moments that sit one degree from the interlude's farce, and the proximity is systematic. The plot of Romeo and Juliet turns on a letter stopped by a quarantine, a friar locked out of a tomb by minutes, a bride who wakes a breath after her husband dies, a watch that arrives in time to narrate but not to rescue. Each of these is a timing accident of the kind that comedy runs on; in a comedy the same accidents resolve, the letter arrives in the second act, the sleeper wakes to an embrace, and the watch brings handcuffs for the villains. The tragedy's own servants, musicians, and clownish citizens keep the comic register warm inside the catastrophe, as if the play were continually showing the audience the other version of itself. The Dream simply takes the hint at full length. It performs the accident-plot with the resolution withheld and the performers unskilled, and lets a wedding audience laugh at the shape of the thing.

That shape explains why the interlude cannot be reduced to a joke at the tragedy's expense. Burlesque of this completeness is a form of analysis. To reproduce a plot's machinery with every part mislabelled and every effect announced is to publish the machine's blueprint, and a playwright does not publish the blueprint of a machine he despises. He publishes it because the machine fascinates him, because he has just built the most affecting version of it in the language and wants to know which parts of the effect were his and which belonged to the story all along. The answer the interlude returns is uncomfortable and exhilarating: the story's parts, unaided, produce laughter. The tears were the playwright's contribution, and they consisted of framing, tempo, diction, and an audience forbidden to comment.

## Thisbe in the Margins of the Tragedy

The tragedy never stages its twin, but it keeps the twin's name in its margins, and the marginal reference is placed with suspicious precision. In the second act of Romeo and Juliet, Mercutio, cataloguing the famous beauties of legend to prove that Rosaline is a nobody beside them, rattles through Laura, Dido, Cleopatra, Helen and Hero, and arrives at "Thisbe, a grey eye or so, but not to the purpose" (2.4). The line is a throwaway, one item in a virtuoso list, and it performs exactly the dismissal Mercutio intends: the Babylonian heroine is filed as a minor precedent, a grey eye, not to the purpose. Yet the filing is the tragedy's only classical roll call of lovers, and Thisbe is in it. At the very moment Romeo is transferring his devotion from Rosaline to Juliet, the play's resident sceptic names the heroine of the other treatment, the girl who died over a corpse at a tomb because she misread the signs, and prices her at nothing. The audience of the mid-1590s, meeting the interlude in the same repertory, was positioned to catch the price changing. What Mercutio dismisses as not to the purpose is the plot Romeo is walking into, named in advance by the man who will die first for laughing at it.

The company of the Dream returns the compliment with interest, mangling the classical names it touches until the mangling becomes a signature. Ninus, the Assyrian king whose tomb is the lovers' rendezvous, dwindles in rehearsal to "Ninny's tomb" (3.1), a christening that converts the monument of an empire into a fool's address. Cephalus and Procris shrink to Shafalus and Procrus. Even the hero's own name is unstable in the company's handling, announced in the court list with the full Ovidian dignity and then worried by the performers into something their tongues can carry. The tragedy's names move in the opposite direction, swelling with use until "Romeo" becomes a common noun for a lover and the balcony speech can ask what a name is and answer that it is nothing. The two plays thus conduct opposite experiments in onomastics. Verona discovers that names are arbitrary and lethal, that a rose by another name would smell as sweet and die as fast. Athens discovers that names are load-bearing only when pronounced correctly, and that a company which cannot pronounce them cannot be killed by them. The mechanicals survive their play partly because they never quite succeed in saying it.

There is a final marginal appearance worth recording, and it is the tragedy's own structure confessing the kinship. Romeo and Juliet opens with a Chorus that behaves exactly like Peter Quince: it stands before the curtain, summarises the entire plot including the deaths, apologises for the performance's limits, and asks the audience to attend with patient ears. The difference in execution is total, sonnet against stumble, but the function is identical, and the function is the mechanicals' own device, the plot announced in advance so that no spectator can be surprised by the ending. The tragedy adopts the device and dignifies it; the interlude adopts it and drops it on its foot. That the most sophisticated tragedy of the decade and the broadest burlesque inside its twin comedy both begin by giving away their endings is the clearest formal evidence that the two works were conceived in one workshop season, by a dramatist thinking hard about what foreknowledge does to an audience. In Verona it manufactures dread. In Athens it manufactures the leisure to laugh. The Prologue and the prologue are the same door, opening onto the two rooms of the same house.

## The Other Couples: The Dream's Own Lovers Run the Same Plot

The interlude does not mirror the tragedy alone; it also mirrors the comedy that contains it, and the double reflection is the Dream's design. The four young Athenians who flee to the wood in the first act are running the Romeo and Juliet plot inside a genre that will not let it kill them. Hermia is a daughter under a father's legal power, threatened with death or the cloister for refusing the husband chosen for her, Demetrius; she elopes at night with her preferred lover, Lysander; a rival suitor pursues; a magical agent intervenes in the dark and scrambles every attachment; swords are drawn in earnest ("I will roar" gives way to real challenges between Lysander and Demetrius); and the resolution arrives only because the presiding powers correct the error before the weapons land. Substitute a friar's potion for the love-juice and a quarantine for Puck's mistake, and the outline is Verona's. The comedy's lovers survive their plot because their accidents are supervised by beings who can reverse them. The tragedy's lovers die of theirs because the only supervisors on offer are a friar with a letter and a prince with a proclamation.

The interlude comments on this survival from inside the wedding that celebrates it. The couples who have just escaped the love-death machine by the width of a corrected spell sit in the front row and laugh at the machine's terminal version, performed by tradesmen. Their laughter is not cruelty; it is the precise response of survivors examining the wreck of the ship they nearly sailed in. Demetrius, who within the fiction was prepared to feed Lysander to his sword and to abandon Helena in a wood full of beasts, caps Pyramus's death with a joke about dice. Helena, who offered to be treated as a spaniel, watches Thisbe's devotion played by a bearded bellows-mender. The comedy builds this irony into its structure and lets the audience feel its temperature: the deaths are funny to the watchers in proportion as the watchers have been reprieved from them. A tragedy cannot offer its audience that proportion. Romeo and Juliet stations its reprieved figures, Benvolio and the Friar and the Nurse, inside the wreck, and denies everyone the front-row seat.

Theseus and Hippolyta frame the whole design as the feud plot's successful alternative. Their marriage opens the play as a conquest being converted into a wedding: "I wooed thee with my sword, and won thy love doing thee injuries" (1.1), the Duke tells his Amazon bride, and the conversion of violence into ceremony is the plot the tragedy attempts and fails to complete, the marriage that should have converted the feud and instead is consumed by it. The Athenian court has already achieved, before the action starts, the settlement that Verona reaches only over corpses: hostile houses joined by a wedding, the sword retired into metaphor. When such a court watches Pyramus and Thisbe, it watches its own prehistory as farce, the version of events in which the sword stayed literal and the lovers stayed dead. The interlude is the court's memento mori, sugar-coated by incompetence until it can be served at the feast.

Even Titania and Oberon, the play's presiding quarrellers, run a scaled-down feud with a changeling child as its prize, threaten consequences out of season, and settle their difference through enchantment and dance rather than through the deaths of their households' heirs. The Dream is thus built as a set of nested demonstrations of the same theorem the interlude states in miniature: conflict between lovers and houses is a constant of the material, and genre is the name for what the surrounding world does with it. In Athens the surrounding world is thick with correctors, fairies, dukes, laws commuted in time. In Verona it is thin to transparency. The interlude, placed at the centre of the Athenian demonstration, is where the theorem becomes visible as theatre: six workmen showing a court exactly how close the wedding came to being a funeral, and being thanked with laughter for the information.

## Barber, Snyder and Holland: The Critics Disagree

Three accounts of the interlude's place in Shakespeare's imagination dominate the discussion, and they do not agree about what the laughter means. C. L. Barber, in Shakespeare's Festive Comedy (1959), reads the Dream whole as a holiday structure in which dangerous energies are released in order to be clarified and contained, and the interlude fits his pattern neatly: the death-dealing plot is brought inside the wedding festivities, acted by clowns, laughed into harmlessness, and dismissed with a dance, so that the comedy performs a kind of inoculation, exhibiting tragedy in a weakened strain to certify the community's immunity. On Barber's reading the laughter is social hygiene, and the interlude's target is not any play but the tragic possibility itself, safely caged.

Susan Snyder's The Comic Matrix of Shakespeare's Tragedies (1979) supplies the strongest contrary pressure. Snyder argues that the early tragedies are built on comic structures that fail: Romeo and Juliet follows the pattern of a festive comedy, young lovers against blocking fathers, toward the marriage that should end it, until the machinery of accident and haste converts the expected resolution into catastrophe. Read through Snyder, the Dream's interlude is not an inoculation but a demonstration of the matrix itself: the same accidents that comedy survives (the mistimed meeting, the misread sign, the separated lovers) are laid out in sequence, and the only reason they end in farce rather than grief is that the surrounding play is a comedy whose presiding powers, Oberon and Theseus alike, keep the damage theatrical. The laughter, on this account, has an edge of recognition: the audience is watching the tragedy's engine with the casing off.

Peter Holland, in the introduction to his Oxford edition of the Dream (1994), shifts the question from genre to spectatorship. Holland stresses that the interlude is a play about watching plays: its real subject is the court's commentary, Theseus's doctrine that the best plays are "but shadows" which imagination must amend, and Hippolyta's rejoinder that the amending imagination must then be the audience's, not the performers'. On Holland's account the burlesque is affectionate toward its amateurs, in the line of a dramatist fascinated by the transaction between stage and house, and it is angled at the audience's responsibilities rather than at any rival text. Chaudhuri's Arden introduction adds the documentary caution that disciplines all three readings: the dating evidence places the Dream and the tragedy in the same phase of work, so any theory that requires one play to be a settled classic before the other mocked it is building on sand.

### Is the interlude a parody of Romeo and Juliet itself?

The interlude is not a parody of the tragedy in the strict sense, because the two plays grew in the same seasons from the same source, and the burlesque's targets are the inherited love-death convention and older tragedy's bombast. Its structural identity with the tragedy means the joke lands on the companion play anyway, as analysis rather than attack.

The adjudication adopted here divides the territory. Barber wins on occasion: the interlude's placement inside a wedding comedy, performed by tradesmen and dismissed with a dance, does contain the tragic matter socially, and no reading should surrender that frame. Snyder wins on structure: the sequence of accidents is the tragedy's own, and the recognition she predicts is the scene's deepest effect, the reason the laughter keeps catching. Holland wins on mechanism: the variable that decides the outcome, in the fiction and in the theatre, is the watching, the willingness or refusal of an audience to amend shadows with imagination, which is why the court keeps interrupting and why Verona, given no court of commentators, must let its watchers arrive as a watch, too late, with torches. The verdict follows: the interlude is the tragedy's mirror rather than its parody, and what it reflects is that the love-death plot has no inherent genre at all. It has a frame, a tempo, a level of diction, and a house that has or has not agreed to believe.

## Death at the Feast: Two Banquets, Two Entertainments

Both plays station their decisive scenes at feasts, and the correspondence is worth a section of its own because it explains the interlude's otherwise baffling programming. The tragedy ignites at a banquet: Capulet's feast in the first act, where Romeo sees Juliet across a dance floor, where Tybalt's rage is lit and banked, and where the lovers' first exchange takes the form of a sonnet traded between courses of a party their houses' hatred has made contraband. The feast in Verona is a room where the feud is suspended by hospitality and therefore at its most dangerous, since every courtesy is a treaty under strain. The comedy culminates at a banquet: Theseus's wedding feast, where the entertainment is a tragedy of love and death performed by the household's social inferiors. In each case the feast is the one room where the whole society is gathered in its finery, obliged to be pleasant, and the playwright uses the obligation as a pressure chamber. Verona's feast produces a love that will kill the feasters' children. Athens' feast consumes a killing as an after-dinner amusement. The rooms are the same room, dressed for opposite weathers.

The parallel casts a cold light backwards onto the tragedy's feast. Capulet, restraining Tybalt from expelling Romeo, argues from the obligations of hospitality with a host's instinct for the occasion's surface, and the tragedy's catastrophe is seeded in that courtesy: the unexpelled guest meets the daughter, the banked rage finds its account in the third act. Theseus, selecting the entertainment, exercises exactly the same host's instinct in the opposite interest, choosing the dangerous item on the menu because graciousness requires that the offerers be honoured, and the choice is rewarded with laughter instead of blood. Two hosts, two feasts, two decisions to let the volatile thing stay in the room. In Verona the volatile thing is a lovesick Montague. In Athens it is a double suicide in rhyme. The outcomes differ because the volatile things differ in kind, a young man against a performance, but the hosts' reasoning is identical, and the identity is the plays' shared joke about festivity: a feast is where a society agrees, for one evening, not to act on what it knows, and drama is what happens when the agreement is tested.

Hippolyta's resistance to the programme gives the comedy its conscience in this matter, and her two interventions bookend the performance with a discomfort the court must talk itself out of. She does not like to see the poor men wronged for their pains, and she is aweary of Moonshine within minutes of his entrance. Her impatience is usually played as aristocratic fastidiousness, but its substance is ethical: she is the only watcher who keeps registering that real people are labouring inside the joke, a bride who was herself won by the sword and has some standing to notice when violence is being served as dessert. Verona distributes her function among figures who arrive too late to exercise it, a friar explaining, a prince sentencing, a nurse lamenting, each registering after the fact the discomfort Hippolyta registers in real time. The comedy gives its audience a conscience on stage and lets the court overrule her with doctrine and dice jokes. The tragedy gives its audience the same conscience and no court to overrule it, which is why the discomfort of Romeo and Juliet has nowhere to sit and must be carried out of the theatre by the audience itself.

## Professionals Playing Amateurs: The Repertory Mirror

One further mirror is built into the casting of the original performances, and it deserves its own section because it converts the interlude from a literary device into a document of the theatre that produced both plays. The mechanicals were not played by amateurs. They were played by the Lord Chamberlain's Men, the most accomplished company in London, the same sharers and hired men who performed Romeo and Juliet in the same repertory seasons. The joke of Snug's slow study and Flute's beard is therefore a professional impersonation of amateurism, executed with a precision that only mastery makes possible: it takes a superb actor to misplace a prologue's punctuation so exactly that the sense reverses, and a disciplined company to sustain a death scene at the precise tempo of incompetence for two hundred lines without letting it curdle into mere noise. The interlude is the company's self-portrait in a distorting mirror, and the distortion is affectionate. Shakespeare wrote for these men the parts of men who cannot do what the men playing them do supremely well.

The repertory context sharpens the reflection. An audience of the mid-1590s could see, within the same season and from the same stage, the tragedy of the Veronese lovers performed in earnest and the tragedy of the Babylonian lovers performed as a cautionary exhibit of earnestness mishandled. The two experiences interpret each other retroactively. A spectator who had laughed at Wall on one afternoon carried the memory into the tomb scene on another, and discovered that the laughter did not protect against the grief, because the grief was never located in the machinery the laughter had exposed. A spectator who had wept at Juliet's dagger and then watched Flute die by the same weapon in rhyme discovered that the dagger had not been diminished either. The company was, in effect, running a public seminar on its own art, alternating the masterpiece with its x-ray. No other dramatist of the period attempted anything like the pairing at this closeness, and the confidence it implies, in the audience's sophistication as much as in the company's range, is part of what the twin-plays chart's dry correspondences cannot capture.

The professional mirror also explains the interlude's odd tenderness toward its bunglers. Quince's company is never punished for its failure. It is paid, thanked, offered the choice of an epilogue or a dance, and folded into the wedding it entertained. Theseus's verdict on the whole performance is the most generous sentence ever passed on bad theatre: the men have "played" their parts with an intent that redeems the execution, and the court that could amend shadows with imagination found the exercise delightful rather than deficient. Set against the Prince's Verona, where every failure of execution, a letter undelivered, a message garbled, a potion mistimed, is paid for in corpses and sentences, the Athenian settlement reads as the theatre's own wish for itself: a world in which getting it wrong is survivable. The tragedy is the world in which it is not. The same company commuted between the two worlds nightly, and the interlude is the door they built between them, with a weaver's corpse left politely on the threshold to prop it open.

## What the Mirror Proves: Genre Is a Frame, Not a Plot

The pairing's final lesson concerns the tragedy more than the comedy, and it cuts against the most common way of praising Romeo and Juliet, which is to treat its ending as the inevitable expression of a great love. The interlude demonstrates, with the rigour of a laboratory control, that nothing in the shared plot is inherently moving. Two households, a wall, a night appointment, a misleading token, a tomb, two suicides: the Athens performance runs the identical sequence and produces helpless laughter in an audience of lovers at a wedding. Whatever makes Verona's version tragic is therefore not in the sequence. It is in the sonnet-framed Chorus that forbids detachment, in verse that sustains belief at the pitch where inspection would break it, in the withholding of any on-stage commentator licensed to joke, and in a closing authority who sentences rather than dances. Genre, the mirror proves, is a set of decisions about reception taken before the first line is spoken, and the plot is merely the machine those decisions operate. The bond between the two modes, their habit of sharing structures while reversing outcomes, is the subject of [The Comic and the Tragic Bound Together](/2024/11/22/comedy-and-tragedy-bound-together/), and the interlude is its most compact exhibit.

The demonstration also explains the tragedy's peculiar afterlife as a byword. A plot that can tip either way under framing is permanently unstable in the culture that inherits it, open to reverence and to ridicule in the same gesture, quoted at weddings it would have emptied and parodied by performers who need its outline intact to make the joke legible. Bottom rising from the dead to explain that the wall is down is not the enemy of the golden statues in Verona. The tradesman and the statues are two settlements of the same account, offered by one playwright in two registers within the same phase of his work, and each is the other's best commentary. The tragedy shows what the machine does when a theatre agrees to believe. The interlude shows what the machine is. Shakespeare, uniquely among the story's many retellers, insisted on showing both, and the insistence is the surest sign that he knew exactly what he had built.

For students of either play, the practical consequence is a discipline: read the tragedy with the interlude's blueprint open beside it, and the interlude with the tragedy's stakes held in mind, and each text grades the other's claims. Neither survives the comparison diminished. Both survive it explained, which is the most that criticism ever honestly offers.

## The Interlude's Afterlife: The Joke That Outlived the Occasion

The interlude's subsequent history confirms the twinship from the far side of four centuries. The mechanicals' scenes became the most durable portion of the Dream in performance, the part of the play that survived every era's taste. The role of Bottom was the company's clown's showpiece from the beginning, associated in the early tradition with Will Kempe, the Chamberlain's Men's great improvising comedian, which means the interlude was built as a star vehicle inside a wedding comedy, a tragedian's plot entrusted to the most famous funny man in London. Restoration taste, which found much of Shakespeare barbarous, kept the Dream alive in adapted form, culminating in Purcell's semi-opera The Fairy Queen in 1692, where the rustics' tragedy sits amid masques and dances as the evening's reliable comic capital. Mendelssohn's overture of 1826 and his incidental music of 1842 then fixed the Dream in the culture as the wedding play par excellence, its march processing brides down aisles to this day, so that the comedy containing a lovers' double suicide performed at a wedding became, in actual social practice, the music weddings are married to. The joke's premise, that a wedding is exactly where this tragedy belongs, turned out to be the culture's own conclusion, reached by way of a seventeen-year-old composer's overture.

The tragedy's afterlife runs in mirror image, as the twinship predicts. Romeo and Juliet became the culture's default citation for love itself, quoted at the weddings the Dream's march accompanies, its balcony reproduced on cards and its tomb politely forgotten, while the interlude became the default citation for amateur theatre, for every village-hall Pyramus whose wall is a man in a sheet. Benjamin Britten's opera of 1960, the twentieth century's most faithful musical Dream, gives the rustics a full mock-tragic idiom of their own, sliding trombones and all, and treats their deaths with the same double vision the original court practised, funny and faintly desolate at once. Modern productions of the tragedy increasingly play the correspondence knowingly, letting the letter's failure or the tomb's timing show the farce inside the mechanism for a beat before the grief closes over it. The twins continue to interpret each other in the repertory, which is all this article has argued they were designed to do. The mechanicals were never an interruption of Shakespeare's serious work in the mid-1590s. They were the control experiment that proved the serious work was serious by art rather than by accident, and four centuries of stages have kept rerunning the experiment because the result never gets old: the same lovers, the same wall, the same tomb, and the whole difference lying in how the room has agreed to watch.

## The Refused Epilogue: How Each Play Lets Go of Its Dead

The endings of the two performances repay a final comparison, because they divide over the question of who owns the dead once the dying is done. In Athens, the corpses get up. Bottom rises from Pyramus's body to answer Demetrius's quibble about the wall, offers the court a choice of entertainments to close the evening, an epilogue or a bergomask dance of the company in its own persons, and is refused the epilogue with courtesy: "No epilogue, I pray you; for your play needs no excuse" (5.1). The dance follows, the tradesmen exit as themselves, and the play's true epilogue is given to Puck, who asks the real audience for its hands and reframes the whole evening as a dream that pardon can dissolve. Death inside the fiction is thus annulled twice over, once by the actors standing up and once by the fairy who declares the shadows harmless if the watchers are gentle. The comedy's contract with its audience ends in restitution: nothing that happened is allowed to keep happening after the applause.

Verona permits no such restitution, and engineers its ending to forbid one. The bodies in the tomb stay down. The living who find them are not offered a dance but a reckoning: the Prince's sentence apportions the punishment to heaven, to the houses, and to himself, the Friar's narrative is entered as evidence, and the fathers compete in memorial expenditure, each promising the other's child a statue in pure gold. The tragedy's epilogue is the Chorus's function inverted: where the Prologue promised "the two hours' traffic of our stage," the closing lines deliver "a glooming peace" and the declaration that no story of more woe exists (5.3.309-316). The audience is sent home in possession of the corpses, which is the tragedy's definition of seriousness. Nothing is dissolved; everything is inherited.

Between these two settlements stands the most quietly devastating correspondence of all, and it concerns explanation. The Dream's performance requires no explanation afterwards and gets none: the shadows are pardoned, the dream is confessed as a dream, and the fiction closes its own account. The tragedy cannot stop explaining. Friar Laurence's long final narrative retells the entire plot to the assembled watchers, converting experience into testimony, because in Verona the events were real inside the fiction and reality demands a record. The interlude's events, being confessed theatre, need no record and leave none, except in the memories of a court that will remember the evening as the one with the wall in it. Shakespeare's two plays thus divide the afterlife of their dead between them: the Babylonian lovers are remembered as a joke told at a wedding, the Veronese lovers as a case tried at dawn. The plots are the same. The paperwork is the genre.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch twin-plays chart, and which eleven beats does it align?

The InsightCrunch twin-plays chart sets the plot beats of Romeo and Juliet beside the corresponding beats of the mechanicals' Pyramus and Thisbe in A Midsummer Night's Dream, row by row. Each row names a shared device, the barrier, the night meeting, the wild danger, the light, the fatal token, the two deaths, the frame, the watchers, and the settlement, and states the tragedy's version, the interlude's version, and the lesson the mirror teaches. The chart exists because the correspondence is too systematic to hold in prose summary: eleven beats align, and in every case the plot element is constant while the treatment reverses. Its purpose is analytical rather than decorative, converting the claim that genre is a frame into evidence that can be checked against both texts.

### Q: Why did Shakespeare place a lovers' double suicide inside a comedy's wedding entertainment?

The double suicide sits inside the wedding entertainment because the Dream is built as a set of near misses, and the interlude is the one version of the love plot in which the near miss is allowed to land. The Athenian lovers escape death by enchantment corrected in time; Theseus and Hippolyta have converted a war into a marriage before the action begins; the mechanicals' lovers receive no rescue, because their story is a performance, and its deaths let the wedding audience contemplate the outcome every other couple in the play has avoided. The placement also serves the comedy's honesty about its occasion. A wedding feast that stages a tragedy of separated lovers acknowledges, under the protection of laughter, the risk that the celebration is organised to deny.

### Q: What does "very tragical mirth" mean, and why does Theseus choose the play anyway?

"Very tragical mirth" is Philostrate's description of the mechanicals' offering in the list of entertainments, a tedious brief scene of Pyramus and Thisbe that promises tragedy and delivers mirth, and Theseus seizes on the contradiction with delight, calling it hot ice and wondrous strange snow. He chooses the play against his master of revels' explicit warning because his stated doctrine is that nothing offered in simpleness and duty can be amiss, and because the choice lets the court honour the artisans' labour while enjoying the failure. The selection is therefore both gracious and self-serving: the Duke performs condescension, secures the evening's best joke, and keeps the authority to define the performance's meaning through commentary while it runs.

### Q: Why must Snug's lion announce that he is not really a lion?

Snug's lion announces his harmlessness because the company has decided in rehearsal that a believed lion is a danger to the enterprise: the ladies might shriek, the performance might be judged an offence, and the players' reward, or worse, might be lost. The disclaimer is drafted as policy, with the actor's face to be visible and his name and trade spoken aloud, so that no spectator can mistake representation for reality. The device inverts the tragedy's practice, where the corresponding dangers, the feud, the letter, the potion, are never labelled and therefore operate at full strength. In the interlude's logic, a danger explained is a danger disarmed, and the lion's certificate of harmlessness is the company's whole theory of theatre stated by its least articulate member.

### Q: Why is Moonshine played by a man carrying a lantern, a thorn bush and a dog?

Moonshine is played by inventory because the company cannot trust the audience to accept a convention it has not itemised. The rehearsal first considers using actual moonlight through an open casement, then settles on Starveling presenting the moon in person: the lantern is the moon, the man is the man in the moon, the thorn bush is his bush, and the dog is his dog, each element of the folk image supplied as a prop and then explained in prose when Theseus quibbles. The comedy exposes how much of tragedy's atmosphere depends on unexplained convention. Romeo swears by the moon and Juliet forbids the oath, and no one on stage or off asks for the moon's credentials. Starveling's lantern is what moonlight looks like when a theatre refuses to let the audience imagine anything unassisted.

### Q: How does the whispering through Wall's chink mirror the balcony scene in Romeo and Juliet?

The whispering through the chink mirrors the balcony scene as the same stage grammar executed at opposite levels of skill. Both scenes place a barrier between lovers that transmits voice and forbids touch, set the exchange at night under threat of discovery, and build the dialogue from vows, precedents, and plans for a later meeting. At the chink the barrier is literal masonry played by a tinker, the senses collapse into Pyramus's "I see a voice," and the vows cite garbled classical lovers. Across the balcony the barrier is distance and darkness, the senses sharpen into the play's finest love poetry, and the vows are audited as they are sworn. The mirror shows that the balcony scene's sublimity is an achievement of execution laid over a device simple enough for tradesmen to operate badly.

### Q: What object does Pyramus misread at the tomb, and what serves the same purpose in Romeo and Juliet?

Pyramus misreads Thisbe's mantle, dropped in flight and bloodied in the lion's mouth, taking a garment as proof that its owner has been killed. The equivalent device in Romeo and Juliet is the Friar's letter, which never reaches Romeo and leaves a servant's bare report to do the mantle's work: an absent, unverifiable sign that a lover interprets in the dark, alone, at speed, and wrong. The correspondence is functional rather than visual, since the letter is not stained or torn, but its evidentiary role is identical, a token whose meaning must be guessed and whose wrong guess is immediately acted on with a weapon. Both plots are engines for converting a semiotic failure into a corpse, and both locate the failure at a tomb at night.

### Q: Why does Pyramus name the exact spot, "that left pap", when he stabs himself?

Pyramus names the exact spot because the burlesque style he dies in cannot leave a gesture unadministered. His death speech is built from the older tragedy's habits of apostrophe and inventory, and the specification of the left pap, where the heart hops, turns the wound into a diagram filed with the audience. The precision is funny because it reverses the function of detail in serious dying speeches, where concrete particulars authenticate feeling. Here the particulars replace feeling, ticking off tears, sword, breast, tongue, moon, and soul in sequence while the court scores the performance. Bottom's Pyramus is not experiencing death but processing it through the recognised stations, and the anatomy lesson is the surest sign that the form has taken over from the event it was built to carry.

### Q: Why does Thisbe's lament over the body keep breaking into questions?

Thisbe's lament breaks into questions because interrogation is the rhetoric of a discovery that the mind refuses to complete, and both plays give that rhetoric to the survivor at the corpse. She asks whether the beloved sleeps, commands him to arise and speak, answers the silence herself, and only then accepts the fact and reaches for the sword. Juliet's discovery of Romeo in the tomb follows the same grammar of unanswered address, shortened by the approach of the watch into a fraction of the space Flute is allowed. The questions are not decoration in either case. They dramatise the interval between seeing and believing, the few lines in which the plot's misreading could still be corrected, and the interlude stretches that interval until its pathos curdles into routine while the tragedy compresses it until it detonates.

### Q: Why do Theseus and the courtiers keep talking through the tragedy they are watching?

The courtiers talk through the performance because commentary is their assigned function in the scene's design: the interlude exists to be watched by watchers whose responses the real audience is meant to study. Theseus annotates from doctrine, Hippolyta from impatience and unease, Demetrius and Lysander from the connoisseurship of young men newly reprieved from a love plot of their own. Their interruptions do the work that the tragedy's structure forbids, releasing pressure, marking the fiction as fiction, and converting every potentially painful beat into a judgement on execution. Romeo and Juliet stations no such licensed commentators inside its tomb scene, so the pressure has nowhere to go. The interlude demonstrates, by supplying them, that on-stage laughter is a technology for managing an audience's belief.

### Q: What does Theseus mean by calling the best plays "but shadows"?

Theseus means that even the finest theatrical representation is insubstantial, a shadow of the thing it figures, and that the worst performance is no worse than the best if the audience's imagination amends the deficiency. The doctrine is delivered as a great man's tolerance before the interlude and tested by everything that follows. Hippolyta's reply exposes its condescension: if imagination must do the amending, then the credit belongs to the watchers, not the players. Applied to the tragedy, the doctrine cuts the other way. Romeo and Juliet is a shadow that audiences refuse to treat as one, amending nothing and believing everything, and the interlude shows the same shadows failing to compel belief in a house that has decided in advance to stand outside them.

### Q: Which play was written first, Romeo and Juliet or A Midsummer Night's Dream, and can the order be proved?

The order cannot be proved from surviving evidence. Romeo and Juliet was in print by 1597, the Dream was known to Francis Meres by 1598 and printed in 1600, and stylistic analysis dates both plays to about 1595 or 1596, within the same phase of Shakespeare's work for the Lord Chamberlain's Men. Editors including Peter Holland and Sukanta Chaudhuri treat the two as near-simultaneous, and the hypothesis that the Dream was written for a particular wedding, which might have fixed its date, remains unverified. The uncertainty is not an inconvenience for the comparison but its foundation: because neither play can be established as the settled original, the interlude cannot be read as a later parody of a finished classic, and the pairing must be understood as twin treatments developed together.

### Q: Why does Bottom want to play Pyramus, Thisbe and the lion in the same performance?

Bottom wants every part because his idea of acting is appetite rather than impersonation: each role is a chance to display a voice, a roar, a death, and the boundaries between characters are, to him, merely obstacles between an audience and more Bottom. Quince's casting session is a negotiation between that appetite and the minimum conditions of intelligible drama, one body to a part. The joke carries the interlude's thesis in miniature. A tragedy requires the fiction of separate persons whose fates can collide; the lover, the beloved, and the beast must be three beings or the plot cannot happen. Bottom's ambition to be all three at once is the plot's dissolution stated as a casting preference, and the company's refusal of it is the first rescue the story receives.

### Q: Why does Hippolyta compare Quince's prologue to a child playing a recorder?

Hippolyta compares the prologue to a child on a recorder because Quince produces the sounds of the speech without governing its sense: the punctuation is misplaced, the clauses attach to the wrong intentions, and the welcome announces the opposite of what the company means. A recorder in a child's hands gives notes without music, and the simile isolates the failure as one of articulation rather than material, which Theseus confirms by calling the speech a tangled chain, nothing impaired but all disordered. The comparison matters because the tragedy opens with the same device perfectly governed, a Chorus whose sonnet delivers the whole plot in order. Between the two prologues lies the whole difference in framing on which the twin plays turn: information mastered, and information dropped.

### Q: Why does the interlude end with a dance instead of the epilogue Bottom offers?

The interlude ends with a dance because Theseus's refusal of the epilogue is the court's final act of generic management. Bottom, risen from Pyramus's corpse, offers a spoken epilogue or a bergomask, and the Duke chooses the dance on the ground that the play needs no excuse. An epilogue would require the performers to step forward and account for the fiction in words, reopening everything the laughter has settled; a dance returns the tradesmen to the wedding as bodies rather than interpreters and dissolves the tragedy into the festivity without commentary. The tragedy permits no equivalent dissolution. Verona's survivors must narrate, sentence, and memorialise, because in that fiction the deaths were real. Athens can dance because its deaths were announced as shadows from the start.

### Q: How does Susan Snyder's comic matrix change the way the interlude should be read?

Susan Snyder's argument that Shakespeare's early tragedies are built on comic structures that fail changes the interlude from a detachable joke into a piece of evidence. On her account, Romeo and Juliet follows the festive pattern of young lovers, blocking elders, and expected marital resolution until accident and haste convert the pattern into catastrophe. Read through that lens, the mechanicals' performance is the matrix with its casing removed: the same separation, night meeting, misread sign, and tomb, presented inside a comedy whose governing powers keep the damage theatrical. The interlude then reads less as relief from the tragedy than as the tragedy's blueprint performed by workmen, and the court's laughter becomes an act of recognition that the audience is invited to share uneasily rather than innocently.

### Q: Why does the bloodied mantle convince Pyramus that Thisbe is dead when no body is present?

The mantle convinces Pyramus because the plot has trained him, and every reader of the tale since Ovid, to treat the beloved's garment as the beloved's proxy: it was last seen in her flight, it carries blood, and the lion's presence supplies the narrative that connects them. His inference is not stupid; it is the correct reading of planted evidence in a world whose author has arranged the evidence to mislead, which is why the same structure governs the tragedy, where a stopped letter and a servant's report are equally well planted. The interlude makes the mechanism visible by slowing it down and letting the court watch a man reason his way to a false corpse. The tragedy's speed conceals the joinery. Both lovers die of evidence handled exactly as evidence is supposed to be handled.

### Q: What does the interlude reveal about how narrowly Romeo and Juliet escapes farce?

The interlude reveals that the escape is a matter of execution and framing rather than of plot, since the tragedy's catastrophe is assembled from materials that comedy uses openly: a wall between lovers, a night assignation, a feigned death, a message delayed by accident, a sleeper waking minutes too late, a watch arriving in time to describe rather than prevent. Each element, announced by a prologue, labelled by its performer, and commented on by an on-stage audience, becomes funny in the mechanicals' hands without being altered in substance. The tragedy holds the identical elements at the opposite settings, unlabelled, uncommented, and sustained in verse that forbids inspection, and the narrowness of the margin is the revelation: one licensed joker inside the tomb scene, one prologue that stumbles, and Verona's ending changes genre.

### Q: Why are the mechanicals' trade names part of the joke about the tragedy?

The trade names are part of the joke because they keep announcing the material world that the love plot must forget in order to be believed. Bottom the weaver, Snug the joiner, Flute the bellows-mender, Snout the tinker, Starveling the tailor, and Quince the carpenter carry their workshops in their names, several of them punning on their crafts, and the fiction of Babylonian aristocracy never survives contact with the payroll. Tragedy depends on persons whose identities are exhausted by their passions; the interlude's persons are exhausted by their jobs, and their tragedy keeps being interrupted by competence, caution, and the need to be home for work. The names are the company's disclaimers worn in advance, guaranteeing that whatever dies on the Athenian stage, the trades of Athens will be at their benches in the morning.

### Q: How can an essay use the twin-plays chart to argue that genre is a frame rather than a plot?

An essay can use the chart by taking any single row and demonstrating that the plot element is identical while the response reverses, then asking what variable the row isolates. The wall row isolates explanation, the token row isolates tempo of interpretation, the watchers row isolates commentary, and the settlement row isolates the authority that closes the account. Accumulated across the eleven rows, the demonstration shows that no row requires a change in what happens, only in how the happening is framed, lit, phrased, and received. The conclusion follows with unusual cleanliness for a literary argument: genre in these twin plays is not a property of stories but of the contracts under which stories are staged, and Shakespeare, writing both contracts in the same seasons, left the comparison as the proof.
