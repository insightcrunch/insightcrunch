Most readers who set out to understand American foreign-intelligence collection law run into the same confusion within the first hour of reading. Two names appear on nearly every page, attached to nearly every controversy, and treated by commentators as though they were rival answers to the same question: the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act of 1978, known as FISA, and the USA PATRIOT Act of 2001. Articles describe programs as "FISA surveillance" in one paragraph and "PATRIOT Act surveillance" in the next, as if the two statutes were competing systems, one older and one newer, and a student had to pick which one to believe. That framing is wrong in a way that corrupts everything built on top of it, and the correction is the whole subject of this comparison. FISA is the framework statute. The 2001 act is an amendment package. Almost every authority that public discussion attributes to the 2001 legislation actually operates through the 1978 statute, because the 2001 act did its most consequential work not by building a parallel system but by rewriting the standards, procedures, and coordination rules of the system FISA had already built. Once that relationship is clear, a series of smaller puzzles dissolve on their own: why the surveillance court belongs to one law and not the other, why some provisions expired on a schedule while the underlying statute never did, why the 2008 addition of Section 702 was an amendment to FISA rather than a third rival system, and why a student who wants to understand how foreign-intelligence collection is authorized should begin with the framework statute and treat the amendment package as the second stop.

The stakes of getting this distinction right are practical rather than academic. A lawyer, journalist, student, or engaged citizen who reads the 2001 act looking for the surveillance court will not find it, because Congress created that court twenty-three years earlier. A researcher who reads the 1978 statute looking for the business-records provision in its original form will not find it either, because the provision that became famous under the 2001 act arrived through a 1998 authorization measure and was then reshaped by the 2001 amendments. The Congressional Research Service, the Government Publishing Office, the Department of Justice, and the federal courts all describe the two enactments in the same structural terms used here, and this article follows those sources rather than the looser language of public debate. The article is organized along four axes the series uses for every comparison: Nature, which asks what kind of legal instrument each enactment is; Scope, which asks how far across the statute books each one reaches; Durability, which asks which parts endure and which parts were designed to lapse; and Function, which asks how each authority actually operates in practice. The verdict at the end names the single deciding factor that resolves the comparison for a reader deciding what to study first.

How to use this article follows from its structure. Readers in a hurry can extract the working method from three elements alone: the namable claim stated in the next section, the which-statute table that assigns every major authority to its creator and modifier, and the verdict's deciding factor, which is study sequence. Readers with more time should follow the four axes in order, because each axis answers a question the previous one raises. Nature establishes what kind of legal instrument each enactment is. Scope shows how far each one reaches across the code. Durability explains which parts were built to last and which were built to lapse. Function demonstrates how the amended authorities operate inside the framework's machinery. Supplementary sections fill in the history before the framework, the title-by-title contents of the package, the reauthorization debates, the courts' interpretation of the amendments, the chapter's layout for first-time readers, and the key terms defined in place. The frequently asked questions then revisit the comparison's central puzzles in self-contained form, with the eight seeded questions first and twelve new ones extending the analysis. Throughout, later developments are explicitly dated as later, in keeping with the article's July 2014 vantage point, so that the state of the law described as current is always the law as it stood on the article's date.

## The framework-and-amendments distinction

The namable claim of this article is the framework-and-amendments distinction, and it can be stated in one sentence: FISA is a framework statute that created a durable legal architecture for foreign-intelligence collection inside the United States, and the USA PATRIOT Act is an amendment package that modified that architecture along with about a dozen other statutes, without creating a parallel surveillance system of its own. Everything else in the comparison is an elaboration of that sentence. A framework statute defines the institutions, the legal standards, and the procedures through which a government activity is authorized and overseen. An amendment package is a legislative vehicle that changes existing statutes, sometimes many of them at once, usually in response to a perceived gap or failure. The 1978 enactment built the house. The 2001 enactment remodeled several of its rooms, knocked down one interior wall, and added wiring to a dozen neighboring houses on the same street. Confusing the remodeler for the architect is the error this article exists to prevent, and it is an error with real consequences, because the two kinds of law answer different questions, expire on different schedules, and are studied in a different order.

Congress passed the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act as Public Law 95-511, recorded at 92 Stat. 1783, and President Carter signed it on October 25, 1978. The measure is codified at Title 50 of the United States Code, chapter 36, beginning at section 1801, where it remains the statutory home of the rules governing electronic surveillance and related collection conducted for foreign-intelligence purposes within the United States. Twenty-three years later, Congress passed the Uniting and Strengthening America by Providing Appropriate Tools Required to Intercept and Obstruct Terrorism Act of 2001, universally shortened to the USA PATRIOT Act, as Public Law 107-56, and President George W. Bush signed it on October 26, 2001. The 2001 measure did not create a new title of the United States Code and did not establish a second surveillance court or a second set of collection procedures. Instead it amended FISA and roughly a dozen other statutes, touching criminal procedure, immigration law, money-laundering law, and intelligence organization alongside the surveillance authorities that dominate public memory. That is what an amendment package does, and the 2001 act is one of the largest such packages in American legislative history, spanning ten titles whose subjects range from victim compensation to critical-infrastructure protection.

The two enactments' legislative histories read like opposite theories of lawmaking, and the vote margins record the difference. The 1978 framework emerged from years of investigation and deliberation: Senate passage 95 to 1 on April 20, 1978, House passage 246 to 128 on September 7, 1978, and conference adoption 226 to 176 on October 12, 1978. The 2001 package emerged in six weeks: House passage 357 to 66 on October 24, 2001, as Roll Call number 398, and Senate passage 98 to 1 the next day, as Record Vote number 313, with Senator Feingold casting the lone negative vote, before presidential signature on October 26. Neither history criticizes the other; emergencies and charters are different tasks. But the histories explain the products. Deliberation produces frameworks, because only deliberation supplies the time to design definitions, courts, and procedures meant to work for decades. Emergency produces amendment packages, because only amendment, striking and inserting words in laws that already work, can move at emergency speed.

### Why do so many discussions treat the PATRIOT Act as the creator of modern surveillance programs?
Because the 2001 package changed the operating rules of the 1978 statute during a crisis, and public memory attached the resulting system to the amendment rather than the framework. The section 218 purpose change, the section 504 coordination rule, and the section 215 business-records rewrite were innovations, but each operates inside the Title 50 chapter Congress enacted in 1978.

The distinction matters because the two enactments fail in different ways when misunderstood. Misread FISA as a 2001 creation and the entire pre-2001 history of the surveillance court, including the original certification standard and the original minimization procedures, drops out of the picture, leaving a reader unable to explain what the 2001 act actually changed. Misread the 2001 act as a framework statute and a reader goes looking for its institutions, its court, its freestanding code chapter, and finds none of them, because amendment packages do not build institutions; they edit them. The Government Publishing Office catalogs the 2001 measure as an act that amends other acts, and the Congressional Research Service analyzes its surveillance provisions as amendments to named sections of Title 50, chapter 36, not as a new chapter. The courts have followed the same structure. When the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court of Review decided In re Sealed Case, 310 F.3d 717, in 2002, it was interpreting a FISA provision as amended by the 2001 act, and the opinion treats the underlying statute as the 1978 enactment throughout. That is the pattern to internalize: whenever a provision is described as a creation of the 2001 act, the disciplined question is which pre-existing statute it amended and what the provision looked like before the amendment.

## Before the framework: why Congress wrote FISA

A comparison of the 1978 statute and the 2001 package becomes sharper when the reader knows what the country looked like before the framework existed, because the 1978 enactment was an answer to a specific constitutional problem that the later amendments never had to solve. For most of the twentieth century, foreign-intelligence collection inside the United States operated without a comprehensive statutory charter. Presidents and attorneys general authorized electronic surveillance for national-security purposes under claims of inherent executive authority, with practices shifting from administration to administration and with no judge reviewing the resulting collection in advance. The governing norms were executive orders, attorney-general memoranda, and unwritten understandings inside the intelligence agencies, none of them visible to the public and none of them tested against a published legal standard. Criminal wiretapping had been brought under statutory control by Title III of the Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act of 1968, which imposed warrant procedures and detailed findings for law-enforcement interception, but Congress had expressly declined in that measure to address national-security surveillance, leaving the field to executive discretion and to whatever limits the federal courts might eventually impose.

The Supreme Court narrowed that discretion without eliminating the open question at its center. In United States v. United States District Court, 407 U.S. 297 (1972), widely called the Keith case after the district judge whose order was at issue, the Court held that the Fourth Amendment required prior judicial approval for electronic surveillance undertaken for domestic-security purposes, even when the executive branch invoked national security as its justification. The opinion reasoned that the risks of unchecked executive surveillance, the potential for abuse against political dissent, and the absence of any neutral check outweighed the government's claimed need for freedom from judicial oversight in the domestic-security context. The opinion, however, expressly reserved the question of surveillance directed against foreign powers and their agents, noting that different considerations might apply where foreign threats were concerned and leaving Congress to legislate for that category. That reservation is the hinge on which the entire 1978 framework turns. The Court told the political branches, in effect, that the foreign-intelligence category was theirs to regulate, and it invited a legislative solution that would supply the judicial check the Fourth Amendment discussion had shown to be necessary.

Lower courts divided over the reserved question through the early 1970s, with some sustaining warrantless foreign-intelligence collection under an executive-power rationale and others expressing doubt about how far that rationale could stretch, and the executive branch continued to authorize such collection on its own say-so while the law remained unsettled. The resulting uncertainty served nobody well. Intelligence officials could not be sure which of their activities a court would later sustain, civil-liberties critics could not point to a published standard against which to measure executive claims, and the public debate proceeded largely in the dark, fed by leaks and partial disclosures rather than by statutory text anyone could read.

The political settlement arrived through investigation. The Senate Select Committee to Study Governmental Operations with Respect to Intelligence Activities, chaired by Senator Frank Church and active across 1975 and 1976, examined the intelligence agencies' domestic activities in public hearings and published reports that documented a sustained record of collection capabilities being turned against domestic political activity. The committee's findings covered the FBI's counterintelligence programs directed at civil-rights leaders, antiwar protesters, and political organizations, as well as National Security Agency watch-listing practices that swept in the communications of Americans. The details filled volumes, but the structural conclusion fit in a sentence: agencies operating without written charters and without external checks had repeatedly exceeded any plausible definition of their missions, and the only durable correction was legislation that defined the missions, created the checks, and published the rules. The committee's work did not dictate the precise design of the answer, and members disagreed about particulars, but it established the premise the 1978 statute embodies. Collection for foreign-intelligence purposes would proceed under a statute, through a court, according to published standards, rather than under unwritten executive authority.

The legislative path from that premise to the enacted framework ran through several Congresses and a genuine constitutional negotiation between the branches. Proposals for a foreign-intelligence surveillance charter circulated for years, with the executive branch resisting any scheme it viewed as intruding on presidential prerogative and with legislators insisting that the Keith reservation demanded exactly such a scheme. The bill that became Public Law 95-511 passed the Senate 95 to 1 on April 20, 1978, passed the House 246 to 128 on September 7, 1978, and emerged from conference to pass the House again 226 to 176 on October 12, 1978, before President Carter signed it on October 25. The lopsided Senate vote and the contested House votes together tell the story of the compromise: broad agreement that a charter was needed, sharp disagreement about how demanding its standards should be, and a final text that balanced judicial oversight against operational flexibility in ways both sides could accept.

The design Congress chose reflects the Church Committee's premise in every structural choice. The definitional passage codified at 50 U.S.C. section 1801 draws the categories the whole framework depends on: foreign intelligence information, agents of foreign powers, and electronic surveillance itself, each defined so that the rest of the statute can operate by reference to settled terms rather than ad hoc judgment. Definitions do quiet but essential work in a framework statute. By specifying what counts as foreign-intelligence information, the statute tells collectors, courts, and overseers which activities belong inside the system and which belong to ordinary criminal law. By defining agents of foreign powers, it draws the boundary around the persons who may be targeted, a boundary the lone-wolf provision would later adjust for one category of terrorism suspects. Without those definitions, the court created by the statute would have no stable jurisdiction and the standards would have no stable object.

The court provisions create a forum whose only business is reviewing foreign-intelligence applications, staffed by sitting district judges designated by the Chief Justice, so that the judicial check the Keith opinion had invited for the foreign-intelligence category would actually exist. The choice of sitting district judges, rather than a new corps of specialized appointees, reflected a judgment that generalist federal judges could evaluate classified applications without becoming a captive tribunal, and the designation mechanism kept the court's composition tied to the ordinary judiciary. The companion Court of Review, which would remain silent until 2002, supplied the appellate layer that any system of judicial authorization requires.

The application and order provisions specify what the executive branch must show and what the court must find, including probable cause that the target is a foreign power or an agent of a foreign power, and they attach minimization procedures that limit the acquisition, retention, and dissemination of information concerning United States persons. Minimization deserves a full explanation because it is one of the framework's distinctive contributions. Collection aimed at a foreign-intelligence target will inevitably sweep in communications of persons who are not targets, including Americans, and the statute requires the government to adopt procedures, approved by the court, that reduce that incidental acquisition and restrict how any incidentally acquired material about United States persons may be kept, shared, and used. The reporting provisions keep Congress informed through regular reports to the intelligence committees, closing the oversight loop that the pre-1978 system had lacked entirely. Taken together, these elements form a complete authorization system, which is why the 1978 enactment qualifies as a framework statute and why it could operate for twenty-three years without the 2001 package.

Two features of the original design deserve emphasis because the later amendments altered them, and a reader who grasps the originals will understand the amendments as edits rather than as mysteries. First, the certification requirement in its original form demanded that the executive branch certify "the purpose" of the collection was to obtain foreign-intelligence information, and the executive branch read that language, through internal guidelines developed in the 1980s and 1990s, as limiting how freely intelligence officials could coordinate with criminal prosecutors. The wall metaphor that dominates later discussion describes the practical culture that grew around that reading: elaborate procedures for keeping intelligence investigations and criminal investigations apart, Justice Department review mechanisms for proposed contacts between the two sides, and a generation of officials trained to treat coordination as legally hazardous. Whether the statute's text truly required all of that apparatus became one of the central legal questions of the post-2001 period, resolved in favor of the amendment by the Court of Review in 2002, but the apparatus itself was a product of the original design's language as interpreted over two decades.

Second, the original business-records authority did not yet exist in 1978 at all. The 1978 statute governed electronic surveillance, and Congress would add physical-search authority in 1994 and pen-register and trap-and-trace authority in 1998, the same year it added the original business-records provision that the 2001 package later rewrote. The framework grew by accretion across those decades, each addition codified in the same chapter and processed through the same court. That growth pattern is worth internalizing because it is the normal life of a framework statute: permanent law, amended as needs arise, with each layer visible in the code. The 2001 package is simply the most famous layer, not a different kind of thing.

The history also explains why the 2001 package never needed to build institutions of its own. The court, the definitions, the application process, and the minimization regime were already there, tested by decades of use and familiar to the officials who would operate the amended authorities. The package's job was to adjust operating rules to a new threat picture: lower the certification barrier that the wall culture had built on the original language, broaden the records provision beyond the categories the 1998 version had listed, permit orders to follow agile targets across facilities, and authorize the coordination the original guidelines had discouraged. None of those tasks required a new court, a new code chapter, or a new set of definitions, because the framework supplied all three. A student who begins with this history will read the 2001 amendments the way their drafters wrote them, as revisions to a working system whose purposes and limits were already settled, and will never be tempted to credit the revisions with inventing the system.

## Nature: one statute builds the system, the other edits it

Nature asks the most basic question in the comparison: what kind of legal instrument is each enactment, and what does its kind imply about everything else? FISA is a substantive framework statute. It defines key terms, creates institutions, sets the legal standards that govern a category of government activity, and establishes the procedures through which that activity is proposed, reviewed, authorized, and overseen. Before October 1978, foreign-intelligence electronic surveillance inside the United States operated without a comprehensive statutory charter; the executive branch asserted inherent authority, and the resulting controversies, documented through the investigations of the 1970s, produced the political demand for a legislative framework. Congress answered with a statute that did the work frameworks do. It defined foreign intelligence information, agents of foreign powers, and electronic surveillance in section 1801. It created the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court, composed of seven district judges designated by the Chief Justice, and the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court of Review, and assigned those courts the job of reviewing government applications for collection orders. It specified what an application must contain, what findings the court must make, how long orders last, and what minimization procedures must accompany collection. A reader can open Title 50, chapter 36, and find the complete operating system for court-authorized foreign-intelligence collection in one place, because that is what a framework statute is for.

### What did each law actually create?
FISA created the surveillance court, the application and order process, the statutory definitions, and the standards for foreign-intelligence collection, while the 2001 act created no court, no new code chapter, and no freestanding collection program. The 2001 measure instead revised existing provisions across FISA and about a dozen other statutes, adding tools, widening definitions, and funding programs.

The 2001 act is a different kind of instrument, and naming its kind precisely is the central discipline of this comparison. It is an omnibus amendment and policy package enacted six weeks after the September 11 attacks, built from proposals that had circulated in various forms and assembled under a unifying title. Its ten titles, described in the Scope section below, range across domestic security funding, surveillance procedure, money laundering, border protection, investigative tools, victim compensation, information sharing, criminal law, intelligence organization, and miscellaneous provisions. Some of its sections create new programs or authorize new spending, and to that extent the 2001 act is not only an amendment vehicle; Title I, for example, provided funding and victim assistance that had no exact statutory predecessor. But on the surveillance question that dominates this comparison, the 2001 act's nature is purely editorial. It took named sections of FISA and rewrote them. Section 218 rewrote the certification language in section 1804. Section 206 rewrote the provisions governing the specification of facilities in surveillance orders. Section 215 rewrote the business-records provision in section 1861. Section 504 added coordination authority. None of those sections would mean anything without the framework they amended, and none of them established an alternative to that framework. The Department of Justice's own descriptions of the 2001 act's surveillance changes present them as modifications to FISA authorities, and the FBI's field guidance from the period treats the new tools as operating through existing FISA procedures with adjusted standards. That is the nature axis in full: a framework that stands on its own, and a package whose surveillance provisions only make sense as edits to the framework.

## Scope: a chapter of Title 50 against ten titles across the code

Scope asks how far across the statute books each enactment reaches, and here the contrast reverses the intuition that the newer, more famous law must be the larger one. FISA's scope is deep and narrow. It occupies a single chapter of Title 50, the title devoted to war and national defense, and within that chapter it governs a defined category of activity: the collection of foreign-intelligence information through electronic surveillance, physical search, pen registers and trap-and-trace devices, and access to business records and other tangible things, all conducted under court order or statutory authorization for foreign-intelligence purposes. The statute's definitions in section 1801 draw the boundaries of that category with care, distinguishing foreign-intelligence collection from ordinary criminal investigation and distinguishing agents of foreign powers from persons merely suspected of crimes. Later amendments added new chapters and sections to the same framework, including the 2008 addition of Title VII on targeting persons outside the United States, but the scope remained conceptually unified: everything in the 1978 statute and its direct amendments concerns the authorization and oversight of foreign-intelligence collection. A student who reads the whole of chapter 36 has read the whole of the framework, and nothing in it governs bank fraud prosecutions, immigration removal, or victim compensation, because those subjects belong to other statutes.

A framework statute often claims its field, and the 1978 enactment is no exception. FISA presents itself as the exclusive means for the foreign-intelligence electronic surveillance it covers, displacing reliance on unwritten executive authority for that category of collection. That exclusivity claim is part of what makes the 1978 measure a framework rather than merely a statute: it does not add one option to a menu of authorities but defines the menu itself, replacing the pre-1978 arrangement in which such surveillance proceeded without statutory standards or judicial approval. The principle sharpens the comparison twice over. First, it explains why the 2001 act could not have created a parallel system even if its drafters had wanted one: the field was occupied by a statute that claimed it exclusively, so the only available legislative move was amendment. Second, it explains the stakes of the wall debate. Because the certification standard sat at the choke point of an exclusive system, changing a few words in section 218 changed the whole field at once, dissolving a barrier that had structured two communities' practices for years.

The 2001 act's scope is broad and shallow by comparison, which is exactly what one expects from an omnibus amendment package assembled under deadline. Its ten titles reach across the United States Code in ways that have little to do with one another beyond the shared theme of responding to terrorism. Title I, Enhancing Domestic Security, dealt with funding, victim assistance, and nondiscrimination provisions. Title II, Enhanced Surveillance Procedures, contained the FISA amendments and related changes to wiretap and stored-communications law that dominate public discussion of the act. Title III, the International Money Laundering Abatement and Anti-Terrorist Financing Act of 2001, rewrote portions of the Bank Secrecy Act framework. Title IV, Protecting the Border, changed immigration law. Title V, Removing Obstacles to Investigating Terrorism, adjusted investigative tools including grand-jury information sharing and DNA collection. Title VI addressed victims of terrorism. Title VII covered information sharing for critical infrastructure protection. Title VIII strengthened criminal laws against terrorism. Title IX addressed improved intelligence, including intelligence organization. Title X collected miscellaneous provisions. A reader who wants to understand the 2001 act as a whole must therefore read across many titles of the code, and must keep track of which title amended which underlying statute, because the package has no single subject the way the framework does.

### Which of the two enactments reaches further beyond surveillance?
The 2001 act reaches far beyond surveillance, spanning money laundering, immigration, criminal law, victim compensation, and intelligence organization across its ten titles, while the 1978 statute stays within foreign-intelligence collection. Anyone studying the 2001 act for surveillance alone is reading roughly one title of a ten-title package.

This difference in scope explains a recurring source of confusion in public discussion. When commentators say that the 2001 act "expanded government power," they are usually conflating several different expansions that happened to ride in the same legislative vehicle: wider FISA standards under Title II, broader money-laundering reporting under Title III, new immigration detention authorities under Title IV, and new federal crimes under Title VIII. Each of those expansions amended a different underlying statute and is governed by a different body of case law. The 2001 act is the vehicle, not the substance, and a careful reader attributes each change to the statute it actually amended. The Congressional Research Service's section-by-section analyses of the 2001 act follow exactly this method, treating each provision as an amendment to a named section of a named title of the code. The habit to build is simple: whenever a claim begins "the PATRIOT Act allows," the next question is "which section, amending which statute," because the answer is almost never "a freestanding PATRIOT Act surveillance system."

The scope axis also clarifies what the 2001 act did not do. It did not create a parallel surveillance system alongside FISA. There is no second court, no second set of application procedures, and no second code chapter for terrorism-related collection that bypasses the 1978 framework. Every court-ordered foreign-intelligence collection authority that the 2001 act touched operates through the institutions FISA created: applications go to the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court, orders issue under the standards of chapter 36, and minimization procedures apply as the statute requires. The 2001 act adjusted the inputs to that system, changing what the government must certify, what facilities an order may cover, and what records may be obtained, but the system itself remained the one Congress built in 1978. That fact is the single most important scope conclusion in this comparison, and it is worth stating without qualification: as of July 2014, there is one framework for court-authorized foreign-intelligence surveillance inside the United States, and it is FISA.

## Reading the 2001 package title by title

The scope axis established that the 2001 package spans ten titles across many areas of law, but the claim stays abstract until a reader sees what each title actually did. This section walks the ten titles in order, naming the underlying statutes each one amended and showing how small the surveillance portion is relative to the whole. The exercise builds the central habit this comparison teaches: whenever a claim begins with the package's name, ask which title, amending which statute, because the package is a vehicle and the substance always lives elsewhere.

Title I, Enhancing Domestic Security, is the least remembered and the most instructive about the package's nature. It authorized funding for counterterrorism activities, established victim compensation and assistance programs, and included nondiscrimination provisions. Nothing in it amended the 1978 framework. It created programs and appropriated money, which an omnibus vehicle can do alongside its amendments, but a reader looking for surveillance authority in Title I will find none. Its presence in the same enactment as the surveillance amendments is a legislative fact, not a substantive connection, and treating the package as a single surveillance law requires ignoring titles like this one entirely.

Title II, Enhanced Surveillance Procedures, is the title that dominates public memory and the only one that substantially rewrote the 1978 framework. Its provisions amended the certification standard, the business-records provision, the order provisions governing facility specification, and the coordination rules, as detailed in the Function section, and it also adjusted related criminal-procedure authorities governing wiretaps and stored communications. When commentators say the package expanded surveillance, they almost always mean Title II, and precision requires saying so. The title's amendments to the framework are codified in chapter 36 of Title 50, where a reader can see the package's textual fingerprints on the 1978 statute's language. Everything else in the ten titles is a different subject.

Title III, the International Money Laundering Abatement and Anti-Terrorist Financing Act of 2001, rewrote portions of the Bank Secrecy Act framework to strengthen reporting, recordkeeping, and enforcement against the financing of terrorism. Its amendments operate in Title 31 of the code, the money-and-finance title, and they are administered through the Treasury Department's financial-crimes apparatus rather than through any surveillance court. A reader who conflates Title III with the framework's collection authorities merges two entirely separate legal regimes that share nothing but their enacting vehicle and their general theme.

Title IV, Protecting the Border, amended immigration law, including provisions on the detention and removal of noncitizens suspected of terrorist activity. Its authorities operate through the immigration system, with its own adjudicators, its own standards, and its own body of case law, and they have no connection to the 1978 framework's courts or procedures. The title illustrates how an omnibus package assembles responses to a crisis from every committee's jurisdiction: the judiciary committees contributed surveillance procedure, while other committees contributed border and immigration measures, and all of it traveled under one public-law number.

Title V, Removing Obstacles to Investigating Terrorism, adjusted investigative tools that sit adjacent to surveillance without belonging to the framework. Its provisions addressed matters such as the sharing of grand-jury information with intelligence officials and the collection of DNA from certain offenders, amending criminal-procedure and related statutes. Some of its information-sharing provisions complemented the coordination authority that the package added to the framework, which is why the titles are sometimes discussed together, but complementing is not the same as amending, and Title V's changes live in their own statutory homes.

Title VI, addressing victims of terrorism, provided compensation and assistance measures that continued the work begun in Title I. Title VII, on information sharing for critical-infrastructure protection, created mechanisms for the government and private sector to exchange threat information, a policy innovation in information policy rather than an amendment to collection authority. Neither title touched the 1978 framework, and neither created surveillance power of any kind.

Title VIII, Strengthening Criminal Laws Against Terrorism, created new federal crimes and enhanced penalties, amending Title 18, the criminal code. Its provisions define offenses, not collection authorities, and they are prosecuted in ordinary federal court under ordinary criminal procedure, not before the surveillance court under the framework's standards. The distinction between criminal law and foreign-intelligence collection law is one the framework itself draws in its definitions, and Title VIII belongs firmly on the criminal side of that line.

Title IX, Improved Intelligence, addressed intelligence organization and related matters, including provisions touching the structure and coordination of the intelligence community. Title X collected miscellaneous provisions that fit nowhere else. Like the other non-surveillance titles, these amended or created authorities far from chapter 36 of Title 50.

The walkthrough yields a quantitative sense of proportion that abstract descriptions cannot convey. Of ten titles, one substantially amended the 1978 framework, a second touched adjacent investigative tools, and the remaining eight addressed subjects as diverse as victim compensation, money laundering, immigration, criminal offenses, and infrastructure protection. A reader who describes the package as a surveillance law has mistaken roughly one-tenth of it for the whole, and a reader who attributes the framework's institutions to the package has reversed the direction of the amendment relationship. The Congressional Research Service's section-by-section analyses follow the method modeled here, treating each provision as an edit to a named statute, and any serious study of the package must do the same. The framework-and-amendments distinction is not only a claim about two statutes; it is a reading method for omnibus legislation generally, and the ten-title walk is where the method becomes a habit.

## Durability: permanent law against provisions built to lapse

Durability asks which parts of each enactment were designed to endure and which were designed to expire, and this axis produces the sharpest structural contrast in the comparison. FISA is permanent law. Congress enacted it without a sunset clause, and it has remained on the books continuously since October 1978, accumulating amendments the way permanent statutes do. The 2001 act amended it, the Protect America Act of 2007 amended it, the FISA Amendments Act of 2008 added an entire new title to it, and the USA FREEDOM Act of June 2015, a later development addressed below, amended it again. Permanent does not mean unchanging; it means the statute does not expire on its own, and any provision Congress wants to remove must be affirmatively repealed or rewritten. The framework endures, and the amendments layer onto it, which is why a reader in July 2014 encounters a chapter 36 that contains 1978 text, 2001 text, 2007 text, and 2008 text side by side, each layer identifiable by its public law citation.

The 2001 act, by contrast, was built with expiration dates inside it, and understanding those dates is essential to understanding what the act is. Sixteen of its provisions carried sunset clauses under the original section 224, meaning they would expire on December 31, 2005, unless Congress reauthorized them. The sunset mechanism was a legislative compromise, a way to enact controversial authorities quickly while promising a future Congress the chance to revisit them. When the reauthorization debate arrived, Congress made fourteen of the sixteen sunset provisions permanent and kept two, plus one related provision, on a continuing sunset schedule. The two that stayed on sunset were section 215, the business-records and tangible-things provision codified at 50 U.S.C. section 1861, and section 206, the roving wiretap provision. The related provision was the lone-wolf authority, added by section 6001 of the Intelligence Reform and Terrorism Prevention Act of 2004, Public Law 108-458, and codified at 50 U.S.C. section 1801(b)(1)(C), which permits FISA orders targeting non-United States persons engaged in international terrorism without a showing that they act for a foreign power. Congress extended all three through a series of short-term reauthorizations, and as of July 2014 they remained in force under the PATRIOT Sunsets Extension Act of 2011, Public Law 112-14, which carried them unchanged to June 1, 2015.

### Why did some PATRIOT provisions carry expiration dates while FISA itself never expired?
Because Congress wrote sunset clauses into sixteen of the 2001 act's most controversial provisions as the price of rapid passage, while the 1978 statute was enacted as permanent law through the ordinary committee process. Fourteen of the sixteen were later made permanent; sections 215 and 206, plus the lone-wolf provision, remained on a reauthorization cycle.

The durability axis carries an important lesson about how amendment packages age differently from framework statutes. When a provision of the 2001 act expired or was reauthorized, what expired or continued was an amendment to FISA, not a freestanding program. Section 215 is a FISA provision, codified in chapter 36, that the 2001 act rewrote; its sunset schedule governs the amended version of that FISA provision. This is why discussions of "PATRIOT Act expiration" are imprecise in a way that matters. What faced expiration in the sunset debates was never the 2001 act as a whole, and never FISA as a whole; it was a defined list of amended authorities, each living inside the permanent framework. The 2011 extension act illustrates the point cleanly: it did not extend the 2001 act, it extended three specific FISA-related authorities to a date certain. A reader who internalizes this mechanism will never again be confused by headlines about expiring surveillance law, because the right question is always which provisions, in which statute, face which date.

Later developments belong in this section with explicit dating, because durability is where the timeline keeps moving. The USA FREEDOM Act, Public Law 114-23, enacted June 2, 2015, after a one-day lapse on June 1, ended bulk collection under section 215 and required that orders use a specific selection term, defined at 50 U.S.C. section 1861(k)(4)(A), with a 180-day transition period that closed on November 29, 2015. That enactment postdates this article and is described here as a later development, in the past tense, flagged as later. Further along the same timeline, the three provisions that had lived on the sunset cycle, sections 215 and 206 and the lone-wolf authority, lapsed on March 15, 2020, subject to a grandfather clause for existing matters, and had not been reauthorized afterward. That lapse is the brief's own anchor point and is included here explicitly dated as a later development. Section 702, added to FISA by the 2008 amendments and codified at 50 U.S.C. section 1881a, was reauthorized in 2018 by Public Law 115-118 and again in 2024 by Public Law 118-49, both later developments relative to this article's July 2014 date. Within the article's own time frame, the state of the law is the one described above: sections 215, 206, and the lone-wolf provision in force under the 2011 extension to June 1, 2015, and section 702 in force under its December 2012 extension, Public Law 112-238.

## The reauthorization debates of 2005 and 2011

The durability axis described the sunset mechanism in outline; this section reconstructs the two reauthorization episodes that gave the mechanism its meaning, because the debates show how amendment packages age and how frameworks absorb the results. The original sunset clause, section 224 of the package, provided that sixteen provisions would expire on December 31, 2005, unless Congress acted. The device was a compromise between legislators who wanted the new authorities enacted immediately and legislators who feared that emergency legislation would harden into permanent law without adequate scrutiny. By writing expiration dates into the most controversial provisions, the 107th Congress bought speed in 2001 at the price of a guaranteed revisiting, and it handed the 109th Congress a structured decision: evaluate each authority on its record, then choose permanence, extension, or expiration.

The 2005 debate produced the pattern that defines the package's afterlife. After hearings, classified briefings, and extended negotiation, Congress made fourteen of the sixteen sunset provisions permanent and kept negotiating over the remainder. The fourteen that became permanent were, in the judgment of the majorities that voted for them, authorities whose operation had proven unobjectionable or whose rationale no longer depended on emergency conditions. Permanence, in this context, did not mean endorsement of every application; it meant the provision would henceforth live or die by ordinary legislative repeal rather than by automatic expiration. The two that stayed on the sunset cycle, the business-records provision and the roving-order provision, plus the lone-wolf authority added by the 2004 reform measure, remained politically contested, and Congress extended them temporarily rather than resolving the underlying disagreement.

That temporary-extension pattern then repeated, and the repetition is itself instructive. Sunset provisions that Congress cannot agree to make permanent but cannot agree to let expire enter a cycle of short-term reauthorizations, each one preserving the status quo while deferring the substantive fight. The business-records, roving-order, and lone-wolf authorities traveled through several such extensions, with debates recurring over the scope of the records provision in particular. Each extension was a freestanding legislative act that had to pass both chambers and win presidential signature, which meant each one supplied a fresh occasion for oversight hearings, public debate, and proposed reforms. The sunset mechanism thus functioned as designed even when it did not produce finality: it forced periodic reconsideration of precisely the authorities whose wisdom remained in dispute.

The PATRIOT Sunsets Extension Act of 2011, Public Law 112-14, is the extension that matters for this article's time frame. It carried the three authorities forward unchanged to June 1, 2015, preserving the 2001-amended versions of the framework's provisions without modification. The unchanged character of the extension deserves emphasis. Congress did not rewrite the records provision's relevance standard, did not narrow the roving-order mechanism, and did not alter the lone-wolf definition; it simply moved the expiration date. For a student of the framework-and-amendments distinction, the 2011 measure is a clean illustration of how durability works for amended provisions. The underlying framework needed no extension because permanent law does not expire. The amendments needed extension because their enacting package had put them on probation. The 2011 act extended the probation, and the framework continued underneath, indifferent to the schedule, as permanent statutes are.

The reauthorization history also clarifies a confusion that recurs in public discussion of expiration. When commentators warned, at various points, that surveillance authorities were about to expire, they were describing the possible lapse of specific amended provisions, not the expiration of the framework and not the expiration of the package as a whole. The framework cannot expire on its own; only affirmative repeal could remove it. The package, as an enacted public law, does not expire either; its provisions persist in the code sections they amended, subject only to the sunset dates written into particular provisions. What can lapse is a defined list of authorities, each living inside the permanent framework, each facing a date certain. The March 2020 lapse of the three provisions, noted as a later development in the Durability section, is the eventual realization of exactly that mechanism: the probation ended without renewal, the amended provisions lapsed subject to a grandfather clause for pending matters, and the framework continued without them.

A final observation about the debates concerns what they reveal about the amendment package's democratic accountability. Defenders of the sunset mechanism argue that it worked, forcing Congress to revisit controversial authorities on a schedule and generating the public record on which later reforms, including the 2015 legislation, were built. Critics argue that short-term extensions became routine, that the threat of expiration was used as leverage rather than as genuine deliberation, and that several extensions passed with minimal changes after perfunctory debate. This article takes no position on that dispute, which concerns legislative process rather than legal structure. The structural point stands regardless: the debates happened because the package's drafters chose probation for its most contested edits, and the framework endured through all of them because permanent law does not need reauthorization. Durability, like nature and scope, sorts the two enactments into their proper categories.

## Function: how each authority actually operates

Function asks how the authorities attributed to each enactment work in practice, and this is where the framework-and-amendments distinction pays for itself in concrete understanding. The right way to read this axis is provision by provision, asking of each famous authority three questions: which statute created it, which statute changed it, and which statute houses it as of July 2014. Applied consistently, that method dissolves the most persistent confusions in the field, including the two the brief singles out for explicit correction: the Section 702 program and the bulk telephony metadata program must not be attributed to the 2001 act as creations, and this section shows exactly where each one belongs.

Begin with the change the brief identifies as the most consequential thing the 2001 act did to FISA: the dismantling of the wall between intelligence collection and criminal prosecution. Before the 2001 amendments, FISA required the government to certify that "the purpose" of the surveillance was to obtain foreign-intelligence information, language codified at 50 U.S.C. section 1804(a)(6)(B). Executive-branch lawyers and courts had read that language, together with Justice Department guidelines, to require a kind of separation between intelligence investigations and criminal investigations, a separation that came to be called the wall. Information gathered under FISA faced restrictions on its use in criminal cases, and coordination between intelligence officials and criminal prosecutors required navigating procedures that many participants experienced as a barrier. Section 218 of the 2001 act changed the certification language from "the purpose" to "a significant purpose," a small textual edit with large operational consequences. The government could then certify that obtaining foreign-intelligence information was a significant purpose of the collection even when criminal prosecution was also contemplated, and section 504 of the 2001 act added explicit authority for coordination between intelligence and law-enforcement officials. The Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court of Review upheld the new standard in In re Sealed Case, 310 F.3d 717 (2002), the first and, for many years, the only published opinion of that court, holding that the amended certification requirement satisfied the Fourth Amendment. The wall metaphor describes a real operational change, but the change is an amendment to a FISA certification standard, adjudicated by a FISA court, under a FISA provision. It is the 2001 act's most important edit to the 1978 framework, and it is still an edit.

### How did the 2001 act remove the wall between FISA collection and criminal cases?
Section 218 changed FISA's certification from "the purpose" to "a significant purpose" of obtaining foreign-intelligence information, codified at 50 U.S.C. section 1804(a)(6)(B), while section 504 authorized coordination between intelligence and law-enforcement officials. The Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court of Review upheld the new standard in In re Sealed Case in 2002.

Turn next to the business-records provision, section 215 of the 2001 act, because its history is the clearest possible demonstration of the framework-and-amendments relationship. The provision did not originate in either 1978 or 2001. Congress first created a FISA business-records authority in section 602 of the Intelligence Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 1999, Public Law 105-272, enacted in 1998, and codified it at 50 U.S.C. section 1861. That original version was narrow, limited to records from common carriers, public accommodations, physical storage facilities, and vehicle rental facilities, and it required specific and articulable facts showing the records pertained to a foreign power or an agent of a foreign power. Section 215 of the 2001 act rewrote the provision, broadening the category of obtainable materials to "any tangible things" and replacing the original standard with a relevance standard tied to investigations to protect against international terrorism or clandestine intelligence activities. The bulk telephony metadata program that later became the subject of intense public debate relied on this amended provision, section 1861, as its claimed legal basis. The program is therefore not a creation of the 2001 act in any simple sense. The underlying authority dates to 1998, the 2001 act amended its scope and standard, and the resulting amended provision lives in FISA's chapter 36. A reader who says "the PATRIOT Act created the metadata program" has compressed three legislative layers into one and lost the ability to explain what each layer contributed. The disciplined account names all three: the 1998 creation, the 2001 amendment, and the FISA codification that houses the result.

### Which statute does Section 702 belong to, and why does the answer matter?
Section 702 belongs to FISA, added by the FISA Amendments Act of 2008, Public Law 110-261, and codified at 50 U.S.C. section 1881a. Neither the 1978 statute nor the 2001 act created it. Attributing it to the 2001 act misdates the authority by seven years and misidentifies the statute that governs its targeting and minimization procedures.

Section 206, the roving wiretap provision, follows the same pattern in miniature. Before the 2001 amendments, FISA orders had to specify the facility or place targeted with particularity, which created practical difficulties when a target switched phones or locations to evade collection. Section 206 amended the statute to permit orders that follow the person rather than the facility, under defined conditions and with continued judicial oversight. The authority operates through the FISA application process, before the FISA court, under FISA's standards as amended. The lone-wolf provision completes the picture of the 2001-era amendments to FISA's targeting rules. Added not by the 2001 act itself but by section 6001 of the Intelligence Reform and Terrorism Prevention Act of 2004, Public Law 108-458, and codified at 50 U.S.C. section 1801(b)(1)(C), it permits the government to seek FISA orders against non-United States persons engaged in international terrorism even when they cannot be shown to act on behalf of a foreign power. Its inclusion in the sunset debates alongside sections 215 and 206 reflects its functional kinship with the 2001 amendments, but its statutory pedigree runs through the 2004 intelligence reform measure, and precision about that pedigree is part of what separates careful study from loose commentary.

Section 702 requires its own extended treatment, because it is the provision most often misattributed and the one whose correct placement most clearly rewards the framework-and-amendments discipline. Section 702 was added to FISA by the FISA Amendments Act of 2008, Public Law 110-261, and codified at 50 U.S.C. section 1881a. It authorizes the targeting of non-United States persons reasonably believed to be outside the United States for the purpose of acquiring foreign-intelligence information, under annual certifications approved by the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court rather than individualized orders. Its short-lived predecessor was the Protect America Act of 2007, Public Law 110-55, which expired in February 2008. Neither the 1978 statute in its original form nor the 2001 act created anything like section 702. The 1978 framework knew only individualized orders based on probable cause that the target was a foreign power or its agent. The 2001 act amended standards and procedures within that individualized-order system but did not create programmatic, certification-based collection. Section 702's architecture, annual certifications, targeting procedures, and minimization procedures approved by the court, with collection directed at persons outside the United States, is a product of the 2008 amendments layered onto the 1978 framework. Within this article's time frame, section 702 stood reauthorized through the December 2012 extension, Public Law 112-238, and its later reauthorizations in 2018 and 2024 are dated here as later developments. The counter-reading this article must address directly is the claim, common in public discussion, that the 2001 act created the legal basis for the collection programs most associated with the post-2001 era. That claim fails on the statute books. The bulk metadata program rested on a 1998-created, 2001-amended FISA provision. The section 702 program rests on a 2008 addition to FISA. The 2001 act's genuine creations in the surveillance field are the amended standards themselves: the significant-purpose certification, the coordination authority, the broadened tangible-things scope, the roving-order mechanism. Those are substantial, and the brief rightly calls the wall change the most consequential, but they are amendments, and the framework they amended is FISA.

The function axis also deserves a note on what did not change, because continuities are as instructive as changes. The 2001 act did not alter the basic architecture of FISA adjudication. Applications still go to the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court. The court still makes findings under the statute's standards. Orders still have defined durations. Minimization procedures still constrain the handling of information concerning United States persons. Congressional oversight mechanisms still operate. The institutions created in 1978 processed the amended authorities of 2001, the added authorities of 2008, and, as a later development, the reformed authorities of 2015. That institutional continuity is the functional signature of a framework statute: it absorbs amendments without becoming a different system. An amendment package, by contrast, leaves no institutional trace of its own in the surveillance field, because it built no institutions there. The 2001 act's durable functional legacy is therefore best described not as a set of programs but as a set of revised rules under which the 1978 system's institutions operate.

## The amended standards in operation

The Function section traced the most famous amended provisions; this section completes the operational picture by showing how the framework's machinery processes those amendments day to day, and by covering the quieter authorities the package adjusted. The goal is to leave the reader able to describe, in concrete terms, what happens inside the system after the amendments, which is the knowledge that separates a structural understanding from a slogan-level one.

Start with the application process, because every amended authority flows through it. To obtain an order for electronic surveillance under the framework as amended, the executive branch prepares an application that identifies the target, describes the facilities or places to be surveilled, sets out the facts supporting probable cause that the target is a foreign power or an agent of a foreign power, and includes the required certifications from high-ranking officials, among them the significant-purpose certification in its post-2001 form. The application goes to the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court, where a judge reviews it, may request additional information, and grants or denies the order based on the statutory findings. Orders have defined durations that vary by target category and by the type of collection, and renewals require fresh showings. The 2001 amendments changed what some of those showings require, most notably the certification language and the facility-specification rules for roving orders, but they did not change the shape of the process: application, judicial review, findings, order, duration, renewal. A reader who pictures the amended system should picture the 1978 process with revised inputs, not a new process.

Minimization procedures are the framework's distinctive constraint on what happens after collection begins, and the package left their architecture intact while the system around them changed. Under the statute, the government must adopt procedures, approved by the court, that are reasonably designed to minimize the acquisition and retention of nonpublic information concerning unconsenting United States persons and to prohibit its dissemination in a manner that identifies such persons, consistent with the need to obtain, produce, and disseminate foreign-intelligence information. In practice this means rules about how long incidentally collected material may be kept, who may access it, when it may be shared with other agencies, and how it must be masked or handled in reports. The procedures are proposed by the executive branch and reviewed by the court, which gives the judiciary a continuing supervisory role beyond the initial authorization. The package's coordination amendments interacted with minimization in ways that generated years of implementation work: once intelligence and law-enforcement officials could coordinate more freely, the procedures had to specify how information could move between those worlds without violating the statute's protections. The details were worked out in classified submissions and court orders over the years following the amendments, which is why public understanding of minimization often lags behind the operational reality.

The quieter collection authorities round out the operational picture. The framework's physical-search provisions, added by Congress in 1994 and codified at 50 U.S.C. sections 1821 through 1829, authorize court-ordered physical searches for foreign-intelligence purposes under standards parallel to those for electronic surveillance, and the package's amendments to coordination and certification apply to that domain as well. The pen-register and trap-and-trace provisions, added in 1998 and codified at 50 U.S.C. sections 1841 through 1846, govern court orders for the prospective collection of dialing, routing, addressing, and signaling information, a category distinct from content interception, and they too operate under the framework's application-and-order model with standards the package adjusted. These authorities attract less public attention than electronic surveillance or business records, but they complete the system, and a student who ignores them will misunderstand the framework's breadth. The package did not create any of them; it inherited them as parts of the framework and amended their operating rules where its drafters saw need.

Congressional oversight supplies the final operational element. The framework requires regular reporting to the intelligence committees on the use of its authorities, including statistics on applications, orders granted, modified, and denied. Those reports, in unclassified summaries, are among the few public windows into the system's operation, and they are products of the framework's own design, not of the package. The oversight structure explains how Congress monitors a secret court without exposing its work: aggregate numbers and legal interpretations flow to the committees, which can then legislate adjustments, as they did in 2008 and, as a later development, in 2015. The 2001 package, as an amendment vehicle, created no parallel oversight mechanism for its surveillance edits; the framework's committees absorbed the amended authorities into their existing jurisdiction. Institutional continuity once again marks the framework as the durable system and the package as the transient edit.

Taken together, these operational details answer the question the Function axis poses in its most practical form: what does it mean, concretely, to say that the package amended the framework rather than replacing it? It means applications still travel the 1978 path to the 1978 court, minimization still constrains handling under standards the court approves, the quieter authorities still operate under their 1990s additions to the framework, and Congress still oversees the whole through the framework's reporting requirements. The amended inputs, significant purpose, tangible things, roving specification, coordination, flow through unamended institutional channels. That is the operational signature of the framework-and-amendments relationship, and a reader who can describe it has moved beyond the labels to the machinery.

## Misattributions, corrected one by one

The brief requires this article to address the counter-reading directly and to refuse two specific misattributions: the section 702 program and the bulk metadata program must not be credited to the 2001 package as creations. This section performs those corrections systematically, taking up the most common misattributions in turn and replacing each with the statute-book account. The format is deliberate. Each correction follows the same three questions the Function section introduced: which enactment created the authority, which one changed it, and which one houses it. Applied repeatedly, the method becomes reflexive.

The surveillance court is the first correction because it is the most basic. Public discussion sometimes implies, without quite stating, that the court that approves foreign-intelligence orders was created by the post-2001 legal regime, perhaps because the court's public profile rose sharply after 2001. The statute books show otherwise. The Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court and its companion Court of Review were created by the 1978 framework at 50 U.S.C. section 1803, staffed by district judges designated by the Chief Justice, and they operated for more than two decades before the package existed. The package created no court, designated no judges, and established no adjudicative procedure. Every order issued under the amended authorities was issued by the 1978 court's judges under the 1978 statute's procedures. Crediting the court to the package is not a minor imprecision; it misidentifies the institution that performs the judicial check the entire system depends on.

The bulk telephony metadata program is the second correction and the most politically charged. The program's claimed legal basis was the business-records provision at 50 U.S.C. section 1861, and the provision's history has three layers that must not be collapsed. Congress created the underlying authority in 1998, in the intelligence authorization measure for that fiscal year, as a narrow provision covering records from enumerated businesses. The 2001 package rewrote it, broadening the obtainable materials to any tangible things and adopting the relevance standard. The resulting amended provision lives in the 1978 framework's chapter, where the 2015 reforms later changed it again. To say the package created the program is to erase the 1998 creation and to mislocate the authority's statutory home. The precise account names all three layers and assigns each its proper role: creation in 1998, amendment in 2001, codification in the framework throughout.

The section 702 program is the third correction and the cleanest, because the dates make the error unmistakable. Section 702 was added to the framework by the FISA Amendments Act of 2008, seven years after the package, and codified at 50 U.S.C. section 1881a. Its programmatic architecture, annual certifications approved by the court, targeting procedures, minimization procedures, collection directed at non-United States persons reasonably believed to be outside the country, has no counterpart in the package's amendments, which operated within the individualized-order system the 1978 statute established. The package's drafters in 2001 did not create, anticipate, or enable the certification-based model; that model emerged from the 2007 predecessor measure and the 2008 amendments' response to technological and legal developments of the intervening years. Attributing section 702 to the package misdates the authority by most of a decade and misidentifies the legislative judgment that created it.

Roving orders supply a fourth correction of a subtler kind. It is true that the package originated the roving-order mechanism in the framework's orders, through the provision numbered 206, and no correction is needed on the question of origin. The correction concerns what origin implies. Creating an amendment to an order provision is not the same as creating a surveillance program, and the roving authority operates entirely through the framework's application process, findings, durations, and minimization requirements. A reader who lists roving orders among the package's creations is correct; a reader who describes them as a freestanding package program, independent of the 1978 court's supervision, has crossed from precision into error. The habit to maintain is attribution at the right level of generality: credit the edit to the package, credit the system to the framework, and never let the edit swallow the system.

The claim that the package itself expired, or might expire, is the fifth correction, and it follows from the durability analysis. Because the package's surveillance provisions live as amendments inside the permanent framework, there is no moment at which the package as a whole lapses. What faced expiration on the sunset schedule was a defined list of amended authorities, and what lapsed in March 2020, as a later development, was that list, subject to a grandfather clause. Headlines about expiring surveillance law compress that precise mechanism into a misleading shorthand, and the compression has consequences: readers who believe the framework might vanish misunderstand the permanence of chapter 36, while readers who believe the package's expiration would end collection misunderstand how amendments persist in the sections they amended. The corrected formulation is always available and always worth the extra words: named provisions, in a named statute, facing a named date.

The lone-wolf provision invites a sixth correction about neighboring enactments. Because the provision traveled with the package's sunset authorities through the reauthorization debates, it is sometimes described as a package creation. It is not. Congress added it in the Intelligence Reform and Terrorism Prevention Act of 2004, as section 6001 of that measure, and codified it at 50 U.S.C. section 1801(b)(1)(C), amending the framework's definitional passage. Its functional kinship with the package's amendments explains the association, but kinship is not authorship, and the statute books assign authorship to the 2004 reform measure. Precision about neighboring enactments matters because the framework accumulated layers from many Congresses, and each layer's public-law citation is the key to understanding what problem its drafters thought they were solving.

Together, the six corrections demonstrate the method this article teaches. None of them requires specialized knowledge beyond the statute books, the public-law citations, and the code locations, all of which are public. Each follows from asking the three questions in order and refusing to let proximity, whether temporal or political, substitute for authorship. A reader who practices the method on these six cases will be equipped to evaluate the next misattribution unassisted, which is the article's deeper aim: not only to correct the record on these two enactments, but to train the habit of correction for the many comparisons the series will draw.

## What chapter 36 looks like to a first-time reader

A comparison organized around study sequence should equip the reader to open the statute itself, so this section maps chapter 36 of Title 50 as a first-time reader encounters it, showing where each piece of the comparison lives in the code. The map reinforces the framework-and-amendments distinction in the most tangible way available: by walking the shelves of the building the 1978 Congress constructed and noting which rooms the later Congresses remodeled.

The chapter opens with its definitional passage at section 1801, the vocabulary list on which everything else depends. Foreign intelligence information, agent of a foreign power, electronic surveillance, United States person, and minimization procedures are defined here, and the lone-wolf language added by the 2004 reform measure sits in this passage as well, at section 1801(b)(1)(C). A reader who masters these definitions has the keys to the chapter, because every operative provision speaks in these terms.

Subchapter I, sections 1801 through 1812, governs electronic surveillance under court order and contains the provisions the package most famously amended. The application requirements, the judicial findings, the order durations, and the certification language live here, which means the section 218 amendment's fingerprints are visible in this subchapter's text. The emergency-authorization procedures and the provisions on the use of collected information also sit here, and the coordination authority the package added operates through these passages.

Subchapter II, sections 1821 through 1829, governs physical searches under court order, added to the framework in 1994. Its structure mirrors subchapter I: applications, findings, orders, durations, minimization, and use provisions, adapted to searches of property rather than interception of communications. The package's certification and coordination amendments reach this subchapter too, a reminder that the framework's growth by accretion extended the same institutional model to new collection methods.

Subchapter III, sections 1841 through 1846, governs pen registers and trap-and-trace devices under court order, added in 1998. These provisions authorize the prospective collection of dialing, routing, addressing, and signaling information, as distinct from content, and they apply a statutory standard suited to that less intrusive category. Subchapter IV, sections 1861 and 1862, contains the business-records and tangible-things authority, the 1998 creation that the package rewrote and the 2015 reforms later reshaped. A reader who finds section 1861 will see the layered history in the provision's scope and standard, and will understand why the which-statute table assigns its creation, modification, and current status to three different entries.

Subchapter V, at section 1871, carries the congressional reporting requirements that feed the oversight structure, and Title VII of the framework, sections 1881 through 1881g, contains the 2008 amendments' programmatic authorities, including section 702 at section 1881a. The chapter thus reads as a geological record: 1978 bedrock in subchapter I, 1990s sedimentary layers in subchapters II through IV, and a 2008 addition in Title VII, with the package's edits visible as revised language within the older strata rather than as a separate formation.

The practical advice for the first-time reader follows from the map. Read the definitions first, then subchapter I in full, because it establishes the institutional pattern the later subchapters repeat. Read the package's amendments against the subchapter I text they revised, using a redline or a section-by-section summary to see the edits in place. Treat subchapters II through IV as variations on the established pattern, noting what changes for different collection methods and what stays constant. Read Title VII last among the framework's parts, because its certification-based model departs from the individualized-order pattern and is best understood after that pattern is familiar. And keep the which-statute table at hand throughout, checking each provision's creator, modifier, and code location against the map. The chapter rewards the framework-first reader at every turn, because it was written to be read that way.

## The courts' voice: how the framework's judges read the amendments

The comparison gains depth from attending to what the framework's own courts said about the package's edits, because judicial interpretation is where the framework-and-amendments distinction becomes lived practice rather than taxonomy. The Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court operates largely in secret, issuing classified orders on classified applications, but its occasional public opinions, and especially the Court of Review's published decisions, show judges of the 1978 system reasoning about the 2001 revisions in exactly the terms this article uses.

In re Sealed Case, 310 F.3d 717 (2002), is the central exhibit. The case arose when the surveillance court, in 2002, imposed minimization and coordination restrictions on the government's applications, reflecting the pre-2001 wall culture's persistence among some judges even after the statutory language had changed. The government appealed to the Court of Review, which convened for the first time in its history to decide the question. The opinion upheld the significant-purpose standard against Fourth Amendment challenge, holding that the amended certification requirement, which asks the court to find probable cause that the target is a foreign power or its agent and to review the required certifications, satisfies constitutional requirements even where criminal prosecution is a contemplated use of the collected information. The reasoning treated the 2001 revision as an amendment to a framework provision and evaluated the framework provision as amended, which is precisely the reading method this comparison teaches.

The opinion's structural significance exceeds its doctrinal holding. Here was the framework's own appellate body, created in 1978 and silent for twenty-four years, speaking for the first time, and its first words concerned a package amendment. The episode demonstrates institutional absorption: the new language entered the old system, the old system's judges interpreted it, and the system's procedures, briefing, argument, published opinion with classified redactions, processed the novelty without structural change. No new tribunal was erected to construe the package's surveillance edits, because none was needed. The framework's courts were competent to the task, which is what framework courts are for.

Later public opinions from the surveillance court, released in redacted form in subsequent years, continued the pattern, addressing the implementation of amended provisions, the adequacy of minimization procedures under new coordination practices, and the scope of the business-records authority. Each opinion took the framework as its jurisdictional home and the package's language as the text under interpretation. A student who reads these opinions with the comparison in mind will notice how naturally the judges move between the 1978 definitions, the amended standards, and the later layers, treating the chapter as a single integrated statute. That integration is the strongest evidence for the framework-and-amendments claim: the very institution charged with applying the law experiences it as one framework, repeatedly amended, rather than as two competing systems.

The courts' voice also supplies the neutrality this article observes. Judicial opinions characterize the statutes in structural terms, describing what each provision requires and how the provisions fit together, without endorsing or condemning the policies Congress chose. This article follows that example, attributing characterizations to named sources and confining judgment to structure and study sequence. The reader who adopts the same discipline will find that most public controversies about these laws become easier to parse, because controversies about policy can then be distinguished from confusions about structure, and only the latter is this comparison's business.

## The comparison's key terms, defined in place

Extended comparisons generate their own vocabulary, and undefined terms are a common source of the confusions this article corrects, so this section defines the key terms in place, each with the specific meaning it carries in the framework-and-amendments analysis.

A framework statute is an enactment that creates the institutions, definitions, standards, and procedures for a category of government activity, designed to operate indefinitely and to absorb later revisions. The 1978 measure qualifies because it built the court, wrote the definitions, set the standards, and established the procedures for foreign-intelligence collection, and because it has done that work continuously while accumulating amendments for decades.

An amendment package is an enactment whose primary legislative technique is revising existing statutes, often many at once, usually assembled around a unifying occasion or theme. The 2001 measure qualifies because its ten titles edit named sections across the code, and because its surveillance provisions make sense only as revisions to the framework's text. The term package emphasizes the vehicle character: many changes, one ride.

A sunset provision is a statutory section written with its own expiration date, after which it lapses unless Congress reauthorizes it. Sixteen of the package's provisions carried such dates, of which fourteen were later made permanent and three related authorities continued on a reauthorization cycle. The device reflects legislative compromise, trading immediate enactment for guaranteed future review.

Certification, in the framework's usage, is the formal attestation by designated executive-branch officials that accompanies a surveillance application, including the attestation about the purpose of the collection. The package's most consequential edit changed this attestation's language, which shows how much operational weight a single defined term can carry in a framework statute.

Minimization procedures are the court-approved rules limiting the acquisition, retention, and dissemination of information concerning United States persons that is incidentally collected during authorized foreign-intelligence collection. They are the framework's principal privacy-protective mechanism and the site of much of the implementation work that followed the package's coordination amendments.

Significant purpose is the certification standard the package substituted for the original "the purpose" language, codified at 50 U.S.C. section 1804(a)(6)(B) and upheld in the 2002 Court of Review opinion. The phrase marks the precise textual location of the wall's dismantling.

Tangible things is the category of obtainable materials that the package's business-records rewrite substituted for the 1998 version's enumerated list of businesses, codified at 50 U.S.C. section 1861. The breadth of the substituted phrase explains both the provision's operational importance and its political controversy.

Lone wolf designates the authority, added by the 2004 reform measure and codified at 50 U.S.C. section 1801(b)(1)(C), to target non-United States persons engaged in international terrorism without showing agency on behalf of a foreign power. The nickname describes the gap it closes in the framework's original targeting categories.

Specific selection term is the requirement the 2015 reforms, a later development, imposed on business-records orders, defined at 50 U.S.C. section 1861(k)(4)(A), ending the bulk-collection practice that the package's broadened language had been invoked to support. The term belongs to the post-article timeline and appears here only as dated later history.

Probable cause, in the framework's usage, is the finding the court must make that the target of collection is a foreign power or an agent of a foreign power, a target-based standard distinct from the criminal-law inquiry into probable cause of wrongdoing. The distinction matters because the framework authorizes collection based on who the target is in relation to foreign threats, not on evidence that the target has committed a crime, and the package's amendments left that structural choice untouched.

United States person is the defined term around which the minimization regime turns, covering citizens, permanent residents, and certain associations and corporations. The framework's protections for this category, acquisition limits, retention limits, and dissemination controls approved by the court, are what keep foreign-intelligence collection from becoming general surveillance of the domestic population, and every amendment to collection scope must be read alongside these standing constraints.

Foreign power is the definitional anchor of the targeting system, encompassing foreign governments, factions, and entities directed or controlled by them, as well as groups engaged in international terrorism. The lone-wolf provision's significance becomes clear against this definition: it extends targeting to terrorism suspects who cannot be tied to any such entity, adjusting the anchor without replacing it.

Defined together, the terms form the comparison's working vocabulary. A reader who uses them precisely will rarely misattribute an authority, because each term points to its statutory home: framework terms to chapter 36 as enacted or grown, package terms to the 2001 revisions, later terms to the dated developments that followed.

## Reading amendments in the code: a drafter's view

One more perspective cements the framework-and-amendments distinction: the view from the drafting table, where the package's provisions were written as instructions for editing the framework's text. Statutory drafters do not write amendments as freestanding essays; they write them as surgical directions, and the package's surveillance title reads exactly that way. A typical provision says, in effect, that a named subsection of Title 50 is amended by striking specified language and inserting replacement language. The instruction presupposes the target text, names its location, and performs a textual substitution, leaving the surrounding framework intact. A reader who consults the Statutes at Large, the chronological record of enacted public laws, will find the package's provisions in this instructional form, while a reader who consults the United States Code will find the results already integrated into chapter 36, with the framework's original language replaced by the amended version.

This duality of sources explains a recurring beginner's error. The Statutes at Large show the package doing the amending, which tempts the inference that the package is the source of the resulting law. The Code shows the framework containing the amended language, which tempts the opposite inference that the framework's text never changed. Both inferences miss the temporal dimension that the which-statute table captures. The correct reading uses both sources together: the Statutes at Large to identify which Congress made which change, and the Code to see the current state of the integrated text. The Government Publishing Office publishes both, and the Congressional Research Service's analyses routinely cite both, modeling the dual-source method for working lawyers and legislative staff.

The drafter's view also clarifies why amendment packages rarely create institutions. Drafting an institution requires writing organic provisions: composition, appointment, jurisdiction, procedure, funding, and oversight, all in one place, which is what the 1978 Congress did for the surveillance court. Drafting an amendment requires only the strike-and-insert instruction, which is far cheaper in legislative effort and far more flexible in an omnibus context. The package's drafters, working under extreme time pressure in the autumn of 2001, chose the cheaper technique throughout the surveillance title, because the framework's institutions were available for reuse. That choice was rational, and it is the reason the distinction this article draws is visible in the drafting itself. Frameworks are built with organic provisions. Amendments are written as edits. The statute books preserve both kinds of writing, and the reader who can tell them apart will never confuse the remodeler for the architect.

## Two timelines, one system

The comparison can be grasped as a pair of timelines that merge into a single system, and laying them side by side resolves several lingering puzzles at a glance. The framework's timeline begins in 1978 with the original enactment: definitions, the court, electronic-surveillance orders, and minimization. It continues in 1994 with the addition of physical-search authority, in 1998 with pen-register, trap-and-trace, and business-records provisions, and in 2004 with the lone-wolf adjustment to the definitional passage. Each entry on this timeline is permanent law added to the same chapter, processed through the same court, and overseen through the same reporting structure. The line runs unbroken from 1978 to the article's date, and a reader following it never leaves chapter 36.

The package's timeline is shorter and differently shaped. It begins in October 2001 with the ten-title omnibus, whose surveillance edits land on the framework's timeline as modifications to existing entries rather than as new entries of their own. It continues in 2005, when fourteen of sixteen sunset provisions become permanent and three related authorities stay on probation, and in 2011, when the extension measure carries those three forward unchanged. The package's timeline has no institutions, no court, and no code chapter of its own; every one of its surveillance entries points back to a location on the framework's timeline, naming the provision it revised and the date the revision took effect.

The two timelines merge in the reader's understanding of the current law. To describe the business-records authority as it stood in July 2014, the reader travels the framework's timeline to the 1998 creation, crosses to the package's timeline for the 2001 rewrite, and returns to the framework's chapter for the codified result. To describe section 702, the reader leaves both timelines for the 2008 amendments' entry, which belongs to the framework's line as its newest layer. The later developments extend the picture further: the 2015 reforms revise the 2001 rewrite, and the 2020 lapse removes three entries from the package's line while the framework's line continues. Timelines are the durability axis made visible, and the habit of placing every authority on its proper line is the surest defense against misattribution.

## Verdict: the deciding factor is study sequence

Every comparison in this series resolves with a verdict, and the verdict here is governed by a single named deciding factor: study sequence. The question is not which statute is more important in some abstract sense, because importance depends on the reader's purpose, and the two enactments are important in different ways. The question is which one a reader should study first, and the answer follows directly from the framework-and-amendments distinction. Study the framework statute first when the goal is to understand how foreign-intelligence collection is authorized, what institutions do the authorizing, and what standards and procedures constrain the activity. Study the amendment package first, or rather next, when the goal is to understand what changed after 2001, why the pre-2001 rules operated the way they did, and how the current version of each provision differs from its original. The deciding factor is sequence because the two enactments stand in a dependency relationship: the 2001 act's surveillance provisions are unintelligible without the 1978 statute they amended, while the 1978 statute is fully intelligible, as the law that operated for twenty-three years, without the 2001 act.

Consider what happens when the sequence is reversed. A reader who begins with the 2001 act encounters section 218's "significant purpose" language with no way to know what it replaced, reads section 215's "tangible things" language with no way to know the 1998 provision it broadened, and studies the sunset provisions with no way to understand what permanent framework they temporarily modified. The reader accumulates edits without a text being edited, which is a recipe for the exact confusion this article diagnoses. A reader who begins with FISA, by contrast, learns the definitions, the court, the application process, the original certification standard, and the original business-records provision, and then each 2001 amendment arrives as a legible change to something already understood. The wall becomes visible as a wall only after one has seen the pre-2001 certification language that built it. The significance of section 218 becomes clear only against the "the purpose" standard it replaced. This is why the study-sequence factor decides the comparison: it is not a preference but a logical requirement imposed by the dependency of amendments on their framework.

### Should a student study FISA or the PATRIOT Act first?
Study FISA first, because its definitions, court, and procedures are the operating system that every later amendment assumes. Then study the 2001 act's changes as edits to that system: the purpose standard, the coordination rule, the tangible-things scope, and the roving-order mechanism each become legible only against the original text.

The verdict also requires addressing the counter-reading with the directness the brief demands. The counter-reading holds that the 2001 act is the true foundation of the modern collection system, on the theory that the pre-2001 framework was so thoroughly transformed that the amendments deserve the credit for what the system became. There is a genuine insight inside that reading: the significant-purpose change, the coordination authority, and the broadened business-records scope did transform how the framework operated, and a history of American surveillance law that minimized those changes would be dishonest. But transformation is not foundation. The institutions that issue the orders, the code chapter that houses the provisions, the definitions that bound the category, and the oversight mechanisms that check the activity all date to 1978 and its direct amendments. The 2008 addition of section 702, which even the counter-reading must acknowledge as the largest structural addition to the system, was an amendment to FISA, debated and enacted as such, codified in FISA's chapter, and adjudicated by FISA's court. If the 2001 act were truly the foundation, the 2008 amendments would have been written as amendments to it. They were not. The statute books settle the question, and they settle it in favor of the framework.

A final element of the verdict concerns the 2001 act's non-surveillance titles, because study sequence applies to the package as a whole as well. A reader whose interest is money-laundering law should study the Bank Secrecy Act framework first and the 2001 act's Title III as an amendment to it. A reader interested in immigration detention authorities should study the Immigration and Nationality Act first and Title IV as an amendment. The pattern generalizes: for every subject the 2001 act touched, the right sequence is the underlying statute first and the 2001 amendments second. The surveillance comparison is simply the most prominent instance of a general rule about how to read omnibus amendment packages. That general rule is worth stating as the verdict's closing formulation: frameworks first, amendments second, always, because amendments presuppose the thing they amend.

Comparisons of statutes risk dissolving into parallel description, with each enactment summarized in turn and no principle for choosing between them, which is why this series requires every comparison to resolve with a verdict governed by a named deciding factor. The factor's job is to convert description into guidance: given a reader's purpose, which enactment takes priority and why. Study sequence earns the role here because the two enactments stand in a logical dependency that no reordering can escape. The framework can be understood without the package, as twenty-three years of history prove, while the package cannot be understood without the framework, as every one of its surveillance provisions demonstrates by naming the framework text it revises. Dependency is not a matter of emphasis or interpretation; it is a structural fact about how amendment language works, visible in the strike-and-insert drafting, in the code's integrated text, and in the courts' opinions. A deciding factor grounded in structure outlasts changes in policy debate, which is why the verdict can be stated without taking any position on whether the authorities described should exist.

### Two worked examples of study sequence

The verdict's deciding factor becomes concrete through worked examples, so consider two readers with different goals and watch how the framework-first sequence serves each one.

The first reader is a law student assigned to explain the significant-purpose standard to a seminar. Starting with the framework, she reads the original certification language, the definitions it depends on, and the application process it governs, and she learns what "the purpose" required before 2001 and how the wall culture grew around it. Turning to the package's section 218, she can then state precisely what changed: one word replaced by two, with the coordination provision alongside it, and she can read the Court of Review's 2002 opinion as an interpretation of an amended framework provision rather than as a mysterious freestanding ruling. Her seminar explanation will distinguish the text, the amendment, and the case law, because her reading order gave her the layers in the order the statute books stacked them.

The second reader is a journalist investigating the history of the business-records authority. Starting with the framework, he discovers that the authority is not original to 1978 at all, which immediately corrects the common assumption that everything in chapter 36 dates to the framework's enactment. He traces the 1998 creation, reads the original narrow categories and the original standard, and then reads the package's rewrite as a deliberate broadening, noting the shift from enumerated businesses to tangible things and from specific and articulable facts to relevance. When he reaches the 2015 reforms as a later development, he can describe them as a narrowing of the broadened provision rather than as a bolt from the blue. His reporting will name three layers and three Congresses, because his reading order followed the codification instead of the headlines.

Both readers illustrate the same principle the verdict states: amendments presuppose their framework, and study must follow the dependency. The student who reverses the order can still reach correct conclusions, but only by reconstructing the framework backwards from its amendments, a laborious route that invites exactly the misattributions this article corrects.

### The counter-reading's genuine insight

Intellectual honesty requires acknowledging the genuine insight inside the counter-reading before rejecting its conclusion. The counter-reading holds that the 2001 package transformed the framework so thoroughly that the amendments deserve the credit for what the system became, and on the question of operational transformation, it has a point worth taking seriously. The significant-purpose revision did not merely tweak a phrase; it restructured the daily relationship between intelligence officials and criminal prosecutors, dissolving a bureaucratic culture two decades in the making and changing how investigations were staffed, supervised, and documented. The coordination authority did not merely permit conversations; it rewired information flows between agencies that had been trained to avoid each other. The tangible-things rewrite did not merely broaden a category; it supplied the legal foundation for collection programs whose scale the 1998 drafters had not contemplated. A history that treated these changes as footnotes would misrepresent their practical magnitude, and any reader who comes away from this article thinking the package was inconsequential has misunderstood it.

The counter-reading goes wrong not in recognizing transformation but in converting transformation into foundation. Foundations are identified by what the system cannot operate without: the definitions that bound its jurisdiction, the court that authorizes its actions, the procedures that structure its applications, and the code chapter that houses its provisions. All of those date to the framework and its direct amendments, and all of them would have to be reinvented if the framework were repealed tomorrow, whereas the package's revisions, however transformative, presuppose every one of them. The test is subtractive. Subtract the package's amendments from the system and a working, if narrower, 1978 framework remains, as the twenty-three years before 2001 demonstrate. Subtract the framework and the amendments have nothing to amend; they become stray paragraphs referencing nonexistent institutions. Transformation describes what the package did to the framework's operation. Foundation describes what the framework is to the package's operation. The verdict honors the first while insisting on the second, which is why the deciding factor remains study sequence: learn the foundation before the transformation, because the transformation is unintelligible without it.

## A study path through the two statutes

A verdict about study sequence is only useful if it can be turned into a path, so this section lays out the order of reading that the comparison recommends, with the series' companion guides named at the points where each one earns its place. The path has four stages, and each stage answers a question the previous stage leaves open.

Stage one is the framework. Read the 1978 statute's core: the definitions in section 1801, the court provisions in section 1803, the application and order provisions in sections 1804 and 1805, and the original certification language. The goal of this stage is to be able to describe, without notes, how an application for electronic surveillance moved from the executive branch to the court and back in the original design. Stage two is the amendment package. The goal is to be able to state, for each amended provision, what the rule was before and what it became after. The series guide for this stage is [the amendment package guide](/2014/03/01/usa-patriot-act-complete-guide/), which treats the 2001 act title by title across its full ten-title scope.

Stage three is the later layering. Read the 2008 amendments' addition of Title VII, including section 702, as the next major edit to the framework, noting how programmatic collection under annual certifications differs from the individualized-order system of the original design. ### A note on pacing the four stages

The study path's four stages are designed for deliberate rather than hurried reading, and a word on pacing will help the reader allocate effort where it pays most. Stage one, the framework, deserves the largest share of time, because every later stage moves faster once the definitions, the court, and the application process are familiar; a reader who rushes the framework will repay the debt with interest when the amendments arrive. Stage three, the later layering, can move more quickly, because the 2008 and 2015 changes follow patterns the first two stages establish. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the difference between FISA and the PATRIOT Act?

FISA is a framework statute that created the legal architecture for foreign-intelligence collection inside the United States, including the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court, the application and order process, the statutory definitions, and the standards governing electronic surveillance, physical search, and business-records orders. The USA PATRIOT Act is an amendment package that modified that architecture along with about a dozen other statutes, without creating a parallel surveillance system. The practical difference is dependency: FISA stands on its own as the complete operating system for court-authorized foreign-intelligence collection, while the 2001 act's surveillance provisions only make sense as edits to named FISA sections. Congress enacted FISA as Public Law 95-511 in 1978 and codified it at 50 U.S.C. chapter 36; it enacted the 2001 measure as Public Law 107-56 across ten titles reaching many areas of law.

### Q: Which came first, FISA or the PATRIOT Act?

FISA came first, by twenty-three years. Congress passed the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act as Public Law 95-511, and President Carter signed it on October 25, 1978, codifying it at 50 U.S.C. chapter 36. The USA PATRIOT Act followed as Public Law 107-56, passed by the House on October 24, 2001, by the Senate on October 25, 2001, and signed by President George W. Bush on October 26, 2001. The order matters because the later enactment amended the earlier one: the 2001 act's surveillance provisions rewrote sections of the 1978 statute, including the certification standard in section 1804, the business-records provision in section 1861, and the order provisions governing facility specification. A reader who knows the chronology will not mistake the amendment for the framework.

### Q: Did the PATRIOT Act create FISA?

No. FISA was enacted in 1978, twenty-three years before the 2001 act, as Public Law 95-511, signed October 25, 1978, and codified at 50 U.S.C. chapter 36. The 2001 act, Public Law 107-56, amended FISA but did not create it, and it did not create a parallel surveillance system alongside it. This is one of the most common errors in public discussion of surveillance law, and the statute books refute it directly: the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court, the application and order procedures, and the definitional section 1801 all date to the 1978 enactment. What the 2001 act created in the surveillance field were amended standards operating inside that pre-existing framework, including the significant-purpose certification in section 218 and the coordination authority in section 504.

### Q: Which law authorizes the surveillance court, FISA or the PATRIOT Act?

FISA authorizes the surveillance court. The Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act of 1978 created the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court, composed of seven district judges designated by the Chief Justice, and the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court of Review, and assigned those courts the review of government applications for foreign-intelligence collection orders under 50 U.S.C. section 1803. The 2001 act created no court and no new adjudicative body; its amendments operate through the courts FISA established. When the Court of Review decided In re Sealed Case, 310 F.3d 717, in 2002, it was a FISA court interpreting a FISA provision as amended by the 2001 act. Any account that credits the 2001 legislation with creating the surveillance court misattributes the 1978 framework's central institution to a later amendment package.

### Q: Did the PATRIOT Act amend FISA?

Yes, extensively. Title II of the 2001 act, titled Enhanced Surveillance Procedures, amended multiple FISA provisions, and those amendments are the source of nearly everything the public associates with the 2001 legislation in the surveillance field. Section 218 changed the certification standard from "the purpose" to "a significant purpose" of obtaining foreign-intelligence information, codified at 50 U.S.C. section 1804(a)(6)(B). Section 215 rewrote the business-records and tangible-things provision at 50 U.S.C. section 1861. Section 206 amended the order provisions to permit roving surveillance. Section 504 added authority for coordination between intelligence and law-enforcement officials. The 2001 act also amended about a dozen other statutes outside FISA, which is why it is best understood as an omnibus amendment package rather than a surveillance statute in its own right.

### Q: Which authority is broader, FISA or the PATRIOT Act?

The answer depends on what breadth means. In subject matter, the 2001 act is far broader: its ten titles span surveillance procedure, money laundering, immigration, criminal law, victim compensation, intelligence organization, and infrastructure protection, amending many different statutes. In the surveillance field specifically, FISA is the broader and deeper authority: it contains the complete framework of definitions, institutions, standards, and procedures for foreign-intelligence collection, while the 2001 act contributes only amendments to that framework. A reader asking which law governs more of American life should look at the ten titles of Public Law 107-56; a reader asking which law governs foreign-intelligence surveillance should look at chapter 36 of Title 50, where FISA lives and where the 2001 amendments landed.

### Q: How did the PATRIOT Act remove the wall between FISA and criminal cases?

Before the 2001 amendments, FISA required certification that "the purpose" of the surveillance was to obtain foreign-intelligence information, language that executive-branch guidelines and court decisions had developed into a practical separation, called the wall, between intelligence investigations and criminal prosecutions. Section 218 of the 2001 act changed the statutory language to "a significant purpose," codified at 50 U.S.C. section 1804(a)(6)(B), so the government could certify foreign-intelligence collection as a significant purpose even where criminal prosecution was also contemplated. Section 504 added explicit authority for consultation and coordination between intelligence officials and law-enforcement officials. The Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court of Review upheld the amended standard in In re Sealed Case, 310 F.3d 717 (2002). The wall was thus dismantled by amending a FISA standard, not by replacing FISA.

### Q: Should a student study FISA or the PATRIOT Act first?

Study FISA first. The 1978 statute supplies the definitions, the court, the application and order process, and the original standards that every later amendment presupposes, and it operated as complete law for twenty-three years before the 2001 act. A student who learns the framework first can then read each 2001 amendment as a legible change: section 218 against the original "the purpose" certification, section 215 against the 1998 business-records provision it broadened, section 206 against the original facility-specification rules. Reversing the order produces edits without a text being edited, which is the source of most confusion about these two laws. The deciding factor is study sequence, and the sequence the dependency requires is framework first, amendments second.

### Q: What does the framework-and-amendments distinction mean for these two laws?

It means FISA and the 2001 act belong to different categories of legislation and must be read differently. A framework statute builds institutions, defines terms, and sets freestanding standards and procedures; FISA does all of that in 50 U.S.C. chapter 36. An amendment package edits existing statutes, usually several at once; the 2001 act did that across FISA and about a dozen other laws without creating a parallel surveillance system. The distinction tells a reader where to look: questions about how collection is authorized go to the framework, while questions about what changed after 2001 go to the amendment package. It also predicts durability: frameworks persist and accumulate amendments, while amendment provisions may carry sunset dates, as sixteen of the 2001 act's sections originally did.

### Q: Which sections of the 2001 act changed FISA the most?

Four sections did the heaviest work. Section 218 replaced "the purpose" with "a significant purpose" in the certification requirement, the change the brief identifies as the most consequential, because it restructured the relationship between intelligence collection and criminal prosecution. Section 215 rewrote the business-records provision at 50 U.S.C. section 1861, broadening obtainable materials to any tangible things and adopting a relevance standard. Section 206 amended the order provisions to allow roving surveillance that follows a target across facilities. Section 504 authorized coordination between intelligence and law-enforcement officials. Together these four redefined the operating rules of the 1978 framework while leaving its institutions, the court, the application process, and the code chapter, intact and in place.

### Q: Why do the sunset provisions belong to FISA if they came from the 2001 act?

Because a sunset clause attached to an amendment governs the amended provision, and the amended provisions live in FISA. Section 215 of the 2001 act rewrote the business-records provision codified at 50 U.S.C. section 1861, which is a FISA section; when that amendment carried a sunset date, what faced expiration was the amended version of that FISA provision, not a freestanding 2001 program. The same logic applies to section 206's roving-order amendments and to the lone-wolf provision added by the 2004 intelligence reform measure and codified at 50 U.S.C. section 1801(b)(1)(C). Headlines about expiring surveillance law are therefore always shorthand for a precise question: which provisions, in which statute, face which date. As of July 2014, the answer was those three FISA-related authorities, extended to June 1, 2015.

### Q: Where is Section 702 codified, and which law added it?

Section 702 is codified at 50 U.S.C. section 1881a, inside FISA's chapter 36, and it was added by the FISA Amendments Act of 2008, Public Law 110-261. Neither the 1978 statute in its original form nor the 2001 act created it. Its short-lived predecessor was the Protect America Act of 2007, Public Law 110-55, which expired in February 2008. Section 702 authorizes targeting non-United States persons reasonably believed to be outside the United States for foreign-intelligence purposes under annual certifications approved by the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court, a programmatic architecture distinct from the individualized orders of the original 1978 design. Within this article's time frame it stood extended by Public Law 112-238 of December 2012; its 2018 and 2024 reauthorizations are later developments.

### Q: Did the 2001 act create the bulk telephony metadata program?

No. The program relied on the business-records provision at 50 U.S.C. section 1861, whose underlying authority Congress created in section 602 of the Intelligence Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 1999, Public Law 105-272, in 1998. Section 215 of the 2001 act amended that provision, broadening it to any tangible things and adopting a relevance standard, and the amended FISA provision supplied the claimed legal basis for the program. Attributing the program's creation to the 2001 act compresses three legislative layers, the 1998 creation, the 2001 amendment, and the FISA codification, into one and misidentifies what each layer contributed. The USA FREEDOM Act of June 2015, a later development relative to this article, ended bulk collection under section 1861 and required a specific selection term.

### Q: What did the Protect America Act of 2007 have to do with FISA?

The Protect America Act, Public Law 110-55, enacted in August 2007, temporarily amended FISA to address collection directed at persons reasonably believed to be outside the United States, and it served as the short-lived predecessor to section 702. It expired in February 2008 and was superseded by the FISA Amendments Act of 2008, Public Law 110-261, which added Title VII, including section 702, to FISA on a longer-term basis. Its relevance to this comparison is structural: it confirms the pattern in which Congress expands collection authority by amending the 1978 framework rather than by building new systems. Like the 2001 amendments, it left no freestanding surveillance code of its own; its provisions were absorbed into chapter 36 or expired, and the durable architecture remained FISA.

### Q: How did the 2001 act change FISA's business-records provision?

Before the 2001 amendments, the business-records authority created in 1998 was narrow: it covered records from common carriers, public accommodations, physical storage facilities, and vehicle rental facilities, and required specific and articulable facts showing the records pertained to a foreign power or an agent of a foreign power. Section 215 of the 2001 act rewrote the provision, codified at 50 U.S.C. section 1861, to cover any tangible things and replaced the original standard with a relevance standard tied to investigations to protect against international terrorism or clandestine intelligence activities. That rewritten provision, a FISA section as amended by the 2001 act, became one of the most debated authorities in the statute, carried a sunset date, and was later reformed by the 2015 FREEDOM Act, a development postdating this article.

### Q: What is the lone-wolf provision, and which statute created it?

The lone-wolf provision permits the government to seek FISA orders targeting non-United States persons engaged in international terrorism without showing that they act on behalf of a foreign power, closing a gap in the original agent-of-a-foreign-power framework. Congress created it in section 6001 of the Intelligence Reform and Terrorism Prevention Act of 2004, Public Law 108-458, and it is codified at 50 U.S.C. section 1801(b)(1)(C), an amendment to FISA's definitional section. Although the 2001 act did not create it, the provision traveled with sections 215 and 206 through the sunset and reauthorization debates because of its functional kinship with the 2001 amendments. As of July 2014 it remained in force under the 2011 extension to June 1, 2015.

### Q: Which law should a researcher read to understand roving wiretaps under FISA?

The researcher should read FISA as amended, because the roving-order mechanism is a 2001 amendment operating inside the 1978 framework. Section 206 of the 2001 act amended FISA's order provisions to permit surveillance orders that follow a target across facilities rather than specifying a single facility with particularity, addressing the problem of targets switching phones or locations to evade collection. The amended authority is codified within chapter 36, applications go to the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court, and the court's findings and minimization requirements apply as the framework provides. Reading the 2001 act alone would show the amendment without the application process it amends; reading FISA alone in its original form would show the facility-specification rule the amendment replaced. The framework-first sequence gives both halves.

### Q: How does the FISA Court of Review fit into this comparison?

The Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court of Review, created by FISA in 1978 under 50 U.S.C. section 1803, is the appellate body for the surveillance court, and its near-total silence for twenty-four years is itself evidence of the framework's stability. It issued its first published opinion in In re Sealed Case, 310 F.3d 717, in 2002, and that opinion concerned a 2001 amendment: the court upheld section 218's significant-purpose certification against constitutional challenge. The episode concentrates the whole comparison into a single case. The institution belonged to the 1978 framework, the question before it arose from the 2001 amendment package, and the opinion treated the underlying statute as FISA throughout. No parallel appellate body was ever created for the 2001 act, because the act built no system requiring one.

### Q: Why did Congress make most of the 2001 act's sunset provisions permanent in 2005?

Fourteen of the sixteen provisions that carried sunset dates under the original section 224 were made permanent in the 2005 reauthorization because a majority in Congress concluded, after hearings and debate, that the authorities had become settled parts of the investigative framework rather than emergency experiments. The sunset mechanism had been the legislative compromise that made rapid 2001 passage possible, promising a future Congress the chance to revisit the most controversial changes. Sections 215 and 206, along with the lone-wolf provision added in 2004, were kept on a continuing reauthorization cycle instead, reflecting lingering disagreement about those particular authorities. The pattern illustrates the durability axis: amendment packages carry their controversial edits on probation, while the framework they edit endures without an expiration date.

### Q: What changed about Section 215 after this article's date?

Two later developments reshaped it, both explicitly dated here as later than July 2014. First, the USA FREEDOM Act, Public Law 114-23, enacted June 2, 2015, after a one-day lapse on June 1, ended bulk collection under 50 U.S.C. section 1861 and required orders to use a specific selection term, defined at section 1861(k)(4)(A), with a 180-day transition ending November 29, 2015. Second, section 215, together with section 206 and the lone-wolf provision, lapsed on March 15, 2020, subject to a grandfather clause for existing matters, and had not been reauthorized afterward. Within this article's own time frame, none of that had happened: section 215 stood in its 2001-amended form, in force under the 2011 extension to June 1, 2015.
