## Counting Twice
The yard behind the bonded store held its heat in stone. Alden Pyke stood with his back to the gate iron and felt the afternoon come off the paving in a slow wash, salt and tar and the green smell of harbour water beyond the warehouse roofs. The store he had inherited took up the long side of the yard, its bonded doors shut and chalked, its lower windows barred. At the far end, set apart by cracked ground where carts had worn two pale tracks, Tobin Rill kept his office in what had once been a tally room. It had a door that fitted, a roof that did not leak in the manner of the other roofs, and a single window with a blind drawn halfway down. Inside that window the air would be cooler, because everything about Rill's side of the yard was kept in the condition a man kept things when he expected other men to notice.

He had come through the gate with the money folded in a canvas packet against his ribs, under his coat, where his own hand kept finding it and pressing it flat. The gate keeper had let him in without a question beyond his name, which had unsettled him more than a question would have done. On his own wharf, at his own store, a man was asked his business as a matter of form even when his face was known. Here he was expected. Expectation made the packet feel heavier than its weight.

Rill's clerk was not in the outer room. The outer room held a table scarred by knife work and cup rings, two chairs that did not match, a ledger press in the corner with its screw down on nothing, and a row of seal boxes on a shelf, each box plain, each lid shut. Through the inner door, which stood open by the width of a hand, Pyke could see the edge of a desk and a man's sleeve resting beside a blotter, still in the way a sleeve rests when its owner has heard a step and chosen not to rise for it. He waited until his breathing had settled, and then he knocked on the open door with one knuckle, although he had plainly been heard already.

![Three bonded doors standing side by side in a Valtessa warehouse, their lead seals catching the light - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-49.webp)

Rill looked up and considered him without surprise. He was a spare man, neat in a way that had nothing to do with vanity, and his face gave Pyke the same feeling the yard had given him, that everything here had been placed and would be found again in its place. He gestured to the chair across the desk. He did not offer water, although there was a jug on the side table and the day was hot enough to make a courtesy of it. Pyke sat and set the canvas packet on his knee and held it there, because putting it on the desk at once felt like a surrender he had not rehearsed.

He had rehearsed other parts. On the walk from his own store he had rehearsed how he would say that the seal on his north door was sound, that it had passed the last inspection without remark, that his Binder was licensed and his books were clean and his father had kept the store for thirty years without a fine worth naming. He had come only because Harlen Dace, who had known his father, had taken him aside by the weighbridge and told him the inspectors were coming harder this season. Harder had been the whole of the advice. Harder, and a name, and a yard, and a suggestion that a prudent man bought what protection was going before the season turned. Pyke said none of it in the order he had planned. He said that Dace had sent him, and Rill listened in the manner of a man who already held the shape of a thing and was waiting to hear whether the speaker would spoil it by talking.

The desk between them was clear except for the blotter, a pen laid parallel to its edge, and a shallow tray in which three seals rested without touching one another. Pyke looked at the tray despite himself. The seals were handsome objects in the way that official things are handsome, the Accord mark cut deep and clean, the numbers set in a true line. One of them might have been made for his north door. He told himself that was the purchase, that a seal was a thing a man could hold and show to an inspector across a doorway, and that there was nothing shameful in buying a better seal than the one he had, even if the one he had was sound. The thought steadied him until Rill, following his look, drew the tray toward his own side of the desk, not as a man protects goods from a thief, but as a man clears a table of something that has finished its part in the conversation.

Pyke understood then, with a discomfort he could not yet name, that the seals on the desk were furniture. They were there so that men like him would have something to look at while they talked about something else. Rill's attention had not gone to the tray at all. Rill's attention was on Pyke's hands, on the packet on his knee, on the way Pyke's thumb kept smoothing the canvas where the fold had creased it. When Pyke at last set the packet on the desk, Rill did not reach for it. He left it lying between the blotter and the tray while Pyke explained, in more words than the matter needed, that he had brought the full sum Dace had named, in the notes of the harbour bank, counted at his own table that morning with the store shut and the bar down.

Rill nodded at the counting. It seemed to interest him more than the sum had done. He asked whether Pyke had counted it once or twice, and Pyke, surprised into honesty, said twice. Rill asked whether the second count had agreed with the first, and Pyke said it had, and felt foolish for the small pride he took in saying so, as if agreement between two counts were an achievement a grown man should be able to claim without colour rising in his face. Rill made no comment on the pride or the foolishness. He watched Pyke's face while Pyke spoke about the counting, in the way a man watches a gauge while a boiler fills, not for the numbers themselves but for what the numbers say about the vessel. The money lay unopened. The seals lay untouched. Outside, a cart crossed the yard and the iron of its wheels found every joint in the paving, and neither man turned toward the sound.

What Pyke was buying settled over him slowly, the way damp settles into a wall. He was not buying the seal, because his seal was sound and both of them knew it. He was not buying Dace's advice, because advice had already been given freely. He was buying the state of affairs in which a man who had paid in this room would be treated, when the harder season came, as a man who had paid in this room. He could not have said what form that treatment would take, or who would extend it, or how they would know. He only knew that Dace, who did not frighten easily, had believed the purchase worth making, and that his own fear, which had woken him two nights running with the thought of a fine large enough to close the store his father had left him, had believed it too.

Rill opened the packet at last, but he did not count the notes. He turned them once with two fingers, as a man turns the pages of a book he does not intend to read, noted the denominations with a glance, and closed the packet again. Pyke felt a protest rise in him that he had to swallow. At his own table the notes had been counted twice, laid in order, squared at the corners. To see them handled as if their exact number were a detail beneath the business of the room was to feel the business of the room shift under his feet. The money mattered to him in exact number. It plainly mattered to Rill in some other way entirely, as a sign, or as a toll, or as evidence that the man across the desk had been frightened in the right direction and by the right amount.

He found himself counting again in his head while Rill spoke. Counting was the part of this he understood, the part with rules that held still while he used them. Rill was saying that a man would call at the store before the season turned, that Pyke should keep his books as he always kept them, that he should answer inspectors as he always answered them, that nothing in his manner should change. Pyke listened and counted. The second count had agreed with the first. If he counted a third time he would reach the same figure, and the sameness was a kind of ground under him. Nothing should change, and yet a man would call, and yet the packet would stay in this room when Pyke left it.

Rill did not name what the caller would do. He did not describe a seal being fitted or a paper being signed or a mark being entered in any book Pyke would ever see. Pyke had questions folded up in him as carefully as the notes had been folded, and each question seemed to reveal more about his ignorance than it would repair. To ask what exactly he had bought would be to admit he had paid without knowing. He asked neither of the questions that pressed on him. He asked whether he should tell Dace that the matter was settled, and Rill said that Dace would know, without any emphasis, as a fact about the weather in another street.

Then Rill stood, and the interview was over in the manner of interviews that end because one man stands. He did not offer Pyke one of the seals from the tray. He did not summon the clerk to write a receipt. He walked Pyke to the inner door himself, and Pyke understood that he was being given the chance to leave with the same face he had brought in, and that the courtesy, like the cool of the room, was deliberate. Nothing in either room had been altered by his visit except the location of his money, and that alteration, he thought, was not the point of the visit either. The point was in Rill's knowledge of him, kept wherever Rill kept such things, and in the knowledge, travelling by routes Pyke would never be shown, of men Pyke would never meet.

Outside, the yard had cooled by a degree and the cart tracks lay in shadow. Pyke crossed with his coat buttoned over the place where the packet had been, and his hand found the empty place once and then let it be. At the gate the keeper nodded to him as a man nods to someone he expects to see again, and Pyke felt the nod settle on him like a second purchase he had not agreed to. He walked home by the long way, along the bonded warehouses, past doors he had known since boyhood, and he counted his steps between the lamp posts without deciding to, twice over the same stretch, arriving at the same number both times, and taking what comfort there was in a world where at least the counting held.

## Watched in Valtessa
The port office in Valtessa had been a customs house before it had been anything else, and it kept the habits of that first purpose in its bones. Mira Kovac stood just inside its main room on the second floor and took in the tall windows giving onto the harbour, the smell of salt worked into the wood of the sills, the iron bound ledgers ranked along one wall, the long table scarred by generations of clerks, and the stone of the stairs still hollowed in the middle of each tread by feet that had climbed with cargo manifests in their hands. She had climbed those stairs for six years in another uniform, or near enough that the building recognised her before the people in it did. The clerk at the door had known her name without reading it from the paper she carried. That recognition sat in her mind alongside the tasking that had brought her, because Arvo Leyden had named it as a question before he had named anything else, and Leyden seldom wasted a question on anything he considered settled.

His tasking had come from Carrowmere by telephone first and by sealed paper after, the paper arriving on the morning boat with the Office mark unbroken and her name written in his clerk's hand rather than his own. The telephone call had been brief in the manner of all his calls, as if the line itself charged by the sentence. The paper was longer, and she had read it three times in the room she kept above a chandler's shop two streets from the docks, with the notebook in her coat pocket pressing, through the cloth, a reminder she did not need, of the register she had agreed to stop keeping and had not stopped keeping. Eighteen years of another man's fear still rode in her from the Highstile work, the heaviest thing she carried, and she had rested since that case, and she entered this one rested, which she noted with the plain gratitude of a practitioner who knows what depletion borrows from judgment. Rested, and on home ground, and watched. Leyden had been exact about the order of those things.

The cost had come first in his paper, before the case itself, which was his way when the cost was the part he most needed her to hear without flinching. The rollup would run in Valtessa, on Valtessa ground, with Valtessa's own service in the room. She would work watched, by arrangement rather than by suspicion, and anything her practice found would be said aloud, in the room, in front of Inspector Juno Sarte of the Valtessan port service, who commanded the Valtessan side of the joint operation and whose signature would stand beside the Office's on whatever report the case produced. There would be no private readings carried back to Carrowmere for later disclosure in a softened form. If a door read as a door should read, she would say so in Sarte's hearing. If a door read otherwise, she would say that too, in the same hearing, with Sarte's clerks writing it down. Leyden had written that she should consider whether she understood what it asked of a Weigher whose work was ordinarily done in the quiet at the edge of a room, with the result reported afterwards and alone. She had considered it. The telephone call had ended with her acceptance, and the sealed paper was the record of an agreement already made.

Sarte herself came into the room with a file under her arm and a clerk at her shoulder. Juno Sarte was in her forties, precise in dress and movement, with the stillness of a person who had learned that a service's standing is lost in gestures before it is lost in documents. She set the file on the long table at a measured distance from the edge, introduced herself without warmth and without coldness, which Mira respected as the more honest register, and then stated, as opening business, exactly what her service would and would not put in a joint report. It would record what was observed, what was measured, what was seized and what was read, in language both services could defend. It would not record speculation about the Accord, or about Binders licensed on these docks, or about failures that belonged in a report upward her service would write in its own time. She said all this looking at Mira directly, and Mira understood that the speech had been prepared and that its preparation did not make it less meant.

Whether the port service would let an Office officer read doors on Valtessa ground, with its own inspector in the room, was therefore a question with two answers, and both were in the room already. The arrangement existed, signed and witnessed, and Sarte's presence at the table was its living form. What remained open was what the arrangement would bear when a reading said something the service had not budgeted for, when the words had to be spoken aloud instead of carried away and weighed in private, and when the clerk's pen had to move on a sentence that might cost the service more than a forgery prosecution would ever earn it. Mira held that question open without dressing it as distrust. Sarte, for her part, set out the evidence in the order of a file that had been settled before Mira entered the building.

The three seized seals lay in a shallow evidence tray lined with cloth, and Sarte's clerk uncovered them as if uncovering something faintly embarrassing. Mira did not need her practice for any part of what was wrong with them. The Accord mark was crude, cut by a tool that had hesitated where the Carrowmere press never hesitated. The room numbers had been cut by a hand that did not know the press, the strokes uneven, the spacing a guess dressed as knowledge. The hairline, the fine worn line that the old Carrowmere die left on every true seal it struck, was missing altogether. She had handled true seals through six years of bonded cargo on these same docks, had turned them under lamps in winter mornings with her gloves off, had rejected cargo for a hairline's absence before she had any other gift worth the name, and the wrongness in the tray was legible to any inspector who had served a full season. Three experts had said forgery, in writing, and their statements were in the file beneath the tray. Forgery was a reasonable reading, the reading any careful service would have reached, and Sarte's file had reached it completely, and in that completeness Mira felt the first chill of the case, because a file that has settled too early is a room with the door already shut.

Leyden's paper had put the open question in his own spare phrasing, which she carried in memory rather than on the page in front of Sarte. Whether the seized seals were the whole of the chain's trade, or only its visible half. A trade in forged seals, sold to the careless of the docks, was a trade the port service knew how to prosecute, with precedents and penalties and experts who would stand behind their statements. It fitted the tray. It fitted the file. What it did not yet fit was the matter of the doors. Six bonded doors across the Valtessa docks were known to have been supplied or serviced by the chain, and those six doors could be opened for inspection, seals examined, bindings read, under the joint arrangement Sarte commanded. The doors, and not the tray, were the reason the Office had been brought in, and until they were opened the seized seals remained either a whole trade in a tray or the shopfront of a trade whose real goods were kept in another room entirely.

Her six years on these docks ran under that discomfort like a current under harbour water. They helped her, plainly. She knew which bonded stores kept honest books and which kept two sets in the same drawer. She knew the gate keepers, the tally clerks, the yard keepers, the men who could tell a true seal by sound when it was set down on stone. All of that was capital she had not had in any other posting, earned in years when her judgments had cost men money and occasionally their licences. The same years compromised her in the same measure. People on these docks remembered her rulings as well as her face. Some owed her their livelihoods, and some owed her a grudge of the same weight, and both kinds of memory would move ahead of her through the yards, opening some doors and closing others with a quietness she might never hear. Whether the chain knew it was being watched before the doors were opened could not be separated from that memory. A watched chain tidies its visible half and moves its real business by other streets, and she might arrive at six doors prepared for her arrival as carefully as Sarte's opening speech had been prepared for hers.

Time made the last of Leyden's questions, and Sarte gave it its sharpest form when she set out her service's timetable. Six doors could be opened and read before the chain moved its trade elsewhere, or they could not, and the difference lay in days rather than weeks. Her service could hold warrants for the six premises in readiness, could place its inspectors at gates, could open doors in an order that gave the chain the least warning between the first door and the last. It could not hold the whole dock still while the Office deliberated, and it would not try. Sarte stated the timetable as a fact of her harbour rather than as a pressure upon her guest, and Mira received it in that spirit. Tomas Rask would carry the practical load on the Valtessa side, and Reni Vosk would hold positions and report what passed her, as she had held a gate at the Teshkar depot in another season. Machinery was not the question. The question was whether doors opened in sequence, under another service's eye, by a Weigher who had to speak her findings aloud as she found them, would give up what doors ordinarily gave up in quiet and in private and afterwards.

She agreed the order of the doors with Sarte at the long table, name by name, while the clerk wrote and the harbour light moved across the ledgers. She did not touch the seals in the tray. There was nothing in them her practice could have added to what her eyes, trained in an older trade, had already taken, and she saved the cost of reading for surfaces that might repay it. When the order was settled she read it back from the clerk's book to be certain both services held the same six names in the same sequence, and Sarte confirmed each name as it was spoken. Outside the tall windows the harbour went on with its work, cranes and carts and the cries of men who had never heard of the Ninth Office, and Mira felt the familiar ground of Valtessa under her feet and the arrangement around her like a frame, holding her in full view. She would read the doors watched. She would say what she found aloud. The case had begun in the only way it could have begun, with the cost named, accepted, and standing in the room beside her in the person of Juno Sarte, watching.

## Six Doors

The Valtessa docks woke before the warehouses did, and the light that came off the water in the first hour was thin and grey and full of salt, laying itself flat across tarred rope and wet stone and the iron runners of the bonded store doors where gulls had left white marks in long, patient lines, and the air smelled of brine and hot oil and the sweet rot of grain dust that had settled into every seam of the quays, so that even the clean uniforms of the port service carried it by noon. Mira Kovac stood with her coat still buttoned against the harbour wind and felt the old familiarity of the place settle in her shoulders before she had consciously named it, the particular width of the Salt Steps where the tide left a dark tidemark at the third stair, the way sound carried too far across the Cannery Row yard when the wind dropped, the iron smell of the Lower Ferry customs house where she had once spent a whole winter checking manifests with numb fingers and a clerk who made tea strong enough to stand a spoon in. Juno Sarte walked half a step behind her on the first quay and said nothing about any of it, and that restraint was itself a kind of statement. Sarte was a precise woman in her forties with her hair pinned close and her port service coat brushed clean despite the dock air, and she carried the joint inspection warrant in a flat case against her side as if she did not want the paper to crease, and she had made it plain at the port service rooms that morning that every door would be opened under her eye, every seal handled first by her own gloved hands, every reading spoken aloud where she could hear it and write it down in her own hand if she chose.

Tomas Rask walked on her other side with a canvas bag over his shoulder that held nothing more dramatic than wedges and a lamp and a coil of marked cord, and he walked as he always walked in a working place, a little apart from the conversation, watching thresholds and sightlines and the places where a man could stand without being in the way of a barrow. Reni Vosk had gone ahead with two port service constables to the Salt Steps store to have the outer gate open and the storekeeper warned, not because the storekeeper was suspected of anything beyond keeping a small bonded store in a hard season, but because a bonded door opened without warning made enemies of honest men and Mira had no wish to spend goodwill she might need later in the day. The Accord mark on the first had been crude in a way that did not improve on a second looking, the lines too thick where the Carrowmere press left them fine, the room numbers cut by a hand that had hesitated at the curves, and the hairline, that fine worn flaw that ran through every true impression from the old Carrowmere die like a scratch remembered in metal, had been missing altogether. The second seized seal had been wrong in a different register, the numbers unpractised and uneven, as if the cutter had learned the shapes from a rubbing rather than from the press itself, and the third had carried a mark so shallow that the light had to be tipped twice before the shape declared itself at all.

The first door on the list was the Salt Steps store door itself, and the storekeeper, a broad man with flour on his cuffs from the dry goods he kept on the unbonded side of his premises, stood in his own doorway with his hands visible and his anxiety visible too, though he tried to cover it with a long explanation about tides and deliveries that Sarte cut short with courtesy and complete finality. The door was iron bound timber, old enough that the grain had lifted along the lower panels where harbour damp had worked for decades, and the seal seating had been repainted so many times that the edges were soft with paint, and the seal that hung there under Sarte's glove was the first of the wrong ones in place, crude mark and no hairline, turning dully under the morning light. The binding read heavy and deliberate and sustained, a great settled pressure in the iron that had been laid there by intent held over time, the kind of weight a Binder's work always left for a Weigher, unmistakable and entirely impersonal, giving her the careful quality of a hand at work without giving her a face or a name to put to it. She said so aloud, in the plain words she had agreed with Sarte beforehand, that the binding behind the wrong seal read heavy and intact, and Sarte wrote it down without comment and the storekeeper let out a breath he had been holding since the gate opened and did not understand what he had been reprieved from.

The Cannery Row bonded door stood at the end of a long, low building where the air was warmer and smelled of fish oil and hot tin even in the cold part of the day, and the yard before it was scored with the arcs of a hundred barrow wheels and stacked with crates that leaked straw at every corner. The seal there was the second wrong pattern, numbers cut by an unpractised hand, the figures leaning where a press would have held them upright, and the warehouse foreman who kept that door did not hover as the Salt Steps man had hovered but stood well back with his arms folded, as if distance could be a kind of innocence. She laid her hand to the Cannery Row door at the hinge plate where the metal was oldest and read there while Rask held the lamp steady without being asked and Sarte stood close enough to see that Mira's hand did not move or signal or do anything at all beyond resting on iron that looked, to any other eye, entirely unremarkable. The binding read again heavy and intact, deliberate in the same sustained register as the first, a Binder's intent laid down and left to hold, and the room behind the door carried its own ordinary weight of use through the iron, preserved goods and repeated labour and the low, constant worry of men who kept perishable stock in a warm building.

The Lower Ferry customs door was different in kind from the first two because it belonged to the service itself, a customs door in the old ferry house where foot passengers had once waited with their bundles while officers chalked and stamped in the rain, and the paint there was service green and carefully kept, and the constable who unlocked the outer office for them did so with the air of a man opening his own house to guests he had not invited. The seal on that door was wrong in the third manner, mark shallow and hairline missing, and it hung against the green paint looking paler than the other two had looked, almost apologetic, as if it knew it would not survive a close inspection and had never been meant to. The reading, when it came, was the same again and therefore more unsettling for being the same, heavy and intact behind a shallow mark, the Binder's sustained intent present in the iron of the strap and the stone of the reveal where she rested her fingers second, to confirm what the first touch had told her, that the binding had been laid and had held and had not thinned in the way an old binding left untended would thin over seasons of neglect. She said it aloud and the clerk wrote it and Sarte wrote it too, in her own book this time as well as the joint sheet, and in the small silence that followed while the seal was bagged, Mira found herself turning over the first of the possibilities that Leyden had laid out in the tasking and that she had carried onto the docks like a case with three compartments. On the evidence of three doors she could not dismiss that possibility and did not try to, because three intact bindings behind three forged seals was exactly what a forgery trade would leave behind it if the buyers had kept their genuine bindings and merely replaced a lost or damaged seal with a copy bought in a hurry, or if the chain had sold seals as ornaments of compliance to men whose real protection had never depended on the seal at all. She held the possibility seriously and said so to Sarte on the walk to the Ninth Pier, because Sarte deserved to hear that the Office had not arrived with its conclusion already written, and Sarte listened with her case against her side and her eyes on the quay ahead and answered only that the port service file had reached the same possibility first and had found it sufficient, which was not agreement and was not challenge but the sound of a woman marking where the two services overlapped and where they did not.

The Ninth Pier store door stood in a newer building where the stone was still pale and the ironwork still carried the blacking of its first seasons, and the wind there came straight off the open water without the shelter of the older quays, carrying spray in fine bursts that beaded on coats and cases alike. The store belonged to a consortium rather than a single keeper, and no keeper came down to watch at all, only a consortium clerk with a ring of keys and a manner that suggested he had been told to cooperate and to record the cooperation in equal measure. The Accord mark stood clean and fine in the metal, the room numbers cut true and upright, and when Sarte tipped it under the lamp the hairline showed, that worn flaw of the Carrowmere die running through the impression exactly where the press would have left it, neither exaggerated nor faint, a fault so particular that a copyist who had not handled true impressions daily would have smoothed it away as an error. Mira looked at it for a long time while the clerk read the record aloud, the licensed binding entered in the port service book with a Binder's name and a date of laying in story reckoning and a renewal that had been signed and witnessed, a full and proper record of a binding that should have been on that door, and she felt the faint, familiar tightening at the base of her attention that came when the evidence in her hands and the evidence in the record began to point in different directions. Where a binding should have been there was nothing, not a thinning, not a damage, not a fading of the kind that years left on neglected work, but an absence with an edge to it, the binding's place left clean and specific while everything around that place read as a room and a door should read. The rooms behind those doors, when she would later consider all three together, kept their goods and their use and their ordinary accumulation of human passage entirely intact to her reading, and that particularity was the thing she could not set aside, that only the binding was not there while nothing else about the door or the store had been stripped or silenced. She spoke the result aloud because she had undertaken to speak every result aloud, and she chose words that did not reach beyond what she could stand over, that behind a perfect seal she could read no binding at all. Sarte wrote it down more slowly than she had written the first three results, and asked, with care, whether the binding might have lapsed and the record failed to show the lapse, whether a binding left without renewal could fade past reading while the door itself still carried other weight, and that question opened the second compartment of the case fairly and had to be answered fairly. Mira answered that a lapsed binding thinned as cloth thinned, unevenly and from the edges of its intent inward, and that she had read lapsed bindings on these same docks in her inspector years that still gave back a ghost of the Binder's sustained purpose long after their strength for any practical purpose had gone, and that what she had found on the Ninth Pier door had no ghost and no thinning and no edge of decay, only a clean nonpresence where the record said a substantial thing had been laid.

The Old Customs yard door was not far, set in the high wall of a yard the service no longer used for its own storage but leased in parcels to traders who needed bonded ground close to the water, and the door was broader than the others, built for barrows and small carts, with iron straps that ran the full width of the timber and a lock plate scarred by decades of keys. The seal there was perfect again, mark and number and hairline true under Sarte's lamp, and the consortium clerk had been replaced by the yard tenant's own man, who kept glancing at the seal as if he had never looked at it closely before and was disturbed to discover how closely other people were willing to look. She thought, while her hand still rested on cold iron and Sarte stood at her shoulder with the joint sheet ready, of another yard and another set of doors in a different season of her work, and the memory came not as an image but as a comparison her practice made before her mind had framed it. [At the Teshkar depot a chain had been taken apart around a sealed store room that held no weight at all, in a depot where every other room read heavily](/2026/11/03/nightjar-bonded-cargo-004/). She spoke the fifth result aloud in the same form as the fourth and saw the clerk frown at the repetition, because repetition in a report suggested either carelessness or a pattern and he had begun to suspect it was a pattern. Rask, who had been silent through five doors in the manner of a man who knew his silence was part of the joint record's credibility, shifted his bag on his shoulder and looked once at Sarte and once at Mira and said nothing yet, because the question that belonged to him had not yet been asked in the hearing of the record, and they all understood without discussion that it would be asked at the sixth door or in the yard that followed, where its answer could be entered once and stand for all the doors together.

The Grain Arcade bonded door closed the list, set under the arcade's vaulted walk where the light fell in stripes through the arches and the dust of milled grain hung in the air so finely that it showed in every shaft and settled on the shoulders of coats within minutes. The arcade smelled of warm sacks and old wood and the faint sweetness of spilled grain that had worked into the stone, and the keeper there was an older woman who had kept bonded grain through three changes of port administration and who watched the inspection with the expression of someone who had survived previous inspections by treating them as weather. Mira did not answer that question directly because the honest answer required the reading first, and she set her hand to the Grain Arcade iron with a deliberateness she hoped the keeper would read as thoroughness rather than theatre, and she found for the third time the same clean absence in the binding's place and the same ordinary fullness everywhere else, the weight of sacks hauled and weighed and recorded, of arguments about moisture and price conducted in the doorway across many seasons, of a keeper's sustained and careful intent to keep her store exactly as the record said it was. That last quality steadied Mira's judgment in a way she noted for the report she would eventually have to shape, because a keeper whose own intent read that carefully was an unlikely author of a fraud upon her own door, and because the absence, wherever she found it that day, had the same boundaries and the same indifference to who kept the store behind it.

When the six doors had been opened and read and resealed with temporary service seals under Sarte's direction, they stood in the lee of the Grain Arcade where the wind could not reach and Sarte spread the joint sheet on top of her flat case and they looked at what the morning had made together, six lines of record that divided themselves without any help from interpretation. What the morning had settled, and what no later argument about motive or method could unsettle without first explaining these six lines away, was that the wrong seals and the true bindings stood on the same doors and the perfect seals and the absent bindings stood on the same doors, without a single exception in either direction. Mira watched Sarte rule a line under the entries with a hand that did not hesitate, and she understood that the table that would be drawn from that sheet in the port service rooms was not a summary of the forgery file but a replacement for it, or at least a challenge no forgery file could absorb unchanged.

Sarte read the six lines back under her breath as a clerk reads a manifest, not for Mira's benefit but for her own, and then she asked the question that any honest inspector in her position had to ask before the pattern hardened into an accusation against the record itself. She asked whether the perfect seals might themselves be copies, copies good enough to stand beside Carrowmere press work under a lamp and under handling, because if the perfect seals were copies then the whole table could be pulled back toward forgery by a longer route, a better cutter, a stolen die, a press obtained and used with skill. Mira took the three perfect seals in turn as Sarte passed them in their bags, holding the evidence plastic away from the metal and tipping each impression to the striped arcade light, and she gave that possibility the full weight of her six years on bonded cargo, turning the seals as she had once turned seals daily, feeling for the small irregularities that betrayed a copied die, the softness at the edge of a figure, the doubling where a second strike had corrected a first. She found none of it, and she said, carefully and on the record Sarte was keeping, that she could not distinguish those three seals from Carrowmere press work by any test she knew how to apply at a dockside table, that they might be press work itself, seals genuinely issued and genuinely impressed and later placed where she had found them, or they might be copies of an order she had never encountered in her inspector years, and that the distinction mattered enormously for where the chain obtained them but did not alter what she had read behind them. The possibility that the seals were true and the bindings removed to order, which was the second of the three compartments she had carried all morning, stood differently after that admission than it had stood at the tasking, because a seller who could leave a true seal or a copy indistinguishable from truth on a door whose binding had been taken away was not selling the seal at all but selling the result the seal was left to announce, the appearance of a bound door behind which goods could move as unbound goods moved, without whatever constraint or accounting a binding imposed on those who respected it.

It was Sarte herself who put the question to Rask, and Mira was grateful for the directness of it, because a question from the port service could not later be described as an Office evasion. She asked whether a Stiller could do to a door what Mira had described, whether suppression could account for a door that read as nothing in the binding's place, and she asked it standing in the arcade with the joint sheet still open, so that the answer would belong to the same record as the six readings. Rask set his bag down on the arcade stone with care and answered as a man answers when he knows his words will be carried into rooms he will never enter, without hurry and without ornament. He said that his own practice, laid on a door as suppression, would leave a reading that announced its own making to any Weigher who touched the iron afterwards, because suppression as he worked it had a source and a slope, the source being himself standing at the centre of the work while he held it, and the slope being the thinning of that held quiet as distance from him increased, so that a door under his hand would read even and sourced and centred on the place where he had stood, strongest where he had stood closest and fading in a regular gradient across the reveal and the straps and the surrounding stone, and anyone reading afterwards, while the suppression held or in the residue after it lifted, would find that gradient and that centring as plainly as a listener finds the direction of a voice in a fog. He said that suppression also required his presence to sustain it, that it was a held condition and not a removal, that the binding beneath it would still be there under the quiet he laid over it, muffled and evened but present to a patient reading that waited or that touched at the edge where his slope thinned, and that he could no more make a binding simply not be there on three doors across a harbour, persisting after he had left the quays and gone to his dinner, than he could make a bell unrung by standing beside it with his hands over his ears. He said it without defensiveness, as a craftsman describes the limits of his own tools when a court has mistaken one tool for another, and he added only that at a hearing in another season he had given the same account on the record and that he gave it again in substance because the distinction mattered here, that what Mira had described on the three perfect doors had neither source nor slope, no centre and no gradient and no point at which the nothing thinned into something, only a boundary where the binding should have been and was not, while the stone a hand's breadth away read with its ordinary accumulation undisturbed. Sarte wrote while he spoke, not every word but the shape of the answer, source and slope, neither present, and when he finished she read her own note back to him and he confirmed it, and that exchange closed the question of suppression as an account for the empty doors as firmly as dockside evidence could close any question, leaving decay and record failure and removal standing as the explanations that still had to be weighed, and among those, after six doors, decay had grown very thin indeed.

The third possibility waited until they had left the arcade and taken the service launch along the quay to the seller's yard, because Mira wanted it discussed where it could be tested rather than in the abstract, and because a protection racket wearing a forgery costume, if that was what the chain was, would show itself not on doors at all but in the rhythm of visits to the man who sold the costume. She put it to Sarte on the launch with the harbour sliding past in grey and green, that the seals, wrong or perfect, might be worthless either way, tokens sold to frightened traders not for their effect on any door but for the exemption the purchase bought, the understanding that a man who had paid Tobin Rill would not be troubled by inspection difficulties that fell promptly and heavily on men who had not paid, and that under such an arrangement the state of the bindings behind the doors would be incidental, a confusion the racket was happy to leave unsorted because confusion itself discouraged questions. The possibility could not be closed on door evidence and should not be, because a racket lived in ledgers and in the timing of visits and in the fears of small keepers like the man at the Salt Steps who paid for seals he did not need because a man he trusted had told him the inspectors were coming harder that season, and the yard behind Rill's office, if it could be read at all, would speak to exactly that class of evidence, to comings and goings and repetition and fear carried in and out by people who did not stay long. Rask said nothing on the launch beyond confirming the yard's layout from the service plan Sarte carried, a walled yard behind a bonded store with Rill's office in a low building at its far side, a single gate wide enough for a cart, stones underfoot that had been laid when the wall was built and iron in the gate that had never been renewed, and Mira listened to the plan with her eyes on the approaching quay and felt the carried weight of the morning shift in her, not the readings themselves, which had cost little so far beyond attention, but the accumulation of other men's anxieties at their own doors, and beneath that the older and heavier presence she had carried since the Highstile case, eighteen years of another man's fear that had never thinned to the weight she had been promised it would thin to, and the small, sharp recollection of a lie told to Leyden's face about a notebook that even then rested in her coat pocket against her ribs, a register she had undertaken to stop keeping and had not stopped keeping.

The yard, when Sarte's warrant opened it and Reni Vosk took her place at the gate with two constables in sight but not in conversation, proved to be a plain and eloquent place, a rectangle of old stones worn smooth in the cart tracks and rough at the edges where feet seldom went, smelling of tar and damp wool and the iron tang of the gate that stood open against the wall, with Rill's office dark at the far end and its windows shuttered from inside in a manner that suggested habit rather than alarm. Rill himself was not there, which Sarte noted for the record without speculation, and no goods stood in the yard beyond a stack of empty crates and a handcart with a mended wheel, so that the yard offered a reader almost nothing to distract from its own fabric, which was precisely why Mira had asked to read it. The reading came up through her palm in layers that were not images and were never names, first the general accumulation of a working yard across seasons, carters and clerks and the sustained intent of a man conducting a business he intended to continue, and then, distinct within that accumulation as a rhythm is distinct within noise when a listener stops trying to hear anything else, the pattern of the visits themselves, a repetition of arrival and brief presence and departure that had the unmistakable signature of fear carried in and fear carried out, men and perhaps women who came pressed and left pressed in a different key, whose stays were short and whose intent while they stayed was narrow and urgent and entirely concerned with completing a transaction and being elsewhere. She moved from the gate to the yard stones along the cart track where feet had worn the smooth path smoother, and then to the wall beside the office door where waiting visitors would have stood with a shoulder or a hand against stone, and at each anchor the rhythm confirmed itself and sharpened, visits clustered on certain evenings in the story week and thinned on others with the regularity of tides or market days, a count building in her not as a figure announced but as a repetition she could tally the way a musician tallies a returning phrase, until she could say with the care the joint record required that the yard had received visits in that pattern a little more than forty times across the span of a month, a trade's rhythm and not a friendship's, too frequent for casual custom and too regular for panic, the shape of appointments kept. The read gave her no face and no name and no voice, she said, no identity for any visitor and no account of what had been said in the office or agreed at the wall, only the rhythm and the number and the fear that had coloured both, and Sarte wrote the limit down in the same ink as the number, which Mira noted and was grateful for, because a service that recorded limits alongside findings was a service that might yet sign an honest report. The cost arrived as it always arrived, not dramatically but with the completeness of weather changing, a month of other people's fear settling into her as she lifted her hand from the last stone, the pressed and hurried dread of more than forty visits by people who had feared fines and feared inspectors and feared each other, layered over her own steadier burdens so that her breathing for a while matched a rhythm that was not hers and her attention kept snagging on the gate at sounds that meant nothing.

The disposition decision was prepared there in the yard with the gate still held and the office still shuttered, because Mira wanted it made on the ground it concerned and in Sarte's hearing rather than produced later as an Office surprise. She laid out the choice as she saw it for the day of the take, whenever Leyden and the port service set that day, that the joint force could take the office first, one room with one door that could be rushed and held and searched while its occupant was still reaching for his coat, or it could hold the yard and the gate and let the office be watched from that gate, unentered, while every customer and courier the chain sent across that yard on the appointed tide walked into a held space from which the only exit was past officers who already knew the rhythm of the visits and the count to expect. She named the risk in the same paragraph as the recommendation, because a recommendation that hid its risk was a kind of forgery itself, that an office left unentered was an office in which a man left inside could do damage the yard party could not prevent, to paper above all, and that the joint force would be accepting that risk in exchange for the completeness of the gathering, and she asked Sarte to carry the decision to her own superiors in exactly those terms rather than in any softened form. Sarte listened with her back to the office windows and her face to the gate, as if she were already seeing the day itself arranged on the stones, and she asked two questions only, whether the yard could be held without the street knowing it was held, which Vosk, called upon from the gate, answered by describing the sightlines and the shuttered upper windows opposite with a competence that made Sarte nod once, and whether Mira would stand the yard herself in her reduced state after the reading she had just taken, to which Mira answered that her state was depletion and not error, that she knew the yard's rhythm in her own carried fear in a way no briefing could reproduce, and that she would stand it.

## The Doors That Read as Nothing

The port service rooms above the Ninth Pier smelled of brine worked into stone, of lamp oil, of paper kept too long in damp air, and of the iron tang that came off wet rope through the open shutters. Below the windows the tide knocked against the pier piles in a slow count that never quite matched the clock on the wall. Mira stood at the long inspection table with her coat still damp at the shoulders, and she looked at what six doors had left behind them when all the argument was taken away. Six seal trays stood in a row, each with its cord and its card. Beside them lay the reading notes in her own hand and in Rask's, the door cards from the bonded registers, and three letters from three experts who had used the word forgery with the confidence of men who had been shown only the seals that were wrong.

Sarte had arranged the trays herself, in door order, because order was the courtesy she could still offer a file that had begun to embarrass her service. The Salt Steps store door first, its seal crude in the tray, the Accord mark thick where it should have been fine, the hairline absent. The Cannery Row bonded door second, its numbers cut by a hand that had never learned the Carrowmere press. The Lower Ferry customs door third, its mark shallow, its hairline missing as the other two were missing theirs. Then came the other three trays, and the room changed around them without anyone raising a voice. The Ninth Pier store door. The Old Customs yard door. The Grain Arcade bonded door. In each tray the seal lay bright and exact, mark and number and hairline true, the sort of work that passed from hand to hand on these docks without a second glance, because a second glance found nothing to correct.

Mira had read all six doors herself, with Sarte in the room each time, as Leyden had agreed, and with Rask at the threshold saying little and missing less. Behind the first seal, the crude one, the binding had read as bindings read when they were genuine and had been laid to last. Heavy. Deliberate. Sustained. It had cost her the familiar price, a portion of the intent that had made it settling into her own chest and staying there after she stepped back. Behind the second wrong seal it had been the same, and behind the third wrong seal it had been the same again. A bad seal on a sound door was a crime with edges. You could point at it. You could weigh it in a tray and write its faults in a fair hand.

Behind the fourth seal there had been nothing to point at, because the binding was not there to be pointed at, and the room behind that door had gone on reading as rooms read, goods and dust and the ordinary accumulation of use, all of it present and all of it ordinary. Only the binding was missing, exactly where a binding should have stood in the iron and the stone, with a clean edge to the absence that she had felt in her palms like a step that was not there in a stair she knew. The fifth door had given her the same nothing in the same place. The sixth door had given it to her a third time, and by then Sarte had stopped offering the small explanations that inspectors offer when a reading embarrasses a record.

She set the experts' letters beside the trays again and read the strongest of them through. The writer had examined two seized seals, both wrong, both crude, and had concluded that the chain's trade was the manufacture and sale of forged Accord seals to men who wished their stores to look bonded when they were not. It was a reasonable reading. It had governed the whole file with the force that reasonable readings gather when every paper in the file has been written to fit them. She thought of the register folded in her coat, of other rooms that had read as nothing, and she kept that thought where it belonged, as context and not as proof. [On the north cut a lock chamber had held an absence with a ceiling, a volume standing in the air where a device had been placed](/2026/11/06/nightjar-charge-that-failed-006/). Here the absence sat in a door where a binding had been entered in a register by a named licence, and the difference between the two was the sort of difference she would have to be able to say aloud, in front of Sarte, without reaching for any case Sarte had not seen.

The forgery reading asked the table to hold together, and the table would not. A forger copies the seal and leaves the binding, because the binding is not his trade and never has been. His skill, if skill was the word for the crude work in the first three trays, ended at the surface of the iron. He sold appearance, the look of a bonded door to men who feared inspection more than they feared theft. On the doors that mattered most in this file the order of things had turned itself around. The seal was the one true thing on them. The binding was the thing that was gone. Mira moved the first tray and the fourth tray until they stood side by side, crude mark against perfect mark, and she felt the whole shape of the case shift under her hands, away from an old certainty that had stopped deserving its place at the head of the file. Forgery could explain the left hand tray. It could explain three seized seals, three experts, a dozen frightened storekeepers. It could not explain why the doors carrying its handiwork still read heavy and intact behind the iron, while the doors carrying perfect seals read as nothing at all. A reading that explained only the half of the door table that flattered it was not a reading any longer. It was a habit, and the habit died there on the inspection table between two trays, while the tide went on knocking below the pier and Sarte watched her move nothing else.

What survived the death of that reading had to be built out of paper first, because paper was what Sarte trusted and what a court on these docks would ask for. The three empty doors might still have been doors that were never bound at all, their registers falsified or their bindings allowed to lapse in some season when the port service had been short of Binders and shorter of clerks. Mira had held that possibility seriously, because a Weigher who cannot tell a removed binding from one that never existed has no business putting the word removal into another service's report. So the records were sent for, and they were checked twice, by two roads, so that neither road could be said to have borrowed its answer from the other.

Dunmore did the Carrowmere side of it by telephone and by paper, in the manner of a man who trusted the second more than the first and used the first only to say what the second would contain when it came. His voice came down the line thin and flattened, with the registry's own sounds behind it, drawers and bells and a door that shut too often. He read the licence entries without ornament. Each of the doors had been bound under a licence that was current when the work was done. Each entry carried the door by its place and its iron number, the fee paid, the press that had struck its seal, and the inspection that had followed the laying. The paper copies followed in a registry sleeve, and when Sarte broke the sleeve at the inspection table the copies said what Dunmore's voice had said, in registry hands that changed from entry to entry. The Binder named in the entries was licensed on these docks and had worked them for years. The three surviving bindings, on the Salt Steps door, the Cannery Row door and the Lower Ferry door, carried that licensed hand in their registers, and when Mira set the reading notes beside the names she found the agreement she had been cautious about wanting. The careful, sustained intent she had read behind those doors belonged to a practice the registry could name. She could not give that hand a face from the reading, but between the two records the surviving bindings stopped being anonymous in the one way that mattered for the file. They had been laid by a licensed Binder, lawfully, and they still read. The three empty doors stood in the same registers, bound by the same lawful process, and inspected afterward.

Sarte did the Valtessan side of the checking herself, with the local counterfoils and the port copies of the inspection returns. For each of the three empty doors the local record matched the Carrowmere record in fee, in door, in licence, and in the inspection that had followed. In one case the inspector who had attended the laying had written a note about a warped frame and a second fitting, the sort of useless, particular detail that no one invents afterward to support a case that has not yet been imagined. A binding that had never been laid does not gather that kind of litter around itself in two registries at once. A binding allowed to lapse does not vanish from iron and stone in this fashion either, and Mira said so carefully, in the language of her own practice, while Rask stood by the shuttered window and listened. Weight fades, she said. Days clear it, months make it faint, years leave almost nothing of an ordinary deposit. But a binding is not an ordinary deposit. It is sustained work, laid to persist, and where such work decays it decays unevenly, with residue at the anchors. On the three perfect doors there was no residue at the anchors. There was no thinning, no ragged edge of a thing wearing out. There was a clean absence bounded by the place where the binding should have been, and the goods behind those doors read with their ordinary weight undisturbed, which no lapse she knew how to describe had ever arranged so neatly.

Rask spoke then, because the next possibility was his to kill and everyone in the room knew it. He had given the comparison before, on the record, in another place, and he gave it again in substance, his voice level and his distaste for the whole question plain. His own suppression, he said, had a source and a slope. It centred on the Stiller who held it and it thinned with distance from that centre, evenly, the way lamplight thins, and any reader coming on a door under his hand would feel the evenness of it and would feel him in it, because suppression is work and work leaves the shape of its working. He could stand in a doorway and quieten what a door held. He could not reach into a door he had never stood at and lift a binding out of it while leaving the room behind the door reading as it had always read. And suppression, while it lasted, pressed on what was there. It did not delete the thing pressed. When he stopped, the weight came back as a tide comes back, because it had never gone anywhere. The three doors had no source anywhere near them and no slope across them. Their absence did not thin toward the edges of the rooms behind them. It ended where the binding ended, as a cut ends. Mira wrote his words into the notes in a deliberately plain hand. A Stiller's hand, as the Office understood that hand on the record, did not account for what had happened on those three doors.

The perfect seals went back to examination last, because they were the part of the table that everyone had been happiest to leave unexamined while the crude seals were available to blame. Sarte recalled her own examiner, a grey man with ink ground so far into his fingers that it looked like a second skin, and he laid the three perfect seals under the glass beside a genuine struck seal from the reference drawer and worked over them in silence for the better part of an hour. The mark was true. The number was true, cut at the press depth and not at the depth a hand tool reaches when it is copying what it can see. The hairline was there, running where the flaw in the worn Carrowmere die ran and nowhere else. At the end of the hour the examiner sat back and gave his answer without choosing between its two halves. Either those seals were Carrowmere press work, he said, or they were copies of a quality that had no business existing in a trade whose other products were the crude trays at the far end of the table. A chain that could make the second could have made all its seals to that standard and charged accordingly. A chain that possessed the first had no need of the crude work at all, except as something to be seized, examined, written up, and believed in. Mira looked at the two ends of the table and felt the forgery trade settle into its proper size. It paid the rent. It kept frightened men coming to a yard office with money counted twice in their pockets. It gave the port service a crime it could hold. What it did not do, any longer, was explain the doors.

In the office behind his bonded yard, where the light came through one salt filmed window and the yard sounds arrived muffled by bales and by the store wall, Tobin Rill received a man who had come to him with a difficulty that had no lawful name. The office smelled of twine and lampblack and the faint sweetness of the tea Rill kept warm and rarely drank. Outside, his yardmen were shifting crates under a foreman's unhurried calls. Inside, there was a desk, a chair for a visitor that sat lower than Rill's own, and paper in piles whose order he never explained. The visitor did the explaining, because visitors always did, the inspections, the new keenness at the piers, the cousin whose store had been fined into the ground the season before. Rill listened without impatience and without encouragement, and when the money came out he watched the visitor count it, and then he watched him count it again at a glance from the desk, because men who count twice understand each other.

What the visitor wanted, when the circling stopped, was not a seal. Men came to the yard for seals often enough, and Rill sold seals the way other men sold rope, to the careless and to the hopeful. This man wanted a result on a door that already stood bound and inspected, a door with papers in order and a binding on it that complicated certain plans he did not name. He asked, once, how such a result was managed, in the lowered voice men use when they believe the method is the valuable part. Rill let the question sit on the desk between them until it lost its importance. He did not know the method. He said so without emphasis, because it had the advantage of being accurate, and because a seller who claims knowledge he does not hold becomes a witness waiting for his season. He arranged. That was his trade, the true one, underneath the trade in seals that furnished the office and explained the yard. He took a difficulty, he took a fee proportioned to the difficulty, he passed a portion of both along a chain whose farther links he did not inspect, and in due course a door that had read as bound doors read was reported back to him as a door that troubled no one any longer. The men beyond him never met the men who came to him. He never stood at the doors himself. He kept no paper that described anything, because paper was how arrangements became evidence.

The visitor worried, near the end, about discovery, and it was there that Rill's patience showed its quality, because he did not reassure the man with inventions. He rested instead on the one piece of ground he had built his arrangements upon for years, ground that had never shifted under him. If officers came to that door afterward, he said, they would find a seal on it, and the seal would bear every scrutiny their examiners could bring, and then the port service would do what the port service always did with a thing it could hold in its hand. It would call the matter forgery. Forgery was a crime. Forgery had forms, and culprits, and a court at the end of it, and a fine or a cell to show for the service's vigilance. What the service would not do was write its name to a report that travelled upward to the Accord saying that a binding laid under its licence, inspected under its stamp, and guarded under its seals was not there any longer, because a report of that kind did not end in a court. It ended in questions arriving from above about every other door the service had ever certified, and men who live by certificates do not volunteer for such questions. The visitor heard him, and was quieted, not by trust, but by recognition, because he too kept books that other men signed. He left by the yard gate with nothing in his hands, which was how Rill preferred a true customer to leave. Rill stayed at the desk a while after, listening to the foreman's calls resume their rhythm, and he turned no page and counted nothing.

Back in the rooms above the Ninth Pier, with Rill's yard no more than an address in a file, Mira found that the proposition he traded on was waiting for her already written into Sarte's instructions, and neither woman needed Rill in the room to prove him right about it. Sarte had the draft report on the inspection table, with two versions of its central page squared beside each other. The first version said that seals had been forged and affixed in the course of a criminal trade, that three doors had been found carrying false seals, and that three further doors required continued inquiry, in the established language of the port service, language Sarte could sign before the evening watch changed and send onward with her conscience and her career equally undisturbed. The second version said, in language Mira had drafted at Sarte's own request and then watched her read three times without touching it, that three doors certified by the service carried bindings that were absent, that the absence was confined to the bindings and to nothing else in the rooms behind the doors, and that no suppression, decay, or failure of record known to either service accounted for it. The second version was the accurate one. It was also a document that would leave Valtessa under the port service's seal and arrive in rooms where the Accord kept its own view of Valtessan competence, carrying Sarte's signature at the foot of the admission.

Sarte did not refuse. Refusal would have been a simpler thing to answer, and Sarte was honest inside her instructions in a way Mira had come to rely on, even when honesty and comfort pulled against each other. She set her hand flat on the first version and she set nothing on the second, and she said, in her precise voice, exactly what her service would sign and exactly where its signature stopped. The service would sign the seals, the trays, the examiner's findings, the crude work and the perfect work alike, because seals were objects and objects could be sworn to. It would sign that its officers had attended each reading, and it would sign the door cards and the registry extracts that Dunmore and her own clerks had produced. What it would not sign, on her authority and on the authority she answered to, was the conclusion that a binding was absent, because that conclusion rested on one woman's reading, however witnessed, and it carried the service's name upward into rooms she could not see the inside of. Give her a Binder who would swear the work was gone. Give her an instrument, a second reader, a residue a clerk could weigh. Without it, the word absent would go into the joint report, if it went at all, as the Office's finding and not as the port service's, and everyone above them both would understand the distance that phrasing kept.

Leyden, when Mira carried the impasse to him by the evening telephone, heard it in the silence he reserved for costs he had named in advance. He had told her at the tasking that she would work watched, and that anything her practice found would be said aloud in front of Juno Sarte. He asked whether the unsigned page changed what the Office would do on the day of the take, and she told him it did not, and he asked whether it changed what the Office could prove afterward, and she told him it might. The report question would stay open, then, while the take was prepared, and Mira understood that a finding the port service would not sign was a finding the chain had counted on not existing, in ink, anywhere that mattered. Whoever stood behind Rill had sold more than a result on a door. They had sold the near certainty that the result, once noticed, would be renamed forgery by the very service charged with noticing it, until no single page accused anyone of anything worse than incompetence with paperwork.

That left the take, and the take left the question Mira had been carrying since the disposition was settled over the yard plan in chalk and cord measurements. The yard was where the chain could be taken whole. Its customers crossed the yard. Its couriers crossed the yard. Every arrangement Rill made sent its human traffic across those stones and through that gate, and the rhythm of that traffic was in her already, a month of visits counted by repetition in the gate iron, the shape of a trade with no face and no name attached to any single crossing. Hold the yard and the net had a mouth. Take the office instead and the Office would hold one room and one seller who never raised his voice, while the yard emptied around them by every gate and lane the docks could offer. The disposition was right, and she made the argument for it again to Sarte and to Rask with the report lying unsigned between the seal trays. But a right disposition still spends its strength somewhere and leaves its weakness somewhere else, and the weakness of this one had a door and a room behind the yard wall. Rill's office would stand unentered while the yard was held. Its paper would stand with it, the book that numbers had been kept in, whatever else a careful arranger kept where he sat. If the chain read the day for what it was, and if a man were left inside that office with instructions older than the morning, the office could be fired from within while every officer's hands and eyes were committed to the yard, and the distance from the yard gate to that door, across stones she had walked and read, was a distance that smoke would measure differently from chalk. She walked it at dusk with Rask, counting nothing aloud. They could watch the door. They could not be at the door and at the gate with the same bodies, and no redeployment either woman proposed on that walk closed the gap without opening the yard. The question stayed open beside the report question, two honest uncertainties ranked above a dead certainty. Some risks are chosen because every other road loses more.

What the collapse left standing, when the trays were covered for the night, was narrower than the file she had arrived with and harder in every line of it. A licensed Binder had laid the bindings that still read, and records in two registries said so. The bindings that did not read had been laid as lawfully, inspected as carefully, and sealed more perfectly than any door in the file. No Stiller's suppression accounted for them, no lapse of record explained them, no decay known to her practice had the shape of their absence. A seller in a yard office arranged a result he did not perform and did not claim to understand, the service whose doors they were would sign the seals in a report and balk at signing the absence, and a yard would be held on the day of the take while an office full of paper waited unwatched behind its wall. The forgery trade was real, and it stood where it had always stood, in the crude trays, in the frightened counting of money, in the experts' letters. It had only stopped being the case. Behind it, something else had been selling the one result the forgery reading could not survive, and the three doors with perfect seals stood in their registry extracts and their reading notes as the plain shape of that result, heavy with nothing where their bindings should have been.

## Hold the Yard
The yard behind the bonded store held the weather of the docks in a close space. Salt worked into the stones underfoot and into the iron of the gate, and the air smelled of rope tar and wet canvas and the faint sour breath of stored grain from the next yard over the wall. Crates stood in ordered ranks along the eastern side, their lids chalked and rechalked, and bales wrapped in oilcloth sweated in the thin sun. At the far end the office sat low and plain, one door, one shuttered window, a roof line that cut the sky into a narrow strip above the yard wall. Mira stood where she could see the gate and the office door in the same turn of her head, and she felt the yard settle into the shape the disposition required. Sarte had placed her people along the wall and at the corners where a man might think a shadow was a way out, and she walked the line once with her hands empty and her voice low, giving each position its limit and its report. Reni held the gate. It was the position she had held elsewhere and she held it the same way here, to one side of the iron where she could see the lane beyond and the yard within, counting in a murmur that carried only as far as it needed to carry.

Mira carried the yard read with her into the holding, and it did not lighten as the morning wore toward the take. A month of other people fear had settled in her during the reading, fear carried in by visitors who had not stayed long and carried out again in a rhythm the stones had kept, and a portion of it had stayed with her as the price of knowing the count. It sat behind her breathing and made ordinary waiting cost more than waiting should cost. Her attention kept thinning toward the gate and toward memories of the counted visits, and she pulled it back by naming what was in front of her. Stone. Iron. Chalk. Sarte at the corner. Reni at the gate. The office door shut. She entered the hold reduced and she knew it. She did not pace. She let Sarte walk the line and Reni do the counting at the mouth of the yard, and kept her own strength for the one moment the disposition could not predict.

The disposition held because it had been chosen for the ground. The yard was where the chain had to cross. Customers came to the yard. Couriers came to the yard. A man with business that could not be written in a port book still had to pass the gate, cross the stones, and be seen by the wall and the corners before he reached any door that mattered. The office was one room, and one room could be watched from the gate side of the yard. To take the office early would have spent officers in a narrow space and left the yard open at three places, and the trade would have flowed around them like water around a post. Mira had argued for the yard and Sarte had agreed in front of her own people, and that agreement was part of the hold as well.

Reni reported in a steady cadence, and each report closed a small question before it could grow. A carter with an empty handcart was stopped inside the line and stayed there, hands on his shafts. Two clerks for a grain factor were counted and held at the tally point, their ledger tube between them on the stones. A courier with a packet in oilcloth under his arm reached the gate, saw Sarte at the corner, and tried to turn in the lane, and Reni spoke his description and his direction in one level sentence and a service man was in the lane before the courier had taken six steps. He was brought back inside the line and set with the carter, the packet still under his arm, unopened. No one had left the yard since the hold began. No one had passed Reni outward. A broad man in a coat too warm for the day asked twice whether his goods would be released before the tide and was told twice by Sarte, in exactly the same words, that nothing would be released until the yard was cleared by her order. He subsided against a crate and stayed where he could be seen.

The waiting sharpened rather than dulled. Mira watched the office because watching it was the cost of leaving it unentered, and she made the watching systematic so fatigue could not make it vague. Door. Shutter. Roof line. The narrow space between the bales and the office wall, where a stack of empty sacks made a poor screen and nothing more. Door again. The door stayed shut. No key turned in it. No face showed at the shutter seam. Sarte stopped beside her for the space of one report from the far corner, and the two of them looked at the office together. Sarte said her people had the yard. Mira said the office was still quiet. Sarte nodded once and went on, and the exchange left the decision exactly where it had been made.

The smell reached Mira before any sight did. It was wrong inside the yard smells, a thin heat under the tar and salt, paper and lamp oil and dry wood taking warmth. She stopped in the middle of a breath. The shutter seam showed nothing. The roof line showed nothing. Then a thread of smoke found the edge of the office door at the top, pale against the dark wood, and thickened while she watched it. She called it aloud, clearly, in words the whole line could act on without repetition. Fire in the office. Sarte answered from the corner with orders of her own, short and placed, her people staying on the yard line, the gate staying held, no rush toward the smoke that would open the wall. Reni repeated the gate report over the rising noise, gate held, lane clear outward, no one passed, and the report steadied the yard behind Mira even as Mira left her position and ran.

She ran across the yard stones and the yard read rose to meet her with every step. The fear in the stones was not hers and it behaved like hers, tightening her chest, urging her toward haste that would spend her badly. She kept her pace at the pace she could finish. Crates passed. Bales passed. The carter shouted a question no one answered and the broad man in the warm coat stood up from his crate and was ordered down again by a voice from the wall. The stones were uneven where carts had worn them and damp where a butt had leaked, and she placed her feet instead of trusting them. Smoke reached her halfway across, low and acrid, making her eyes water and her throat close, and the office door grew in front of her, iron hinges and a handle blackened by hands. Heat came through it in a dull pressure against her face. Behind the wood something shifted and fell with a soft collapse that sent a brighter thread of smoke out at the seam.

The door was not locked against her. It gave inward a hand span and stopped against fallen material, and smoke rolled out over the sill and around her boots. Inside, the room was dim with it. Flame showed at the far side where papers had been heaped to burn, orange low and busy, eating upward through stacked sheets, and the heat inside the doorway was a wall she could lean against and feel push back. On a low shelf just inside the door sat a heavy book bound for yard use, its cover dark, its edge already curling dark where heat had taken the outer leaves on one side. The seller's book. It had not burned through. It had not been placed in the heart of the fire, and heavier than loose sheets and set apart from the heap, it was burning slower than the purpose required. Mira marked its position and stepped back from the threshold to clear the smoke from her eyes, and in that step she heard a boot strike stone behind her, too fast and too close to belong to the line.

He came from the screen of empty sacks along the office wall, a lean man in work clothes with his sleeves pushed up, his face already streaked from the office air, and he came for the door with his shoulder low and his eyes fixed past Mira on the shelf inside. He had fired the office. The knowledge arrived as shape rather than accusation. He knew where the book sat. He knew the fire had not finished it. He had to get past her once, only once, and take the book deeper into the flame or out of reach, and the yard full of officers would matter nothing if he managed those steps. Mira set herself in the doorway. Her body took the frame. Her feet found the sill and the stone. Reduced as she was, she had no spare strength to trade in a long struggle, so she made the struggle short in her mind before it began. Door. Frame. Shelf behind her. Sarte coming. Hold until Sarte arrived. Nothing else counted.

He hit the threshold at speed. She met him off centre. His shoulder struck her forearm. The frame took her back. Breath left her in a grunt. She turned his weight aside. Not through. He recovered fast. He drove again. His hand caught at her coat. Cloth tore at a seam. She seized his wrist. She pulled it across her body. His balance went with it. He kicked at the sill. Stone rang. Smoke poured between them. Her grip slipped on sweat and heat. She reset it lower. She set her hip. She used the frame as a second body. He tried to wrench free. He tried to duck under her arm toward the shelf. She blocked with her knee. She blocked with her shoulder. The book sat behind her. The fire breathed behind her. Heat pressed her back. Her chest burned for air. The fear from the yard stones screamed to run. She stayed. Short. Plain. Door held.

Sarte arrived without wasted motion and took the other side of the man. She caught his free arm high and turned it into its own limit, speaking to him once, close and even, telling him he was held and that further effort would only be counted against him. Mira shifted her grip to a control hold that kept his taken arm folded safely against his own body, pressure steady, the kind of hold that removed choices without breaking anything. Together they brought him down from the threshold to the stones, his knees finding the yard, his weight guided rather than dropped, and they held him until a service man came up with a cord and bound his wrists in front of him under Sarte's direction. He struggled once against the cord, testing it, and then stopped. No weapon had shown. Nobody had been killed in the yard or at the door, and the man sat breathing hard in the smoke drift, held, his eyes going once to the office and then away.

Mira turned back to the doorway with the smoke thicker and the flame inside brighter along the heap of papers, and she knew the interval left to her was measured in moments rather than minutes. The book had begun to take fire along one edge in earnest, outer leaves blackening and lifting, and if it stayed on the shelf while the desk heap beside it collapsed inward it would be finished where it sat. She reached in from the threshold without entering the room whole, one foot on the sill, her body kept to the cooler side of the frame, and got her fingers under the book cover. The frame edge was hot through the wood where the fire had worked along it, and the book was heavier than its size promised, dense with entered pages. Her first grip failed her. The hand was late answering when she told it to close, and the book tipped toward the shelf edge and had to be caught with a clumsy second effort that cost her the clean hold she had planned. She changed hands without letting the book fall, bringing her other hand under it and pinning the bulk against her forearm, and drew it out past the door frame in one committed pull that brought a wash of heat across the side of her face and left her blinking in the yard air. The book came clear. Half of it was burned along one side, leaves fused and black at the margin, and half of it remained whole and legible in weight and shape, the unburned pages still flat under the cover. She held it against her body with the hand that answered properly and felt the other hand lag when she tried to adjust the load, fingers slow to take the order she gave them, grip wrong on the cover edge until she shifted the book again and settled it under her arm instead. The cost was plain in use and she paid it without pausing to examine it, because the office behind her was still burning and the yard still had to be held.

Sarte took charge of the office fire with buckets from the yard butt and service men working in a chain that did not break the wall line, water thrown from the threshold, and the flame on the paper heap sank to a sullen glow and then to wet ash and steam. Mira stayed near the door with the book until Sarte confirmed the room held no second person. The search was made from the doorway with a lamp and a careful entry by one service man once the air had cleared enough to breathe, and it returned empty. The man who had fired the office sat under guard by the bales, bound and silent. He had come back for the book. He had failed to reach it. Both facts stood on the yard stones in plain view, needing no practice to read them.

Reni reported again from the gate, her voice steady under discipline, carrying across the settling noise with the count the disposition had been built to produce. Gate held throughout. No customer out. No courier out. The carter, the two clerks, the courier with the oilcloth packet, the broad man in the warm coat, all present inside the line and accounted for by description. One man had passed her inward before the hold began and had not passed her outward, and she gave his description, and it matched the man bound by the bales in every particular she could supply from the gate distance. Sarte received the report without interrupting and entered it in her own book with the time by the yard clock. The hold had kept every customer and courier inside the line.

Mira carried the book to the tally point herself because handing it over required a grip she could not trust to the lagging hand, and she set it down with the sound hand guiding and the other hand arriving a fraction late to steady it, so that the book settled crooked on the tally stones and had to be squared by Sarte without comment. Sarte looked at the burned half and the whole half and said, for the record of the yard, that the seller's book had been recovered from the fired office by the Office officer in the presence of the port service, half burned, and that the burned half would be entered as burned and not reconstructed by guess. Mira agreed in the same formal tone. The decision that had left the office unentered had cost the book its burned half and Mira the clean use of one hand for the work still ahead, and it had bought the yard entire, every crossing counted, every customer present. She stood at the tally point with the fear of a month of strangers still working in her chest and the book safe on the stones in front of her, and waited for Sarte's order to move to the next stage of the take, her sound hand flat on the tally stone, her other hand beside it and closing, when she asked it to close, a fraction behind the intention.

## A Hand With No Face
The outer room of the yard office smelled of wet ash and lamp oil, and beyond its open door the yard had gone quiet, buckets still, voices lowered, the tally stones damp where water had slopped from the chain of hands. Mira sat at the yard table and let the stillness do its work before she spoke. Her sound hand lay flat on the table wood. Her other hand lay beside it, slower to obey, and she rested it without asking it for anything. The fear from the yard read was still in her, a month of strangers passing through stone and iron, and she let it settle like silt in water, present and no longer clouding the whole.

She held the case in the order the doors had taught it. Three doors carried wrong seals, crude in mark, cut by an unpractised hand, missing the hairline, and behind each of those doors a binding read heavy and deliberate and intact. Three other doors carried perfect seals, mark and number and hairline true, and behind each of those doors there was no binding at all to read. Not a damaged binding. Not a faded one. Nothing where a binding should stand, while the rooms behind those doors read as rooms read, goods and use and ordinary passage all present in their weight, untouched and ordinary. The absence kept to the binding alone, exactly at the place a binding should be and nowhere else in the room beyond.

Rask had given the comparison again in substance during the work, and she held his words beside the doors. His own suppression, a Stiller suppression, had a source and a slope. It centred on the Stiller. It thinned with distance. It could be felt approaching its centre and easing as a reader moved away, even and sourced and owned by the person who laid it. These doors had neither source nor slope. There was no centre to approach. There was no thinning to measure. The binding was not suppressed. It was absent. She turned that distinction over without dressing it in any larger claim, because the evidence would carry that claim and no larger one.

On the doors that read she had a careful hand and no face. A Binder sustained intent, deliberate over time, told her a hand had worked with care and told her nothing of who it belonged to, and no means the Office held could give that hand a face from weight alone. On the three doors that did not read she had less. Not even a hand. She considered whether she would write the distinction into a report the port service must sign, a hand with no face on one side of the table and not even a hand on the other, knowing Sarte service would have to set its name to words its own experts had not supplied. She considered the register in her coat, the notebook she had agreed to stop keeping, and the three doors waiting for a place in it beside four earlier rooms, and did not decide that question aloud at the table. She considered the sting, built on the yard count and the door table and a Weigher reading no court on these docks had ever taken alone, and whether it could spring on such a reading held up by procedure and witnessed presence at every door. She held all of it assembled whole, and only then lifted her head and looked at Sarte and Rask in turn, ready to say what the doors had said to her.

## The Sting Springs

The yard behind the bonded store smelled of wet stone and rope oil and the iron tang of the gate chain where the tide air had worked on it through the night. Gulls settled and lifted again from the low roof. The stones underfoot held the cold through boot leather. A lantern burned at the gate post though the light had come up enough to read by, and its flame showed in the puddles between the cart ruts. Mira stood where she could see the gate, the yard table, and the office door without turning her head, and she kept her burned hand clear of her coat edge where the cloth kept catching it.

Sarte had set the disposition before the tide turned and she held to it without ornament. The port service held the yard gate and the lane gate. The Office held the table and the count. Reni Vosk stood at the lane gate with a clear view down to the water stairs, as she had held a gate at the depot in another case, and she was to let no cart and no person pass without a name spoken aloud and answered. Rask stood near the store wall where his presence was declared and visible, so that no one could afterward say a suppression had been worked in secret. Mira had asked for procedure over speed and Sarte had agreed in one sentence. Doors held. Names read. Hands shown. No one taken by a grab or a shout.

The first store reported by runner as the yard gate closed. The Salt Steps store had been entered on a port service order at the same tide mark, its keeper present, its doors held open for the reading of the order. Two men who kept the store books for the chain were taken there without resistance and seated to wait for the wagon. The second runner came from the Grain Arcade end as the yard count began. Two more were taken there, a clerk and a courier who had arrived with a sealed packet and found the door held against him. The sting had sprung in three places on one tide, as it had been set to do.

In the yard itself the taking was quieter still. Sarte read the order in a level voice that carried to the store wall and no farther than it needed to. She named the yard, the store behind it, and the authority under which the port service acted with the Office present. She named the persons to be taken as their names were confirmed at the table. Each man stepped forward when his name was read, showed his hands empty at her direction, and was directed to the bench by the wall where a port service officer sat with the count book open. No one ran. No one raised a voice against the reading. The fifth man looked once toward the office door and then sat down again at a word from Sarte, and the look was recorded as a look and nothing more.

Tobin Rill came out of the office outer room last, at his own pace, with his coat buttoned. He had watched other people count for years and he did not count himself. He stood at the yard table while Sarte read his name and the ground of his taking, which was the conduct of a trade in seals and in the removal of bindings from bonded doors under Accord record. He did not deny the trade in seals. When the wording reached the removal of bindings he gave a small shake of the head, not as denial but as a correction he considered important, and he said that he arranged results for men who asked and asked no method of those who provided them. Sarte had the words written down exactly as spoken. Rill showed his hands at her direction, set them flat on the table edge for the count, and took his place on the bench. He was the sixth taken in the yard.

With the two stores the count stood at nine. Sarte read the count aloud from the book and Mira confirmed it from her own tally, and the two tallies agreed. Nine taken, Rill among them, no person unaccounted for against the yard count and the store lists combined. Reni reported from the lane gate that no cart had passed and no person had left by the water stairs during the taking. Rask confirmed that he had worked no suppression at any point in the yard and that his declaration stood open on the record for any later questioning. The yard was then closed. The port service set its own seal on the yard gate and on the store door, in the ordinary manner, with the seal numbers read aloud and entered in the count book, and the lane gate was chained and sealed in the same way. From that moment the yard was a sealed place under port service keeping, to be opened only on a written order.

The seller's book came to the yard table in a port service evidence cloth, carried by an officer who had taken it from the fired office and kept it apart since. It was a heavy yard book, bound for hard use, and half of it was burned. The burned half was named as burned at the table before anything else was done with it, and Sarte entered that fact in the count book in plain words. What fire had taken was not guessed at and not reconstructed. The unburned half remained, its leaves stiff and smoke darkened at the edges, its ruled lines still visible under a film of soot that no hand at the table attempted to clean.

Mira took the book with her unburned hand first and found the grip wrong at once. The weight of it pulled in the burned fingers when she tried to steady the cover, and the hand was late answering when she asked it to hold. She changed hands and set the book flat on the table and read it there, with the leaves held by a plain stone weight from the yard wall so that she need not grip at all. That month of other people's fear from the yard read still sat in her and made a crowded room of any close work, and she set it aside by attention rather than by strength. She opened the unburned half and laid her palm light along the margin where the entries ran.

Paper holds weight worst of all the common materials and the fire had thinned what little the leaves could keep. She said so at the table before she said anything else, so that the faintness would be on the record beside whatever followed. What remained came in patches, stronger where a hand had returned to the same page across many entries, weaker where a leaf had been turned once and left. In the unburned half she found the hands of two people and no more than that. One hand was careful over a long span, its entries carrying sustained intent laid down entry after entry, the pressure even, the pauses regular, the work of a person who expected the book to be read later and meant it to hold. The other hand was hurried. Its weight came in short, crowded runs, intent pressed hard and lifted fast, with long gaps where the careful hand alone had carried the book. The pattern of two hands was clear. Nothing in the leaves gave her a face for either hand, or a name. Weight does not give identity and the book gave none. She lifted her palm and reported the pattern exactly as she had found it, and she named the limit in the same breath.

Sarte wrote the report of the read as Mira spoke it, then read it back without softening the limit. Rill listened with his hands flat on his knees and said nothing about the book at all, which Sarte also recorded. The book was then entered as evidence with its burned half named as burned and its unburned half named as read, and it was wrapped again and sealed with the yard seals.

The joint report was made at the yard table before the wagon came down. It was a plain document in two hands, the port service hand and the Office hand, and it had to carry findings the port service had never signed before in Valtessa. Sarte read each section aloud as it was completed. The door table was set out in full, six doors with their seal condition and their read result in the fixed words used since the doors were first opened. The yard count was set out, and the yard hold disposition with its consequence for the office, stated plainly and without excuse. The fire was entered as a fact with its cost, and the recovery of the book after it.

When the report reached the findings on the doors, Mira wrote the distinction herself, in her own section, and asked that it stand in the joint text rather than in an Office appendix. The notebook was in her coat as she wrote and she did not mention it in the report. What she wrote for the report was narrower and harder than any private line. On the doors that read, she wrote, a Weigher finds a careful hand with no face, the sustained intent of a Binder's work held in stone and iron, heavy and deliberate, and no means in the Office or in the port service gives that hand a name. She looked once at the sealed store door across the yard, where her own service seal sat bright and ordinary, and then she signed below the distinction without altering a word. Mira signed after her for the Office. The joint report was signed by both services and the distinction stood in it as a joint finding.

On the strength of that report and of the yard count beneath it, the case question that had opened the file in Valtessa received its answer in the same sitting, and Sarte required that the answer be entered as a finding rather than left as talk at a table. The chain had not lived by its forgery trade. The crude seals, the shallow marks, the numbers cut by an unpractised hand, all of that was real and all of it was the shopfront, the visible half that paid the rent and satisfied any inquiry that stopped at seals. Behind the shopfront the chain had been selling the removal of real bindings from bonded doors, arranged to order for men who needed a door that looked bound and was not, and the perfect seals on the empty doors were the receipt left in place after the work. Rill, invited to correct the finding as he had corrected the wording at his taking, said only that men had paid well for doors that gave them no trouble, and he let the sentence stand as his whole answer. Before the wagon arrived, the three empty doors were photographed for the file, as the report required. The exposures were made in sequence on the same tide, and the photographer's list was checked against the door table line by line afterward. Nothing in the photographs could show an absence, and Sarte said so for the record, and Mira agreed. The wagon came down the lane as the light began to thin off the water. The nine were taken up in count order, Rill last, each name read again at the wagon door and answered. Reni held the lane gate until the wagon had passed. Mira stood at the yard table with the joint report under its cover, her burned hand resting open on the table edge because a closed grip still came late and wrong, and she watched the sealed gate settle into its frame. The yard was empty and sealed and quiet, its trade stopped mid rhythm. The fear she carried from its stones had not lessened for the success of the day. It would go with her into the next file, as costs do, unpaid by any signature.

## Three Photographs

The room Sarte lent her for the file work was a small port service room above the bonded stores, with a wide sill, a smell of lamp oil and damp paper, and a window that looked down on sealed doors and quiet water. Rain had left the glass spotted. Below, gulls walked the coping stones as if they owned them. On the table lay the joint report under its cover, the photographer's list, and three photographs still separate in their paper sleeve. Mira worked with her unburned hand for the cutting and used the edge of the table to steady each sheet, because the burned fingers still answered late when she asked them to pinch or hold.

Everything tactical in the case had closed. She went through it once, in order. The sting had sprung at the yard and at the two stores on the same tide. Nine had been taken, Rill among them, and the yard stood closed and sealed under port service keeping. The seller's book had been recovered and entered with its burned half named as burned, and its unburned half had given a pattern of two hands and no identity. The joint report carried the distinction between a hand with no face and not even a hand, and both services had signed below it. The photographs of the three doors had been taken for the file and checked against the door table. Each of those matters was finished. She let each one settle as closed before she opened the sleeve.

What remained was not tactical at all. It was the notch, the single new learning of the whole case, and she turned it over with the care she had not been able to give it at the yard table. The absence appears where a binding should be. That was the whole of it and it was enough to change how every earlier line in her private thinking had to be held. On the three empty doors the seals were perfect, mark, number and hairline true, and behind those seals there was no binding to read. The absence did not spread. It did not take the rooms. She had read the rooms behind the three doors herself and they read as rooms read, with the ordinary weight of use in their stones and ironwork, goods handled and set down, doors opened and shut across seasons, the small repeated pressures of work. Only the bindings were not there. The absence concealed bindings specifically and nothing else. It sat exactly in the shape a binding should have occupied, with a sharp edge where the binding ended and the room began, and it left everything else untouched and legible.

That precision separated it from every other empty reading she had carried into Valtessa. A Stiller's suppression, as Rask had put on the record again in this case, has a source and a slope, a centre and a thinning with distance. These doors had neither source nor slope. Decay thins and leaves a residue a reader can follow down. These doors left no residue to follow. Work laid by licensed Binders under port service record, heavy and deliberate work that stone and iron should have held for years, was absent in its own place only, while the lesser weights around it survived entire. She did not know how such a thing was done. No one in the case knew. Rill arranged a result and claimed no method. The notch stood without an explanation behind it, and she left it that way, a shape recorded honestly rather than a gap filled for comfort.

She drew the three photographs from the sleeve and laid them side by side on the table. The Ninth Pier store door. The Old Customs yard door. The Grain Arcade bonded door. In the photographs the seals sat bright and true, each mark clean, each number board held straight beside its door. Anyone looking at the three images without the report would see three well kept bonded doors and nothing amiss in the world. That was the point of them and the horror of them at once. She cut the photographs out along their borders with the office shears, working slowly, resting the sheets against the table edge, and setting the burned hand aside again whenever its grip came late. The cut edges came away clean.

From her coat she took the notebook Leyden had told her to stop keeping. It held four lines in her own hand, four rooms that read as nothing, written before Valtessa. She opened it to the page after those lines and laid the three cut photographs in together, door beside door beside door, and closed the notebook on them. The register received the three doors. She did not write that to Leyden. No letter of hers would carry it and no report would name the notebook, and the first lie she had told him to his face, when she agreed to stop, sat a little heavier for this honest addition to its weight. The month of fear from the yard still sat in her as well, undiminished by paperwork.

She put the notebook back in her coat and squared the joint report for its bag. The file would go forward complete and signed, and it would answer every question the Office and the port service had opened in Valtessa except one. That one she carried out of the room with her, past the sealed doors below and the quiet water beyond them, and it would still be open when the next case began. Three doors with perfect seals and nothing behind them.

## The Case File

### Q: What is an Accord seal and what does it bind?

An Accord seal is the pressed metal mark set on a bonded door under Accord authority to certify that the door stands bound and that its contents stand under bonded control until lawful opening. The seal itself is a physical token. The binding it certifies is separate work, laid by a licensed Binder and recorded by the port service at the time of laying. A seal therefore attests to a binding; it does not create one. Inspectors are instructed to treat seal and binding as distinct findings. A door may carry a correct seal over an absent binding, or a defective seal over an intact binding, and each condition is reported in its own terms.

### Q: How does Valtessa run its own ports?

Valtessa administers its ports through its own port service, under its own officers, orders and records, without direction from any outside service in ordinary operations. The port service controls bonded stores, yard gates, seal issue, inspection rounds and fines within Valtessa territory. Its inspectors hold authority to open bonded doors on written order, to hold yards and stores pending inquiry, and to seal premises after a taking. Cooperation with outside bodies, including the Ninth Office, takes place only under a joint arrangement agreed for a stated case. In such arrangements the port service retains command of its own ground, premises and personnel at all times.

### Q: How are joint operations commanded when the Office works with the port service?

Command in a joint operation is divided by ground and by function, and the division is recorded before any action begins. The Valtessan port service commands its own territory, its premises, its seals and its officers. The Ninth Office commands its own officers and is responsible for findings that rest on Office practice, including any reading offered for the record. Orders affecting Valtessan premises are given by the port service commander for the operation. Findings offered jointly are entered in a joint report and signed by both services. Neither service signs a finding it has not examined, and a disagreement is recorded as a disagreement rather than resolved by seniority.

### Q: What is a licensed Binder licensed to do?

A licensed Binder is licensed to lay, renew and, on lawful instruction, lift bindings on bonded doors and bonded rooms within the territory named in the licence. Each laying is recorded with the door, the date of the work in service reckoning, and the Binder's licence mark. A Binder is not licensed to certify seals, to issue seals, or to attest to a binding laid by another Binder. A Binder may decline work that falls outside the licence or outside lawful instruction without penalty. Work performed outside the terms of the licence is void for record purposes and is referred for inspection proceedings by the issuing authority.

### Q: How does the forged seal trade operate in bonded ports?

The forged seal trade supplies imitation Accord seals to persons who wish a bonded door to appear regular without submitting to inspection or record. Forged seals are commonly identified by a crude Accord mark, incorrectly cut room numbers, and the absence of the hairline left by the worn Carrowmere press die. The trade is ordinarily a trade in appearance. A purchaser gains a door that satisfies a casual glance and fails any competent inspection. Port services prosecute the making, sale and knowing use of forged seals as forgery. Investigators are cautioned that a forgery finding accounts only for the seals examined and does not, without further evidence, account for the condition of any binding.

### Q: What happens on a port service seal round in Valtessa?

A seal round is a scheduled circuit of bonded doors by port service inspectors for the purpose of comparing each seal in place against the seal register. Inspectors verify the Accord mark, the room number and the hairline, note any damage or substitution, and record the condition of the door furniture. Rounds are conducted in pairs and findings are entered on the round sheet before the inspectors leave the premises. A discrepancy stops the round at that door until a senior inspector attends. Round sheets are retained as service records and may be produced in proceedings. Keepers of bonded stores are required to admit inspectors on a round without prior notice.

### Q: How are licensed Binder records kept and inspected?

Each licensed Binder maintains a work book recording every binding laid, renewed or lifted, with the door identification, the instructing authority and the port service record reference for the work. Work books are produced on demand to port service inspectors and to the licensing authority. An annual inspection compares a sample of recorded work against the doors named, by attendance at the doors with the register open. Discrepancies between book and door are recorded individually and are not aggregated into a general finding. A Binder whose records cannot be reconciled with inspected doors is suspended from new work pending a full inspection of all recorded doors.

### Q: What may a Weigher put in a joint report?

A Weigher may put in a joint report only what was read, where it was read, the material in which it was held, and the limits of the reading in plain terms. A report states the character of sustained intent where such intent is found, and states absence where a reading that should be present is not found. A Weigher does not enter identity, motive or spoken truth as findings, because weight records none of those things. Faintness, damage to the anchor material and interference from crowds or recent disturbance are entered as qualifications. A joint report signed by another service adopts the reading as recorded, including its stated limits.

### Q: How is Stiller suppression declared on the record?

A Stiller serving in a joint operation declares the fact of service, the capacity in which the Stiller serves, and any use of suppression, before any reading is taken in the Stiller's presence. The declaration is entered in the operation record and repeated in any hearing where readings from the operation are considered. Suppression that has been used is described by its source and its extent, centred on the Stiller and thinning with distance, so that a later reader of the record can distinguish suppressed weight from absent weight. Undeclared suppression invalidates readings taken during the period concerned and is treated as a serious breach of joint procedure.

### Q: What is a warehouse yard book and what standing does it have as evidence?

A yard book is the working record kept at a warehouse yard for arrivals, departures, payments, seal numbers noted at the gate, and instructions given to clerks and couriers. Entries are ordinarily made by more than one hand over the life of the book. A yard book is admitted as evidence of the transactions it records in the ordinary course of the yard's business, subject to examination of the hands and the continuity of the entries. Damage to a yard book, including burning, is recorded at the point of recovery. Missing or destroyed portions are named as missing or destroyed, and their contents are not reconstructed from recollection or inference.

### Q: How is a bonded store licensed in Valtessa?

A bonded store is licensed by the port service for a named premises, a named keeper and a stated class of goods. The licence specifies the doors subject to binding, the seal register entries for those doors, and the inspection access the keeper must allow. Licences are renewed on inspection and may be suspended where seals, records or door furniture fail to meet the conditions of issue. A licence does not transfer with a sale of the premises. A new keeper requires a new licence and a fresh inspection of every bonded door. Trading under an expired or transferred licence is treated as unbonded trading for fine and seizure purposes.

### Q: What is the Carrowmere press die and why does its hairline matter?

Accord seals for bonded doors are pressed on the Carrowmere press, whose die has carried a fine hairline flaw across many years of service. The hairline appears on every genuine pressing as a faint line in a fixed position relative to the Accord mark. Because the flaw belongs to the die itself, it cannot be omitted by careful genuine work and is difficult to reproduce by imitation, since a copyist working from a finished seal commonly treats the line as damage and removes it. Inspectors therefore treat the presence of the hairline in its correct position as a strong indication of genuine pressing, and its absence as grounds for seizure and examination.

### Q: What fines apply for inspection offences on the Valtessa docks?

Inspection offences in Valtessa attract fines assessed by the port service according to a published scale, graded by the risk created rather than by the value of the goods concerned. Obstructing an inspector, presenting a door under a seal not recorded for that door, and trading under an expired bonded licence fall in the higher grades. Late production of records and defective door furniture fall in the lower grades unless repeated. Fines are recorded against the licence holder and against the premises, and unpaid fines suspend renewal. A fine is an administrative penalty and does not replace prosecution where forgery, theft or unlawful removal of a binding is alleged on evidence.

### Q: How are couriers licensed for work on the docks?

A courier working the Valtessa docks under bonded instructions holds a dock licence issued by the port service, naming the courier, the yards and stores the courier may serve, and the classes of packet the courier may carry. Licensed couriers sign at the gate on entry and on departure, and sealed packets are recorded by seal number at each handover. A courier may not open a sealed packet, substitute a packet, or carry for an unlicensed yard under cover of a valid licence. Licences are withdrawn where a courier is found carrying outside the recorded chain of handover, and withdrawal is notified to every yard named on the licence.

### Q: How is a yard sealed after a taking?

After a taking, the port service seals the yard in the presence of a second service witness where a joint operation is in force. Gates, store doors and office doors giving access to bonded areas are closed, chained where chaining is the ordinary practice for the premises, and sealed with port service seals whose numbers are read aloud and entered in the count book. Keys are taken into port service custody and listed. The sealed condition is inspected daily until the yard is reopened on written order or disposed of by direction of the competent authority. Breaking or tampering with a seal set after a taking is prosecuted as a separate offence.

### Q: How is burned paper treated as evidence?

Burned paper is recovered as it lies, without cleaning, flattening or attempted separation of leaves beyond what is required to lift it safely. At the point of entry into evidence, the burned portion is named as burned and the unburned portion is described by extent and legibility. No attempt is made to reconstruct burned text from memory, from copies held elsewhere, or from the expectations of investigators. Where a reading of the surviving material is offered, the faintness proper to paper as an anchor, and any further loss caused by fire, are stated beside the reading. A document half destroyed by fire is evidence of what survives and of the destruction itself, and of nothing further.

### Q: What standing do photographs have as case evidence?

Photographs taken under service direction are admitted as records of the visible condition of doors, seals, premises and objects at the time of exposure. The photographer's list, identifying each exposure by subject and sequence, is retained with the photographs and checked against any table or register to which the photographs relate. A photograph can establish the appearance of a seal, the position of a door number board, and the state of door furniture. A photograph cannot establish the presence or absence of a binding, and no photograph is offered for that purpose. Prints cut for filing retain their list references so that a filed print can be traced to its exposure.

### Q: What is the Accord mark and how is it recognised?

The Accord mark is the device struck at the centre of every genuine Accord seal to identify the seal as issued under Accord authority. In genuine pressings the mark is cut to a consistent depth, its edges are clean, and its proportions correspond to the master held with the Carrowmere press. Imitations commonly fail in depth, in edge quality or in proportion, and frequently in more than one of those respects at once. Recognition is taught to inspectors by comparison against retained genuine pressings, not by description alone. A mark that satisfies a glance but fails comparison is seized as suspect and submitted for examination, and the door it closes is held pending the result.

### Q: Why do Accord seals carry room numbers?

Room numbers are cut into an Accord seal at issue to tie the individual seal to the individual bonded room or door for which it was issued. The register records seal number, room number, premises and date of issue in service reckoning, so that a seal found on a different door is detectable on inspection. Numbers are cut on the Carrowmere press equipment to a standard hand and depth. Numbers cut elsewhere characteristically differ in spacing, alignment or depth, and such differences are recorded as findings in their own right. A correct room number is a necessary condition of a regular seal; it is never, by itself, a sufficient one.

### Q: What happens to the customers of a chain that is taken?

Customers of a taken chain are dealt with according to what each customer bought and knew, as established by records and examination, and not by association alone. A customer who purchased a forged seal is liable for the offences attaching to knowing use, and the customer's doors are inspected and rebound at the customer's expense where a binding is required. A customer in whose case a binding is found absent has the door held under port service control until a licensed Binder lays a new binding under record. Goods under a door found unbound are inventoried before any movement is permitted. No customer is promised exemption from inspection as part of any settlement.
