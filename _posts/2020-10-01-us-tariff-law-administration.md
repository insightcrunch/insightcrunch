US tariff law begins as a line in a schedule. A rate printed beside a description of goods states a percentage or a specific amount, and that printed figure appears complete on the page. Administration gives that figure a second existence. A tariff exists twice, once as a rate in a schedule and once as an administered system of agencies, investigations, deposits, reviews, and a court. The second existence decides what an importer pays on a particular shipment, because a rate reaches a shipment only after classification, valuation, origin, and column selection have placed the shipment under one rate line rather than another.

![How US tariff law is administered across four agencies and four remedy tracks - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-41.webp)

Four bodies divide that administered system. Customs and Border Protection classifies goods, determines customs value and origin, and collects duties at entry. The International Trade Commission publishes the Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States and determines material injury in trade remedy cases. The Department of Commerce, through the Enforcement and Compliance unit of the International Trade Administration, calculates dumping margins and subsidy rates as the statutory administering authority. The Office of the United States Trade Representative conducts investigations of foreign acts, policies, or practices under section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974. Each body applies a different standard to a different question, and no single body performs the whole sequence from schedule to collection.

Four remedy tracks run beside ordinary collection. Antidumping and countervailing duties under title VII of the Tariff Act of 1930 require affirmative determinations from Commerce and the Commission before an order issues. Safeguards under sections 201 to 204 of the Trade Act of 1974 require increased imports as a substantial cause of serious injury, with the President deciding the remedy. National security action under section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 rests on a Commerce investigation and a presidential decision. Action under section 301 addresses a foreign act, policy, or practice that denies United States rights or burdens United States commerce, with the Trade Representative conducting the investigation.

Two features separate the American system from a simple border charge. Antidumping and countervailing duties are retrospective, so cash deposits collected at entry are estimates and final liability is settled after importation through administrative reviews, with liquidation suspended in the interval. Judicial review sits in the Court of International Trade under a deferential substantial evidence standard, with appeal to the Federal Circuit, while presidential decisions on the discretionary tracks stand outside that ordinary review structure. The namable claim of the administration described in the paragraphs that follow is plain. Importers write the check. Duties are legally owed by the importer of record, and research on the 2018 tariff increases by Mary Amiti, Stephen J. Redding, and David E. Weinstein in the Journal of Economic Perspectives in 2019 and by Pablo D. Fajgelbaum, Pinelopi K. Goldberg, Patrick J. Kennedy, and Amit K. Khandelwal in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2020 found the cost passed through into prices paid inside the United States.

## The Schedule That Turns Statute Into a Price

A tariff rate as Congress enacts a rate occupies a single line of legal text, fixed in wording and silent about the shipment that will one day meet that wording at a port of entry. The administered reality of a rate is something else entirely. Between the enacted line and the amount collected on a particular import stands an apparatus of classification, interpretation, and rate selection that has to decide what an article is in the language of the schedule, where that article belongs among thousands of competing descriptions, and which of several printed rates governs an import from a particular source. A rate that reads as a simple instruction in a statute reaches the border as a sequence of decisions, and the price an importer pays emerges from that sequence rather than from the statutory sentence alone. The distance between the two is the distance US tariff law exists to manage, and the management takes place almost entirely inside one enacted document.

US tariff law, understood as a working system rather than as a collection of isolated rates, is the body of legal action and administrative structure through which the United States converts decisions about trade into duties collected on imports. The definition matters because a duty is never collected in the abstract. A duty is collected on an article, presented on a day, from a place, under a description, and each element of that presentation has to be translated into the terms a statute supplies before any amount can be computed. Translation is the central administrative act in the field. Congress supplies authority and rates through legal action, and the schedule supplies the shared vocabulary in which authority and rates become applicable to goods that Congress never saw and could never have described one by one. Without that shared vocabulary, an enacted rate would govern everything that vaguely resembled its subject and nothing with precision, and collection would dissolve into argument at every entry.

The formal identity of the system can be stated in a single citable pass, and the precision of that pass repays attention because each element carries a distinct legal consequence. Duties on imports into the United States are administered under the Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States, enacted by subtitle B of title I of the Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988, cited as Public Law 100-418, and effective on January 1, 1989. The enactment replaced the Tariff Schedules of the United States, the predecessor compilation under which duties had been administered before the 1988 act took effect. Section 1204(a) of the 1988 act, codified at 19 U.S.C. 3004(a), performs the enactment itself, giving the schedule the status of law rather than the status of a reference work about law. That status explains why the schedule is quoted, litigated, and amended as legislation is quoted, litigated, and amended, and why a printed rate in its columns carries authority that a summary or a guide could never carry on its own.

Replacement of the predecessor schedules in 1989 was more than a change of title or a republication of familiar material in a new binding. A tariff schedule is the operating map of a duty system, and replacing the map rearranges the ground that importers, administrators, and adjudicators share. Under the Harmonized Tariff Schedule, classification rests on an international nomenclature shared with the other participants in the Harmonized System, so a description developed for United States purposes begins from terms that trading partners also use for their own schedules. The older schedules had grown inside a purely domestic framework of descriptions. The 1988 enactment tied the domestic framework to an international one at the level of structure, while leaving the rates themselves, and the authority to change rates, entirely in domestic hands. That combination, international structure and domestic rate setting, defines the schedule as an instrument and shapes every question that administration of the schedule presents.

### What is the Harmonized Tariff Schedule?

The Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States is the enacted schedule that sets out the classification system and the rates of duty applied to imports into the United States, organized under the international Harmonized System and maintained for publication by the International Trade Commission.

Structure explains how the schedule performs the work assigned to the schedule by the 1988 act. The foundation is the Harmonized System maintained by the World Customs Organization, an international nomenclature for goods that allows participating countries to describe articles in a common sequence of categories before any national rate is attached to a category. The Harmonized Tariff Schedule carries that nomenclature in chapters numbered 1 to 97, a span that orders the whole field of merchandise into a single progression from the opening chapters to the closing chapters. Each chapter gathers a family of articles, each family divides into headings, and each heading divides into subheadings, so the schedule reads from the general toward the specific at every step. An article enters the system at the level of the broad category that captures the article, and classification proceeds downward until the article rests at the most precise description that the schedule provides for goods of that kind.

Precision in the schedule is expressed in digits, and the digits mark the boundary between the international system and the national system built on top of that system. The international nomenclature runs through six digits, and those six digits carry the shared meaning of the Harmonized System from one participating country to another. Within the United States schedule, the nomenclature is subdivided at the eight digit level for United States purposes, producing the rate lines at which duties are actually stated. The distinction between the six digit level and the eight digit level is therefore a distinction between description shared across borders and description settled inside domestic law. Two additional digits may appear to be a small refinement, yet the refinement decides real cases, because articles that share an international subheading can carry different United States rates once the national subdivision separates one rate line from a neighboring rate line. Administration lives at that level of detail, in the space opened by national subdivision of an international category.

Movement through the chapters, headings, and subheadings follows a prescribed discipline rather than an administrator's preference for a plausible home for an article. Classification follows the General Rules of Interpretation, applied top down through headings and subheadings, so the analysis begins with the broadest applicable terms and narrows only as the rules direct the analysis to narrow. The top down character of the rules performs several functions at once. A top down sequence prevents an administrator or an importer from starting with a desired rate and reasoning backward to a description that yields that rate, because the rate appears only at the end of the sequence, after the description has been fixed. A top down sequence also gives two analysts working on the same article a common path to follow, so disagreement can be located at a particular step, at a particular heading, rather than spread across the schedule as a general difference of impression. The rules convert classification from an exercise in resemblance into an exercise in ordered interpretation, and ordered interpretation can be reviewed, corrected, and applied consistently across entries in a way that impression cannot.

Sequence carries a further consequence that reaches every later stage of administration. Because the rate attaches at the end of the classificatory path, an error at the head of the path propagates through everything that follows. An article placed under the wrong heading is measured against the wrong subheadings, assigned to the wrong rate line, and charged under the wrong column analysis, even if every later step is performed with care. Conversely, a correct placement at the heading level disciplines the remaining steps, since each later choice is confined within the boundaries that the earlier choice established.

Against that structural background, the schedule presents not one rate for an article but three columns of rates, and reading the columns correctly is as much a part of administration as placing the article on a rate line. The three columns are column 1 general, column 1 special, and column 2. Each column answers a different question about the relationship between the United States and the source of an import, and the same article, classified identically and arriving on the same day, can bear a different duty depending on which column supplies the governing rate. The plurality of columns explains a feature of administered tariff practice that puzzles an observer who expects a single published price for a single article. The schedule publishes a structured set of possible prices, ordered by legal status, and administration consists in part of determining which member of that set the law selects for a given import. The selection is a legal determination, not a choice open to the importer or to the officer who receives the entry.

Column 1 general carries the rates described as normal trade relations rates, the standard rates that apply to imports from countries that hold that treatment with the United States. General in the title of the column signals the default position in the schedule, the rate against which the other columns are understood as departures. For the great body of United States import trade, the column 1 general rate is the operative figure, the rate that a classification produces when no preference, no agreement, and no denial of treatment displaces the standard analysis. The column also serves as a benchmark inside the system itself. A preference is measurable only against the general rate that would otherwise apply, and a penalty rate acquires meaning only by contrast with the general rate that a favored source would have paid. Administration therefore returns to column 1 general constantly, even in cases where another column finally governs, because the general column states the norm from which the governing analysis begins.

Column 1 special sits beside the general column and carries the rates available under preference programs and free trade agreements. The special column records, article by article and rate line by rate line, the concessions that the United States has extended through those programs and agreements, expressed in the same schedule and at the same level of detail as the general rates. Placement of the special rates inside the schedule, rather than in a separate compilation attached to each program or agreement, gives the schedule its character as a consolidated statement of applicable rates. An administrator reading a rate line sees the general rate and the special treatment for that line within a single framework, and an importer comparing sources can read the difference between standard treatment and preferential treatment at the point where the difference will be collected. The special column also disciplines the concessions themselves, because a negotiated or conferred preference has practical value only to the extent that the preference is stated at the rate line where an article is classified, in a form an entry can claim and an administration can allow or deny.

Column 2 carries the statutory rates descended from the Tariff Act of 1930, preserved in the schedule and applied to the small set of countries denied normal trade relations treatment. The descent of the column matters as much as the content of the column. Rates with a lineage reaching back to the 1930 act sit in the same document as rates produced by the 1988 enactment and by later legal action, so the schedule holds several generations of tariff policy in a single ordered presentation. For the small set of sources within the scope of the column, column 2 states the governing figure, and the figure reflects the older statutory settlement rather than the normal trade relations settlement recorded in column 1 general. The narrowness of the column's application should not obscure the function the column performs for the system as a whole. Column 2 marks the outer boundary of the rate structure, the treatment reserved for sources outside the normal relationship, and the existence of that boundary gives the concessions recorded elsewhere in the schedule a defined edge.

Read together, the three columns convert the schedule from a price list into a map of legal relationships expressed in rates. Classification fixes what an article is for tariff purposes, and the columns then ask a separate question about the standing of the place from which the article came and the programs or agreements that might govern trade from that place. Two shipments of identical articles can therefore lawfully bear different duties without any inconsistency in administration, because the schedule was built to register differences in legal status alongside differences in the character of goods. An account of US tariff law that treated the rate for an article as a single number would miss the design entirely. The design separates the question of description, answered once for the article, from the question of treatment, answered by column selection for the import, and the separation allows rates to change for a source without disturbing the classification of the article, or allows a classification to be corrected without reopening the treatment extended to a source.

One institutional feature of the schedule completes the formal picture and prevents a persistent misunderstanding about where rates come from. The International Trade Commission publishes the Harmonized Tariff Schedule and maintains the schedule as a current statement of the law, under the direction given in section 1207 of the 1988 act, codified at 19 U.S.C. 3007, to compile, publish, and keep the schedule up to date. Publication and maintenance are editorial and custodial functions of the highest practical importance, since traders and administrators alike work from the published schedule and depend on the published schedule to reflect every legal action that has altered a rate or a description. Custody of the text, however, is not authorship of the rates. Rates of duty are established by United States legal action, by statute or by proclamation, and never by the Commission's act of publication. A rate changes when the underlying legal action changes the rate, and the Commission's maintenance of the schedule records that change for public use. Confusing the publisher with the author would invert the constitutional and statutory order of the system, attributing to an act of compilation a power that the structure of US tariff law reserves to legal action taken through the channels that the Constitution and the statutes provide.

The distinction between publication and rate setting also explains the unusual authority that attaches to the schedule as a document in daily commercial use. Traders plan purchases, price contracts, and route shipments against the figures the schedule prints, and those figures bind because the figures restate legal action, not because the figures appear in a respected compilation. When the published schedule and an underlying legal action were ever to diverge, the legal action would control, since the schedule derives force from enactment under section 1204(a) and from the legal actions that amend rates within the enacted framework. Administration depends on the two functions remaining distinct and remaining synchronized, distinct so that responsibility for rates rests where the law places responsibility, and synchronized so that the working text in the hands of every participant states the law as the law stands at the date of an entry.

The distance between statute and shipment, with which the account began, can accordingly be restated with the structure in view. Congress acts, and the act takes form as rates inside a schedule enacted in 1988, organized on an international nomenclature, subdivided for national purposes, interpreted under rules that run from the general to the specific, and printed in three columns that register the legal standing of the source of an import. The Commission keeps that schedule published and current under section 1207, while the rates themselves move only when United States legal action moves the rates. Every element in that restatement is institutional before any element is economic, because a duty reaches a price only after the institutional sequence has run. The sequence begins with an article and a description, and the sequence ends with a figure collected at entry, and the schedule is the instrument that connects the beginning of the sequence to the end. Who holds the authority to operate that schedule at the border and beyond the border is the question that follows from its structure.

A reader approaching the schedule as enacted legal text rather than as a price list can follow [a guide to reading a federal statute as structured legal text](/2022/05/01/how-to-read-a-federal-statute/) for the interpretive discipline that classification under the General Rules of Interpretation requires.

## Four Bodies, Four Mandates in US Tariff Law

Which agency sets tariffs in the United States has no one name answer, because no single agency performs the whole act that the phrase implies. A rate of duty must be established by United States legal action, by statute or by proclamation. A schedule must be compiled, published, and kept up to date so that importers and officials can find the rate for a described article. Goods at a port must be classified under that schedule, valued, and assigned an origin, and money must be collected from the importer of record. In trade remedy proceedings, an authority must calculate whether dumping or subsidization occurred and by how much, while a separate body decides whether a domestic industry suffered material injury. Under a different track, an investigator must examine the acts, policies, or practices of a foreign government and prepare the ground on which concessions could be suspended or duties or import restrictions imposed. Each act belongs to a different institution, applying a different standard to a different kind of question.

Four bodies divide the work. United States Customs and Border Protection classifies goods, determines customs value and origin, and collects duties. The United States International Trade Commission determines material injury in trade remedy cases and publishes the tariff schedule. The Department of Commerce, acting as the statutory administering authority through the Enforcement and Compliance unit of the International Trade Administration, calculates dumping margins and subsidy rates. The Office of the United States Trade Representative conducts investigations under section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974. The mandates overlap at the edges, because a single shipment or proceeding can pass through more than one body, but the mandates do not merge. The body that publishes a rate does not collect it. The body that collects a duty does not decide injury. The body that calculates a dumping margin does not make the injury finding that an antidumping order also requires. The body that investigates a foreign practice does not liquidate entries at a port.

The division separates kinds of judgment as well. Classification concerns what an article is under the General Rules of Interpretation and the headings and subheadings of the Harmonized Tariff Schedule. Valuation concerns price, principally transaction value, the price paid or payable. Injury concerns the condition of a domestic industry. A dumping margin is a comparison, the amount by which normal value exceeds export price or constructed export price. A section 301 investigation concerns foreign government conduct, measured against standards that distinguish denial of United States rights and unjustifiable acts from unreasonable or discriminatory acts.

### Which agency actually collects the duties?

United States Customs and Border Protection collects the duties. As the agency stationed at the ports of entry within the Department of Homeland Security, it receives payment from importers of record, holds cash deposits where trade remedy orders apply, and liquidates each entry to fix the amount finally owed.

Collection gives Customs and Border Protection a place no other body shares, and the history behind that place runs in two layers. The agency sits in the Department of Homeland Security, where it has belonged since March 2003 under the Homeland Security Act of 2002. Behind that placement stands a longer lineage. Customs and Border Protection is the heir to the Customs Service founded in 1789 in the Treasury Department. The 2002 act and the March 2003 transfer moved an old revenue function into a department organized around homeland security, without changing the identity of the function. Classification, valuation, origin, and collection remained customs work, performed by the successor to the service that had performed customs work since 1789. A body descended from a Treasury revenue service, relocated into a security department, joins border operations to the financial settlement of each entry.

Leadership follows a single head model. The Commissioner of Customs and Border Protection is appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate. That structure concentrates operational responsibility in one confirmed official inside a cabinet department. The contrast with the International Trade Commission is sharp. Injury decisions emerge from six commissioners serving long terms under a partisan cap, while entry decisions emerge from a departmental agency under one commissioner. Collection and entry processing demand a chain of command capable of applying the schedule uniformly at ports across the country, entry after entry, and a single commissioner model supplies that chain. Accountability runs through the President who appoints, the Senate that confirms, and the department in which the agency sits since March 2003, because the agency is a component of the Department of Homeland Security rather than an independent establishment.

The Customs and Border Protection slice begins before money changes hands, because the amount owed depends on three determinations at entry. The first is classification. Goods must be placed under the correct heading and subheading of the Harmonized Tariff Schedule, following the General Rules of Interpretation. Classification is the importer declaration in the first instance, since importers of record bear legal responsibility for duties and for exercising reasonable care in classification and valuation under 19 U.S.C. 1484, the standard set by the Customs Modernization Act of 1993. That responsibility does not transfer the decision away from the agency. Customs and Border Protection issues binding rulings in advance and decides classification at entry, testing the declared classification against the General Rules of Interpretation. The second determination is customs value, which rests principally on transaction value, the price paid or payable, under 19 U.S.C. 1401a. The third is origin, which turns on substantial transformation and drives country of origin marking under 19 U.S.C. 1304. Origin also decides which column of rates and which trade remedy orders or preference claims can attach to an entry. Classification fixes the rate line, value fixes the base, and origin fixes the treatment, and collection follows from the three together.

Final settlement belongs to the agency as well. Entries are liquidated by Customs and Border Protection, and liquidation is the final computation of duties owed on an entry, subject to the protest route for an importer contesting a liquidation or classification decision. Civil penalties for false statements or omissions are graduated by culpability under 19 U.S.C. 1592, across negligence, gross negligence, and fraud, and a prior disclosure of a violation before discovery limits penalty exposure. None of that work sets the rate in the schedule. The agency applies rates established by statute or proclamation, and the distinction between applying a rate and establishing a rate marks the boundary of its mandate.

The International Trade Commission occupies the opposite institutional pole from a departmental operating agency. The Commission is an independent agency. Its six commissioners are appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate for nine year terms, and no more than three commissioners may belong to one political party. Independence separates the Commission from direction by a cabinet department. A nine year term extends beyond the term of the President who makes the appointment, so a commissioner deciding an injury question may owe appointment to an earlier administration. The partisan cap prevents either party from holding a majority of the six seats by appointment alone, so a majority on a contested determination must rest on votes that cross or complicate a purely partisan division of the membership. Senate confirmation applies here as it applies to the Commissioner of Customs and Border Protection and to the Trade Representative, but with a different result. Confirmation at Customs and Border Protection installs a single operating head inside a department. Confirmation at the Commission installs one of six long term members of an independent body whose decisions are collegial.

The Commission owns two slices that appear unrelated until the schedule and the injury inquiry are seen as the paper and adjudicative sides of the same system. The first slice is publication. Section 1207 of the Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988, codified at 19 U.S.C. 3007, directs the Commission to compile, publish, and keep up to date the Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States, the schedule that section 1204(a) of the same act, codified at 19 U.S.C. 3004(a), enacts. The schedule is the text against which classification occurs, the text in which column 1 general rates, column 1 special rates for preference programs and free trade agreements, and column 2 rates appear for each rate line. An importer reading a rate in the published schedule reads a document maintained by the Commission. Maintenance, however, is not rate setting. Rates of duty are established by United States legal action, by statute or by proclamation, never by the publication work of the Commission. The Commission keeps the book current as legal action changes the contents, and confusing the publisher with the author of the rates is a common version of the one name error.

The second Commission slice is the injury determination. In antidumping and countervailing duty proceedings under title VII of the Tariff Act of 1930, an order requires two affirmative determinations from two different bodies. Commerce must determine that sales occurred at less than fair value or that a countervailable subsidy exists, and the Commission must determine material injury, threat of material injury, or material retardation of the establishment of a domestic industry. A negative finding by either body ends the case. The Commission standard looks away from the pricing conduct of foreign exporters and the subsidy practices of foreign governments, toward the condition of the industry in the United States. The alternatives of threat and material retardation extend the inquiry to injury that impends and to injury that holds back the establishment of an industry rather than damaging an industry already established. Commerce answers whether the pricing or the subsidy crossed the statutory line and by how much. The Commission answers whether the domestic industry suffered the statutory degree of harm. Neither answer substitutes for the other, and an order directing Customs and Border Protection to collect additional duties rests on both answers given affirmatively.

The Department of Commerce holds the other half of the title VII pair, and its form differs from both neighbors. Commerce performs the work through the International Trade Administration, and within that administration the Enforcement and Compliance unit serves as the statutory administering authority. The phrase administering authority is a term of the statute rather than a courtesy title, and it identifies the place charged with the calculations on which antidumping and countervailing duty exposure depends. Where Customs and Border Protection is a departmental agency with its own confirmed commissioner, and the Commission is an independent body of six long term commissioners, the Commerce function sits inside a department as a specialized unit. Margin calculation is a sustained technical exercise conducted case by case, comparing prices, values, and subsidy amounts under statutory definitions, and Congress lodged the exercise in a department unit built for that repetition rather than in a multimember commission or in the border agency that would later collect whatever the calculation produced.

The Commerce standard begins with the definitions that bound the calculation. In an antidumping inquiry, the question is whether merchandise was sold at less than fair value, and the dumping margin expresses the answer as the amount by which normal value exceeds export price or constructed export price. In a countervailing duty inquiry, the question is whether a countervailable subsidy supports the merchandise, and the administering authority calculates the subsidy rate expressing the extent of that support. The inputs differ between the tracks. A dumping margin compares values across markets and transaction structures, while a subsidy rate measures government support as the statute defines that support. In both tracks the administering authority produces a number, and the number has direct financial consequence once paired with an affirmative injury determination from the Commission, because the number becomes the basis for cash deposits and for duties that Customs and Border Protection collects. A margin computed too high or too low changes what importers pay, which is one reason the statute pairs the calculation with an independent injury screen in another agency rather than leaving the whole remedy decision inside the department that performs the arithmetic.

Commerce also illustrates why tariff setting misleads as a description of trade remedies. The administering authority does not judge that imports caused material injury. Its determination addresses the foreign side of the transaction, the pricing of the exporter or subsidization by a foreign government, measured against fair value and against the definition of a countervailable subsidy. The rate from a completed title VII proceeding is a composite product, assembled from a margin or subsidy rate calculated in one department, an injury finding issued by an independent commission, and collection executed by an agency in a second department.

The Office of the United States Trade Representative works at the greatest distance from the ports. The office sits in the Executive Office of the President. Its head holds ambassador rank, and that head is appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate. Placement and rank together describe the mandate. An office inside the Executive Office of the President operates as an instrument of presidential direction in trade matters, rather than as an independent commission or a line component of a department administering entries. Ambassador rank signals work that faces outward, toward foreign governments, negotiation, and settlement of disputes under trade agreements. Confirmation here installs the trade representative of the President within the Executive Office itself. Of the four bodies, the Trade Representative stands closest to the President institutionally, and that proximity fits an assignment defined by foreign government conduct and by remedies that operate as acts of national trade policy.

The Trade Representative slice is the investigation under section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2411 and following. Under that authority the Trade Representative investigates foreign acts, policies, or practices, consulting and seeking dispute settlement where a trade agreement applies. The statute divides outcomes by standard. Action is mandatory in defined cases, where a foreign act denies United States rights under a trade agreement or where an act is unjustifiable and burdens United States commerce. Action is discretionary where an act is unreasonable or discriminatory and burdens United States commerce. Remedies include suspending concessions and imposing duties or import restrictions. A section 301 duty therefore emerges by a route unlike any other in the system. It does not emerge from classification in the ordinary course of entry, and it does not emerge from a margin calculation paired with an injury finding under title VII. It emerges from a finding about a foreign government practice, made by an office in the Executive Office of the President after investigation, consultation, and, where an agreement applies, an effort at dispute settlement. The same word duty covers outcomes produced by entirely different mandates.

Set side by side, the four structures form a pattern that a one name answer would erase. Customs and Border Protection answers through a single Senate confirmed commissioner inside the Department of Homeland Security, heir to a Treasury service founded in 1789, applying classification rules, transaction value, and substantial transformation to entries and collecting the money owed. The International Trade Commission answers through six Senate confirmed commissioners on nine year terms under a three member partisan cap, independent of any department, publishing the schedule under section 1207 and deciding material injury. Commerce answers through the Enforcement and Compliance unit of the International Trade Administration as administering authority, calculating dumping margins and subsidy rates. The Trade Representative answers through a Senate confirmed head of ambassador rank inside the Executive Office of the President, investigating foreign acts, policies, and practices under section 301 under mandatory and discretionary standards. Department, independent commission, department unit, and executive office are four different forms of public authority in a single connected system.

By October 1, 2020, the four body division rested on statutes enacted across the twentieth century and on a departmental reorganization completed in March 2003, and the division governed every route by which a duty reached an importer. The ordinary route ran through the published schedule and the entry process. The trade remedy route ran through a Commerce calculation and a Commission injury determination before collection began. The section 301 route ran through an investigation of foreign conduct in the Executive Office of the President. No route passed through a single agency from rate to collection, and no agency held authority over every stage of any route. Tariff administration in the United States is a relay among four mandates, and the body holding each stage determines the standard applied, the record considered, and the consequence for the goods, the industry, and the importer of record.

## The Border Agency at Work: Classification, Value, Origin

Tariff law reaches a shipment at the border through an administrative routine that assigns the goods a classification, a customs value, and an origin, and then converts those determinations into a sum of money owed to the United States. The agency that performs that routine for ordinary entries is U.S. Customs and Border Protection, housed in the Department of Homeland Security since March 2003 under the Homeland Security Act of 2002 and descended from the Customs Service founded in 1789 in the Treasury Department. Its Commissioner is appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate. The work described in this section is the front line of administration, distinct from the investigations that produce special duties or restrictions under other tracks. At the front line, no separate petition and no injury inquiry stands between the goods and the duty. The importer presents the merchandise, the facts about the merchandise, and a declaration of how the tariff schedule should treat it, and the agency tests that declaration, computes the amount due on the basis that survives examination, and closes the entry through liquidation.

That party is the importer of record. Under 19 U.S.C. 1484, the importer of record bears legal responsibility for the duties owed on an entry and for exercising reasonable care in the classification and valuation of the merchandise. The reasonable care standard entered the statute through the Customs Modernization Act of 1993, and the date carries the meaning of the change. The 1993 act framed importation as a shared process in which the agency furnishes the legal framework, rulings, and examination, while the importer furnishes accurate information about what the goods are, what they are worth in the transaction that brought them to the border, and where they acquired their character as a product. Reasonable care, in that design, is not a slogan attached to the customs laws. It is the measure against which the importer's conduct is judged when a classification proves wrong, when a value proves understated, or when an origin claim proves unsupportable. An importer that kept records adequate to explain its declaration, that sought guidance where the schedule presented a genuine ambiguity, and that disclosed material facts to the agency stands in a different legal position from an importer that guessed, copied a supplier description without examination, or withheld information bearing on duty. The statute does not demand that every declaration be correct in the end. It demands that the declaration be the product of care proportionate to the legal consequence that follows from it, because the amount collected at the border depends in the first instance on facts that the importer, and not the officer, is best placed to know.

Classification is the first of the three determinations and, in many entries, the one that decides the economic result before any arithmetic begins. The importer declares a classification for the goods at entry, placing them within the Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States, and that declaration is tested against the General Rules of Interpretation. The rules operate as an ordered method rather than as a menu of equally available choices. Classification follows those rules from the top down through headings and subheadings, so that the analysis begins with the terms of the headings and the relevant legal notes and proceeds to more specific provisions only through the sequence the rules prescribe. Under the General Rules of Interpretation, the answer must be reached by working the rules in order, and a later rule is reached only when an earlier rule leaves the question unresolved. The importer's declaration therefore has the character of a legal claim, not a mere label. It asserts that the goods, described accurately, fall under one heading and subheading rather than another, and that the rate line attached to that place in the schedule is the rate Congress prescribed for goods of that description.

Because a classification claim can be contested and because commerce requires an answer before goods move, the statute and the agency practice built around it provide for rulings in advance. Customs and Border Protection issues binding rulings, and it also decides classification at entry. The two functions belong to different moments in the life of a shipment. A binding ruling obtained in advance resolves the treatment of described merchandise before the transaction is committed, giving the importer a determination on which planning, pricing, and contracting can rest, provided the goods that arrive match the goods described in the request. A decision at entry, by contrast, addresses the merchandise as presented, on the entry documentation and on any examination the agency conducts. The coexistence of the two functions reflects a practical judgment about where certainty is most valuable. For a product line imported repeatedly, an advance ruling removes a recurring legal question from every individual entry and allows both the agency and the importer to apply a settled answer. The reasonable care standard and the ruling system fit together in this respect. An importer uncertain of a classification and proceeding without seeking an available ruling has chosen a course that may be difficult to describe as careful if the chosen classification proves unsustainable, whereas an importer that presented the facts fully and obtained a ruling has placed the ambiguity before the agency at the moment when the agency could resolve it without disrupting the movement of goods.

The second determination is customs value, and its function is to establish the base to which an ad valorem rate applies. Under 19 U.S.C. 1401a, customs value rests principally on transaction value, defined as the price paid or payable for the merchandise. The primacy of transaction value expresses a preference for the commercial reality of the sale over hypothetical measures of worth. Where goods have been sold and a price has been paid or is payable for them, that price, adjusted as the statute directs, is the measure the customs laws reach for first, because it records what a buyer in fact agreed to give for the very goods under appraisal. The importer of record bears responsibility for supporting the declared value under the reasonable care standard of 19 U.S.C. 1484, and the declaration must therefore be able to connect the number entered on the customs documents to the price generated by the sale. A declared value detached from the transaction, drawn from a list price never paid, from a price for different goods, or from an invoice that omits elements of the consideration, fails not merely as arithmetic but as a representation of the transaction the statute makes central. Valuation disputes accordingly tend to turn on what the price paid or payable in fact included, and on whether the documentary record presented to the agency matches the commercial record of the purchase. The classification determines the rate. The value determines the amount to which that rate is applied. An error in either reaches the revenue in full, which is why the statute couples them within a single duty of care owed by the same party.

Origin is the third determination, and it governs both the rate treatment that depends on the country from which goods come and the marking that the goods or their containers must bear. Origin turns on substantial transformation. The test asks where the merchandise last underwent a process that changed its character to the point that a new article, with a distinct name, character, or use, emerged from the processing. Mere passage through a country, minor assembly that leaves the essential character of the components untouched in the sense the law requires, or operations performed to disguise the true source of a product do not answer the question in the same way as a transformation that produces a different article of commerce. Customs and Border Protection determines origin as part of its administration of an entry, and the determination carries consequences beyond the arithmetic of a single duty rate. Rate treatment under the columns of the tariff schedule and under preference arrangements depends on origin, so a mistaken origin claim can place goods under a rate to which they were never entitled or deny them a rate their production history would have supported.

The marking requirement gives the origin determination a visible and independent legal form. Under 19 U.S.C. 1304, articles of foreign origin imported into the United States must be marked to indicate their country of origin. Marking is not a courtesy to purchasers and not a labeling preference left to the importer. It is a statutory condition administered by the customs authorities, with the origin determined under the substantial transformation analysis supplying the country that the mark must name. Failure to mark, marking that names the wrong country, and marking obscured or removed before the article reaches its purchaser therefore present distinct violations from an underpayment of duty, even where the same mistaken view of origin produced both. An importer exercising reasonable care must accordingly attend to origin twice over, once as an element of the rate claimed at entry and once as the statement physically borne by the merchandise, and an error examined by the agency may require correction on both planes. The three determinations together explain why entry documentation asks for more than a description of goods. Classification states what the goods are in the language of the schedule. Value states what was paid or is payable for them. Origin states where they became the article they are. The duty follows from the three answers combined, and none of the three can be supplied reliably by an officer who sees only the exterior of a shipment at a port.


Entry is the formal act by which merchandise is presented for admission and the declarations described in this section are made, and liquidation is the act by which the agency brings the entry to a close. Liquidation is the final computation of duties by Customs and Border Protection. Before liquidation, amounts paid or deposited in connection with an entry retain a provisional character, because the agency may examine the declared classification, value, and origin and may reach conclusions different from those the importer asserted. Liquidation fixes the computation on the basis the agency accepts, and the entry then stands assessed in a definite amount, whether that amount confirms the importer's figures or displaces them. The finality of liquidation explains the remedy that accompanies it. Under 19 U.S.C. 1514, an importer may protest a liquidation or a classification decision within 180 days. The protest is the administrative route by which the party that bore responsibility for the declaration challenges the agency determination that displaced it, and the 180 day period runs as a condition of that route. A protest presents the dispute within the customs system in the first instance, on the record of the entry and the determinations the agency made. The protest period also disciplines the importer's own record keeping, because a challenge framed within 180 days must rest on documents assembled at entry, on the ruling history of the merchandise, and on the value and origin evidence that reasonable care required the importer to gather before the goods arrived at the border in the first instance and before any dispute arose between the parties over the amount owed.

Errors and false statements in that cycle carry consequences beyond a corrected computation, and the penalty provision grades those consequences by the state of mind that produced the violation. Under 19 U.S.C. 1592, civil penalties for false statements or omissions in customs matters are graduated by culpability across three levels, negligence, gross negligence, and fraud. The graduation is a substantive feature of the administration of the tariff laws, not a sentencing detail. A negligent failure, in which the importer did not exercise the care the statute required, is treated as a lesser violation than gross negligence, in which the disregard of a known or obvious duty was more serious, and both are treated as lesser than fraud, in which a false statement or omission was made with intent to deceive. The same inaccurate entry can therefore produce different penalty exposure depending on the conduct behind it, and the reasonable care standard of 19 U.S.C. 1484 supplies the benchmark against which the lower levels of culpability are measured. An importer that cannot show the inquiries, records, or reliance that care would have produced has difficulty confining a violation to the least serious category, whereas documented care, while no guarantee against a penalty where a statement was false, bears directly on the category the conduct fits. Fraud stands apart because it converts an administrative error into a deliberate deception of the revenue system, and the statute reserves its most severe treatment for that conversion.

The penalty structure contains a safety valve that rewards disclosure made before the government finds the violation on its own. A prior disclosure of a violation before discovery limits penalty exposure. The mechanism serves the revenue as well as the importer. Customs administration depends on information held by importers about their own transactions, and a rule that treated voluntary disclosure and concealment alike would remove the incentive to bring errors forward. By limiting exposure for a party that discloses before discovery, 19 U.S.C. 1592 encourages the correction of entries on the importer's initiative and gives the agency information it might otherwise obtain only through audit or investigation. The timing element is decisive. Disclosure after the agency has discovered the violation does not perform the same function and does not earn the same limitation, because the statute rewards the act of bringing forward what the government did not yet know.

### What is de minimis treatment in customs law?

De minimis treatment in customs law is the duty free admission of low value articles under section 321 of the Tariff Act of 1930, codified at 19 U.S.C. 1321, where the fair retail value in the country of shipment does not exceed 800 dollars for one person on one day.

The provision rests on a judgment about the cost of collection. Section 321 lets the Secretary admit articles free of duty where collection would cost the government expense disproportionate to the revenue produced. On a shipment of small value, the duty computed under the ordinary machinery of classification, valuation, and liquidation may amount to a sum smaller than the administrative cost of computing, assessing, and collecting it, and the statute authorizes the government to forgo the duty rather than spend more to collect than the collection yields. The threshold in force on October 1, 2020 stood at 800 dollars of fair retail value in the country of shipment, for articles imported by one person on one day. The measure is fair retail value in the country of shipment, not a price assigned after arrival, and the limit is framed by person and by day, so that the channel serves individual low value importations rather than the division of a larger commercial shipment into pieces each falling under the line. The 800 dollar figure was a legislative revision of a much lower line. The Trade Facilitation and Trade Enforcement Act of 2015 raised the threshold from 200 dollars, and the increase took effect in March 2016. Articles within the threshold enter without duty under section 321, and the entry is relieved of the duty computation that the general system would otherwise require, although the provision is an exemption from duty on grounds of administrative economy and not a finding that the articles fall outside the customs laws. The term de minimis also appears in the antidumping and countervailing duty investigations, where margins below the thresholds stated in the law of those investigations are disregarded, and that separate use belongs to the remedy track rather than to the border channel described in this section. At the border itself, de minimis completes the picture of administration proportioned to the amount in controversy. The system that demands reasonable care in the declaration of a commercial entry, that liquidates entries in a final computation subject to protest within 180 days, and that grades penalties from negligence to fraud also declines, by statute, to pursue duties whose collection would cost the government more than the revenue is worth.

## The Two-Determination Track: Dumping and Subsidy Cases

Title VII of the Tariff Act of 1930, as amended, supplied the adjudicated track within the administration of the tariff laws of the United States. The track differed in kind from a general grant of authority to raise or lower a rate of duty. A rate in the Harmonized Tariff Schedule rested on legal action by statute or proclamation, and the ordinary collection of that rate belonged to the customs process at entry. A Title VII case began from a different premise. The question presented concerned identified merchandise, sold or subsidized under conditions that the statute treated as actionable, and imported in a manner that injured or threatened a domestic industry. The answer did not follow from the schedule alone, from a classification ruling, or from the amount an importer declared at entry. The answer followed from an investigation conducted under a defined sequence, by two separate bodies, each charged with a distinct determination, and each capable of ending the case by a negative finding.

The statutory anchors for the two branches of the track were precise. Antidumping duties rested on 19 U.S.C. 1673 and following, and countervailing duties rested on 19 U.S.C. 1671 and following. The pairing reflected a structural similarity, and the similarity should not obscure the difference between the conduct each branch addressed. The antidumping branch concerned sales at less than fair value, and one branch measured a pricing relationship. The countervailing duty branch concerned a countervailable subsidy, and the other branch measured a subsidy rate. Congress placed the two inquiries inside a common procedural frame, with common injury screening, common stages of preliminary and final decision, and a common consequence when the required findings aligned. That common frame explained why petitions, determinations, and orders in the two kinds of cases followed parallel calendars even though the substantive calculation assigned to the Department of Commerce differed between them.

The division of labor followed the institutional design described in the governing statutes. The Department of Commerce, through the International Trade Administration and its Enforcement and Compliance unit, acted as the administering authority. That authority carried responsibility for the calculation at the center of each branch, the dumping margin in an antidumping investigation and the subsidy rate in a countervailing duty investigation. The United States International Trade Commission carried the separate responsibility for the injury determination. The Commission was an independent agency of six commissioners, appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate for nine year terms, with no more than three commissioners from one political party. Independence mattered to the structure of the track. The body that calculated whether dumping or subsidization had occurred did not decide whether the domestic industry had suffered the injury the statute required, and the body that decided injury did not perform the margin or subsidy calculation. Each determination rested on a different record emphasis, a different expertise, and a different statutory question.

### What must be proven before an antidumping duty order issues?

An antidumping duty order requires two affirmative determinations. The Department of Commerce must find sales at less than fair value, and the International Trade Commission must find material injury, threat of material injury, or material retardation of an industry's establishment. A negative determination by either body ends the case and prevents any order.

The two determination requirement operated as a structural check built into the statute rather than as a formality layered over a single decision. An affirmative finding on dumping established that sales had occurred at less than fair value within the meaning of the antidumping authority, and an affirmative finding on subsidization established that a countervailable subsidy existed within the meaning of the countervailing duty authority. Neither finding, standing alone, authorized an order. Material injury had also to be found, or threat of material injury, or material retardation of the establishment of an industry. The three forms of the injury determination were alternatives within a single requirement, and satisfaction of one alternative satisfied that element. Omission of the element altogether left the statutory case incomplete, however large a margin or subsidy rate the Department of Commerce had calculated.

The converse proposition gave the check its force. A negative determination by either body ended the case. A negative Commerce determination on dumping or subsidization removed the foundation on the conduct element, and no order could issue on that petition. A negative Commission determination on injury removed the foundation on the domestic effect element, and no order could issue even after an affirmative margin or subsidy finding. Neither agency acted as a reviewing court over the other, and neither agency supplied the finding assigned to its counterpart. Termination followed from the absence of a required element, not from disagreement between the agencies about a shared question. The design prevented a remedy founded on dumping or subsidy alone without the prescribed domestic injury, and a remedy founded on domestic injury alone without the prescribed dumping or subsidy.

No counterpart to that divided requirement governed the discretionary remedies elsewhere in the trade laws. Under the safeguard authority in sections 201 to 204 of the Trade Act of 1974, the International Trade Commission investigated increased quantities and serious injury, and the President then decided the remedy within statutory limits. Under section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, the Department of Commerce investigated and reported, and the President decided within the periods the statute prescribed. Under section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, the Office of the United States Trade Representative investigated foreign acts, policies, or practices and determined the response under the standards that section supplied. None of those tracks imposed the Title VII condition that two independent affirmative determinations, one on dumping or subsidization and one on material injury, had both to be secured before a duty order could issue. The Title VII order was the terminal act of a case in which every statutory element had been affirmatively determined by the body assigned to determine that element.

The sequence began with a petition filed simultaneously with both agencies. Simultaneity was not a clerical detail. Filing with both bodies placed the conduct question and the injury question inside the same proceeding from the outset, and the statutory clocks that followed were measured against that common start. The agencies did not receive the case in succession. The inquiries proceeded in an ordered overlap. The Commission reached a preliminary view on injury early in the calendar, the Department of Commerce reached preliminary and then final views on dumping or subsidization at later points, and the Commission returned to injury for a final determination after the Commerce determination had taken final form. Early screening on injury spared the system a complete margin investigation in a case that failed at the threshold, and the final injury decision was deferred until any dumping or subsidy had been finally determined.

After a petition was filed, the Department of Commerce decided initiation, generally within 20 days. Initiation marked the formal opening of the Commerce investigation and started the periods from which the preliminary determination deadlines were counted. Without initiation, the investigation did not advance to preliminary determinations, suspension of liquidation, or cash deposits. With initiation, the case entered a calendar in which each subsequent stage had a prescribed interval and a prescribed consequence. The discipline of the calendar formed part of the administration of the law, and each deadline converted an analytical question into a decision with legal effect at a known point in the proceeding.

The International Trade Commission made its preliminary injury determination within 45 days of the petition. The standard at that stage was reasonable indication. The phrase described a screening standard suited to a preliminary determination, and the determination answered whether a reasonable indication existed of material injury, threat of material injury, or material retardation by reason of the imports under investigation. A negative preliminary determination ended the investigation. That early exit gave practical content to the two determination design. A case could fail on injury before the Department of Commerce had completed a preliminary margin or subsidy calculation, and failure at that point terminated the matter without an order, without suspension of liquidation under a Commerce preliminary determination, and without the later final stages. An affirmative preliminary determination, by contrast, decided only that the case survived the screening stage. Affirmance at the preliminary point did not itself establish material injury for purposes of an order, because the statute assigned the Commission a final injury determination later in the sequence and reserved the order for the alignment of final affirmative determinations.

The preliminary determinations of the Department of Commerce followed on different timetables for the two branches. In a countervailing duty case, the Commerce preliminary determination fell 65 days after initiation, extendable to 130 days. In an antidumping case, the Commerce preliminary determination fell 140 days after initiation, extendable to 190 days. The difference in the ordinary periods was a feature of the statutory calendar rather than a judgment made case by case about relative difficulty. Extension altered the date of decision within a stated ceiling and did not alter the legal character of the determination. A preliminary determination remained preliminary under either the ordinary period or an extended period, and the determination supplied the estimated margin or rate on which the immediate consequences at the border then rested. Extension did not convert an estimate into a final calculation and did not relieve the Department of Commerce of the obligation to reach a final determination under the next stage of the calendar.

An affirmative Commerce preliminary determination triggered suspension of liquidation and the posting of cash deposits at the estimated margin. Suspension of liquidation held open the final computation of duties on covered entries, and the cash deposit translated the preliminary calculation into a sum collected in connection with entry while the investigation continued. The deposit rested on an estimated margin, because the preliminary determination preceded the final Commerce determination and the final injury determination of the Commission. The deposit also operated as a consequence of a stage in the proceeding rather than as the order itself. An investigation could produce suspension and deposits at the preliminary stage and then end without an order if a later determination was negative. The border effect therefore tracked the procedural posture of the case, attaching when the statute prescribed attachment and carrying only the weight the preliminary stage could bear.

The Department of Commerce then reached its final determination, generally 75 days after the preliminary determination. In antidumping cases that period was extendable to 135 days. The final determination superseded the preliminary conclusion as the administering authority's definitive statement, for purposes of the investigation, on whether sales at less than fair value had occurred or whether a countervailable subsidy existed, and on the margin or rate calculated for the merchandise under investigation. The sequence of a preliminary determination followed by a final determination served distinct functions. The preliminary determination supplied an early, estimate based basis for suspension of liquidation and cash deposits, and the final determination supplied the concluded Commerce finding on which the closing stages of the case depended. A negative final determination by the Department of Commerce ended the case under the same principle that governed a negative determination at any required point. An affirmative final determination did not by itself produce an order. The case passed instead to the final injury determination of the International Trade Commission, and the order depended on the outcome of that determination.

The Commission made its final injury determination within 120 days after the preliminary determination of the Department of Commerce or 45 days after the final determination of the Department of Commerce, whichever was later. The formulation tied the Commission deadline to both Commerce milestones and selected the later of the two resulting dates, a choice that reflected the ordered overlap built into the track from the simultaneous filing onward. At the final stage the Commission addressed the same statutory alternatives presented at the preliminary stage, material injury, threat of material injury, or material retardation of the establishment of an industry, but the final determination carried a different legal consequence. An affirmative final determination completed the set of required findings when the Department of Commerce had already reached an affirmative final determination. A negative final determination ended the case without an order, regardless of the margin or subsidy rate in the Commerce record.

An order issued about a week after an affirmative final injury determination by the Commission. The interval was brief because the substantive work of the investigation had been completed in the determinations themselves. The order was the instrument that gave operative form to the aligned findings, and the order marked the end of the investigative sequence that began with simultaneous petitions. Every stage before the order had a screening, estimating, or deciding function directed toward the question whether an order should issue at all. The Commerce final determination settled the conduct element for the investigation, and the Commission final determination settled the injury element. The order followed when, and only when, both settled elements were affirmative.

The measure at the center of the antidumping branch was the dumping margin. A dumping margin meant the amount by which normal value exceeded export price or constructed export price. The definition was comparative and arithmetic in form. Normal value supplied the benchmark figure, export price or constructed export price supplied the figure against which the benchmark was compared, and the margin was the excess of the benchmark over the comparison figure where such an excess existed. If normal value did not exceed the comparison price, the arithmetic that defined a margin produced no positive excess on that comparison. If normal value exceeded the comparison price, the size of the excess expressed the magnitude of the dumping found for the merchandise concerned. The margin thus performed two tasks in the administration of the statute. The margin recorded the outcome of the price comparison in a single percentage or amount suitable for use in the determination, and the margin supplied the estimated rate at which cash deposits were posted after an affirmative preliminary determination and the rate associated with the duty consequence when an order issued.

The presence of two comparison prices in the definition, export price and constructed export price, reflected provision for more than one transactional posture in which merchandise reached the United States. The two terms were not interchangeable labels for a single figure. Each term named a price concept that the administering authority applied under the governing provisions to the transactions before it, and the margin in either posture remained the amount by which normal value exceeded the applicable comparison price. Precision about the definition mattered because loose descriptions of dumping in general terms added elements the governing definition did not state in that form. The Department of Commerce calculated the margin from normal value and the applicable export price or constructed export price, and the resulting figure entered the determinations on which suspension, deposits, and any eventual order depended.

In the countervailing duty branch, the corresponding calculation produced a subsidy rate rather than a dumping margin. The administering authority determined whether a countervailable subsidy existed and calculated the rate that expressed the subsidy for the merchandise under investigation. That rate occupied the position in a countervailing duty case that the margin occupied in an antidumping case for purposes of the preliminary consequence and the final alignment of determinations. An affirmative Commerce determination on subsidization satisfied only the conduct element, and a negative injury determination ended a countervailing duty investigation as completely as a negative injury determination ended an antidumping investigation. The parallel structure explained why the two branches shared a procedural vocabulary of petitions, initiation, preliminary determinations, final determinations, suspension of liquidation, cash deposits, and orders, even though the conduct each branch addressed arose from a different source, pricing in one branch and a subsidy in the other.

Both branches applied de minimis rules that disregarded margins or rates beneath a stated threshold. In an antidumping investigation, a weighted average dumping margin below 2 percent was de minimis and disregarded. In a countervailing duty investigation, a countervailable subsidy rate below 1 percent was de minimis, and the threshold stood at 2 percent for developing countries. The rules performed a filtering function at the small end of the scale of calculated results. A calculation could proceed through the price comparison or the subsidy analysis and produce a positive figure, and the de minimis rule then determined whether a figure of that size counted for purposes of the investigation. Disregard of a de minimis margin meant that the small excess of normal value over export price or constructed export price did not sustain the consequence that a larger margin sustained. Disregard of a de minimis subsidy rate meant that a subsidy measured beneath the applicable threshold did not sustain the corresponding consequence in a countervailing duty case. The distinction between the 1 percent general threshold for subsidies and the 2 percent threshold for developing countries formed part of the governing rule and was not a discretionary adjustment available to the administering authority in an individual case.

The de minimis concept in Title VII investigations stood apart from the separate de minimis rule for low value shipments under section 321 of the Tariff Act of 1930, codified at 19 U.S.C. 1321, where the general threshold stood at 800 dollars of fair retail value in the country of shipment for articles imported by one person on one day. That threshold measured the value of a shipment. The Title VII thresholds measured a margin or a rate produced by an investigation.

Read as a whole, the Title VII calendar distributed decision across time as deliberately as the statute distributed decision across institutions. Each period placed a specific decision in specific hands: initiation within 20 days, preliminary injury within 45 days under the reasonable indication standard, a Commerce preliminary estimate at 65 days or 140 days depending on the branch, a Commerce final determination generally 75 days after the preliminary determination, and a final injury decision tied to the later of the two Commission periods tied to the Commerce milestones. The order, issuing about a week after an affirmative Commission final determination, closed the sequence. Administration under Title VII was a chain of dated determinations, each with an assigned author, an assigned standard or measure, and an assigned effect on the survival of the case.

## Three Tracks That End in Executive Discretion

Most of the tariff system operates through declarations, classifications, and computations that follow rules fixed in advance. The three remedies taken up in this section depart from that pattern at the decisive step. Each begins with an investigation conducted by a designated body, and each assembles a record and a recommendation through a structured statutory process. Each then places the final choice, whether to act at all and in what measure, in the hands of an executive decision maker exercising discretion within limits that vary sharply from one track to the next. The investigation disciplines the choice, defines the question the decision maker must answer, and fixes the outer bounds of the relief available, but the investigation does not itself impose a duty, a quota, or any other restriction. That separation between finding and remedy is the defining feature the three tracks share, and the differences among them, in trigger, in the role assigned to unfairness, in the identity of the investigator, and in the breadth of the discretion conferred, explain why Congress built three separate instruments rather than one general power to raise tariffs on executive say so.

The architectural point deserves emphasis because it reverses the sequence that governs an ordinary duty. Under an ordinary provision of the schedule, the legal action that established the rate came first, in a statute or a proclamation, and administration afterwards consists of applying that settled rate to particular goods. Under each of these three tracks, administration comes first and the rate change, if any, comes last. A domestic industry, a cabinet department, or the Trade Representative sets the machinery in motion, the investigating body develops the facts under a statutory standard, and only at the end does the discretionary decision determine whether the schedule will change for the goods in question, by how much, and for how long. Congress retained control through the standards, the timetables, and the ceilings written into each statute, and delegated the application of those standards, case by case, to the executive branch.

### Which tariff remedies require no finding of unfairness?

The safeguard remedy under sections 201 to 204 and the national security remedy under section 232 require no finding of unfairness. One rests on serious injury caused by increased imports, the other on effects on national security. Section 301, by contrast, addresses foreign acts, policies, or practices.

The safeguard track, set out in sections 201 to 204 of the Trade Act of 1974 and codified at 19 U.S.C. 2251 and following, addresses a problem that involves no accusation against anyone. Its trigger is a surge of imports and the injury that surge inflicts at home. The United States International Trade Commission investigates whether an article is imported in increased quantities so as to be a substantial cause of serious injury, or threat of serious injury, to the domestic industry. Every element of that formulation does work. The quantities must have increased. The increase must be a substantial cause, which places the imports among the important causes of the industry condition rather than treating any import presence during a period of decline as sufficient. The injury must be serious, a standard the statute sets apart from lesser measures of harm used elsewhere in the trade laws, and a threat of such injury, established on the record, can support a determination before the injury has fully arrived. The comparison runs to the domestic industry as a whole, so the inquiry concerns the condition of an industry and not the fortunes of a single firm within it.

No finding of unfairness is required at any point in that inquiry, and the absence is a deliberate feature of the design rather than an omission. Foreign producers whose goods enter under the safeguard track may have priced fairly and complied with every applicable agreement, and the investigation proceeds in exactly the same way, because the statute asks a question about effects rather than a question about conduct. Increased imports that arrive through ordinary competition, through a shift in consumer taste, through an exchange rate movement, or through the success of a more efficient foreign producer all stand on the same footing for purposes of the trigger, provided the statutory elements of quantity, causation, and serious injury are met. The remedy that follows is correspondingly framed as relief for an injured industry rather than as a penalty for a wrongful act, and that framing shapes both the duration of the relief and the expectation, built into the structure of sections 201 to 204, that relief serves as an interval during which the domestic industry adjusts to import competition rather than as a permanent exclusion of the competing goods.

The investigating body in a safeguard case is the International Trade Commission, an independent agency of six commissioners appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate for nine-year terms, with no more than three commissioners from one political party. That institutional placement matters to the character of the proceeding. The Commission asks the serious injury question as an adjudicative matter, on a record, under the statutory standard, and its determination either opens or closes the door to relief. An affirmative determination sends the matter to the President with the Commission work complete. A negative determination ends the case, because the discretionary stage never begins unless the statutory trigger has been found. The Commission does not set the remedy and does not weigh the wider consequences of granting relief. Those judgments belong to the second stage, and the statute reserves them to a different decision maker precisely so that the injury finding remains a finding, made by a body constituted for fact finding, rather than a policy verdict dressed in factual language.

The President decides the remedy in a safeguard case, and the choice among remedies is broad within the limits the statute sets. Relief may take the form of duty increases, quotas, tariff rate quotas, or adjustment assistance, and the selection among those instruments, and the calibration of any one of them, rests with the President rather than with the Commission that found the injury. A duty increase raises the price of the imported article across the board. A quota limits quantity directly. A tariff rate quota combines the two, admitting a stated quantity at one rate and applying a higher rate to imports above that quantity. Adjustment assistance directs support to the workers and firms of the injured industry rather than restricting the imports themselves, and a presidential response may rest on assistance where restriction is judged the worse instrument. The common feature is that each option, and the decision to grant relief at all, sits at the discretionary stage. The Commission determination establishes that the statutory conditions for relief exist. It does not establish that relief must follow, and it does not dictate the form relief takes if the President grants it.

Duration gives the safeguard remedy its final defining limit. Relief runs for an initial period of up to four years, and that period may be extended to a total of eight years. The ceiling reflects the adjustment logic of the whole track. Because no unfairness has been found, because the imports that caused the injury may have been traded entirely fairly, and because the remedy restricts trade that violated no rule, Congress wrote the authority as temporary from the outset, long enough for an industry to retool, retrain, reposition, or contract in an orderly way, and not long enough to convert an emergency interval into a permanent feature of the schedule. The eight year outer limit also disciplines the discretionary decision at the moment the President makes it, since a remedy chosen under sections 201 to 204 carries its own expiration within the statutory period rather than persisting until some later authority removes it, and that difference from an ordinary duty in the schedule traces directly back to the absence of any unfairness finding beneath the safeguard remedy.

The second track, national security adjustment under section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, codified at 19 U.S.C. 1862, proceeds from an entirely different trigger and confers a far wider discretion. Its home statute is the act of 1962, and the attribution matters, because section 232 predates the Trade Act of 1974 by more than a decade and belongs to an earlier statutory settlement in which Congress tied import adjustment to the defense posture of the United States. The question the section poses is not whether imports have injured an industry, seriously or otherwise, and not whether any foreign government or foreign producer has acted unfairly. The question is whether imports of an article affect national security in the manner the statute contemplates, so that adjustment of those imports becomes a measure of national defense administered through the tariff and related import controls. An industry may be profitable and growing and the section 232 question remains available, because the inquiry runs to the security consequences of dependence on foreign supply rather than to the commercial condition of the producers who filed or prompted the case.

The Department of Commerce conducts the section 232 investigation. The statute allows Commerce 270 days to investigate and report, a timetable that gives the department a defined period in which to assemble the record on the security question and deliver findings and recommendations to the President. The sequence then moves quickly. The President decides within 90 days of receiving the report, and implementation of a presidential decision to act follows within a further 15 days. Read together, the three intervals describe a proceeding built for deliberation first and speed at the end. The long investigative period accommodates the breadth of the inquiry, and the short decision and implementation periods then place the matter squarely before the President as a single, prompt, executive determination. The timetable itself expresses the allocation of authority that defines the section, under which the department investigates and recommends, and the President alone decides.

Presidential discretion under section 232 is very wide, wider than under the safeguard track in both the trigger and the remedy. No finding of unfairness is required, and no finding of serious injury to a domestic industry is required either, since the statutory question concerns national security rather than industrial condition. The President who concurs in a Commerce finding may adjust imports of the article, and the form and extent of that adjustment rest largely in presidential judgment under the terms of the section. Where the safeguard power operates inside explicit ceilings on duration and inside a remedial menu shaped by an adjustment rationale, the section 232 power answers to the security rationale that justified it, and that rationale sets a less determinate boundary. The contrast is structural rather than accidental. Congress in 1962 wrote a provision for contingencies that resist specification in advance, on the theory that threats to security arising through import dependence cannot be catalogued beforehand with the precision that an injury standard allows, and the breadth of the discretion is the direct consequence of that choice.

The third track, action against unfair foreign practices under section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2411 and following, restores unfairness to the center of the inquiry and relocates discretion once again. The subject of a section 301 proceeding is not an imported article in the abstract and not the condition of a domestic industry, but a foreign act, policy, or practice. The Office of the United States Trade Representative conducts the investigation. The Trade Representative sits within the Executive Office of the President, and the head of the office holds ambassador rank, appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate. That placement suits the character of the work, because a section 301 case is at once an investigation into foreign conduct and an exercise in negotiation with the government responsible for that conduct. The statute directs the Trade Representative, where a trade agreement applies, to consult and to seek dispute settlement under the agreement as part of the process, so the proceeding runs on two rails at once, building a record on the foreign practice while pressing the foreign government through the channels the applicable agreement provides.

Section 301 divides its cases between mandatory action and discretionary action, and the division is the most precise allocation of discretion among the three tracks. Action is mandatory in the defined cases, principally where the Trade Representative determines that the rights of the United States under a trade agreement are being denied, or that a foreign act, policy, or practice is unjustifiable and burdens or restricts United States commerce. In those cases the statute removes the choice whether to respond and leaves the administration of the response, its timing, its composition, and its calibration against the offending practice, to the Trade Representative acting under the statute. Where the case falls outside the mandatory categories, action is discretionary. A foreign act, policy, or practice that is unreasonable or discriminatory and burdens or restricts United States commerce may support action, and the Trade Representative decides whether to act and how. The vocabulary of the section therefore grades the foreign conduct. Denial of agreement rights and unjustifiable practices compel a response. Unreasonable or discriminatory practices permit one. The burden on United States commerce appears across the categories as the connecting requirement, tying the foreign conduct to a concrete effect on American trade rather than to disapproval of the conduct in the abstract.

The remedies available under section 301 match its character as an instrument aimed at foreign conduct rather than at imports as such. They include suspending concessions that the United States previously granted under a trade agreement and imposing duties or other import restrictions. Suspension of concessions withdraws, in measured form, benefits that the foreign country received through negotiation, which makes the remedy a reversal of a bargain rather than a freestanding penalty, calibrated to press the foreign government to change the practice that prompted the case. Imposed duties and import restrictions operate directly on trade, raising the cost of goods from the offending country or limiting their entry. Because the target of the proceeding is a practice, the remedy carries an inherent condition that the other two tracks lack in the same form. A safeguard remedy expires on a statutory clock, a section 232 adjustment answers to a security judgment, and a section 301 remedy answers to the conduct of a foreign government, and its rationale weakens or strengthens as that government maintains, modifies, or abandons the act, policy, or practice under investigation.

Placed side by side, the three tracks reveal how much institutional variety Congress built into the discretionary side of tariff administration. The safeguard track assigns the finding to an independent commission and the remedy to the President, demands serious injury caused by increased imports, and demands no unfairness from anyone. The national security track assigns the investigation to the Department of Commerce and the decision to the President under a security standard, on a timetable of 270 days for investigation, 90 days for decision, and 15 days for implementation, and it demands neither unfairness nor injury. The unfair practices track assigns the investigation and the action to the Trade Representative, grades its cases between mandatory and discretionary responses, and demands a foreign act, policy, or practice that fits one of the statutory categories and burdens United States commerce. Three of the principal tariff instruments therefore end in executive discretion rather than in an adjudicated finding that itself fixes the duty, and the discretion sits in different hands in each, with the President choosing the safeguard remedy on a commission finding, the President deciding the security adjustment on a departmental report, and the Trade Representative acting on foreign practices from within the Executive Office of the President.

A fourth variant once stood beside the three, and its history closes the account because Congress wrote it with an expiration date and allowed that date to arrive. Section 421 of the Trade Act of 1974 created a product specific safeguard for imports from China, added to the statute in 2000. As a safeguard, the section belonged to the family of sections 201 to 204, addressing imports of a particular product from a particular source rather than imports of the product from all sources, and it operated as a discretionary track in which an injury type finding opened the way to an executive decision on relief. The section expired in December 2013, and after that expiration the authority ceased to be available for new cases. The episode carries its own lesson about the architecture described in this section. A discretionary remedy exists because a statute creates it, on the terms the statute sets, for the period the statute allows, and when Congress fixes a term and declines to extend it, the instrument lapses completely, leaving the general safeguard, the security power, and the unfair practices authority to cover the ground that remains.

The statutory homes of the three tracks reward separate attention, in [the complete guide to the Trade Act of 1974](/2020/06/01/trade-act-1974-complete-guide/) for sections 201 to 204 and section 301, in [the guide to the Trade Expansion Act of 1962](/2020/08/01/trade-expansion-act-1962-guide/) for section 232, and in [the guide to the China permanent normal trade relations legislation](/2020/07/15/china-pntr-2000-guide/) for the enactment that added the section 421 safeguard that expired in December 2013.

## Duties Settled After the Fact: The Retrospective System

Most duty collection ends at the border. An importer presents entries, Customs and Border Protection classifies the goods and determines value and origin, a rate from the Harmonized Tariff Schedule applies, and liquidation closes the account. Antidumping and countervailing duties break that pattern in a way that has no close equivalent elsewhere in American tariff administration. For merchandise covered by an antidumping or countervailing duty order, the payment collected when goods enter the United States does not settle the obligation. The payment opens an account that remains provisional for months and, in contested or repeatedly reviewed cases, for years. Final liability is determined after importation, through a second administrative proceeding that looks backward at completed transactions and recalculates what the duty should have been. The Commerce Department stated the distinction in the text of its own regulation. Section 351.212(a) of title 19 of the Code of Federal Regulations opens by contrasting the United States system with the systems of other countries on exactly this point, a drafting choice that treats the contrast itself as the first fact an administrator, an importer, or an exporter needs to grasp.

The distinction matters because the two remedial duties rest on margins that move. A dumping margin measures the amount by which normal value exceeds export price or constructed export price, and a subsidy rate measures the benefit conferred by a countervailable subsidy. Neither figure describes a physical property of the merchandise. Each figure describes a relationship between prices, costs, and government programs during a defined period, and each figure can change when the period changes, when an exporter adjusts prices, when exchange conditions shift the comparison, or when a subsidy program alters its terms. The retrospective system accepts that difference and builds the collection machinery around it. Rather than fixing liability at entry on the basis of margins calculated during an investigation that preceded the entries, the system collects an estimate at entry and reserves the definitive calculation for a review of the entries themselves. Accuracy, as the administering authority defines accuracy, takes precedence over finality at the border. The price of that precedence is delay, and the delay falls on every participant in a covered transaction.

Cash deposits supply the provisional payment. After an order issues, and after each completed administrative review, Commerce sets a cash deposit rate and instructs Customs and Border Protection to collect deposits of estimated antidumping or countervailing duties at that rate as entries of covered merchandise occur. The word estimated carries legal weight in that instruction. A deposit is not an assessment. A deposit creates no entitlement for the government to retain the amount deposited, and a deposit creates no protection for the importer against a demand for more. The deposit functions as security collected in advance against a liability that a later proceeding will quantify. The mismatch between the period that generated the rate and the period to which the rate is applied is not an accident or an oversight. The mismatch is the mechanism by which the system operates, and every consequence described in the administration of an order traces back to that gap between the estimate collected and the liability eventually assessed.

Liquidation gives the gap its legal form. In ordinary customs administration, liquidation is the final computation of duties owed on an entry, performed by Customs and Border Protection, after which the entry closes subject to protest and reliquidation within defined limits. For entries covered by an antidumping or countervailing duty order, liquidation does not proceed on the ordinary timetable. Liquidation of covered entries is suspended while the order runs and while the possibility of an administrative review remains open. Suspension preserves the entries in an unliquidated state, with the deposit held and the rate undecided, until Commerce completes a review for the relevant period or until the opportunity to request a review passes without a request. Suspension is therefore more than a pause in paperwork. Suspension is the procedural device that keeps the government's claim and the importer's exposure alive across the interval between entry and assessment. With suspension, the entries wait, and the commercial parties to the transactions wait with the entries, holding contingent liabilities and contingent refunds on books that cannot close the covered period until the administering authority acts.

### Why is the duty paid at entry only an estimate?

The duty paid at entry is an estimate because cash deposits are collected at a rate set before the entries are examined in an administrative review. Final liability depends on the margins Commerce calculates for the period of review, and liquidation applies that later assessment rate.

The administrative review under section 751(a) of the Tariff Act of 1930, codified at 19 U.S.C. 1675(a), supplies the proceeding that converts estimates into assessments. The review is not automatic. In the anniversary month of an order, any interested party may request a review of entries covered during the period of review, and the annual rhythm of that request window structures the life of an order after the investigation ends. The anniversary anchor ties the review cycle to the order itself rather than to the calendar year, so orders issued in different months generate review seasons in different months, and a party participating under several orders faces several distinct request deadlines across a year. A request sets Commerce to work recalculating margins for the exporters and producers covered by the review, using the transactions of the period of review rather than the transactions examined in the investigation or in an earlier review. The recalculation is retrospective in the strict sense. Commerce examines sales that have already occurred, at prices already charged, for merchandise already entered, and determines the margin that those sales produced. The review cannot change the transactions. The review can only measure the transactions and translate the measurement into a duty.

A completed review produces two rates, and the separation between the two rates explains much of the confusion that the system generates for parties encountering an order for the first time. The first rate is the assessment rate. Commerce applies the assessment rate to the entries covered by the review, and Customs and Border Protection liquidates those entries at that rate when Commerce issues its instructions following the review. If the assessment rate exceeds the rate at which deposits were collected, the importer owes the difference, with interest. If the assessment rate falls below the deposit rate, the importer receives a refund of the excess deposit, with interest. The second rate is the cash deposit rate for future entries. Commerce sets the deposit rate for merchandise entered after the review on the basis of the margins calculated in the review, so the freshest measurement of an exporter's pricing or of a subsidy benefit becomes the estimate applied to the next interval of trade. One proceeding thus looks in two directions. The assessment rate settles the past period on measured facts, and the deposit rate projects the measurement forward as the provisional price of entry until another review measures the next period.

The default rule for periods without a review gives the deposit a second character. Where no interested party requests an administrative review for a period, duties are assessed at the cash deposit rate in effect when the merchandise entered. In that circumstance the estimate hardens into the assessment by operation of the default, not because Commerce examined the entries and confirmed the rate, but because no party invoked the proceeding that would have tested the rate. Liquidation then proceeds at the deposit rate, and the period closes. The default rule allocates the burden of action with precision. An importer or exporter that believes the deposit rate overstates the margin for a period must request a review and participate in the recalculation, or the overstated rate becomes the final rate. A domestic producer that believes the deposit rate understates the margin faces the same choice from the opposite direction. Silence ratifies the estimate. The estimate is therefore provisional in law and conditional in practice, provisional for any period a party chooses to reopen and final for any period that passes without a request.

New shipper reviews address a gap that the annual cycle would otherwise leave open. An exporter that did not ship covered merchandise during the original investigation, and that begins exporting after an order issues, enters the market without a rate of its own. Under the order, entries of that exporter's merchandise would carry a deposit rate calculated for other firms, derived from transactions in which the new exporter played no part. A new shipper review lets a new exporter obtain its own rate, calculated from that exporter's own sales. The device matters for the coherence of the retrospective system. A system that justifies retrospective assessment by the claim that liability should rest on measured transactions of the party assessed loses coherence if a party with no measured transactions remains indefinitely subject to a rate drawn from the conduct of competitors. The new shipper review extends the measurement principle to entrants, at the cost of another proceeding layered onto the administration of the order. For the new exporter, the proceeding offers the only path to a deposit rate, and eventually to assessment rates, that reflect its own pricing rather than an average or a rate assigned to established shippers.

Sunset reviews operate on a longer cycle and ask a different question. Under section 751(c) of the Tariff Act, every order faces review at intervals of five years, in a proceeding in which Commerce and the International Trade Commission decide whether revocation of the order would be likely to lead to continuation or recurrence of dumping or subsidization and of material injury. The administrative review under section 751(a) measures what happened during a past period and sets rates accordingly. The sunset review under section 751(c) projects what would happen after revocation and decides whether the order survives at all. Commerce addresses the dumping or subsidization side of the projection, and the Commission addresses the injury side, and revocation requires the likelihood showing to fail, while an order that clears the review continues in force for another five year interval. An order can therefore persist across decades through successive sunset reviews, with annual administrative reviews adjusting rates within the order and five year reviews renewing the order itself. The two review tracks interlock. Administrative reviews assume the order continues and refine the duty. Sunset reviews test the assumption and decide whether refinement has an object to refine.

Interest completes the financial architecture of deferred settlement. Under 19 U.S.C. 1677g, interest accrues on overpayments and underpayments of estimated duties. When a review sets an assessment rate above the deposit rate, the importer pays the shortfall plus interest on the shortfall, compensating the government for the interval during which the full liability went uncollected. When a review sets an assessment rate below the deposit rate, the government refunds the excess plus interest on the excess, compensating the importer for the interval during which funds sat in government hands beyond the amount ultimately owed. The symmetry of the provision reflects the structure of the system. A regime that deliberately collects an estimate and settles the true amount years later must price the time value of the difference in both directions, or the delay itself becomes a penalty on one side or a subsidy to the other. Interest does not remove the burden of the delay. Interest converts an unpriced burden into a priced one, and the price accumulates for as long as liquidation waits on review.

The prospective systems used by most other major trading partners resolve the same problem at the opposite end. Under a prospective system, the duty assessed at entry is the duty owed, and the amount paid when goods cross the border is final. An administering authority in a prospective system may revisit the level of a measure through refunds procedures or through reviews that adjust duties for future entries, but the liability for an entry closes when the entry clears, at the rate applied at that moment. The importer in a prospective system buys certainty at the price of whatever inaccuracy the entry rate contains. The importer in the American system buys measured accuracy at the price of certainty, and the exchange is compulsory. The Commerce regulation that opens on the international contrast, 19 C.F.R. 351.212(a), frames the two approaches as systemic alternatives rather than as variations in technique, and the framing holds up under examination of the consequences. Prospective assessment treats the duty as a border charge. Retrospective assessment treats the duty as an account, opened at the border and settled by audit.

The commercial consequence follows directly from that treatment, and the consequence deserves statement without softening. An importer of covered merchandise cannot know its true liability on the day goods enter, and in a reviewed period the importer cannot know that liability for a span that extends well beyond delivery, beyond resale, and sometimes beyond the useful commercial life of the transaction itself. Goods are priced, sold, and consumed while the duty on the goods remains an estimate. A supply contract negotiated under an order therefore prices more than merchandise, freight, and a known tariff line. The contract prices risk. The parties must allocate between buyer and seller the possibility that the assessment rate will exceed the deposit rate, the possibility that a refund will materialize if the assessment rate falls below the deposit rate, and the financing cost that interest under 19 U.S.C. 1677g attaches to either outcome. Contract language on duty adjustment, on cooperation in administrative reviews, on the ownership of refunds, and on responsibility for additional assessments becomes a standard feature of covered trade because the statute leaves the central cost figure open. An importer that fails to secure such language carries the exposure alone, and an exporter that accepts such language carries exposure for pricing decisions made during a period that a review will judge long after the commercial relationship for that period has closed.

The exposure also shapes behavior during the period of review itself, in ways that a prospective system does not replicate. Because Commerce recalculates margins from the transactions of the period, pricing decisions made while an order runs are never merely commercial decisions. An exporter's price in a given month becomes evidence in a later review and a component of a later margin, and an importer purchasing at that price acquires both merchandise and a contingent duty position tied to the price. Parties that understand the system conduct covered trade with the review in view, aware that the assessment will revisit the period and that the deposit collected at entry offers no shelter against an adverse recalculation. Parties that misunderstand the system, or that treat the deposit receipt as a settled duty payment in the manner of an ordinary tariff, discover the error at liquidation, when instructions from Commerce convert years of deposits into assessments at a different rate and Customs and Border Protection bills or refunds the difference with interest.

Administration of an order thus never reaches a resting point while trade continues. Each period of review generates a request window in the anniversary month of the order. Each completed review resets deposits for the entries that follow and settles assessments for the entries that preceded. Each five year interval generates a sunset review that tests the order's survival. A new exporter can interrupt the pattern with a new shipper review and emerge with a rate of its own, after which the annual cycle absorbs that exporter into the regular sequence of recalculation. The unit of administration is never the product alone. The unit of administration is the product as sold by a particular exporter during a particular period, measured after the fact and charged at a rate particular to that measurement. That granularity is the retrospective system's claim to precision, and the same granularity explains the administrative weight that the system places on Commerce, on Customs and Border Protection, and on the parties that must participate in reviews to protect positions that deposits alone cannot protect.

A final measure of the system lies in what liquidation means when liquidation at last occurs. For an ordinary entry, liquidation is a computation. For a covered entry that passed through an administrative review, liquidation is the execution of a judgment formed in a separate proceeding, applying a rate that did not exist when the goods entered, to transactions that the parties completed without knowledge of the rate, with interest bridging the interval between estimate and settlement. The sequence inverts the ordinary expectations of a tax collected at a border. Liability precedes knowledge of liability, payment precedes determination of the amount, and finality arrives last, after the commerce that generated the duty has run its course. The United States has administered antidumping and countervailing duties on that inverted sequence as a matter of deliberate design, stated in the opening lines of the governing regulation as a conscious departure from the practice of other countries. Every feature of the administration follows from the departure: the deposit that estimates, the suspension that waits, the review that recalculates, the default that ratifies silence, the new shipper proceeding that individualizes, the sunset proceeding that renews, and the interest provision that prices the wait. An account of tariff administration that treated the deposit at entry as the duty would misdescribe all of it. The duty, under an American order, is the figure that liquidation applies, and liquidation speaks at the end of the process rather than at the beginning.

## Review in a Specialist Court, and the Question of Who Pays

Administration of the tariff laws does not end with a decision by an agency. A classification ruling, a denied protest, a dumping margin, and a subsidy rate all alter the amount an importer must pay on goods that have crossed the border, and a system that collects revenue through thousands of individualized determinations requires a place where an affected party can test whether a determination rested on the record and on the statute. Congress placed that function in a specialist court rather than leaving import disputes scattered among district courts applying local practice to national questions. Tariff classification turns on the structure of the Harmonized Tariff Schedule and on the General Rules of Interpretation. Antidumping and countervailing duty determinations turn on constructed values, price adjustments, questionnaires answered by foreign firms, and findings about the condition of a domestic industry.

The United States Court of International Trade is an Article III court seated in New York and created in its modern form by the Customs Courts Act of 1980. Article III status places the court within the judicial branch, with the independence that attends a federal court, rather than within an executive department or a tribunal housed inside an agency whose decisions come under review. The Customs Courts Act of 1980 gave the institution its modern name, jurisdiction, and remedial powers, replacing an older customs court structure with a trial court designed to hear the full range of civil actions arising from the import laws.

Exclusive jurisdiction is defined by 28 U.S.C. 1581, and the structure of that section maps the principal routes by which an administrative tariff decision reaches a judge. An importer with a claim within the section cannot choose a district court instead, and an agency determination within the section is reviewed in the Court of International Trade or not at all at the trial level. The statute divides that exclusive jurisdiction by the kind of action involved, and the subdivisions matter because each corresponds to a different administrative path and a different relationship between the court and the agency decision under challenge.

Subsection (a) of 28 U.S.C. 1581 covers actions contesting denied protests. The protest is the administrative mechanism by which an importer challenges a decision of Customs and Border Protection, including decisions about classification, rate of duty, and liquidation, after the agency has acted on an entry. Denial of a protest, in whole or in part, converts an internal dispute within the customs process into a justiciable action, and subsection (a) supplies the jurisdictional basis for bringing that action in the Court of International Trade. The importer first presents the claim to the agency that made the decision, through a procedure governed by 19 U.S.C. 1514 and subject to a filing period of 180 days, and judicial review follows an adverse administrative outcome rather than displacing the agency process at the outset.

Subsection (c) of 28 U.S.C. 1581 covers a different category, actions brought under section 516A of the Tariff Act of 1930, codified at 19 U.S.C. 1516a, reviewing antidumping and countervailing duty determinations. Title VII determinations differ from ordinary customs decisions in structure, and the difference explains the separate jurisdictional grant. An antidumping or countervailing duty order rests on determinations by two agencies, the Department of Commerce on dumping or subsidization and the International Trade Commission on material injury, and section 516A defines which determinations may be challenged, which parties may challenge them, and the timing for bringing an action. Parties on more than one side of an investigation can appear, because a determination that favors domestic producers can be challenged by foreign exporters and importers, and a determination that ends an investigation or produces a low margin can be challenged by the domestic industry that filed the petition.

Subsection (i) of 28 U.S.C. 1581 operates as residual jurisdiction over administration of the import laws. Subsection (i) grants jurisdiction over civil actions against the United States, its agencies, or its officers arising out of a law that provides for revenue from imports or for administration of tariff and related requirements, where the more specific subsections do not supply a basis. Without a residual category, a claimant could fall between enumerated grants and face an argument that no court held power to hear the case, an outcome at odds with the purpose of concentrating jurisdiction in one court.

The standard that the court applies in section 516A cases is stated by 19 U.S.C. 1516a(b). Under that provision, the court holds unlawful any determination, finding, or conclusion found to be unsupported by substantial evidence on the record, or otherwise not in accordance with law. The sentence contains two distinct tests. The substantial evidence test directs attention to the administrative record, meaning the information, documents, and explanations that were before the agency, and asks whether that record contains evidence that a reasonable decision maker could accept as adequate to support the conclusion reached. The accordance with law test directs attention to the statute, to regulations, and to legal requirements governing procedure and interpretation, and asks whether the agency acted within the authority and according to the methods that the law prescribes. Section 1516a(b) makes both defects grounds for holding a determination unlawful.

In operation, the standard is deferential, and deference does not amount to abdication. The court reviews the record that the agency created rather than conducting a fresh investigation, taking new evidence, or recalculating a dumping margin according to the preference of an individual judge. Where the record supports more than one reasonable conclusion, evidence pointing toward an opposite result does not by itself render the agency conclusion unsupported, because the test evaluates the conclusion against the whole record rather than asking whether the court would have weighed the evidence differently. The common outcome of a successful challenge is a remand for a fuller explanation. On remand, the agency must explain a step in reasoning found inadequately supported, address evidence that the original determination passed over, correct a legal error and reconsider the affected conclusion, or supply the analysis connecting the facts in the record to the result. The agency then issues a remand determination, which returns to the court for review and can be sustained, or remanded again if the explanation remains deficient. Remand for explanation, far more often than substitution of judgment, is the characteristic pattern, because the defect most often identified concerns the adequacy of support for a decision that the statute commits to an agency.

Appeal from the Court of International Trade lies to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, under 28 U.S.C. 1295(a)(5), with certiorari to the Supreme Court beyond that court. The Federal Circuit holds exclusive appellate jurisdiction over these cases, completing the pattern of concentration that begins at trial. One trial court with exclusive jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. 1581 feeds one court of appeals with exclusive appellate jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. 1295(a)(5), so that doctrine on classification, valuation, dumping, and subsidization develops within a single appellate body rather than diverging among regional circuits. Beyond the Federal Circuit, review depends on a petition for certiorari to the Supreme Court, a discretionary step granted in a small share of cases, so that for most tariff litigation the Federal Circuit supplies the final judicial word.

Against that structure stands an asymmetry that defines the limits of judicial review in tariff law. Presidential determinations on the discretionary tracks stand, in practice, almost entirely outside the review structure described in 19 U.S.C. 1516a(b) and 28 U.S.C. 1581. The discretionary tracks include safeguard action under sections 201 to 204 of the Trade Act of 1974, national security action under section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, and action against foreign practices under section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974. On each track, an agency investigates and reports, and the President decides whether to act and what remedy to impose. That decision is a presidential determination, an exercise of discretion that Congress framed in broad terms and committed to the head of the executive branch, and the review provisions governing Commerce and Commission determinations in Title VII cases supply no equivalent mechanism for a court to weigh the evidence behind a presidential decision or to hold that decision unlawful for lack of substantial evidence. Litigation touching the discretionary tracks has reached questions of procedure, including whether required steps and deadlines were observed, and section 232 litigation reached the merits of procedure, not the finding of the President. On the Title VII track, the substantive judgment of two agencies is reviewable on the record under a statutory standard. On the discretionary tracks, that judgment stands largely beyond such review.

### Who pays a tariff in the end?

The importer of record pays the duty to Customs and Border Protection as a matter of law. The economic cost falls more broadly, and in the 2018 episode studied by economists, passed through completely into domestic prices, leaving domestic consumers and importing firms to bear the burden.

The distinction between the legal answer and the economic answer organizes the incidence research. The administrative answer is fully determined by the collection system. A tariff is collected at the border by Customs and Border Protection, and the party liable is the importer of record, the person or firm that brings goods into the United States and bears legal responsibility for duties and for reasonable care in classification and valuation under 19 U.S.C. 1484. The importer of record writes the check to Customs and Border Protection. No statute directs the foreign exporter to remit the duty, and the mechanics of entry, deposit, and liquidation all run against the domestic importing party. The economic question begins after payment and asks what happens to prices as the cost imposed at the border works through later transactions. An importer can absorb the cost within a margin, pass the cost forward to the next buyer, negotiate a lower price from the foreign supplier, or combine those responses. A foreign exporter that values continued access to the United States market can reduce the price received so that the duty inclusive price changes by less than the full duty, while an exporter with alternative markets or a product that buyers cannot readily replace has less reason to make that concession. The division of the burden turns on the flexibility of buyers and sellers, the availability of substitutes, and the capacity of each side to turn elsewhere. A tariff is passed through when the duty inclusive domestic price rises by the amount of the duty, and absorbed on the foreign side when the price received by the exporter falls.

Mary Amiti, Stephen J. Redding, and David E. Weinstein, in "The Impact of the 2018 Tariffs on Prices and Welfare", published in the Journal of Economic Perspectives in 2019, examined the 2018 tariff increases and found that the increases passed through completely into the domestic prices of imported goods. Complete pass-through, as Mary Amiti, Stephen J. Redding, and David E. Weinstein reported in the Journal of Economic Perspectives in 2019, means that prices paid on the domestic side rose by the full amount of the tariffs, so that the full incidence fell on domestic consumers and importers. The prices received by foreign exporters did not fall. Mary Amiti, Stephen J. Redding, and David E. Weinstein estimated in the Journal of Economic Perspectives in 2019 that the increases had reduced aggregate United States real income by 1.4 billion dollars per month by the end of 2018.

Pablo D. Fajgelbaum, Pinelopi K. Goldberg, Patrick J. Kennedy, and Amit K. Khandelwal reached a consistent conclusion in "The Return to Protectionism", published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2020. Pablo D. Fajgelbaum, Pinelopi K. Goldberg, Patrick J. Kennedy, and Amit K. Khandelwal found in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2020 that prices of imports targeted by the tariffs did not fall before duty. A price measured before duty is the price received by the foreign side, excluding the tariff itself. If that price remains unchanged while a duty is added, the duty inclusive price rises by the full amount of the duty, which is complete pass-through stated from the exporter side. Pablo D. Fajgelbaum, Pinelopi K. Goldberg, Patrick J. Kennedy, and Amit K. Khandelwal measured losses to United States consumers and firms buying imports at 51 billion dollars in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2020, about 0.27 percent of gross domestic product, including households purchasing finished goods and firms purchasing imported inputs. After accounting for tariff revenue and gains to domestic producers, Pablo D. Fajgelbaum, Pinelopi K. Goldberg, Patrick J. Kennedy, and Amit K. Khandelwal estimated an aggregate real income loss of 7.2 billion dollars in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2020, about 0.04 percent of gross domestic product. The distance between the two figures is the distance between a distribution question and an aggregate question. Much of what buyers lost was transferred rather than destroyed, moving to the Treasury as revenue and to domestic producers as higher returns. The remaining 7.2 billion dollars represents the estimated loss that no domestic recipient captured.

Read together, the two studies support a consumer cost case and a protection case, each with named attribution. The consumer cost case rests on the price findings of Mary Amiti, Stephen J. Redding, and David E. Weinstein in the Journal of Economic Perspectives in 2019, showing that buyers paid the 2018 increases through higher prices rather than sharing the cost with foreign suppliers, and on the buyer loss of 51 billion dollars measured by Pablo D. Fajgelbaum, Pinelopi K. Goldberg, Patrick J. Kennedy, and Amit K. Khandelwal in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2020, a figure that includes firms buying imported inputs. The protection case rests on elements that the same accounting makes visible. Pablo D. Fajgelbaum, Pinelopi K. Goldberg, Patrick J. Kennedy, and Amit K. Khandelwal counted gains to domestic producers as a real offset in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2020, and tariff revenue as a real receipt of the government, so a judgment favoring protection can point to identifiable domestic beneficiaries, including firms and workers in industries sheltered from import competition and a Treasury that collects revenue at the border. A judgment from the buyer side can point to identifiable domestic payers and to a net aggregate loss that remained after the gains were counted. Neither judgment can honestly claim that the other side of the ledger was absent from the measurements.

The counter-reading begins with limits that both studies carry on the face of the research design. Mary Amiti, Stephen J. Redding, and David E. Weinstein in the Journal of Economic Perspectives in 2019 and Pablo D. Fajgelbaum, Pinelopi K. Goldberg, Patrick J. Kennedy, and Amit K. Khandelwal in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2020 each measure one episode, the tariff increases of 2018, over a short window. A short window captures the first adjustments that buyers and sellers make under contracts, inventories, and supply arrangements inherited from the period before a tariff, and those first adjustments need not predict adjustments that emerge after contracts expire, after buyers qualify new suppliers, after exporters build or abandon capacity for the United States market, and after firms reorganize production across borders. A finding of complete pass-through in 2018 answers who bore the 2018 increases during the period observed, and extending the result to every tariff, on every product, from every supplying country, and at every horizon would claim more than the evidence in either study contains.

Three mechanisms can shift incidence away from the pattern measured for 2018, and each requires full strength. Exporters can price to market, a practice under which a seller sets different prices for different national markets and absorbs part of a tariff in the market where the tariff applies in order to defend market share, accepting a lower margin on sales to the United States rather than losing volume. Where exporters price to market, the price received falls and part of the incidence moves abroad, in a degree that depends on competition and the importance of the market to the seller. Exchange rates supply a second mechanism. Movement in the value of the dollar against the currency of a supplying country changes the dollar price of imports independently of any tariff, and depreciation of a foreign currency can offset part of a duty by reducing the dollar cost of goods before duty, while appreciation can compound the duty. Longer horizons supply the third mechanism, because time allows substitution that a short window forecloses. Buyers develop sources in countries not subject to a duty, exporters reroute or reconfigure production, and domestic capacity expands or contracts, each response altering the bargaining position from which prices are set.

The terms-of-trade argument gives the counter-reading its strongest theoretical form, and the relationship between that argument and the 2018 evidence must be stated exactly. A country that accounts for a large share of world demand for a product can, in theory, use a tariff to press down the price that foreign suppliers receive, because suppliers facing reduced demand from a major buyer may accept lower prices rather than lose sales. The evidentiary point from 2018 is narrower. Mary Amiti, Stephen J. Redding, and David E. Weinstein found in the Journal of Economic Perspectives in 2019 that prices received by foreign exporters did not fall, and Pablo D. Fajgelbaum, Pinelopi K. Goldberg, Patrick J. Kennedy, and Amit K. Khandelwal found in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2020 that targeted import prices did not fall before duty, so the terms-of-trade gain was not found in the window that both studies observed. A gain not found in one window is not a gain proved impossible. The studies establish a finding about 2018, not a law of tariffs, and no rule requires the same division of burden to recur whenever a duty is imposed. For the increases measured, domestic consumers and importers bore the full incidence and the country sustained a net real income loss after revenue and producer gains were counted. For tariffs in general, incidence remains contingent on pricing behavior, currency movements, and the horizon over which adjustment occurs.

The gap between statutory authority and delivered policy runs through the whole structure described in the preceding sections. Congress enacts a schedule and a set of remedy statutes, and delivered policy emerges only after four agencies have applied four different standards to four different kinds of question. Customs and Border Protection decides classification, value, and origin at entry. Commerce calculates a margin or a subsidy rate from transactions in a defined period. The International Trade Commission decides injury to a domestic industry and keeps the schedule published. The Trade Representative investigates the conduct of a foreign government. A deferential court reviews agency determinations on the record under the substantial evidence standard, remanding for explanation far more often than substituting judgment, while presidential decisions on the discretionary tracks stand outside that review structure almost entirely. Authority in a statute and policy delivered at a port are therefore separated by design.

Retrospective assessment widens that gap in time as the division of labor widens the gap across institutions. A deposit collected at entry estimates a liability that an administrative review can recalculate years after the goods have been sold, and liquidation speaks at the end of the process rather than at the beginning. The return to the namable claim follows from that machinery rather than from any slogan about trade. Importers write the check. The importer of record bears legal responsibility for duties under 19 U.S.C. 1484, pays the deposit, and bore the full incidence of the 2018 increases in the measurements reported by Mary Amiti, Stephen J. Redding, and David E. Weinstein in the Journal of Economic Perspectives in 2019 and by Pablo D. Fajgelbaum, Pinelopi K. Goldberg, Patrick J. Kennedy, and Amit K. Khandelwal in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2020, each study confined to the 2018 episode over a short window.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who actually collects duties under US tariff law?

Customs and Border Protection collects duties under US tariff law. The agency sits in the Department of Homeland Security, where the function has belonged since March 2003 under the Homeland Security Act of 2002, and the agency descends from the Customs Service founded in 1789 in the Treasury Department. At entry the agency classifies goods under the Harmonized Tariff Schedule, determines customs value and origin, and receives payment from the importer of record. The agency also holds cash deposits where a trade remedy order applies and liquidates each entry as the final computation of duties owed. Collection by the border agency differs from rate setting, because rates are established by United States legal action, by statute or by proclamation, and no collection function creates a rate.

### Q: Which agency decides dumping margins in tariff law?

The Department of Commerce decides dumping margins in tariff law. Commerce acts through the International Trade Administration, and the Enforcement and Compliance unit serves as the statutory administering authority charged with the calculation. A dumping margin is the amount by which normal value exceeds export price or constructed export price, and Commerce calculates that amount for the merchandise and period under investigation or review. The same administering authority calculates subsidy rates in countervailing duty cases. Margin calculation is only one half of a title VII case. The International Trade Commission separately determines material injury, threat of material injury, or material retardation, and an order requires affirmative determinations from both agencies. A negative determination by either body ends the case.

### Q: What must be proven for an antidumping duty in tariff law?

An antidumping duty requires two affirmative determinations by two different bodies. The Department of Commerce must determine that merchandise was sold at less than fair value, expressed as a dumping margin equal to the amount by which normal value exceeds export price or constructed export price. The International Trade Commission must separately determine material injury, threat of material injury, or material retardation of the establishment of a domestic industry. Neither determination substitutes for the other. A negative determination by Commerce removes the conduct element, and a negative determination by the Commission removes the injury element, and either negative result ends the case without an order. A weighted average dumping margin below 2 percent is de minimis and disregarded.

### Q: Why is US tariff law retrospective?

US tariff law is retrospective for antidumping and countervailing duties because final liability is determined after importation rather than at entry. Regulation 19 C.F.R. 351.212(a) opens by contrasting the United States system with the systems of other countries on exactly that point. Cash deposits collected at entry are estimates based on a rate set for an earlier period, and liquidation of covered entries is suspended pending review. In an administrative review under section 751(a), Commerce recalculates margins from transactions in the period of review, and the resulting assessment rate fixes the amount applied at liquidation. Interest accrues on overpayments and underpayments under 19 U.S.C. 1677g. Most other major trading systems assess such duties prospectively, so the deposit paid at entry is final.

### Q: Who pays a tariff under tariff law?

The importer of record pays a tariff as a matter of law. That party bears legal responsibility for duties and for reasonable care in classification and valuation under 19 U.S.C. 1484, and Customs and Border Protection collects payment, deposits, and any later additional assessment from that party. The economic burden is a separate question. Mary Amiti, Stephen J. Redding, and David E. Weinstein reported in the Journal of Economic Perspectives in 2019 that the 2018 tariff increases passed through completely into domestic prices, with the full incidence on domestic consumers and importers and no fall in prices received by foreign exporters. Pablo D. Fajgelbaum, Pinelopi K. Goldberg, Patrick J. Kennedy, and Amit K. Khandelwal reached a consistent pass through finding in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2020.

### Q: What court reviews tariff law decisions?

The United States Court of International Trade reviews tariff law decisions. The court is an Article III court seated in New York and created in modern form by the Customs Courts Act of 1980, with exclusive jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. 1581 over civil actions arising from the import laws. Subsection (a) covers actions contesting denied protests, subsection (c) covers actions under section 516A of the Tariff Act reviewing antidumping and countervailing duty determinations, and subsection (i) supplies residual jurisdiction over administration of the import laws. Under 19 U.S.C. 1516a(b), the court holds unlawful a determination unsupported by substantial evidence on the record or otherwise not in accordance with law. Appeal lies to the Federal Circuit under 28 U.S.C. 1295(a)(5), with certiorari to the Supreme Court beyond.

### Q: What is de minimis in tariff law?

De minimis in tariff law at the border is the duty free admission of low value articles under section 321 of the Tariff Act of 1930, codified at 19 U.S.C. 1321. The provision lets the Secretary admit articles free of duty where collection would cost the government expense disproportionate to the revenue. The general threshold stands at 800 dollars of fair retail value in the country of shipment, for articles imported by one person on one day. The Trade Facilitation and Trade Enforcement Act of 2015 raised the threshold from 200 dollars, effective in March 2016. A separate use of the term in investigations disregards a dumping margin below 2 percent and a subsidy rate below 1 percent.

### Q: What is the harmonized schedule in tariff law?

The harmonized schedule in tariff law is the Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States, the enacted schedule that sets out classification and rates of duty for imports. Subtitle B of title I of the Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988, Public Law 100-418, enacted the schedule effective January 1, 1989, replacing the Tariff Schedules of the United States. Section 1204(a), codified at 19 U.S.C. 3004(a), performs the enactment, and section 1207, codified at 19 U.S.C. 3007, directs the International Trade Commission to compile, publish, and keep the schedule up to date. The schedule rests on the World Customs Organization Harmonized System, with chapters 1 to 97, six digit international nomenclature, eight digit United States rate lines, classification under the General Rules of Interpretation, and three columns of rates.

### Q: How is a customs duty calculated on an imported product?

A customs duty is calculated from three determinations made at entry. Classification places the product under a heading and subheading of the Harmonized Tariff Schedule under the General Rules of Interpretation, applied top down, and fixes the rate line. Customs value supplies the base for the rate and rests principally on transaction value, the price paid or payable under 19 U.S.C. 1401a. Origin, determined under the substantial transformation test, fixes the treatment and selects between column 1 general rates for normal trade relations sources, column 1 special rates under preference programs and free trade agreements, and column 2 rates for sources denied normal trade relations treatment. Customs and Border Protection tests the importer declaration on each element, and liquidation states the final computation.

### Q: What is a cash deposit rate in an antidumping case?

A cash deposit rate in an antidumping case is the estimated rate at which Customs and Border Protection collects deposits of estimated duties on covered entries. After an affirmative Commerce preliminary determination, liquidation is suspended and importers post cash deposits at the estimated margin. After an order issues, and after each completed administrative review, Commerce sets the deposit rate for entries that follow from the margins calculated in the investigation or review. A deposit is an estimate rather than a final duty. Final liability depends on the assessment rate produced by an administrative review for the period of review, and the difference between deposit and assessment is billed or refunded at liquidation, with interest under 19 U.S.C. 1677g.

### Q: What happens when the injury determination in a trade case is negative?

A negative injury determination ends a title VII trade case without an order. The International Trade Commission must determine material injury, threat of material injury, or material retardation of the establishment of a domestic industry before an antidumping or countervailing duty order can issue, and that requirement stands beside the separate Commerce determination on dumping or subsidization. A negative preliminary injury determination, made within 45 days of the petition under the reasonable indication standard, ends the investigation at the screening stage. A negative final injury determination ends the case after Commerce has reached a final determination, regardless of the size of the margin or subsidy rate calculated. Neither agency supplies the determination assigned to the other body.

### Q: How long does an antidumping investigation take from petition to order?

An antidumping investigation runs on a sequence of dated stages from petition to order. The petition is filed simultaneously with Commerce and the International Trade Commission. Commerce decides initiation generally within 20 days, and the Commission makes a preliminary injury determination within 45 days of the petition. The Commerce preliminary determination falls 140 days after initiation, extendable to 190 days, and an affirmative preliminary determination brings suspension of liquidation and cash deposits. The Commerce final determination follows generally 75 days after the preliminary determination, extendable to 135 days in antidumping cases. The Commission final injury determination falls within 120 days after the Commerce preliminary determination or 45 days after the Commerce final determination, whichever is later, and an order issues about a week after an affirmative Commission final determination.

### Q: What is a sunset review of an antidumping duty order?

A sunset review is the five year review of an antidumping duty order under section 751(c) of the Tariff Act of 1930. Commerce and the International Trade Commission decide whether revocation of the order would be likely to lead to continuation or recurrence of dumping or subsidization and of material injury. Commerce addresses the dumping or subsidization side of that projection and the Commission addresses the injury side. An order that clears the review continues in force for another five year interval, and successive sunset reviews can extend an order across decades. The sunset question differs from an administrative review, which measures transactions in a past period and sets assessment and deposit rates.

### Q: How can an importer challenge a customs classification decision?

An importer challenges a customs classification decision first through the protest procedure and then in court. Customs and Border Protection decides classification at entry, testing the importer declaration against the General Rules of Interpretation, and liquidation states the final computation of duties for the entry. Under 19 U.S.C. 1514, an importer may protest a liquidation or a classification decision within 180 days. The protest presents the dispute inside the customs system on the record of the entry. Denial of a protest, in whole or in part, converts the dispute into an action in the Court of International Trade under 28 U.S.C. 1581(a). Before entry, the separate ruling procedure supplies an advance answer, because Customs and Border Protection issues binding rulings on described merchandise.

### Q: What does substantial transformation mean for country of origin?

Substantial transformation is the test that determines country of origin for customs purposes. Origin asks where merchandise last underwent a process that changed the character of the merchandise to the point that a new article, with a distinct name, character, or use, emerged from the processing. Mere passage through a country or operations that leave the essential character of the components untouched do not produce a new origin under that test. Customs and Border Protection determines origin as part of administration of an entry. The determination drives rate treatment under the columns of the schedule and under preference arrangements, and the origin also supplies the country that marking must name under 19 U.S.C. 1304 for articles of foreign origin imported into the United States.

### Q: What is liquidation in customs administration?

Liquidation in customs administration is the final computation of duties owed on an entry, performed by Customs and Border Protection. Before liquidation, amounts paid or deposited retain a provisional character because the agency may examine classification, value, and origin and reach conclusions different from the importer declaration. Liquidation fixes the computation on the basis the agency accepts, and an importer may protest a liquidation within 180 days under 19 U.S.C. 1514. For entries covered by an antidumping or countervailing duty order, liquidation is suspended pending an administrative review, and liquidation then applies the assessment rate produced by that review. Where no review is requested for a period, duties are assessed at the cash deposit rate in effect when the merchandise entered.

### Q: How does a safeguard duty differ from an antidumping duty?

A safeguard duty differs from an antidumping duty in trigger, standard, decision maker, and duration. A safeguard under sections 201 to 204 of the Trade Act of 1974 requires increased imports as a substantial cause of serious injury or threat of serious injury to the domestic industry, investigated by the International Trade Commission, and no finding of unfairness is required. The President decides the remedy, which may include duty increases, quotas, tariff rate quotas, or adjustment assistance, for an initial period of up to four years extendable to a total of eight years. An antidumping duty requires dumping, measured as sales at less than fair value by Commerce, plus material injury determined by the Commission, and an order issues only when both determinations are affirmative.

### Q: What remedies can the Trade Representative impose after an investigation of unfair foreign practices?

Remedies under section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 include suspending concessions granted under a trade agreement and imposing duties or other import restrictions. The United States Trade Representative investigates a foreign act, policy, or practice, consulting and seeking dispute settlement where a trade agreement applies. Action is mandatory where United States rights under a trade agreement are denied or where a practice is unjustifiable and burdens United States commerce. Action is discretionary where a practice is unreasonable or discriminatory and burdens United States commerce. Suspension of concessions withdraws benefits the foreign country received through negotiation, while imposed duties and restrictions operate directly on trade, and each remedy answers to the foreign practice that the investigation examined rather than to the condition of a domestic industry alone.

### Q: Which duties apply to goods from countries without normal trade relations?

Goods from countries without normal trade relations treatment bear column 2 rates in the Harmonized Tariff Schedule. Column 2 carries the statutory rates descended from the Tariff Act of 1930, preserved in the schedule beside column 1 general rates and column 1 special rates. Column 1 general states the normal trade relations rates that govern imports from sources holding that treatment, and column 1 special states rates under preference programs and free trade agreements. Column 2 applies to the small set of countries denied normal trade relations treatment, and the column marks the outer boundary of the rate structure. Classification of the goods proceeds in the ordinary manner under the General Rules of Interpretation, and column selection then supplies the governing rate for the source.

### Q: What is a new shipper review in an antidumping case?

A new shipper review lets a new exporter obtain an antidumping rate of its own. An exporter that did not ship covered merchandise during the original investigation and that begins exporting after an order issues enters the market without a rate based on that exporter transactions. Entries of that merchandise would otherwise carry a deposit rate calculated for other firms from transactions in which the new exporter played no part. In a new shipper review, Commerce calculates a rate from the new exporter own sales, and that rate supplies the basis for deposits and later assessments for that exporter. The procedure extends the measurement principle of the retrospective system to entrants and adds a separate proceeding to the administration of the order.
