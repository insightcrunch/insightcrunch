On the evening of Saturday, 19 July 1969, the Government of India took ownership of the fourteen largest Indian commercial banks by the signature of a single ordinance, and it did so roughly forty-eight hours before Parliament was due to assemble for its monsoon session. Bank nationalisation, the shorthand name for that acquisition and for the second, smaller acquisition that followed in 1980, was therefore not merely an economic decision. It was a legislative event of a very particular kind: a law made at the one moment when no legislature was sitting, defended afterwards on the ground that surprise was of its essence, struck down by the Supreme Court within seven months, re-enacted in corrected form within weeks of the judgment, and finally placed beyond the reach of the same challenge by a constitutional amendment. A reader who can narrate that sequence, and who understands why each step followed from the step before, understands the single most instructive episode in the legislative history of independent India.

The stakes at the time were concrete rather than rhetorical. Commercial banking in India served a narrow geography and a narrow clientele. The Reserve Bank's figures record 8,262 bank offices in the whole country in June 1969, of which only 1,833 stood in rural areas, and the average office served a population of about 64,000 people. Aggregate deposits of the commercial banks stood at about Rs 4,646 crore in June 1969 on the Reserve Bank's figures, a small sum for an economy of India's size, and the flow of institutional credit to agriculture and small industry was a small fraction of the whole. The largest banks were intertwined with the largest industrial houses, whose directors sat on bank boards and whose concerns drew credit on terms unavailable to a cultivator or a small manufacturer. Whether ownership was the right instrument for changing that pattern is a question this article treats as genuinely open, because the evidence supports a serious case on each side. What is not open is the sequence of legal events, and the sequence is the subject here.

## A note on the record this history rests on

The documentary base for what follows is unusually complete, and the reader is entitled to know its shape. The primary texts are the two acquisition Acts of 1970 and 1980, the repealed Act of 1969, and the ordinances they replaced, each with its Statement of Objects and Reasons, in which the governments of the day explained themselves in their own words; this article follows the record's practice of treating those statements as evidence of intention, to be weighed rather than merely quoted. The parliamentary debates supply the arguments as they were made in the chamber. The judgment of 10 February 1970 is public and its outcome is stated here at the level of what it decided, the reasoning being the property of the companion article in this series. The Reserve Bank's official history, whose third volume covers the years 1967 to 1985, assembles the administrative record, the branch statistics, and the sequence of instruments with the access of the institution that operated much of the programme, and where this article gives figures for offices, deposits, and the growth of the network, they are the Reserve Bank's figures or computations from them attributed in the text. The political narrative, the Bangalore note, the resignation of the Finance Minister, the presidential election, and the split, belongs to the standard record of the period and is stated only where it bears directly on the legislation's timing and form. Where the authorities differ, most conspicuously on compensation amounts in the various accounts of the 1970 settlement, this article describes the statutory mechanism and declines the contested aggregate, a choice the handoff documentation records. A legislative history that cannot say where its facts come from has not finished its work; this one can.

## The banking order that the ordinance interrupted

The fourteen banks taken over in 1969 were not marginal institutions. They were, by the statutory test the government chose, every Indian banking company holding deposits of Rs 50 crore or more, and between them they held the greater part of the deposit base and the branch network of Indian commercial banking. The State Bank of India and its subsidiaries already stood in public ownership, the State Bank having been created in 1955 out of the Imperial Bank, so the commanding heights of the system were divided between one public institution and a group of large private ones. The private banks were companies like any others, governed by the Companies Act and regulated under the Banking Regulation Act, 1949, and their shareholding was concentrated enough that a small number of industrial families exercised decisive influence over the credit decisions of institutions gathering deposits from the general public.

That interlocking of industrial and banking capital was the core of the case against the existing order. The criticism, pressed through the nineteen sixties by the left parties, by a section of the Congress itself, and by commentators on the working of the licensing system, was that a bank whose board was populated by the directors of large industrial concerns would lend to those concerns and their associates first, and that the pattern of credit in the economy showed exactly that result. Agriculture, which employed the overwhelming majority of the working population, received a very small share of commercial bank credit. Small-scale industry complained of being rationed out of institutional finance altogether. Districts without a branch of any bank were unbanked in the literal sense: their savings, such as they were, stayed outside the deposit system, and their credit came from moneylenders. The defenders of the private banks answered that lending follows bankable demand, that rural lending carried costs and risks a commercial institution could not ignore, and that the remedy for any proven abuse was tighter regulation by the Reserve Bank rather than a transfer of ownership. That argument, regulation against ownership, is the strongest counterfactual in the whole story, and it returns at the end of this article.

One further fact about the pre-1969 order belongs here, because it shaped what the public thought it was being asked to approve. The public stake in banking's safety was already real. Bank failures had punctuated the first decades of independence, small institutions collapsing with their depositors' savings, and Parliament had answered with machinery rather than ownership: deposit insurance, introduced under the Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation's predecessor statute of 1961 among the earliest such schemes in the world, guaranteed the small depositor against failure while leaving the failing bank's owners in possession. The two decades before 1969 therefore presented a genuine fork, and the debates of 1969 were in part an argument about which lesson the failure record taught. One path extended the regulatory answer: stronger inspection, compulsory amalgamation of the weak, insurance of the depositor, and social control of the strong. The other concluded that an industry whose failures the public already underwrote, whose deposits the public supplied, and whose direction the public interest required should be owned by the public outright. The nationalisers' rhetoric about bankers lending other people's money carried force precisely because the other people in question were already standing behind the system through a public insurance corporation; the takeover could be presented as aligning ownership with a risk the public had borne for a decade.

The government of the day had in fact tried the regulatory route first, and the attempt had a statutory form. The policy known as social control, carried into law by the Banking Laws (Amendment) Act, 1968, in force from 1 February 1969, reconstituted the boards of the larger banks, required a majority of directors drawn from outside the industrial houses with professional or sectoral backgrounds, restricted advances to directors and to concerns in which directors were interested, and created the National Credit Council as a forum for steering credit toward the sectors the plan prioritised. A meeting of that Council in July 1968 had already emphasised that commercial banks should increase their financing of agriculture and small-scale industry, the sectors that came to be described as the priority sectors. Social control was therefore not a slogan but a working statutory scheme, barely five months old, when the decision was taken to go beyond it.

### What could the Reserve Bank already do under the existing law?

The Reserve Bank held wide powers under [the Banking Regulation Act, 1949, which supplies the regulatory framework for Indian banking](/2012/12/15/banking-regulation-act-1949-explained/): it licensed banks, inspected books, issued directions in the public interest, controlled advances, and could seek the winding up of an institution. What it could not do was change who owned a bank or rewrite its lending priorities from within.

The limits of the regulatory framework matter to the story because they explain the choice of instrument in 1969. A direction can forbid a loan; it cannot easily compel thousands of loans of a kind a bank's management believes to be unprofitable. A licensing policy can steer new branches toward unbanked centres only when a bank applies to expand. The advocates of nationalisation argued that every regulatory instrument depended on the cooperation of managements whose commercial judgement pointed the other way, and that two decades of exhortation and a year of statutory social control had moved the pattern of credit too little. The critics of nationalisation answered, with equal force, that the Reserve Bank's powers were formidable and largely untested at full stretch, and that a government confident in its regulatory case should have used them before reaching for ownership. Both arguments were available in July 1969. The political events of that month decided which of them would be acted on.

## The long pedigree of the demand

The ordinance of July 1969 was sudden; the demand it satisfied was twenty years old, and a history that begins on the Saturday night misleads the reader about why the weapon was loaded. The nationalisation of banking had been in the Congress programme since the first years of independence, debated when the Reserve Bank itself was nationalised with effect from 1 January 1949, and embodied in the compromise of 1955: the Imperial Bank became the State Bank of India, public but operating beside the private system, a halfway house that satisfied neither the demand's supporters, who wanted the system's commanding heights, nor its opponents, who wanted the state out of commercial banking altogether. The Avadi session of the Congress in 1955, which committed the party to a socialistic pattern of society, gave the demand an ideological address; the annual arguments at party sessions kept it warm through the nineteen sixties.

Evidence, as distinct from ideology, accumulated from official inquiries. The committee on the distribution of income and levels of living chaired by P. C. Mahalanobis, reporting in 1964, documented the concentration of economic power in the Indian economy. R. K. Hazari's study of industrial planning and licensing, published in 1967, mapped the interlocking of the largest industrial houses with the licensing system and with the banks whose boards their directors sat on. The Congress's ten-point economic programme of May 1967, adopted after an election that had cut the party's majority and strengthened its left, included the social control of banks among its commitments, alongside the abolition of privy purses, ceilings on urban property and income, and state trading in food grains. The programme is the hinge between the old demand and the events of 1969: it committed the party to act on the banks, while leaving the instrument unspecified, and social control, the compromise instrument, was what the party's left accepted in 1967 and judged a failure by 1969.

The failure judgement had a specific content that readers should hold onto, because it explains why regulation lost the argument inside the government. Social control changed who sat on bank boards and whose concerns those boards could finance, but it did not change who owned the banks, who appointed the boards it reconstituted, or what the banks were for. A board reconstituted under the 1968 amendments still served shareholders whose interest lay in deposit growth and lending profit, and the priority sectors' claims on credit still ranked behind that interest whenever the two conflicted. Between February 1969, when social control took effect, and July, the government could point to no measurable redirection of credit toward agriculture or small industry that it could attribute to the new machinery. Whether five months was a fair trial is a question the policy's defenders never had to answer in Parliament, because the political logic of the presidential election overtook the administrative argument; but the honest record is that nationalisation was chosen over a regulation that had been tried for less than half a year, by a Prime Minister who had announced her dissatisfaction with the pace of credit redirection at Bangalore days before she moved. The choice was political in its timing and ideological in its direction, and the evidentiary basis for it was the concentration studies of the nineteen sixties rather than any demonstrated collapse of social control. Stating that plainly does not weaken the nationalisers' case so much as locate it: their strongest evidence was never that the alternative had failed in five months, but that the structure Hazari and Mahalanobis had documented could not be reformed from within by the interests that owned it.

## July 1969: the political week

Bank nationalisation was decided inside a struggle for control of the ruling party, and any honest legislative history has to set the two stories side by side. The Congress Party that returned to power in 1967 with a reduced majority contained an organised group of senior leaders, known in the press and in the party's own vocabulary as the Syndicate, who had placed Indira Gandhi in the prime ministership expecting to guide her, and who by 1969 intended instead to constrain her. President Zakir Husain died in office on 3 May 1969, and the contest to succeed him became the occasion of the trial of strength. The party's parliamentary board, over the Prime Minister's known reservations, selected Neelam Sanjiva Reddy, the Speaker of the Lok Sabha and a figure acceptable to the Syndicate, as the official Congress candidate for the presidency.

The economic argument inside the party ran on a parallel track. At the Bangalore session of the All India Congress Committee in July 1969, the Prime Minister circulated a note of economic proposals whose contents included the nationalisation of the major banks and other measures directed at the concentration of economic power. Morarji Desai, the Deputy Prime Minister and Finance Minister, was identified with the contrary position. Desai favoured social control, doubted the administrative and fiscal wisdom of acquisition, and was opposed in principle to settling the question in haste. The disagreement was therefore not between a government and an opposition but between the two senior-most members of the same Cabinet, and it could not be carried into a collective decision while Desai held the finance portfolio.

On 16 July 1969 the finance portfolio was taken from Desai, and he resigned from the government; the Prime Minister took charge of finance herself, holding it alongside the prime ministership. Three days later the ordinance was promulgated. The sequence is not in dispute, and it is the foundation of the most persistent political reading of nationalisation: that the measure was the decisive stroke in an intra-party contest, timed to seize the initiative before the presidential election, and calculated to present the Syndicate with a popular fait accompli it could not oppose without branding itself as the defender of the banks. Historians who resist that reading point out, correctly, that the demand for nationalisation had a long pedigree inside and outside the Congress, that it featured in party resolutions and in the public debate of the nineteen sixties, and that the policy was implemented and extended for decades after the factional quarrel that produced it had been forgotten. The timing supports the political reading; the durability supports the policy reading. A legislative history has to carry both, because each explains something the other cannot: the political reading explains the week, and the policy reading explains the half-century.

The presidential election completed the manoeuvre. V. V. Giri, who had been discharging the functions of President after Zakir Husain's death, resigned that charge on 20 July 1969, the day after signing the ordinance, in order to contest the election as an independent candidate with the Prime Minister's support. The Prime Minister called on Congress legislators to vote according to conscience rather than the party's official nomination. In the election held in August 1969, Giri defeated Sanjiva Reddy by a narrow margin once second preferences were counted. Within three months the party split openly: in November 1969 the organisational wing expelled the Prime Minister, her supporters constituted themselves as the real Congress, and the two factions went forward as rival parties. Nationalisation was the first great policy fact of that split, and it fixed the ideological identity of the Prime Minister's faction for the election of 1971.

### Why did the ordinance come on a Saturday?

The ordinance was promulgated on Saturday, 19 July 1969, with Parliament not in session and its monsoon session imminent. A Saturday promulgation meant that when banks reopened on Monday, the undertakings already stood vested in public ownership, the boards had vacated office, and no interval existed in which deposits could be withdrawn or assets moved in anticipation.

The case for the ordinance route, stated at its strongest, rests on the character of the thing being acquired. A bank is a confidence institution: its assets are other people's claims and its liabilities are payable on demand. Announce a takeover for a future date and the interval between announcement and vesting becomes an invitation to every interested party to alter the position: large depositors to move funds, borrowers connected to the management to settle or restructure obligations, shareholders to deal in the shares on inside knowledge, and managements themselves to transact business that a new owner would not have chosen. The government's stated reason for legislating by ordinance was that immediate action was necessary precisely because the subject matter would not hold still while a bill was printed, debated, and passed. Defenders of the measure have called it the strongest justification for an ordinance in the Republic's history, and the argument deserves to be put that plainly, because the general question of ordinance-making is a contested one that this episode does not settle: [the ordinance-making power has its own owner article in this series](/2022/03/01/ordinance-making-power-india/), and the bank case is best understood as the extreme instance that tests the rule rather than as a precedent that dissolves it. The opposing case is also real. The ordinance was not forced on the government by the calendar in the ordinary sense: the monsoon session opened within days, the measure commanded a majority, and a bill introduced on the Monday could have been law within weeks. Choosing the ordinance was choosing surprise over deliberation, and it taught every later government the uses of the device for measures that were merely urgent in a political sense. That the first use in this instance was substantively defensible did not prevent the technique from being imitated in instances that were not.

The promulgating authority added one more layer to the week's singularity. Because the presidency was vacant, the ordinance was promulgated by V. V. Giri in his capacity as the person discharging the functions of the President, acting on the advice of the Council of Ministers. The man who signed the most consequential economic ordinance of the Republic thus did so on his last full day in that office, resigning the following day to fight the election that the ordinance's author was determined he should win.

## What the ordinance and the 1969 Act actually did

The instrument promulgated on 19 July 1969 was the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Ordinance, 1969, Ordinance 8 of 1969, and its design was repeated with little change in the Act that replaced it. Its unit of acquisition was not the share capital of the fourteen companies but their undertakings: the whole business, assets, rights, and liabilities of each named bank. For every existing bank the statute created a corresponding new bank, a body corporate bearing a corresponding name, and vested the undertaking in that new bank with effect from 19 July 1969. The old company was left in existence as a shell, stripped of its business, and the statute dealt with it accordingly: it could not carry on banking business, it could not use the word bank in its name in a manner the Banking Regulation Act forbade to unlicensed entities, and it was liable in due course to be dissolved. Its surviving right was the right to compensation.

The human machinery of the takeover was equally direct. The chairman and directors of each named bank vacated office on vesting. The chief executive of each old bank became the Custodian of the corresponding new bank, so that the person who had run the institution on Friday ran it on Monday under a new title and a new owner, and the services of the staff stood transferred to the new bank on their existing terms. Continuity of deposits, accounts, and branches was preserved by the simple device of changing the owner of the undertaking overnight while changing almost nothing else that a customer could see. The scheme explains a fact that surprises readers who expect drama: the branches opened, the accounts continued, and the cheques were honoured, because the statute had been drafted to make the takeover legally total and operationally invisible.

The fourteen banks named in the First Schedule were the Central Bank of India, the Bank of India, the Punjab National Bank, the Bank of Baroda, the United Commercial Bank, the Canara Bank, the United Bank of India, the Dena Bank, the Syndicate Bank, the Union Bank of India, the Allahabad Bank, the Indian Bank, the Bank of Maharashtra, and the Indian Overseas Bank. The criterion that gathered exactly these names into the schedule was deposits of not less than Rs 50 crore, a threshold that did the political work of the measure as well as its economic work: it captured every Indian bank of commanding size, it left the smaller Indian banks in private hands, and it left foreign banks untouched, a distinction that would matter in the litigation. A threshold expressed in deposits also had a self-limiting quality the drafters may or may not have intended: it classified banks by a published measure of scale rather than by any judgement about their conduct, so the Act could present itself as a general law about size rather than a punishment directed at named offenders, even though its schedule named names.

Compensation was where the design was weakest, and the weakness was structural rather than accidental. Under the 1969 scheme, each existing bank was to receive compensation determined in accordance with principles set out in the Second Schedule to the Act, with the amount, failing agreement, to be settled by a tribunal, and payment to be made in marketable securities of the Central Government. The principles valued the undertaking essentially at the sum of its assets less its liabilities, and they left out of account elements that a seller of a going concern would have priced first: the value of the banking business itself, of the branch network as an operating system, and of the goodwill embodied in decades of customer relationships. Shareholders who had owned a functioning bank were offered, in substance, a balance-sheet residue and government paper in exchange. The government defended the method as the honest measure of what a bank's assets net of its deposit liabilities were worth, and pointed out that a bank's balance sheet is mostly other people's money, which the acquirer was assuming along with the assets. The shareholders' answer was that the undertaking as a going concern was worth more than the arithmetic difference the Schedule allowed, and that a compensation principle which excludes the value of the business being taken is not a principle of compensation at all. That disagreement, precisely framed, is what the Supreme Court was asked to resolve.

### What happened to the fourteen old banking companies themselves?

Each old company survived as a legal shell with no business to conduct. Its undertaking, staff, branches, and liabilities had vested in the corresponding new bank, its directors had vacated office, and it was barred from carrying on banking business. What remained to it was a single asset: the statutory claim to compensation for the undertaking it had lost.

The design deserves a moment's attention, because it shows the drafters working at the edge of what acquisition law could do. Parliament did not dissolve the companies, confiscate the shares, or expropriate the shareholders directly. It took the undertaking, which is where the public interest was said to lie, and left the corporate person standing in order to receive the price. The distinction mattered for the litigation, because it framed the question as the adequacy of the price for the undertaking rather than the lawfulness of taking it, a framing the majority of the Supreme Court would in the end accept as the right battleground while deciding the price question against the government.

## The passage of the 1969 Act: the introduced text is the enacted text

Parliament replaced the ordinance with an Act in a matter of weeks. Petitions challenging the ordinance were lodged in the Supreme Court on 21 July 1969, two days after its promulgation, but before they could be heard the government placed its Bill before the monsoon session, and the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, 1969, Act 22 of 1969, was enacted on 9 August 1969. Assent was given during the same interregnum in which the ordinance had been promulgated: with the presidency passing between holders that summer, the functions of the office were being discharged by the Chief Justice of India, M. Hidayatullah, when the Act completed its passage, so that the statute nationalising the banks received assent from the head of the judiciary whose institution would strike it down six months later. By its own terms the Act came into force on 19 July 1969, the day of the ordinance, so that no gap opened between the ordinance regime and the statutory regime, and the ordinance itself was repealed and replaced by the statute whose long title reproduced its own. The undertakings were deemed to have vested in the corresponding new banks from the commencement date, and things done under the ordinance were carried over into the Act's authority.

The parliamentary debate rehearsed the arguments the country had been hearing all month at a higher level of organisation. The government, with the Prime Minister holding the finance portfolio and presenting the measure, rested on the stated objectives: to control the heights of the economy, to extend banking to areas the private system had not reached, to direct credit toward agriculture, small industry, and exports, to encourage new classes of entrepreneurs, and to break the connection between the largest banks and the largest industrial houses. The opposition, led in this argument by the Swatantra Party and the Jana Sangh and joined by members who distrusted the method as much as the measure, attacked the ordinance route as an abuse of executive legislation, the acquisition as a blow against property and against the federal distribution of the subject in the Seventh Schedule, and the compensation principles as confiscation wearing the dress of valuation. Morarji Desai's absence from the Treasury front bench was itself an argument, since the man who had been Finance Minister a month earlier had resigned over the policy his successor was carrying through the House.

The session that passed the Act was not an ordinary session, and its calendar explains the compression the critics complained of. Members of Parliament were simultaneously the electors in the presidential election, polling for which fell on 16 August 1969, a week after the Act's enactment, with the Prime Minister's appeal for a conscience vote still resounding through the party. The legislature that ratified the takeover was therefore a body in the middle of choosing the head of state, divided by the same quarrel that had produced the takeover, and voting on the Bill with the presidential contest as the unspoken division in every lobby. A measure carried in such an atmosphere carries it permanently in the record: the 1969 Act cannot be read as the settled product of a legislature at peace with itself, because the legislature that passed it dissolved, first into factions and within eighteen months into a general election, around the very questions the Act embodied. The government's whips could count the votes for the Bill; what they could not supply, and what the Supreme Court in February may be thought to have noticed, was the deliberative leisure whose absence the ordinance route had made structural. Passage records matter to later readers precisely because they reveal how much consideration a text received, and by that measure the 1969 Act received the least consideration of any major economic statute of its era, a fact its defenders would answer, fairly, by pointing out that the country had debated bank nationalisation for twenty years before Parliament voted on it in three weeks.

For the legislative historian, the feature of the passage that matters most is how little the text moved. Template histories of legislation often turn on the distance between the bill as introduced and the Act as passed, the committee amendments, the concessions extracted, the clauses recast. Here there was effectively no distance to measure. The Bill followed the ordinance, the Act followed the Bill, and the operative scheme, the schedule of names, the compensation principles, and the vesting date survived the parliamentary process essentially untouched. That fact had two consequences. In the short term, it confirmed the critics' charge that Parliament had been asked to ratify a decision rather than to make one. In the longer term, it meant that when the Supreme Court examined the Act, it was examining the executive's original design in its original form, unsoftened by any legislative second thoughts, and the design's weakest element, the compensation scheme, went into court exactly as drafted on the eve of 19 July.

The lesson the episode teaches about passage records generally is worth stating, because it is the analytical heart of this article's framework. The speed of a passage is itself evidence. A statute enacted over months of committee scrutiny carries the presumption that its vulnerable points were seen and priced by the legislature. A statute enacted in three weeks, in the shadow of an ordinance already in force and a presidential election in progress, carries no such presumption, and a court asked to test it against the Constitution is correspondingly less inclined to assume that the legislature has done the constitutional arithmetic for itself. The 1969 Act's journey through Parliament did not merely precede its defeat in court; in an intelligible sense it contributed to it.

## The challenge and the judgment, stated as an event

The constitutional challenge was filed with a speed that matched the government's own. Rustom Cavasjee Cooper, a shareholder in several of the named banks, petitioned the Supreme Court under Article 32 on behalf of the interests the Act had displaced, and his petition stood at the head of a group of petitions brought by shareholders, directors, and banking companies themselves; the petitions against the ordinance, filed on 21 July 1969, were overtaken by the Act and redirected against it. The case was heard by a bench of eleven judges, the largest constitutional bench the subject could have drawn, through the closing months of 1969. The questions ranged wider than compensation: the competence of Parliament to legislate on the subject at all, the discrimination said to arise from acquiring fourteen banks while leaving others, Indian and foreign, free to bank, the rights of shareholders to complain of injury done to their companies, and the validity of the ordinance itself. The doctrinal analysis of those questions, the reasoning by which the majority answered them, and the dissent of Ray J. belong to [the companion article on the bank nationalisation case judgment](/2013/01/15/bank-nationalisation-case-judgment/), which owns the litigation in this series; what belongs here is the outcome and its place in the chronology.

The hearing occupied the closing months of 1969, and its circumstances deserve record because they formed the atmosphere in which the re-enactment was planned. While eleven judges heard argument on whether the acquisition was lawful at all, the acquisition itself was fully performed and operating: the custodians ran the banks, the boards that would eventually govern them were being prepared under the new ownership, branches opened, and the government's programme of credit redirection proceeded on the assumption of victory. No interim order disturbed the working of the Act, so the litigation ran, in effect, against a completed fact, and both sides argued in the knowledge that a judgment for the petitioners would have to unscramble seven months of public administration of a third of the country's economic machinery. That knowledge is part of the explanation for the government's speed in February: the contingency of defeat, with the undertakings reverting and the depositors' position thrown into legal doubt, had been contemplated since the petitions were filed, and the correcting instrument could be prepared in outline while the Court deliberated. Governments that legislate first and litigate later acquire, among the other advantages of the sequence, the advantage of drafting their remedy at leisure during the hearing.

The outcome, delivered on 10 February 1970, was that the Supreme Court, by a majority of ten to one, declared the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, 1969 void. The power to nationalise banks as such was not denied: the Court accepted that Parliament could legislate for the acquisition of banking undertakings. What failed was the Act's compensation scheme, which the majority held did not provide compensation in the sense the Constitution's property article required, because the principles in the Second Schedule excluded relevant elements of value and rested in part on principles the majority considered irrelevant to the loss being compensated; the majority also found against the Act on the treatment of the named banks as compared with other banks left free to carry on the business. The practical effect was stark. The undertakings of the fourteen banks, which the statute had vested in the corresponding new banks from 19 July 1969, reverted in law to the old banking companies. Seven months of public ownership rested, overnight, on a statute that had ceased to exist.

### What was the legal position on the morning after the judgment?

On the morning of 11 February 1970 the acquisition statute was void and the undertakings had reverted to the old companies, yet the branches opened, the custodians remained at their desks, and deposits stayed where they were, because the judgment changed the legal title faster than any administration could change its facts. The government's response was already being drafted.

That interval, between a legal position that had collapsed and an administrative reality that had not, is the pivot of the whole story, because everything the government did next was done inside it. The banks could not be handed back in any operational sense: the boards that had vacated office in July could not be reconstituted by a judgment, the staff who had been transferred could not sensibly be transferred back, and the depositors whose accounts had been the political justification of the takeover could not be told that their bank was private again pending further legislation. The judgment had restored a legal position that events had made unrestorable, and both sides understood this. The shareholders' victory was real but it was a victory about the terms of acquisition, and the only question the next weeks had to answer was on what terms the acquisition, which nobody in authority proposed to undo, would be made lawful again.

## The eleven days: the round trip in one table

The findable artifact of this episode is its chronology, because the chronology is the argument. Read down the rows and the constitutional round trip completes itself: acquisition by ordinance, ratification by Act, annulment by judgment, resumption by a second ordinance, settlement by a second Act, and insulation by amendment. The final column records what the law allowed the two sets of private actors, the banks and their shareholders, to do at each moment, which is where the speed of the sequence did its real work: at no point in the entire round trip did the shareholders possess an interval in which the legal position and the administrative facts were aligned in their favour.

## Four days in February: the re-enactment of 1970

The government's answer to the judgment took four days to appear and about six weeks to complete, and its speed was the message. On 14 February 1970, with Parliament not yet in session for the purpose, the President promulgated the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Ordinance, 1970, by which the acquisition of the fourteen undertakings was resumed with effect from 19 July 1969, the original date. The Statement of Objects and Reasons attached to the Bill that followed stated the position with unusual candour: the Supreme Court had declared the 1969 Act void, the effect was that the undertakings had reverted to the banks, and the ordinance was promulgated with a view to resuming control over them. The Bill to replace the ordinance was introduced on 27 February 1970, passed by both Houses toward the end of March without amendment, and received the President's assent on 31 March 1970 as the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, 1970, Act 5 of 1970, deemed to have come into force on 19 July 1969.

The drafting of the second statute is a small masterclass in legislating against a judgment, and it repays attention because the technique became the template this article's brief asks the reader to learn. The government did not attempt to argue that the majority had been wrong about the original scheme, and it did not re-enact the condemned provisions in defiance of the Court. It read the judgment as a specification. The compensation principles in the old Second Schedule, which the majority had dismantled, were abandoned altogether; in their place the 1970 Act fixed, against the name of each existing bank in its Second Schedule, a stated sum of money, so that the transaction ceased to be the application of a valuation method a court could test for relevance and became the payment of an amount the legislature had itself determined. The provisions of the earlier Act that the majority had held against it were, in the words of the official statement, duly amended or deleted. And the vesting date was held at 19 July 1969, so that the seven months of public ownership that the judgment had annulled were annulled in turn: in the eye of the 1970 Act, the undertakings had been in public ownership continuously since the Saturday of the first ordinance, and the judgment survived as an event in the litigation history rather than as an interruption in the ownership of the banking system.

The compensation terms themselves recorded the negotiation the judgment had forced. A reader comparing the two statutes will find the change easiest to see in the schedules themselves: the 1970 Act's Second Schedule, with its fixed sum against each bank, set against the 1969 Act's schedule of valuation principles, is the judgment's effect made visible on the page, and opening the two Acts side by side in [an office file viewer for Excel, DOCX and PPTX documents](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) is a practical way to check the bank-by-bank amounts against each other before relying on any secondary account of them. Each bank could take its fixed amount in cash, in which case payment came in instalments, or in Central Government securities, a ten-year security at par carrying interest at four and a half per cent or a thirty-year security at par carrying five and a half per cent, or partly in each. The banks exercised the options differently: two took their whole amount in cash, ten took securities, and two divided their claims between the two. No second judgment undid the settlement. The constitutional question had not been answered so much as retired, and the manner of its retirement, correction of the statute first and amendment of the Constitution second, is the sequence that distinguishes this episode from a simple legislative override. The government behaved as though the Court's objections to the instrument were entitled to be met on their merits, while reserving and then exercising the constituent power to remove the ground on which the objections had stood.

## The second passage: Parliament debates the re-enactment

The passage of the 1970 Act deserves its own account, because it was a different kind of parliamentary event from the passage of 1969 and the difference is the point. In 1969 Parliament had been asked to ratify, in weeks, a decision the executive had taken and executed; the debate was about the decision itself, and it was conducted by members many of whom were still absorbing the fact of the takeover. In February and March 1970 the House debated a Bill whose text had been written against a judgment every member had read, with the majority opinion's objections to the earlier statute laid out in print for the draftsmen to answer clause by clause. The opposition's argument changed accordingly. It could no longer rest on the inadequacy of the compensation principles, because the principles were gone, replaced by figures; it rested instead on the charge of defiance, that a government which re-acquires by ordinance within four days of an adverse judgment treats the Supreme Court's authority as an inconvenience to be engineered around, and on the deeper objection that retrospective vesting from 19 July 1969 wrote the judgment out of the legal record as though the seven months of litigation had been a clerical error.

The government's reply, in the debate and in the Statement of Objects and Reasons, was a studied act of compliance theatre that was also, on its face, genuine compliance. The statement recorded that the Court had declared the Act void, that the undertakings had consequently reverted, and that the government proposed to resume control of them by a new law that duly amended or deleted the provisions the majority had held against. Nothing in the new Bill asserted that the Court had been wrong; everything in it asserted that the policy remained necessary and that the Constitution, as read by the Court itself, left Parliament free to pursue the policy by a corrected instrument. The Bill was passed by both Houses toward the end of March 1970 without amendment, as the 1969 Bill had been, but the absence of amendment meant something different the second time: not that the legislature had been denied a role, but that the executive's drafting against the judgment was accepted, by the majority the government commanded, as a complete answer to it. Assent followed on 31 March 1970.

The episode fixes a distinction that runs through the whole series and is worth stating as this article's contribution to it. A legislature overriding a judgment changes the law to nullify what the court decided about the parties before it. A legislature legislating against a judgment accepts the decision, changes the legal basis on which it rested, and takes the policy forward on the new basis. The 1970 Act, taken alone, was the second kind of act: the shareholders received the amounts the new statute fixed, the acquisition rested on the new text, and the old Act was not revived. It was the Twenty-fifth Amendment, two years later, that belonged to the first kind, withdrawing the ground itself. Conflating the two moves, as most accounts do, misdescribes both: it makes the re-enactment look more defiant than it was and the amendment look more routine than it was. The sequence's discipline, compliance first and constitutional change second, is precisely what distinguishes the bank nationalisation round trip from cruder legislative responses to adverse judgments elsewhere in the record this series covers, and the fourteen banks' shareholders, who were paid under the second statute and barred by the amendment from reopening its adequacy, experienced the distinction in their pockets.

## The constitutional amendment: removing the ground

The second half of the answer took longer and reached further, and the interval was filled by a political event that changed its meaning. The Lok Sabha elected in 1967 was dissolved on 27 December 1970, and the mid-term election of March 1971 was fought by Indira Gandhi's Congress on the slogan of Garibi Hatao, abolish poverty, with bank nationalisation, the measure the courts had obstructed and Parliament had restored, exhibited as the proof of the government's intentions toward the entrenched interests. The election returned her party with a two-thirds majority in the Lok Sabha, a strength sufficient by itself for constitutional amendment in that House. The Twenty-fifth Amendment was therefore not the act of a government scraping together a majority for a contested manoeuvre; it was carried by a Parliament freshly elected on a programme of which the amendment formed part, and its supporters could claim, as they did, a specific popular mandate for removing the obstacle the bank case had placed in the programme's path. That claim does not answer the constitutional objection to the amendment, which was about the limits of the amending power rather than the size of the majority behind it, but it explains why the amendment was framed and defended as the democratic completion of the episode rather than as its constitutional escalation.

The Constitution (Twenty-fifth Amendment) Act, 1971, passed by the Lok Sabha in December 1971 and by the Rajya Sabha on 8 December 1971, received the President's assent on 20 April 1972 and came into force the same day. Its Statement of Objects and Reasons named its target without euphemism: in the bank nationalisation case, it said, the Supreme Court had held that the Constitution guaranteed a right to compensation, meaning the equivalent in money of the property acquired, and had thereby made the adequacy of compensation and the relevancy of the legislature's principles justiciable. The amendment substituted a new clause in Article 31 under which property could be compulsorily acquired for an amount fixed by law or determined in accordance with principles a law specified, and provided expressly that no such law could be called in question in any court on the ground that the amount was not adequate. The word compensation, with the case law it had attracted, disappeared from the property article. The amendment further provided that the right to practise a profession or carry on a trade under Article 19(1)(f) would not affect an acquisition law, and it inserted Article 31C, protecting laws giving effect to the directive principles in Articles 39(b) and 39(c), the clauses about distributing the ownership of material resources and preventing the concentration of wealth, from challenge under Articles 14, 19, and 31.

The Twenty-fifth did not travel alone, and its companion explains the government's constitutional method in that season. Weeks earlier, on 5 November 1971, the Constitution (Twenty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1971 had received assent, amending Articles 13 and 368 to place beyond argument Parliament's power to amend any part of the Constitution, including the fundamental rights, in answer to the Supreme Court's contrary holding of 1967 in the Golaknath case. The two amendments of 1971 thus formed a pair: the Twenty-fourth repaired the amending power in general, and the Twenty-fifth exercised it on the specific subject the bank case had made urgent. Read together, they show a government working through the constitutional obstacles to its programme in sequence and in the open, first the general power, then the particular application, each step debated and carried by the special majorities the amending process requires. That is the constituent half of the round trip this article chronicles, and it is why the episode cannot be summarised as a government simply defying a court: the defiance, such as it was, was conducted through the most demanding legislative procedure the Constitution contains, twice in one year, with the whole country watching.

The amendment's later career belongs to the constitutional doctrine articles of this series, and [the fate of Article 31C in the basic structure litigation](/2010/02/01/basic-structure-doctrine-explained/) is told there. For the legislative history of nationalisation, the point is narrower and is the one this article's brief insists on. Parliament's response to losing in court was two-staged and deliberate: first, repair the statute so that it satisfied the Constitution as the Court had read it; second, alter the Constitution so that the reading itself could not be repeated. The order of the stages mattered. A government that had amended first and re-enacted afterwards would have presented the country with a plainer confrontation between Parliament and Court. By re-enacting first, the government conceded the judgment's authority over the statute before it; by amending second, it withdrew the concession's subject matter from future litigation. Six years later the Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1978, completed the trajectory by deleting Article 31 and the fundamental right to property altogether, leaving property protected as a constitutional right under Article 300A. The bank case was not the sole cause of that long arc, which ran through the zamindari litigation of the nineteen fifties and the land ceiling cases as well, but it was the arc's decisive modern episode: the last occasion on which the compensation guarantee, at full strength, stopped a major economic statute in its tracks.

### How did the Twenty-fifth Amendment change the ground for later acquisitions?

It replaced the justiciable guarantee of compensation with a legislative power to fix an amount. After 20 April 1972, a law acquiring property for a public purpose stated the amount or the principles for determining it, and no court could entertain the objection that the amount was inadequate. The question Cooper had won on was removed from the Constitution's text.

## The second round: April 1980

The second acquisition, eleven years later, shows what nationalisation looked like once the constitutional ground had been cleared and the political drama removed. Indira Gandhi returned to office in January 1980 at the head of a majority government. On 15 April 1980 the President promulgated the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Ordinance, 1980, acquiring the undertakings of six more banking companies, and Parliament replaced it with the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, 1980, Act 40 of 1980, which received assent on 11 July 1980 and came into force on 15 April 1980. The six banks were the Andhra Bank, the Punjab and Sind Bank, the New Bank of India, the Vijaya Bank, the Corporation Bank, and the Oriental Bank of Commerce. The criterion was again a published measure of scale, raised to match a decade of growth: each of the six had demand and time liabilities of not less than Rs 200 crore, measured as on 14 March 1980.

Three contrasts with 1969 repay attention. The first is legal. No constitutional obstacle stood in the path: Article 31 no longer guaranteed compensation in the old sense, the fundamental right to property had been deleted two years earlier, and the acquisition proceeded against fixed amounts set out in the new Act's Second Schedule on the 1970 pattern. No judgment disturbed it. The second contrast is political. The 1980 round was an extension of an existing settlement by a government acting from strength within its own party, not the opening stroke of a factional war; it drew criticism, but it did not split the ruling party, bring down a finance minister, or decide a presidential election. The third contrast is in the official justification. The Statement of Objects and Reasons for the 1980 Act argued from performance rather than from first principles: since 1969, it said, bank services had grown rapidly in the under-banked rural and semi-urban areas, deployment of credit to the neglected sectors had progressively increased, and the public sector banks had undertaken to raise priority sector credit to forty per cent of their advances over five years; six more banks were being taken over, in the language carried forward from the original Act, further to control the heights of the economy and serve better the needs of development. By common estimate the two rounds together placed about nine-tenths of the country's banking business in public ownership.

The six banks of 1980 had profiles of their own, and their character qualifies the idea that the second round merely repeated the first. They were, in the main, institutions of strong regional rooting that had grown past the threshold in the decade since 1969: the Andhra Bank, founded in 1923 and identified with the Telugu-speaking region; the Corporation Bank, founded in 1906 at Udupi on the Karnataka coast; the Vijaya Bank, founded in 1931 and rooted in the same coastal country; the Oriental Bank of Commerce, founded in 1943 at Lahore and re-established in India after Partition; the Punjab and Sind Bank, founded in 1908 at Amritsar; and the New Bank of India, founded in 1936 at Lahore and, like the Oriental Bank, a Partition transplant. None commanded the metropolitan weight of the great fourteen, and their acquisition changed the texture of the public sector more than its commanding share: it brought the system's regional middle into public ownership and left the remaining Indian private banks, with a handful of exceptions, small institutions by the standard the threshold had set. It is also the round in which the system's one subsequent failure was already present, since the New Bank of India, chronically weakened by its lending book, would be the bank amalgamated out of existence in 1993.

### Who signed the 1980 ordinance, and why does the name carry an irony?

The ordinance of 15 April 1980 was promulgated by President Neelam Sanjiva Reddy, the Congress candidate for the presidency in 1969, whose nomination precipitated the crisis of which nationalisation was the opening stroke and who was defeated by the Prime Minister's candidate. Eleven years on, his signature completed the second round of the policy he had been chosen to restrain.

The twenty banks created by the two rounds, together with the State Bank group, constituted the public sector banking system that the rest of this cluster of articles takes as its subject. Their subsequent history is one of consolidation rather than of further acquisition, and it is told in outline below, because the seed of it lies in the design choices of 1969 and 1980: a system created by acquiring undertakings at a date and on terms fixed by statute could be reshaped by the same statutory technique, and amalgamation schemes under the acquisition Acts themselves became the ordinary instrument by which the number of public banks was later reduced.

## The counterfactual chamber: the July decisions that were not taken

Legislative history is clarified by the drafts that failed, and the July 1969 record contains, in the debates of the party and the press, three alternative instruments that were considered in substance and rejected. Setting them out is not speculation about a different India; it is an account of the design choices the ordinance embodied, each of which had a price the government declined or accepted.

The first alternative was a higher or lower threshold. A threshold of Rs 50 crore of deposits gathered fourteen banks. A lower threshold would have gathered more, including some institutions of primarily regional importance whose takeover would have multiplied the administrative burden and the compensation bill while adding little to the system's commanded share. A higher threshold would have gathered only the largest few and left banks of the second rank in private hands as a standing argument that the policy was aimed at particular houses rather than at scale. The fifty crore line had the draftsman's virtue of capturing the commanding share of deposits and branches in a single schedule of manageable size, and its vice, which the litigation exposed, of leaving banks below it, and the foreign banks beyond it, free to carry on the very business the Act forbade to the companies it acquired. Classification by size was defensible; its edges were the case.

The second alternative was acquisition of a controlling interest rather than of the whole undertaking: the government might have bought or compulsorily acquired fifty-one per cent of each bank's shares, leaving the companies in existence, the minority shareholders in place, and the banks' legal personality untouched. The device would have avoided the Cooper battleground almost entirely, since the taking of shares at a price is the classical subject of compensation law, and it would have preserved the banks' accumulated goodwill in the owner of the shares. Its disadvantages were of control and of candour. A fifty-one per cent owner directs a company through its board but remains entangled with minority interests, listing obligations, and the fiduciary law of companies; and a government intending to run the banks as instruments of policy would hold that intention inside a corporate form designed for a different purpose. The wholesale acquisition of undertakings, leaving the companies as shells to be paid and dissolved, was chosen because it was cleaner in law once compensation was settled, and its authors accepted the litigation the cleaner device invited.

The third alternative was the one actually recommended by caution: defer acquisition, intensify social control, and revisit the question when the Council and the reconstituted boards had been given years rather than months to work. Its cost was political time. The government was a minority in effect within its own divided party, the presidential election was days away, and a deferred measure was a measure the Syndicate's candidate, as President, and the organisational wing, in the working committee, would have had every opportunity to strangle. The ordinance's speed is unintelligible until that cost is priced. Instruments that look reckless in a drafting manual look different in a calendar, and the calendar of July 1969 is set out in this article's table.

## The banks the Acts left behind

Every acquisition statute is also a decision about the institutions it does not acquire, and the two thresholds, Rs 50 crore of deposits in 1969 and Rs 200 crore of demand and time liabilities in 1980, drew a line whose other side had a history of its own. Below the line in 1969 stood the smaller Indian scheduled banks, regional institutions with strong local franchises, and the non-scheduled banks beneath them; above the line, untouched for a different reason, stood the foreign banks. The smaller Indian banks carried on in private ownership, competing for deposits against public giants whose branches multiplied under instruction and whose safety, in the public mind, was underwritten by the state itself. Some prospered on local knowledge and personal service. Others weakened over the following decades, and the Reserve Bank's standing practice of amalgamating a failing bank into a stronger one, usually a public sector bank, quietly transferred a series of private failures into public ownership without any nationalisation statute at all, an accretion of the public sector by the rescue route that the legislative record understates because it was executed bank by bank under regulatory powers rather than in a single dramatic Act.

The foreign banks present the sharper case, because they were above the line in substance and excluded by design. Their Indian operations were branches of banks incorporated abroad, they held a significant share of the metropolitan and trade business, and the government chose not to acquire them. The reasons given at the time ran from reciprocity, Indian banks had branches abroad whose treatment might answer in kind, to the needs of trade finance and foreign exchange, where the foreign banks' networks were the working infrastructure of India's commerce. The exclusion was nonetheless the challengers' best rhetorical material in 1969 and after: a law that acquired Indian banks in the name of controlling the heights of the economy while leaving the foreign occupants of some of those heights in place invited the charge that the heights were not the real object. The government's answer, that a domestic acquisition programme was a lawful classification and that the foreign banks remained subject to Indian licensing and regulation, prevailed in the constitutional litigation on the classification question and prevails in the record; but the episode left a permanent feature in the Indian banking landscape, a foreign sector licensed and constrained but never owned, whose share of the business was held down thereafter less by acquisition than by the branch licensing regime that the nationalised system's expansion was busy making irrelevant to growth. The boundary the Acts drew in 1969 and redrew in 1980 thus proved as durable as the acquisitions themselves: public giants, surviving Indian private banks old and new, and foreign branches, the three-part structure the Indian system still exhibits, was fixed by the decisions about what not to nationalise as much as by the decisions about what to take.

## The constitution the Acts gave the new banks

The acquisition Acts did more than change owners; they wrote the internal constitution under which the new banks would be governed for the rest of their existence, and that constitution explains features of Indian banking that readers otherwise attribute to habit or to the Reserve Bank. Each corresponding new bank was created a body corporate with perpetual succession, owned wholly by the Central Government, and placed under a board of directors constituted under schemes framed by the Central Government in consultation with the Reserve Bank, the Nationalised Banks (Management and Miscellaneous Provisions) Scheme of 1970 being the instrument that settled the boards' composition for the first round. Whole-time directors, including the chairman, who after the ordinance phase took the place of the custodians, were appointed by the Central Government. Employee directors and officer directors found places on the boards in due course, as did directors drawn from depositors, farmers, workers, and other interests the schemes enumerated, a composition that made the board of a nationalised bank a representative body in a sense no commercial board had been, with consequences for the speed and character of its decisions that critics of the system have not been slow to identify.

Two provisions of the Acts deserve to be singled out because they mark the legal distance between ownership and ordinary shareholding. The first is the direction power: every corresponding new bank is required, in the discharge of its functions, to act on business principles and to be guided by such directions in matters of policy involving public interest as the Central Government gives it after consultation with the Reserve Bank. The clause is the statutory root of the programme described in the next section. Branch expansion plans, lending targets, and interest rate structures for the public sector banks ran, in law, through that power of direction, and the boundary disputes of the following decades, between the Finance Ministry as owner and the Reserve Bank as regulator, were disputes about where the direction power ended and supervisory autonomy began. The second is the capital and audit structure: the new banks' capital was wholly subscribed by the Central Government, their accounts were audited under arrangements the Acts prescribed with the Central Government and the Reserve Bank in the frame, and their annual reports were laid before Parliament, so that the legislature which had acquired them received, year by year, the formal account of their stewardship. A shareholder meeting was replaced by parliamentary oversight, a substitution whose theoretical neatness exceeded its practical effect, since the reports were voluminous, late, and rarely debated, but which mattered in one respect: it made the banks' performance a standing item of the public record, available to the committees of Parliament and to the Comptroller and Auditor General's orbit, and therefore available, decades later, to the historians of the bad loan era.

What the Acts did not do is as instructive. They did not touch the State Bank of India, which was already public under its own statute of 1955 and its subsidiary legislation of 1959; the State Bank group stood outside the acquisition Acts entirely, which is why the count of nationalised banks and the count of public sector banks have never been the same number. They did not nationalise the foreign banks, whose Indian branches continued to operate under licence, a choice the government defended on grounds of reciprocity and trade but which handed the challengers their discrimination argument ready-made. They did not abolish private banking: the smaller Indian banks below the threshold carried on, and new private banks would in time be licensed under the Reserve Bank's guidelines of the nineteen nineties, entering the system the Acts had created as competitors within it. And they did not merge the fourteen into a single monolith, as some advocates of nationalisation had urged; each bank kept its name, its branches, its staff, and its regional character, competing with its fellow public banks for deposits in the same streets. That preservation of separate identities, a drafting choice made for continuity in July 1969, had a long tail: it is the reason the consolidation of the two thousand seventeen to two thousand twenty period had separate institutions available to amalgamate, and the reason the amalgamation debates of that period were conducted in the language of individual banks' histories reaching back, through the acquisition Acts, to the private institutions of the century before.

## The programme that followed the statutes

A legislative history that stopped at the statutes would misdescribe what nationalisation was, because the Acts were the enabling condition of a programme that was executed by other instruments: the Reserve Bank's branch licensing, the lead bank scheme, the regional rural banks, and the priority sector targets. The programme is part of the evidence on which nationalisation is judged, for both sides, and its outlines are these.

Branch expansion was the programme's most visible achievement and its least contested one. Nationalisation converted branch opening from a commercial calculation into an instrument of policy. The Reserve Bank's published figures, set out in its Report on Trend and Progress of Banking in India and reproduced in its official histories, record commercial bank offices rising from 8,262 in June 1969 to 60,570 in 1991, with the average population served by an office falling from about 64,000 to about 14,000 over the same span. The composition changed as much as the total: rural offices, 1,833 of 8,262 in June 1969, a little over a fifth of the network, grew to well over half of all offices by the turn of the nineteen nineties, and branch licensing was used deliberately as a lever, with permission to open in already banked centres linked to openings in unbanked ones. New branches in places with no banking presence created deposit points where none had existed, which is why the deposit base grew with the network: the habit of keeping savings in a bank had first to be given a physical address within reach of the saver.

The lead bank scheme, adopted by the Reserve Bank in December 1969, supplied the administrative machinery for the expansion. Districts across the country were allotted to particular banks, public sector banks in the main, which were charged with surveying the district's credit potential, identifying centres where branches should open, and coordinating the lending of the institutions working there. The scheme's significance for this article is that it shows the state using its new property exactly as the theory of nationalisation had promised: not merely owning the banks, but assigning them territorial responsibilities that no private owner pursuing deposit growth would have volunteered for. The regional rural banks, the first five of which opened in October 1975 under sponsorship of the public sector banks, carried the same logic one step further, into institutions designed from the start for a single region's agricultural and artisan borrowers.

Priority sector lending converted the same ownership into a direction of credit. The description of the priority sectors had been formalised in 1972, on the basis of a Reserve Bank study group; in November 1974 banks were advised to raise the share of priority sectors in their aggregate advances to one-third, thirty-three and a third per cent, by March 1979; and in March 1980 it was agreed, at a meeting of the Finance Minister with the chief executives of the public sector banks, that the proportion should reach forty per cent by March 1985, the undertaking the 1980 Act's Statement of Objects and Reasons records. Agriculture, small-scale industry, small transport operators, retail trade, and the weaker sections were the named beneficiaries. The targets were not in the acquisition Acts themselves, a point worth underlining for readers who meet the claim that nationalisation legally compelled the lending pattern. The Acts changed the owners; the targets, the licensing, and the administrative pressure changed the lending. The causal chain ran through ownership, but it ran through several links, and assessments of nationalisation that credit or blame the statutes directly for everything the programme did skip the links where most of the discretion, and much of the failure, actually lived.

Two of those links deserve individual mention. The intellectual bridge between nationalisation and the lead bank scheme was the study group on organisational framework for the implementation of social objectives, chaired by D. R. Gadgil, whose report in October 1969 recommended the area approach: the adoption of districts by particular banks as the unit for planned banking development, which the Reserve Bank converted within two months into the lead bank scheme. The second link ran to the smallest borrowers directly. The differential rate of interest scheme of 1972 required the public sector banks to lend a small share of their advances at a concessional rate of four per cent to borrowers from the weakest sections, selected by income criteria, an early statutory-adjacent attempt to reach, through directed credit, households that collateral-based lending had never touched. The regional rural banks completed the architecture in a different statutory form: begun under an ordinance of 1975 with five banks, they were placed on a permanent footing by the Regional Rural Banks Act, 1976, sponsored by the public sector banks and owned jointly by the Central Government, the state governments, and the sponsor banks. None of these instruments was an acquisition Act, and none could have been operated by a merely regulatory state with the same ease; each presupposed that the government could instruct the banking system as its owner. At the same time, none was compelled by the acquisition Acts, and a government of different temper could have owned the banks without building any of them. The programme was nationalisation's chosen use, not its necessary consequence, and the assessment of the statutes should keep the distinction in view.

## The stated objectives, audited one by one

The Acts' own texts and the statements that accompanied them named the objectives, and a legislative history owes the reader an audit of each against the record, since the brief for this article makes the objectives the standard of the assessment.

The first objective was to control the heights of the economy, the phrase the long titles and statements used, meaning that the institutions commanding the community's savings should answer to public purpose. This was achieved in the only sense it was ever capable of meaning: ownership passed, the direction power passed with it, and the banks' strategies thereafter were set within the government's policy frame. Whether control of the heights required ownership, as against the regulation the critics proposed, is the instrument question this article has already examined; that ownership delivered control is not in dispute.

The second was to extend banking facilities to unbanked areas and to spread the banking habit. This is the objective the record vindicates most completely, in the branch and deposit figures the programme section records, and it is the achievement least often denied even by the settlement's critics, whose argument is about price rather than about the fact.

The third was to give larger credit to agriculture, small industry, and exports, and to reduce the share of the large industrial houses. The redirection happened, through the priority sector framework and the lead bank machinery, and the interlock between the great banks and the great houses was broken at a stroke by the takeover itself, since the directors who embodied it vacated office in July 1969 and no equivalent connection re-formed under public ownership. The cost side of this objective, the quality of the directed credit, belongs to the counter-record.

The fourth was to encourage a new class of entrepreneurs, borrowers whose claims rested on their projects rather than their connections. The record here is genuinely mixed in a way the public argument rarely allows: the priority sector and the differential interest arrangements did open bank credit to borrowers the old system would not have entertained, and the branch network gave them somewhere to apply; but a credit culture administered by targets rewards the borrower who fits the target's description over the borrower with the better enterprise, and the new class of entrepreneurs emerged alongside a new class of borrowers adept at the descriptions. The objective was served in breadth and compromised in method.

The fifth, professionalising bank management and training bank personnel for the system's new tasks, reads in retrospect as the quietest and most nearly achieved of the five. The nationalised banks built training establishments, professionalised their officer cadres, and created the managerial class that later staffed the new private banks of the nineteen nineties and the regulatory institutions themselves. It is an irony the assessment should record that the system nationalisation created trained the people who would later compete with it.

The mobilisation of deposits carries the same lesson in aggregate form. Computations by the economist Rakesh Mohan, drawn from Reserve Bank data and widely used in the scholarly assessment of the period, place bank deposits at about thirteen per cent of gross domestic product in 1969 and about thirty-eight per cent by 1991, a near trebling of the banking system's weight in the economy within one generation, achieved in the main by the branch programme described above rather than by any growth in the incomes of existing depositors. Agricultural credit tells a parallel story: the share of agriculture in the lending of the public sector banks, negligible at the takeover, rose through the nineteen seventies under the priority sector framework until the sector stood among the system's largest heads of advance. These are the figures the defenders of nationalisation lead with, and they are strong figures, but the legislative historian should note what kind of evidence they are. They measure the programme the owner instructed, not the statutes Parliament passed, and they measure quantities, deposits gathered and credit directed, rather than the quality of the assets the direction created, which is the subject of the later sections. The two bodies of evidence, the mobilisation record and the bad loan record, are not rival accounts of different systems. They are two ledgers of the same system, kept in different decades.

## The counter-record: directed credit and its price

The case against the settlement was built slowly, on evidence that accumulated over decades rather than in a single controversy, and it has to be stated at the strength its best proponents give it. Its first element is the quality of directed credit. Lending targets expressed as shares of a portfolio reward volume over appraisal; lending administered under political expectation rewards the borrower with access over the borrower with a project. The critics' charge, made by banking economists and by the Reserve Bank's own internal critics across the period, is that a system in which credit decisions answer partly to non-commercial direction will manufacture bad loans as a by-product, and that the public sector banks did exactly that: lending to favoured borrowers, restructuring loans that commercial judgement would have called in, and carrying the consequences on balance sheets the taxpayer ultimately owned. The second element is political interference in particular decisions, the telephone call on behalf of a constituent or a concern, which no statute authorised and which public ownership made structurally easy, because the government was at once the owner of the lender, the appointer of its board, and a political machine with uses for its credit. The loan melas of the nineteen eighties, mass disbursement camps at which a Union minister of state for finance publicly presided over the handing out of bank loans, became the emblem of this element in the public mind: whatever their defenders said about carrying credit to borrowers intimidated by bank offices, the melas displayed the owner-government treating the banks' lending as an instrument of political theatre, and they taught a generation of borrowers that repayment was a negotiable courtesy rather than a term of the contract. The repayment culture of the directed lending era, and the waiver politics that periodically refreshed it, form part of the causal chain to the bad loan mountain of later decades in a way that the portfolio targets alone do not explain. The third element is fiscal. When bad loans matured into losses, the owner paid: the Central Government recapitalised the nationalised banks repeatedly, in substantial tranches in the nineteen nineties and again in the two thousand tens, including a recapitalisation programme of Rs 2.11 lakh crore announced in October 2017 after the Reserve Bank's asset quality review forced the recognition of bad loans that had been carried as performing. On the Reserve Bank's published ratios, the gross bad-loan share of the public sector banks rose above fourteen per cent at its peak in 2018. [The recovery machinery eventually built to pursue those loans, above all the SARFAESI Act, is treated in its own article](/2013/02/15/sarfaesi-act-2002-explained/), because the recovery statutes of the two thousand two onwards are unintelligible except as the sequel to the lending regime the nationalised system had run.

The defenders' answer is not a denial of the bad loans but a dispute about the counterfactual. Private banks in the same economy and the same decades also failed and also lent badly; the pre-1969 record included hundreds of small bank failures in the decades after independence, which were the original motive for the deposit insurance and the regulatory tightening of the nineteen fifties and sixties. The credit cycle that produced the bad loans of the two thousand tens ran through infrastructure and corporate lending in a boom, a kind of lending the private and foreign banks shared in, and through decisions taken under governments of every complexion. And the social returns of the programme, the branch network, the deposit habit, the agricultural credit flow, were real and are not cancelled by the losses; the honest comparison is between two systems each with a failure mode, one excluding the countryside from credit, the other including it at a fiscal price. That is the shape of the genuine dispute, and this article does not pretend the record resolves it. What the record does settle is the mechanism question: the losses were not an accident that befell nationalisation from outside, but a predictable cost of governing credit by direction, just as the branch network was not a windfall but a predictable product of governing expansion by instruction. A reader assessing the settlement should price both products of the same method.

One further argument belongs to the counter-record because it goes to the choice of instrument in 1969 rather than to its operation. The objective of reducing the concentration of economic power, which the Acts' own long titles invoke through the language of Article 39, was pursued by transferring ownership of the lenders. Regulation offered a different route to the same end: interlocking directorships could have been forbidden outright, advances to connected concerns capped or prohibited as a matter of banking law, and credit planning imposed as a condition of licence. Social control had begun on exactly that path and had barely begun when it was superseded. The critics' question, why the government did not simply regulate harder, has never received a complete answer from the policy's defenders, whose strongest response is institutional rather than logical: that regulation in the India of 1969, with a thin inspectorate and banks adept at formal compliance, would have been evaded in substance while obeyed in form, and that only ownership gave the state the appointment power and the instruction power that made the programme move. That response may be right. It is, at any rate, an argument about administrative capacity rather than about the merits of private banking, and readers weighing the episode should notice that its best defence and its best attack agree on one thing: the choice turned on what the Indian state of the time could actually execute.

## Reading the two acquisition Acts side by side

The 1970 and 1980 Acts are a matched pair, drafted from the same template a decade apart, and reading them together shows both what the template fixed and what the decade changed. The long titles are nearly identical, each an Act to provide for the acquisition and transfer of the undertakings of certain banking companies in order to control the heights of the economy and to meet progressively the needs of the development of the economy, language lifted from the directive principle in Article 39(b). The machinery is identical in structure: a First Schedule of existing banks, a corresponding new bank created for each, vesting of the undertaking on the appointed day, custodians and then boards, a Second Schedule of compensation amounts, and the Central Government armed with the same direction power and scheme-making power. A lawyer who had mastered the 1970 Act could operate the 1980 Act in an afternoon, which was the intention: the second acquisition was designed to be legally boring, and it succeeded.

The differences mark the decade. The criterion changed its measure, from deposits of Rs 50 crore to demand and time liabilities of Rs 200 crore, a more technical test drawn from the banks' own returns, and its date of measurement, 14 March 1980, was written into the official justification, the mark of a government that expected its classification to be examined and wanted the examination to end quickly. The appointed day changed its character: 19 July 1969 was the date of an ordinance promulgated in a political crisis and retrospectively sanctified by two statutes; 15 April 1980 was an ordinary ordinance date at the start of a parliamentary recess, chosen administratively, and the Act followed in July with no litigation, no caretaker presidency, and no split in the ruling party to remember it by. The compensation device, novel and contested in the 1970 Act, was in 1980 a formality, the amounts fixed in the Second Schedule and payable on the same cash-or-securities pattern, because the constitutional ground for contesting them had been removed eight years earlier. And the political signature changed most of all: the first Act was the instrument of a Prime Minister fighting her own party's establishment, the second of the same Prime Minister restored to unchallenged command of it, which is why the second acquisition, though it took six more banks and completed the system, occupies so much smaller a place in the country's political memory. Legislative history should resist that disproportion. The 1980 Act is the better evidence of what nationalisation meant once it had become normal administration, and normal administration, not the drama of July 1969, is what the following forty years of public sector banking actually consisted of.

## Consolidation: the long sequel that reversed neither way

No government has denationalised a bank, and no government has nationalised one since 1980. The direction of travel since has been a third thing, neither reversal nor extension: consolidation of the public sector into fewer and larger institutions, capital raised from the public within a statutory floor of government ownership, and competition admitted from new private banks. The Narasimham Committee of 1991 set the intellectual terms, recommending a leaner, more autonomous, better capitalised banking structure and no further nationalisation. The acquisition Acts themselves were amended in the nineteen nineties to let the nationalised banks issue shares to the public, subject to the Central Government's holding remaining at or above fifty-one per cent, which converted the banks into listed companies with a permanent public majority and left the underlying statutes of 1970 and 1980 as their constitutions.

Consolidation began inside the family. The New Bank of India, the weakest of the 1980 six, its losses having overwhelmed it, was merged into the Punjab National Bank in September 1993, the first and for long the only extinction of a nationalised bank's separate identity. The State Bank absorbed its remaining associate banks in April 2017. The Dena Bank and the Vijaya Bank were amalgamated into the Bank of Baroda with effect from April 2019. Then, with effect from 1 April 2020, ten public sector banks were amalgamated into four: the Oriental Bank of Commerce and the United Bank of India into the Punjab National Bank, the Andhra Bank and the Corporation Bank into the Union Bank of India, the Syndicate Bank into the Canara Bank, and the Allahabad Bank into the Indian Bank. The amalgamations were effected by schemes framed under the acquisition Acts and the State Bank's own statute, the same statutory family that had created the system being used to compress it. Stated durably, the position the amalgamations produced is a system of twelve public sector banks, a count that changes only when a further amalgamation or a privatisation alters it, and that includes the State Bank of India alongside eleven banks tracing their public ownership to the Acts of 1970 and 1980 or to amalgamations under them. The twelve are the State Bank of India, the Punjab National Bank, the Bank of Baroda, the Canara Bank, the Union Bank of India, the Bank of India, the Indian Bank, the Central Bank of India, the Indian Overseas Bank, the UCO Bank, the Bank of Maharashtra, and the Punjab and Sind Bank. The arithmetic of descent is worth performing, because it measures the consolidation the statutes were made to perform: of the fourteen banks of 1969, ten survive as institutions in their own names, the Allahabad Bank having been absorbed into the Indian Bank, the Syndicate Bank into the Canara Bank, the United Bank of India and the Oriental Bank of Commerce into the Punjab National Bank, and the Dena Bank into the Bank of Baroda, while the United Commercial Bank trades in the shortened style of UCO Bank under the same corporate identity the 1970 Act created; of the six banks of 1980, the Punjab and Sind Bank alone survives unamalgamated in name, the Andhra Bank and the Corporation Bank having gone into the Union Bank of India, the Vijaya Bank into the Bank of Baroda, and the New Bank of India having begun the process as long ago as 1993. Extinction in this system has been administrative throughout: no nationalised bank has been liquidated, sold, or returned to private hands, and every disappearance has been a merger into a sister institution under the very Acts that created it.

Privatisation has been proposed within that structure without being executed against a nationalised bank. The Union Budget for 2021-22 announced an intention to take up the privatisation of two public sector banks, and the commitment was repeated in policy statements thereafter, but no acquiring statute has been amended to release a named bank from the fifty-one per cent floor, and the announcement stands as an intention recorded in budget speeches rather than as a step in the legislative record. The finding this article's brief asks for is precisely that asymmetry, and it is worth stating as a finding: across governments of the left, the centre, and the right, across the fiscal crises and the reform decades, nationalisation has been neither reversed nor repeated. The statutes of 1970 and 1980 have proved to be among the most durable economic legislation in the Republic's history, amended for capital and consolidation, never for retreat. A settlement that every successor government criticises in part and none will undo is a settlement whose costs are judged, in the event, to be lower than the costs of disturbing it, or whose benefits are judged to be load-bearing in ways its critics' models do not capture. Either explanation is a substantial concession to the original design.

## The parallel front: privy purses and the same season

Bank nationalisation was not the only front on which the government of those months tested the property provisions of the Constitution, and the parallel campaign against the privy purses of the former rulers belongs to this history because the two campaigns shared a season, a method, and ultimately a constitutional amendment each. The abolition of privy purses stood in the ten-point programme of 1967 beside the social control of banks. In September 1970, months after the re-enactment of the bank acquisition, the government carried a constitutional amendment bill to abolish the purses through the Lok Sabha but failed to secure the required two-thirds majority in the Rajya Sabha, falling short by a fraction of a vote. It then attempted the same result by executive action, withdrawing recognition from the rulers, and in December 1970 the Supreme Court struck that derecognition down as well. The government answered with the Constitution (Twenty-sixth Amendment) Act, 1971, which abolished the privy purses and privileges by constitutional text, as the Twenty-fifth had answered Cooper on compensation. The pattern across the two fronts is the template in stereo: an economic objective pursued by the fastest available instrument, a judicial or parliamentary check, and a resort to the constituent power to place the objective beyond the check. Readers of this series will meet the privy purse litigation in its own place; the point for the bank nationalisation history is that the events of 1969 to 1972 were not an isolated quarrel about fourteen banks but the central engagement of a general campaign about what the property articles of the Constitution would be allowed to prevent, a campaign the government of Indira Gandhi fought on every front its programme opened and won, in the end, on all of them, at the price of the basic structure doctrine that the accumulated amendments called into being.

## The second life of the Acts: amendment without retreat

The acquisition Acts did not retire into the statute book after 1980. They remained the living constitutions of the twenty banks, and their amendment history across the following decades tells the system's story in miniature, because every amendment moved in the same direction: never toward restoring private ownership, always toward making public ownership workable within a changing financial system. Amendments in the nineteen nineties raised the nationalised banks' authorised capital and opened their share capital to the public, protecting the Central Government's holding at not less than fifty-one per cent of the paid-up capital; the first public issues followed, converting the banks into listed companies whose share prices, disclosure obligations, and quarterly results introduced a market commentary on their management that the parliamentary reports had never supplied. The capital so raised was equity the government did not have to find from the budget in years when the banks needed to grow their balance sheets to meet capital adequacy norms the Reserve Bank had adopted from international standards. Listing changed the banks' accountability without changing their ownership, and it changed their politics as well: a bank with public shareholders and a market price is a harder institution to direct informally, which is one reason the direction power, though unamended, has been exercised with greater formality in the listed era.

Proposals to go further have repeatedly entered the legislative record and repeatedly failed to leave it. Bills to reduce the government holding floor below fifty-one per cent, which is the legal precondition of any privatisation of a nationalised bank, have been introduced and have lapsed; the Budget announcement of 2021-22, recorded earlier in this article, has not been followed by the amendment that alone could execute it. The sticking point is structural rather than merely political. The fifty-one per cent floor is not an incidental clause but the clause that reconciles the banks' status as government companies with their status as listed ones, and lowering it requires Parliament to decide, bank by bank in effect, whether the 1970 settlement is to be revised at its foundation. Successive governments have preferred the settlements the existing text permits: amalgamation by scheme, capital by issue, and reform by direction. A statute that can be used to shrink the system from twenty-seven public banks to twelve, to raise outside capital, and to restructure boards, without ever being reopened on the question of ownership, is a statute whose original design has proved more adaptable than its authors had any reason to expect. The Acts' second life is therefore the strongest evidence for this article's closing finding: the obstacle to denationalisation has never been sentiment alone, but the fact that the governing text keeps yielding workable instruments for every purpose short of it.

## The re-enactment template

The namable claim of this article, the one the brief directs the reader to carry away, can be stated in its general form. When the Supreme Court struck down bank nationalisation, the government did not abandon the policy, did not accept the judgment's reasoning as a permanent limit on its economic programme, and did not defy the Court. It did three things in order. It re-enacted the acquisition within four days by ordinance and within weeks by Act, correcting the specific defects the judgment had identified, fixing by the legislature's own hand the amounts the Court had found the valuation principles failed to guarantee. It held the operative date at the date of the original acquisition, so that the legal defeat left no mark on the continuity of ownership. And it then amended the Constitution to withdraw the ground of the judgment, replacing compensation with an amount whose adequacy no court could question. Judgment, re-enactment, amendment: the template was complete by April 1972, and it recurs through the rest of this series wherever a determined government meets an adverse constitutional ruling on economic policy, because the episode taught the technique to everyone watching. Recognising the template in its first full performance is what makes its later, partial performances legible: a reader who has watched the whole sequence executed in thirty-three months, from ordinance to amendment, will identify the opening moves when they recur and will know which moves remain available.

The template also carries the episode's constitutional moral, which the closing assessment should not blur. Nothing in the sequence was unlawful. The ordinance power, the legislative power to acquire, the power to fix compensation, and the amending power were each exercised in the forms the Constitution prescribes, and the Supreme Court itself upheld the power to nationalise while striking the first attempt at its price. The contest was conducted entirely within the constitutional order, by institutions using their assigned weapons in turn: executive speed, legislative majority, judicial review, constituent power. If the outcome was that the judicial victory proved temporary, that is a fact about the distribution of the last word under the Constitution as it then stood, not a scandal within it. The basic structure doctrine, announced the year after the Twenty-fifth Amendment took effect, was the judiciary's eventual answer to the template itself, a claim that the amending power used in the third move had limits the first two moves did not. That answer belongs to the doctrine articles; its existence is the final entry in this chronology, because it means the template of 1969 to 1972 cannot be performed again in exactly its original form.

This account is legal and historical information about statutes and events, not advice on any individual claim, banking relationship, or compensation question arising from them.

## Closing assessment

What did the passage record of 1969 predict about how the system would work? Read as a whole, it predicted both halves of the sequel. A statute carried by ordinance, ratified without amendment, and defended by constitutional amendment announced a system that would be governed by direction from the owner rather than by the commercial judgement of autonomous boards, and the branch network, the lead banks, and the priority targets were that announcement kept. The same record, a compensation scheme struck down for excluding the value of what was taken and a lending programme built on instructed shares of a portfolio, announced a system whose costs would arrive late and land on the owner, and the recapitalisations were that announcement kept as well. The assessment this article leaves is therefore neither of the two verdicts the public argument demands. Nationalisation achieved the geographic and sectoral widening of Indian banking that its authors promised, at a fiscal and institutional price its authors did not price; it was not reversed because the widening became part of the country's economic furniture; and it was not repeated because the price, once visible, was one no later government chose to pay again for a further widening it could pursue by licensing and regulation. The statutes that did all this began as an ordinance signed on a Saturday by a caretaker head of state, and they remain in force. Legislative history rarely offers so complete a specimen of its subject.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Why were Indian banks nationalised in 1969?

The stated objectives, recorded in the Acts' own preambles and the official statements of the time, were to control the heights of the economy, to extend banking to unbanked areas, to direct more credit to agriculture, small industry, and exports, to encourage new classes of entrepreneurs, and to reduce the concentration of economic power. The immediate background was a banking system in which the largest banks were interlocked with the largest industrial houses, rural India had 1,833 bank offices out of 8,262 in June 1969 on the Reserve Bank's figures, and the regulatory policy of social control, in force from February 1969, was judged by the government to be redirecting credit too slowly. The timing, in July 1969, was set by the Congress party's internal struggle and the presidential election, but the objectives themselves had been in the party's programme for years.

### Q: How many banks were nationalised and on what criterion?

Fourteen banks were nationalised in the first round, those Indian banking companies whose deposits were not less than Rs 50 crore, named in the First Schedule to the acquisition Acts: the Central Bank of India, the Bank of India, the Punjab National Bank, the Bank of Baroda, the United Commercial Bank, the Canara Bank, the United Bank of India, the Dena Bank, the Syndicate Bank, the Union Bank of India, the Allahabad Bank, the Indian Bank, the Bank of Maharashtra, and the Indian Overseas Bank. A second round in 1980 added six more banks, tested by demand and time liabilities of not less than Rs 200 crore. Foreign banks were not acquired in either round, and smaller Indian banks below the thresholds remained in private ownership.

### Q: Why was bank nationalisation done by ordinance?

Because of the calendar and the party struggle. Parliament was not in session in mid-July 1969, the monsoon session was days away, and the presidential election, in which the Prime Minister's faction and the party organisation were backing different candidates, fell in August. On 16 July the finance portfolio was taken from Morarji Desai, who resigned; the Prime Minister took finance herself and promulgated the policy's instrument three days later, on Saturday 19 July 1969, through an ordinance issued by the acting President, V. V. Giri. An ordinance took effect at once, vesting the undertakings that day, where a bill would have required a session, a debate, and the party machinery's cooperation. Parliament replaced the ordinance with an Act within weeks, enacted on 9 August 1969 with effect from 19 July.

### Q: What did Parliament do after the 1969 nationalisation Act was struck down?

Parliament re-enacted the acquisition in corrected form. The Supreme Court declared the 1969 Act void on 10 February 1970 in the Cooper case. Four days later, on 14 February 1970, a fresh ordinance resumed the acquisition with effect from the original date of 19 July 1969, and Parliament then passed the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, 1970, which received assent on 31 March 1970. The new Act abandoned the compensation principles the Court had condemned and instead fixed a stated sum against each bank in its Second Schedule. Two years later Parliament also amended the Constitution itself, by the Twenty-fifth Amendment in force from 20 April 1972, replacing compensation in Article 31 with an amount whose adequacy no court could question.

### Q: Which banks were nationalised in the second round?

Six banks, under the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, 1980, which took effect from 15 April 1980: the Andhra Bank, the Corporation Bank, the New Bank of India, the Oriental Bank of Commerce, the Punjab and Sind Bank, and the Vijaya Bank. Each was an Indian banking company whose demand and time liabilities, measured as on 14 March 1980, were not less than Rs 200 crore. The 1980 round followed the 1970 Act's template, with corresponding new banks, a First Schedule of names, and compensation fixed as amounts in a Second Schedule, and it was not disturbed by litigation, since the constitutional ground on which the 1969 Act had fallen had been removed by amendment in 1972.

### Q: How many nationalised banks remain?

Stated durably, India has twelve public sector banks, a position produced by the amalgamations completed on 1 April 2020 and unchanged unless a further amalgamation or a privatisation alters it. The twelve include the State Bank of India and eleven banks descending from the acquisition Acts. Of the fourteen banks of 1969, ten survive in their own names; the Allahabad Bank, the Syndicate Bank, the United Bank of India, the Oriental Bank of Commerce, and the Dena Bank were amalgamated into other public sector banks. Of the six banks of 1980, only the Punjab and Sind Bank survives unamalgamated in name; the New Bank of India was merged into the Punjab National Bank in 1993, and the Andhra Bank, the Corporation Bank, and the Vijaya Bank were absorbed in the amalgamations of 2019 and 2020.

### Q: Did bank nationalisation achieve what it promised?

In part, on the evidence. The extension of banking was achieved: the Reserve Bank's figures record bank offices rising from 8,262 in June 1969 to 60,570 in 1991, the rural share of the network rising from a little over a fifth to well over half, and deposits rising from about thirteen per cent of gross domestic product to about thirty-eight per cent by 1991 on computations from Reserve Bank data. Credit was redirected to agriculture and small industry through priority sector targets that reached forty per cent of advances by March 1985. Against that record stand the costs of directed credit: accumulated bad loans, repeated recapitalisation of the banks by the government, and a bad loan ratio for the public sector banks that rose above fourteen per cent at its peak in 2018 on the Reserve Bank's published figures. The branch network is rarely disputed; its price is.

### Q: Was there political opposition to bank nationalisation?

Yes, organised and immediate. The Swatantra Party and the Jana Sangh attacked the measure in Parliament and outside as an assault on property and as executive legislation by ordinance; business opinion opposed it; and within the Congress itself the organisational wing, the Syndicate, treated the ordinance as a factional manoeuvre in the presidential election struggle, a conflict that ended in the party's split in November 1969. Morarji Desai resigned as Deputy Prime Minister and Finance Minister over the policy days before the ordinance. The shareholders of the fourteen banks carried the opposition into court, where it succeeded once, in February 1970, before Parliament re-enacted the acquisition and amended the Constitution. Opposition continued in later decades as criticism of the public sector banks' performance, but no government has legislated a reversal.

### Q: What did the 1970 Act change compared with the 1969 Act it replaced?

The acquisition itself was unchanged: the same fourteen undertakings vested in the same corresponding new banks with effect from the same date, 19 July 1969. What changed was the compensation scheme and the provisions the Supreme Court's majority had held against the earlier Act. The 1969 Act had set out principles in its Second Schedule for computing compensation, essentially the value of assets less liabilities, with disputes going to a tribunal and payment in government securities. The majority in the Cooper case held those principles defective. The 1970 Act deleted the valuation method and fixed, against the name of each bank in its Second Schedule, a stated sum payable under the Act, so that the legislature itself determined the amount instead of prescribing a formula a court could test.

### Q: Who was Morarji Desai, and why did he resign in July 1969?

Morarji Desai was Deputy Prime Minister and Finance Minister in Indira Gandhi's government, a senior leader of the Congress old guard and a known sceptic of bank nationalisation, favouring the regulatory policy of social control that his ministry had carried into law with effect from February 1969. At the Bangalore session in July 1969 the Prime Minister circulated a note of economic proposals that included nationalisation of the major banks. On 16 July 1969 the finance portfolio was taken from Desai, who was offered another portfolio and declined it, resigning from the government. The Prime Minister took charge of finance herself and promulgated the nationalisation ordinance three days later. His resignation removed the policy's most senior internal opponent at the moment it was executed.

### Q: How was compensation for the nationalised banks calculated and paid?

Under the final settlement in the 1970 Act, compensation was not calculated by formula at all: the Second Schedule to the Act fixed a stated sum against the name of each of the fourteen banks. Each bank could elect to take its amount in cash, in which case it was payable in three equal annual instalments with interest at four per cent, or in Central Government securities, a ten-year security at par bearing interest at four and a half per cent or a thirty-year security at par bearing five and a half per cent, or partly in cash and partly in securities. The banks exercised the options differently: two took their whole amount in cash, ten took securities, and two divided their claims. This replaced the 1969 scheme of valuation principles and tribunal determination that the Supreme Court had struck down.

### Q: What role did the 1969 presidential election play in bank nationalisation?

A decisive one in the timing. President Zakir Husain died on 3 May 1969, and the election to succeed him divided the Congress: the party organisation nominated Neelam Sanjiva Reddy, while the Prime Minister, after first acquiescing, backed the acting President V. V. Giri as an independent and appealed for a conscience vote. The ordinance of 19 July 1969 was promulgated by Giri the day before he resigned to contest the election in earnest, and nationalisation became the popular centrepiece of the Prime Minister's campaign against her own party's establishment. Giri won narrowly after second preferences were counted. The split the election exposed became formal in November 1969, when the organisational wing expelled the Prime Minister and the party divided into two Congresses.

### Q: What was social control over banks, and why was it judged not enough?

Social control was the regulatory policy carried into law by the Banking Laws (Amendment) Act, 1968, in force from 1 February 1969. It reconstituted the boards of the larger banks with a majority of directors from outside the industrial houses, required professional and sectoral representation, restricted advances to directors and their concerns, strengthened the Reserve Bank's hand over bank managements, and created the National Credit Council to steer credit toward priority sectors. The government judged it insufficient because it changed boards without changing ownership: the reconstituted boards still served private shareholders, the redirection of credit to agriculture and small industry remained an exhortation rather than an instruction, and in the five months the scheme operated no measurable shift in lending patterns could be shown. Its supporters answered that it was never given a fair trial.

### Q: What is a corresponding new bank in the nationalisation Acts?

It is the statutory body the acquisition Acts created to receive each acquired undertaking. For every existing bank named in the First Schedule, the Act constituted a corresponding new bank, a body corporate with the corresponding name, and vested the undertaking of the old company in it from the appointed day. The device kept each bank's identity, branches, staff, and customers continuous while changing its legal owner overnight: the chief executive of the old bank became Custodian of the new one, the staff were transferred on their existing terms, and depositors dealt with the same bank under the same name. The old company survived only as a shell entitled to the compensation the Second Schedule fixed, barred from carrying on banking business.

### Q: Were foreign banks operating in India nationalised too?

No. Both rounds acquired only Indian banking companies meeting the statutory thresholds. Foreign banks, which operated in India through branches of banks incorporated abroad and held a significant share of metropolitan and trade business, were left untouched in 1969 and in 1980, a choice the government defended by the needs of trade finance and foreign exchange and by considerations of reciprocity for Indian banks' offices abroad. The exclusion gave the challengers of the 1969 Act a discrimination argument, that Indian banks were forbidden a business foreign banks remained free to conduct, and the Supreme Court's majority treated that complaint seriously even while accepting Parliament's power to acquire banking undertakings. Foreign banks continued under Reserve Bank licensing, constrained by the branch licensing regime rather than by any transfer of ownership.

### Q: In what way did the Twenty-fifth Amendment alter the law governing acquisition of property?

The Constitution (Twenty-fifth Amendment) Act, 1971, in force from 20 April 1972, rewrote the compensation guarantee in Article 31 of the Constitution. Where the Article had promised compensation for property compulsorily acquired, a standard the courts had read as requiring the money equivalent of what was taken, the amendment substituted an amount fixed by the acquiring law or determined by principles the law specified, and provided that no such law could be called in question in any court on the ground that the amount was inadequate. It also inserted Article 31C, shielding laws that gave effect to the directive principles on distribution of material resources and prevention of concentration of wealth from challenge under the equality and freedom articles. The amendment was Parliament's direct answer to the bank nationalisation judgment of February 1970.

### Q: What was the Lead Bank Scheme that followed nationalisation?

The Lead Bank Scheme was introduced by the Reserve Bank in December 1969, on the recommendation of the study group chaired by D. R. Gadgil, which had proposed an area approach to banking development. Districts across the country were allotted to designated banks, the lead banks, predominantly from the public sector, which were charged with surveying each district's credit needs and potential, identifying centres where branches should be opened, preparing district credit plans, and coordinating the institutions lending there. The scheme converted the new public ownership into territorial responsibility: a lead bank answered for its district's banking development in a way no private bank pursuing its own deposit growth would have undertaken, and it supplied the administrative machinery through which much of the branch expansion of the nineteen seventies was planned.

### Q: Why has the number of public sector banks fallen since 2017?

Because of amalgamation, not privatisation or closure. The government chose to consolidate the public sector into fewer, larger banks under schemes framed under the acquisition Acts and the State Bank's statute. The State Bank absorbed its associate banks in April 2017; the Dena Bank and the Vijaya Bank were amalgamated into the Bank of Baroda in April 2019; and on 1 April 2020 ten banks were combined into four, the Oriental Bank of Commerce and the United Bank of India into the Punjab National Bank, the Andhra Bank and the Corporation Bank into the Union Bank of India, the Syndicate Bank into the Canara Bank, and the Allahabad Bank into the Indian Bank. The result is twelve public sector banks, each enlarged institution carrying the branches, staff, and loan books of the banks it absorbed.

### Q: Has any government reversed or extended bank nationalisation since 1980?

Neither. No banking company has been nationalised after the six of April 1980, and no nationalised bank has been returned to private ownership by any government of any party. The movement since 1980 has been in a third direction: consolidation of the public sector banks into fewer institutions, public issues of shares by the nationalised banks subject to the Central Government retaining at least fifty-one per cent of their capital, and the licensing of new private banks from the nineteen nineties, which added competitors within the system the acquisition Acts created. The Union Budget for 2021-22 announced an intention to privatise two public sector banks, but the statutory amendment required to release a named bank from the fifty-one per cent floor has not been enacted, and the announcement stands unexecuted in the legislative record.

### Q: How did nationalisation change the number and location of bank branches?

Radically, on the Reserve Bank's published figures. In June 1969 India had 8,262 commercial bank offices, of which 1,833 were rural, and the average office served about 64,000 people. By 1991 the number of offices had risen to 60,570 and the average population per office had fallen to about 14,000. The composition changed as much as the total: the rural share of the network rose from a little over a fifth in 1969 to well over half by the turn of the nineteen nineties, as branch licensing was used as a policy lever linking permission to open in banked centres with openings in unbanked ones. The mechanism was the ownership itself: public sector banks opened branches as an instructed programme of the owner, through the lead bank machinery, rather than as a calculation of each branch's expected profit.
