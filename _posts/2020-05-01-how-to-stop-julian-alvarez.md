The plan is a communication plan, not a marking plan, and the price of it is that you will spend the whole ninety minutes talking instead of tackling. That is the compressed answer to how to stop Julian Alvarez, and it is the answer almost nobody prepares for, because the instinct in every opposition meeting is to name a defender, give him the forward, and move on to set pieces. Name a defender against this profile and you have already lost the argument, since the forward in question spends most of the contest in the places where no single defender is standing. He is not beaten in a duel. He is beaten in a conversation that happens before the duel exists.

Start from the observation that makes the rest of this coherent. A forward whose threat is positional rather than physical does not create advantage by winning contests. He creates advantage by living on the boundary between two defenders' responsibilities, which is the one place on a pitch where nobody is fully accountable and everybody assumes somebody else is. The goal that follows does not come from the metre he was faster over or the shoulder he leaned through. It comes from the metre where a centre-back and a holding midfielder each glanced at each other, each read the other as the owner, and each stepped a half-second late. That is the whole problem, and it is why the counter-plan below is built out of names, zones, and trigger words rather than out of aggression.

![How to stop Julian Alvarez, a defensive counter-plan for a forward who lives between the lines - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-41.webp)

The complication worth confronting immediately is the assumption that a smaller, less explosive forward is a lesser threat. Alvarez is not the tallest forward in this cluster and he is not the fastest, and a defensive staff that has just finished preparing for a forward who runs through a back line at full speed will feel a certain relief when this fixture arrives. That relief is the mistake. Physical superiority is close to irrelevant against a forward who never enters the duel on your terms, because your centre-back's advantage in a contest only pays when a contest occurs, and this profile is built to convert before a contest occurs. Meet him with a bigger, stronger defender and you have prepared for an event that will not happen while the events that do happen go unattended.

## How to Stop Julian Alvarez: The Plan in One Sentence

Defend him with a zone-ownership scheme in which every part of the pitch he can reach has a named owner agreed before kick-off, execute the hand-off between owners with an audible trigger rather than with eye contact, protect the seam behind the holding midfielder with a body position rather than a body, and accept that his side will be allowed to circulate the ball in front of you at a tempo of their choosing. That is the plan. Everything from here is either the mechanism that justifies it, the detail that makes it executable, or the honest account of what it costs.

What it costs is worth stating before the argument rather than after it. A scheme built on ownership and hand-offs asks your midfield line to stay compact and connected, which means it cannot also jump forward to pressure the ball at the moments it would like to. You will therefore concede the sideways pass, the back pass, and the slow rebuild, over and over, for long stretches. You will look passive to anyone watching from the stand who wanted to see pressure. Your reward is that the pass into the pocket, which is the pass this forward's entire game is built to receive, arrives into a space with an owner already facing it. Trade the harmless possession for the dangerous reception. That is the deal, and a team that cannot emotionally accept the first half of it will not get the second half.

The alternative plans fail in ways that are predictable enough to name. Man-marking him with a centre-back pulls a defender out of the line every time the forward drops, which is exactly the movement he uses to open the space his teammates run into, so the marking does the attacking team's work for them. Passing him between defenders without a rehearsed protocol produces the double-track, where two defenders follow the same movement and a third attacker arrives unattended into the vacated area. Ignoring him and holding rigid zones produces the free reception between lines, facing forward, with the whole defensive structure obliged to react to a player who is already turned. Each of those is a real plan used by real teams, and each of them has a specific failure signature that shows up on video within twenty minutes.

## The Ownership Rule

Here is the claim this article advances and returns to throughout. Against a forward who lives between lines, every metre of the pitch must have a named owner before kick-off, because the goal comes from the metre where two defenders each assumed the other was responsible. Call it the ownership rule. It sounds administrative rather than tactical, which is why it is underused, and it is the single highest-return preparation decision available against this profile.

The rule has three parts and all three have to hold. Ownership must be exhaustive, meaning there is no reachable area of the pitch about which the answer is unclear. Ownership must be exclusive, meaning exactly one player owns a given area in a given phase, so two defenders never both believe they have him. And ownership must be transferable by signal, meaning that when the forward crosses from one owner's area into another's, the transfer happens through something both players can perceive under crowd noise, which in practice means a called word and a physical gesture rather than a shared glance. A scheme that satisfies two of the three fails, and it fails in the same place every time, which is the boundary.

Ownership is not man-marking. A man-marker follows a player anywhere and accepts the structural damage that follows. An owner is responsible for a player only while that player is inside his area, and his responsibility ends the moment the hand-off completes. The distinction matters because it decides what happens to your shape. Man-marking deforms the block by design; ownership preserves the block and moves responsibility across it. Against this forward, preserving the block is the whole point, because the space he is trying to manufacture is created by pulling a defender out, not by beating one.

### What makes Julian Alvarez difficult to mark?

He does not stay inside one defender's area long enough to be marked by one defender. His receiving positions move between the centre-back and holding midfielder seam, the weak-side channel, and the pocket outside the box, so the defensive problem is not who is quick or strong enough, but who is accountable at the moment the pass is played.

## The Mechanism: Three Seams and How He Uses Them

Everything this forward does that hurts a defending side can be traced to three boundaries in your structure. Each one is a communication failure rather than a marking failure, which is a claim worth defending carefully, because it changes what you rehearse on the training pitch.
### Seam one: between the holding midfielder and the centre-back

The first and most productive boundary sits in the vertical corridor between your deepest midfielder and your nearest centre-back, roughly between the edge of your penalty area and eight to ten metres beyond it. In a 4-3-3 with a single pivot this corridor is wide and lightly staffed. In a 4-2-3-1 it is narrower but the two holding players tend to sit shoulder to shoulder and orient toward the ball, which opens it behind them. In a back three it is the smallest of the three seams, which is one reason the shape recommendation later in this article leans where it does.

The forward's method here is unglamorous and extremely effective. He walks into the corridor while the ball is in a wide area, so that he arrives before anybody's attention is on him. He checks his shoulder twice, not once, and the second check is the one that matters, because it happens after your holding midfielder has already turned his head toward the ball. He then adjusts his standing position by a metre or two so that he is directly behind the midfielder's blind side and directly in front of the centre-back's forward view, which is the exact geometry that makes the midfielder feel covered and the centre-back feel that stepping would be reckless. Both feelings are correct in isolation. Together they produce a free reception.

What follows is the part that punishes you. He does not receive to hold the ball. He receives on a half-turn with the front foot already pointed toward your goal, and the first touch travels forward, not sideways, which means the defender who steps late is now stepping into a player who is already moving away. From there the outcomes fan out: a shot from the edge of the box before your goalkeeper has finished setting, a slipped pass into the channel that a wide runner has been holding for two seconds, or a lay-off and a run beyond, which is the sequence that leaves your centre-backs defending a runner from behind with no reference point.

The observable evidence is easy to check without any analysis budget. Watch four of his receptions in the corridor and count how many times your equivalent midfielder was facing the ball rather than facing the pocket at the moment the pass was played. It is almost always all four. The midfielder is not lazy and he is not badly positioned in the conventional sense. He is oriented to the wrong information, and orientation is coachable in a way that speed is not.

### Seam two: between the weak-side centre-back and the weak-side full-back

The second boundary opens on the side away from the ball. When your block shifts toward the ball, the far-side full-back tucks in, the far-side centre-back covers across, and the two of them create a diagonal gap that widens as the ball travels wide on the opposite flank. Every defensive structure has this gap. It is not a flaw; it is the cost of being compact where the ball is. The question is whether a forward is disciplined enough to occupy it before the switch arrives, and this one is.

His movement into that gap is late by design. He drifts across the line while the ball is being circulated in the opposite half-space and arrives on the far side just as the switch is being loaded, which is the moment your far-side defenders are still resolving whose shoulder he is on. Because he is not the fastest forward in the cluster, he does not need or want a foot race from that position. What he wants is the two-yard start into a first-time reception, from which the finish comes early and low, or the cutback arrives before your recovering midfielders are goal-side.

The failure mode here is specific and it repeats. The far-side full-back sees a forward in his zone and steps up to engage, assuming the centre-back holds the line behind him. The centre-back sees his own full-back stepping and holds, assuming the full-back has it. The forward receives in the two metres between them, at an angle where neither can make contact without conceding a penalty, and the shot is away. Nobody made an error you could show them on video as a mistake, which is exactly why it happens repeatedly. Both defenders were following a reasonable default. The default was never agreed.

### Seam three: between your build-up and your midfield line, created by his press

The third boundary is the one that most opposition plans do not treat as part of defending at all, because it happens while your team has the ball. His pressing is not a work-rate ornament attached to a finisher. It is an attacking action that manufactures possession in the area of the pitch where possession is worth the most, and any counter-plan that does not extend into your own build-up decisions has only covered two thirds of the problem.

The pattern is a curved run at your ball-playing centre-back that arrives from a starting position slightly higher and slightly wider than your centre-back expects. The curve does two things at once: it takes away the pass into your holding midfielder by shadow, and it forces the ball toward the touchline, where your full-back receives it under a compressed set of options with the sideline acting as an extra defender. He does not need to win the ball himself. He needs to make your third pass predictable, at which point the second presser arrives on the receiving full-back and the ball is turned over thirty metres from your goal with your defensive structure still shaped for possession.

Notice the compounding effect. Your midfield line has pushed up to offer for the build-up, so the gap between it and your back line is wider than it would ever be in a settled defensive phase. The turnover therefore occurs at the exact moment your seam-one corridor is at its widest. That is the sequence that produces his most damaging moments, and it is why the ownership rule has to include a possession phase clause: the corridor has an owner even while you have the ball.

### Why all three are communication problems rather than marking problems

Take the three seams together and a pattern emerges. In every case, two of your players had enough information individually to prevent the reception, and the reception happened because the information was never combined. The holding midfielder knew where the ball was; the centre-back knew where the forward was. The full-back knew a forward had entered his zone; the centre-back knew nobody had passed him a call. The centre-back under press knew his inside pass was covered; the holding midfielder knew he had drifted out of the passing lane. None of these are physical deficits and none are fixed by selecting a more imposing defender.

This is what separates a positional forward from a physical one in preparation terms. Against a forward whose threat is depth running at pace, the counter-plan is mostly about geometry: line height, distance to your own goal, and the angle your defenders take on the first step. Against this one, the geometry matters less than the protocol. The cluster's hub article on [stopping elite strikers as a category](/2020/01/01/stopping-elite-strikers-complete-guide/) sets out the threat-type frame that makes the distinction usable, and the sharpest contrast inside the cluster is with [the plan against a forward whose threat runs through the channels at speed](/2020/04/15/how-to-stop-victor-osimhen/), where compression and line height carry most of the load and talk carries very little.

## Shape and Line Height: The Frame the Plan Sits In

The scheme has to live inside a shape, and the shape decision follows from one question: which structure gives the seam-one corridor a permanent owner without asking any player to do two jobs at once?

A back four with a double pivot is the most reliable answer for most teams. The two holding midfielders can split responsibility so that one is always oriented to the pocket while the other engages the ball, which is the single behavioral change that turns seam one from an open door into a defended area. The cost is that a double pivot commits numbers to a zone the opponent may not always attack, and against a side that overloads wide areas you may find your midfield outnumbered on the flanks. That is a real cost and it is worth paying here, because the pocket is where the forward's value is created and the flank is where it is only assembled.

A back three with wing-backs is the second-best answer and, at some levels, the best available one. Its advantage is that the middle centre-back can step into the corridor without breaking the line, since two covering defenders remain behind him. That single structural feature removes the hesitation that produces the free reception, because stepping is no longer a risk decision. The cost is the space between wing-back and outside centre-back, which is seam two in a slightly different location, and a side that circulates quickly will find it.

A back four with a single pivot is the shape most likely to be punished, and it is also the most common shape in amateur and youth football, which is why the small-squad section later in this article deals with it directly rather than pretending everybody can field a double pivot.
### Line Height: Higher Than Instinct Suggests

Higher than instinct suggests, and this is the counter-intuitive part of the plan. Because the forward's threat is not primarily a foot race, dropping deep does not reduce it. Dropping deep makes the pocket bigger, moves the reception closer to your goal, and shortens the distance from his first touch to a shot. A mid-block with the defensive line around the halfway line in settled phases, compressing to roughly thirty to thirty-five metres of vertical distance between the deepest defender and the highest presser, gives you the compactness that shrinks the corridor without offering the running lane behind that a different forward profile would punish.

The line height decision is genuinely different against him than against a depth runner, and confusing the two is one of the more common preparation errors. A depth runner makes you drop because the cost of being caught high is a clean run at your goalkeeper. A between-the-lines forward makes you hold because the cost of dropping is that you hand him the space he wanted in the first place, and he will happily receive it thirty metres from goal and turn. Judge line height by what it does to the pocket, not by what it does to your comfort.

There is a caveat that has to be stated honestly. Holding a line that high requires a defensive unit that steps together and a goalkeeper willing to defend a large area behind. If your centre-backs cannot step in unison, the high line will produce offside failures and through balls, and you will have traded a manageable problem for an unmanageable one. In that case, sit slightly deeper and accept a bigger pocket, then compensate with the double pivot and a tighter hand-off protocol. The plan degrades gracefully in that direction. It does not degrade gracefully if you drop deep and also fail to protect the corridor, which is the most common combination in sides that lose to this profile.

### Does physical strength help against Julian Alvarez?

Marginally, and only in the moments after he has already received. Strength decides duels, and his game is built to complete its decisive action before a duel begins. A powerful centre-back who cannot be dragged out of position is useful. A powerful centre-back who steps out to impose himself is exactly the defender the movement is designed to remove.

## The Individual Assignment: Which Defender Profile Fits

Three profiles matter in this plan, and the first of them is not a centre-back.

The most important individual selection is the deepest midfielder, because seam one is the primary threat and he is its permanent owner. The profile is a player who is comfortable defending with his body open, who scans behind himself as a habit rather than as an instruction, and who is willing to hold a position that looks passive while the ball is elsewhere. Ball-winning ability matters far less here than orientation. A midfielder who wins tackles by facing the ball will be turned by this forward all evening, because the tackle he is good at will be attempted after the reception rather than before it. If your options are a physically dominant destroyer who faces the ball and a smaller, calmer player who plays half-turned and talks constantly, pick the second one for this fixture. That recommendation will be unpopular in your dressing room and it is the right one.

The second selection is the centre-back who owns the step into the corridor when the hand-off calls for it. The profile is a defender who steps early and cleanly rather than one who steps hard, who reads the pass rather than reacting to the touch, and whose recovery angle is good enough that a failed step does not become a foot race he cannot survive. Aggression is a liability here in a way that would surprise most coaches. A centre-back who is desperate to make contact will bite on the drop, the forward will spin the other way, and the space behind the step is the space the wide runner has been waiting for. What you want is a defender who arrives with control at the moment of the touch and forces the reception backward.

The third selection is the full-back on the weak side, who owns seam two more often than anybody realizes. The profile is a defender who is disciplined about the tuck, who does not follow the winger blindly toward the touchline when the ball is on the opposite flank, and who talks with his centre-back about a player neither of them can comfortably see. Weak-side full-back is an unglamorous fixture-specific selection and it will decide more of this contest than the marquee centre-back duel.

Notice that none of the three profiles is defined by height, strength, or top speed. That is not an accident and it is the practical expression of the argument this cluster has been building. Athleticism is a defensive variable whose value depends entirely on the threat type it is being spent against. Against one forward it is decisive. Against this one it is nearly inert, and a staff that selects for it will field a physically imposing back line that loses to a series of receptions it never got to contest.

## The Hand-off Protocol

This is the artifact of the article and the operational core of the plan. The hand-off protocol assigns every zone the forward can occupy to a named owner, defines the signal that transfers ownership, states the covering adjustment behind the transfer, and names the failure signal that tells your analyst the hand-off was missed. Rehearse it, and the boundaries stop producing free receptions. Skip it, and everything above is a description of a problem rather than a plan.

| Zone he moves into | Owner in that zone | Trigger signal | Covering adjustment behind | Failure signal on video |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Central pocket in front of the back line, ball in a wide area | Deepest midfielder, body open | Owner calls "mine, pocket" and raises the inside arm | Nearest centre-back drops one metre and squares the line | Midfielder facing the ball at the moment of the pass |
| Central pocket, forward turns to face goal | Nearest centre-back steps, midfielder recovers goal-side | Centre-back calls "stepping" as the ball leaves the passer's foot | Far centre-back covers the space behind the step, full-backs narrow | Two defenders arriving at the same shoulder |
| Weak-side channel between centre-back and full-back | Weak-side centre-back, not the full-back | Centre-back calls "inside mine", full-back answers "wide yours" | Full-back holds width and delays the winger, midfield slides late | Full-back stepping up while the centre-back holds |
| Short drop toward his own midfield, away from your block | Nobody follows; block holds and compresses | Deepest midfielder calls "leave, drop off" | Line steps up two metres to shrink the corridor | A centre-back following him past the halfway line |
| Run off the shoulder behind the back line | Far-side centre-back, with the line stepping together | Line leader calls "step" on the passer's back-lift | Goalkeeper starts on the front foot and takes the through ball | Line stepping in a staggered sequence |
| Wide starting position drifting to the back post | Full-back on that side, centre-back tracks the near post | Full-back calls "back post mine" as the cross is loaded | Deepest midfielder drops onto the penalty spot for the cutback | Both centre-backs attacking the same delivery |
| Pressing your centre-back in your build-up phase | Ball-carrying centre-back plus the free pivot behind the shadow | Pivot calls "shadow left" or "shadow right" | Far centre-back offers the switch, full-back stays deep as the release | Pivot standing inside the cover shadow at the moment of the press |
| Second-ball area after a clearance, ten to twenty-five metres out | Second midfielder, permanently, in every phase | Standing assignment, called once before kick-off | Deepest midfielder screens the pocket behind him | Nobody within five metres of the drop zone |

Two features of that table are worth stating explicitly, because they are the parts that get quietly dropped when a staff simplifies the plan.

First, the weak-side channel is owned by the centre-back and not by the full-back. This is deliberate and it reverses the default instinct in most back fours. The full-back's natural read is that anybody entering his zone is his responsibility, and against this forward that read pulls the full-back inside, which opens the flank for the runner arriving behind him. Giving the channel to the centre-back keeps the full-back's width intact and gives the reception a defender who is already facing the right way.

Second, the short drop is owned by nobody. That entry is the one coaches find hardest to accept, because it reads as a licence to ignore a dangerous player. It is not. It is the recognition that following him thirty-five metres from your goal is the movement he wants most, since it converts one defender into a passenger and turns your organized block into a broken line. Let him have the ball facing his own goal with nobody near him. He can do very little from there that hurts you, and your block gains the two metres of compression that makes the next reception contested.
### Why the trigger has to be a word

Most defensive units hand off by eye contact and proximity. It works well enough against a forward who stays in one area, and it fails against one who crosses boundaries at the moment your defenders are looking at the ball. Eye contact requires two players to look at each other, which means neither is looking at the pass. A called word requires nothing but hearing, which leaves both sets of eyes where they belong.

The words themselves are unimportant as long as they are short, distinct, and used identically by every player. What matters is that the call happens at the trigger moment rather than after the reception. A "mine" shouted once the forward has already turned is a description of a problem, not a hand-off. The call belongs to the instant the passer's body shape commits, which for most passes is roughly half a second before contact, and rehearsing that timing is the actual content of the session. The cluster's session article on [rehearsing a containment plan on the training pitch](/2020/10/15/training-to-contain-a-superstar/) carries the drill design; this article carries the protocol the drill installs.

There is a second reason to insist on audible triggers, and it survives contact with a hostile crowd. A called hand-off is auditable. After the contest you can watch the sequence and establish whether the protocol was executed and failed, or was never executed, and those two situations require completely different responses from a coach. Without the call you are guessing at intent, and guessing at intent is how a staff ends up blaming a defender for a structural gap nobody assigned.

### What does a missed hand-off look like on video?

Two defenders converging on the same shoulder while a third attacker enters the vacated space unattended. Or the mirror image, which is two defenders both holding, with the forward receiving on a half-turn between them. Both patterns are visible in real time and neither requires tracking data to identify.

## Collective Triggers and Cover Behaviour

The ownership rule tells your players who is responsible. Collective triggers tell them when the whole block moves, and without them ownership degrades into eleven individual assignments that never coordinate.

The first trigger is the backward pass in the opponent's build-up. When his side plays backward, your block steps up together by three to five metres, which compresses the corridor before the next forward pass is loaded. This is a small movement and it is the highest-leverage collective behavior in the plan, because it directly attacks the geometry the forward relies on. He positions himself relative to your line, and every time your line moves without him noticing, his reference point is wrong by the width of the movement he did not track. Do it consistently and you will see him check his shoulder a third time, which costs him the half-second the whole method is built on.

The second trigger is the pass into the wide area at the height of your midfield line. That is the cue for your deepest midfielder to abandon the ball entirely and turn his body to the pocket. Not to look at the pocket while facing the ball. To turn, so that his hips point across the pitch and his back foot is toward his own goal, which is the posture from which he can step forward into a reception rather than spinning after one. Ball-side midfielders press the wide receiver; the pocket owner does not. That division is the difference between a block that concedes a wide pass and a block that concedes the pass that follows it.

The third trigger is the moment the forward's supporting midfielder turns to face your goal with time. This is the loading cue for a pass into the pocket, and it is the moment your centre-backs shorten the distance to the pocket owner rather than dropping. The instinct on seeing a player with time is to give ground. Against this forward, giving ground turns the reception into a free turn thirty metres out, and the shot that follows arrives early. Shortening instead means the reception is contested from in front, which is the only version of it your defenders can win.

The fourth trigger is the loss of possession by your own team in the middle third, which switches the plan into its transition clauses immediately rather than after your players have finished being disappointed. That trigger is discussed in its own section below, because transition is where the plan is most often abandoned.

Cover behavior sits underneath all four. Every time an owner steps forward, someone must own the space behind the step, and that ownership is a standing instruction rather than a decision. When the deepest midfielder steps into the pocket, the ball-side centre-back covers behind him. When the centre-back steps, the far centre-back covers across and both full-backs narrow. When the weak-side centre-back takes the channel, the deepest midfielder drops into the resulting hole rather than following the ball. Those three cover rules are short enough to be memorized in one meeting and they resolve the majority of the second-phase damage this forward's movement creates.

### How many defenders should track Julian Alvarez at once?

Exactly one at any given instant, and that one changes as he moves. Two defenders on the same movement is the failure the plan exists to prevent, since the second defender is always taken from a zone where a teammate of his is arriving. Ownership is exclusive by design, not by accident.

## Defending His Press: The Build-Up Clause

A counter-plan against this forward that stops at your defensive phase has covered the smaller half of the problem. His pressing manufactures possession high, and possession won high is worth several times possession won deep, which means the most efficient way to reduce his output is to remove the turnovers rather than to defend the attacks that follow them.

Start with the geometry of the press itself. He approaches your ball-playing centre-back on a curve that closes the inside passing lane first and the space second, which means the pass he is inviting is the one down the line to your full-back. That pass is not forbidden. It is a trap, and knowing it is a trap is what makes it usable. If your full-back receives it with his back to the touchline and no forward option, you have walked into the plan. If your full-back receives it already turned, with a midfielder inside him and a winger holding depth ahead, the same pass breaks the press and leaves the pressing forward thirty metres from the ball with his side's shape stretched.

The mechanism that makes the difference is your pivot's position relative to the cover shadow. A holding midfielder who stands inside the shadow is invisible to his own centre-back and is the reason the ball goes wide under pressure. A holding midfielder who moves laterally as the presser curves, staying half a metre outside the shadow, restores the inside option and turns the press into a two-versus-one against the presser. That lateral movement is the single technical detail worth rehearsing more than any other in your possession phase this week. It is small, it is unglamorous, and it defuses the highest-value part of his game.

The second mechanism is your goalkeeper's involvement. A goalkeeper who receives, holds the ball for a beat, and forces the pressing forward to commit before releasing changes the arithmetic of the press entirely, because a curved run that has committed cannot be re-aimed. The pass you want from that beat is the one into the space the presser has just vacated, which is normally the far centre-back or the pivot who has slid across. What you are doing is converting his aggressive starting position into a hole behind him, and this is the only phase of the contest in which his willingness to press is a cost to his own side rather than a gain.

The third mechanism is a deliberate reduction in build-up ambition when the state of the contest does not require it. There is no virtue in playing out from the back against a side that presses well when you are protecting a lead in the closing stages. A long ball into a contested area, with your second-ball owner already positioned, is a legitimate tactical choice and it removes the turnover risk entirely for that possession. The mistake is making that choice for ninety minutes, which hands the opponent the ball and eventually the pocket. Use it as a state-dependent option rather than as an identity.
## The Plan Phase by Phase

A protocol survives contact with a contest only if the staff has decided in advance what each stage of the ninety minutes is supposed to look like. What follows is the sequence, written as the coach would deliver it.

### The opening quarter: establish ownership and let him find nothing

The first phase is not about winning the ball. It is about making the boundaries visible to your own players and invisible to him. Your block sits in its mid-block shape, the pocket owner turns on every wide pass, the hand-off calls are loud enough to be heard by the crowd, and your team accepts sideways circulation without chasing it. What you are looking for in this phase is not a statistic. It is a behavior: the forward receiving twice in the corridor and finding a defender already facing him, then adjusting his starting position.

He will adjust. That is the compliment this profile pays a well-organized side, and it is also the information you need. When the pocket is defended, he moves to seam two or begins to start his movements from a wider position, which is your cue to check that the weak-side ownership call is being made. If your far-side centre-back and full-back have not spoken once in fifteen minutes, they will not speak at the moment it matters, and your assistant should be listening for that specifically rather than watching the ball.

The second thing to establish early is the pivot's cover-shadow discipline in your own build-up. Two or three clean escapes from the first press change the opponent's pressing enthusiasm noticeably, because a press that fails twice becomes a risk calculation rather than a reflex. Get those two escapes in the first quarter and you have bought yourself a calmer half.

### The second quarter: expect the pattern change and pre-decide the answer

Around the twenty-minute mark, a side built around this forward typically begins to manipulate your ownership rather than attack it directly. The common device is a second attacker occupying the pocket owner while the forward runs beyond, which asks your deepest midfielder to choose between two players in the same instant. That is the moment your protocol either holds or collapses, and the answer has to be decided before it happens.

The answer is that the pocket owner never follows the runner. He owns the space, not the person, and the runner belongs to the back line under the standing cover rule. What tempts a midfielder into following is the visual drama of a run beyond, which feels urgent, while holding the pocket feels passive. Tell him in the meeting that his discipline in this exact moment is the plan, because the moment he follows, the second attacker receives with a free turn and the whole structure is chasing.

The second device is the drop into wide areas, where the forward appears near the touchline in a position that belongs to nobody in your scheme. The instruction is that the wide areas are owned by whoever normally owns them, with no adjustment for his presence. He is not more dangerous near the touchline than another attacker would be. He is dangerous when your structure is reshaped in response to him, and the reshaping is the actual threat.

### Approaching the interval: manage the risk window

The ten minutes before the interval carry a specific risk against a side that presses well, because your players are managing fatigue for the first time and the temptation to play a lazy pass in your own half is at its highest. Your instruction is simple and it applies regardless of the state of the contest: no ambitious build-up in your own third in the last five minutes of the half. Take the safe option, get to the interval with the structure intact, and spend the half-time meeting on adjustments rather than on damage.

### The half-time adjustment to expect from the other side

Assume the opposing staff has watched your ownership scheme for forty-five minutes and has identified the boundary you defend least well. In most cases that will be seam two, because it requires the two defenders with the worst view of each other to communicate about a player neither can see. Expect a second-half increase in switches of play and in the forward starting from the far side.

Your counter to that is prepared rather than improvised. The weak-side full-back moves his default position two metres narrower, the deepest midfielder takes responsibility for calling the switch before it arrives (which he can do, because he is the player facing the field), and your ball-side winger drops onto the opposing full-back to slow the switch at its source. None of those is dramatic. Together they add roughly a second to the switch's delivery time, which is enough for the far-side pair to resolve ownership before the ball arrives.

### The hour mark: the substitution decisions that matter

Two substitution triggers belong in the plan before kick-off. The first is fatigue in the pocket owner, because the position demands constant scanning and constant talking, and both degrade before running capacity does. A tired pocket owner keeps his shape but stops turning, and the failure looks like a positional error when it is an attention error. Watch for two consecutive receptions where he was facing the ball, then make the change. Have a replacement who can execute the same protocol rather than a like-for-like athlete.

The second trigger is the introduction of a second forward by the opponent, which changes the ownership map because a runner beyond now occupies your back line permanently and the pocket owner loses his cover. The answer is either an extra body in midfield or a shift to a back three, and either decision should be pre-agreed with the players who will be affected rather than shouted from the technical area.

### The closing stages: protect the seam, not the scoreline

Sides that have defended this forward well for seventy minutes lose the contest in the last fifteen by dropping deeper as a group. The instinct is universal and here it is wrong, because dropping deep gives him the one thing your organization has denied him all evening, which is a large area of space in front of your back line with your midfield line behind the ball and unable to face the pocket. If you are protecting a lead, hold the same line height, keep the compactness, and use the ball rather than the touchline. The article on [defending the other between-the-lines forward in this cluster](/2020/03/15/how-to-stop-harry-kane/) makes the same argument from a different direction, since a forward who drops to build play rewards a deep block even more directly.

The corollary is that your set-piece and long-clearance behavior in the closing stages should aim for the flanks and for throw-ins in the opponent's half rather than for maximum distance. A clearance down the middle to nobody hands possession back at the exact height where the pocket becomes reachable again, which is why the second-ball owner in the protocol table has a standing assignment in every phase rather than a situational one.
## The Transition Addendum

Transition is where ownership schemes are abandoned, because ownership is a settled-phase concept and a turnover produces four seconds in which nobody feels settled. Against this forward those four seconds are the most dangerous of the contest, since his starting position when your team has the ball is usually higher and more central than a wide forward's, and his first movement after a turnover is toward the space your midfield has vacated rather than toward the ball.

The rest-defense answer has three components and all of them are decided before you attack, not during. First, one of your two holding midfielders is a permanent screen in your own attacking phase, positioned in the corridor rather than beside the ball, which means your attacking overload is smaller by one player and your counter-protection is real. Second, your centre-backs stay split rather than tight, because a split pair covers the two channels the first pass after a turnover will target and a tight pair covers neither. Third, the full-back on the side away from the attack stays home as a matter of instruction, which is the cost of defending this profile properly and it will limit your own width.

The trigger on the turnover is not a press. It is a delay. Your nearest player closes the ball carrier's forward passing angle without committing to a tackle, buying the two seconds your structure needs to reset. Committing to the tackle is what produces the clean break, because a beaten presser removes himself from the recovery entirely. Against a forward whose first movement is into your vacated corridor, the two seconds of delay are worth more than the ball.

If the first pass does reach him in transition with your line broken, the last defensive principle is to force him wide rather than to attempt to win the ball. He finishes early and accurately from central areas and his shot arrives before a goalkeeper is set, so the recovering defender's job is to run the angle that moves the shot to the side rather than the angle that gets a foot in. A recovering run aimed at the ball nearly always concedes the shot at the moment of the touch. A recovering run aimed at the near post shoulder pushes the reception outward and buys the goalkeeper the half-second that changes the save.

## The Set-Piece Addendum

His dead-ball threat is not aerial dominance and defending him as if it were will waste your best header of the ball on a player who is not attacking the first contact. The relevant dead-ball behaviors are three.

The first is the near-post flick and the second-contact position. A forward with good timing and a low centre of gravity is more valuable arriving late at the penalty spot than contesting the first ball at the near post, and a zonal scheme that assigns all its bodies to the flight of the delivery is systematically vulnerable to that arrival. Assign a defender to the penalty spot whose job is nothing but the second contact, and tell him he is not permitted to attack the first ball under any circumstance.

The second is the short-corner and edge-of-box routine, which is where an early, accurate striker of the ball is genuinely dangerous. The defending answer is a committed pair rather than a single defender pushed out late, because a single defender invites the return pass and the shot from the resulting angle. Two players out means the shot has to be taken from further out or the routine has to be abandoned.

The third is his own defensive set-piece role, which matters because his side will often hold him high as an outlet when defending corners. That is a transition problem disguised as a set-piece problem. Your instruction is that the defender assigned to him at the halfway line does not join the attack, does not drift, and does not treat the assignment as a formality, and that your deepest attacking player is responsible for the first pass out rather than for the delivery. Sides concede on this pattern regularly because the assignment is given to whoever happens to be nearest at the moment the corner is won.

## The Goalkeeper's Job

The goalkeeper's brief against this forward is different from the brief against a physical nine, and giving him the wrong brief costs goals that look unstoppable and are not.

The defining feature to prepare for is the early shot. He strikes the ball before the goalkeeper has finished the last adjustment step, often from the edge of the box, often low and across, and often with a shorter back-lift than the goalkeeper's timing expects. A goalkeeper who is still moving when the shot leaves is a goalkeeper who is falling backward at the moment he needs to be loaded. The practical answer is set position discipline: get to the ready position earlier and hold it longer, accept being marginally out of position rather than being in perfect position and unset, and treat any reception in the pocket as a shot cue rather than as a build-up cue.

The second element is starting depth. Because the plan holds a higher line than instinct suggests, the goalkeeper is defending more space behind, and his starting position has to reflect that or the plan's biggest concession becomes its biggest weakness. Sweeping ability matters here, and if your goalkeeper is not comfortable defending space, the line has to come down and the pocket has to be defended with numbers instead. That is a legitimate variation of the plan rather than a failure of it, and it is the version most amateur sides should adopt.

The third element is communication, since the goalkeeper is the only player who sees the entire ownership map at once. His most valuable calls are not the routine ones about the ball. They are the ones that name the unowned player: telling the pocket owner that the forward has entered behind him, telling the far-side pair that the forward has crossed the line, telling the line when to step. A goalkeeper who does this well is worth more to this specific plan than one who is marginally better at shot-stopping, and that is a selection thought worth having honestly.

### How does a defensive line know when to step against him?

The cue is the passer's back-lift, not the forward's movement. Stepping on his movement means stepping after the pass is already travelling. Stepping as the passer loads moves your line while the pass is still a plan, which is the version that catches a well-timed run off the shoulder.
## Fallbacks: What to Do When the First Plan Fails

Plans fail. A counter blueprint that does not carry its own fallbacks is a wish, and the staff that improvises fallbacks in real time nearly always chooses the option that feels most urgent rather than the one that addresses the mechanism. Three failure modes account for almost everything that goes wrong here, and each has a prepared answer.

### Failure mode one: the pocket owner keeps getting turned

The symptom is the forward receiving in the corridor and turning cleanly, repeatedly, with your deepest midfielder arriving from behind. The cause is almost never a lack of effort. It is either orientation (he is facing the ball at the moment of the pass) or overload (a second attacker is occupying him so that ownership becomes ambiguous).

If the cause is orientation, the fix is verbal and immediate, and it is delivered as a body-shape instruction rather than as encouragement. Tell him to stand with his hips open to the touchline and his eyes moving, and have the goalkeeper call his orientation for ten minutes until the habit resets. If the cause is overload, the fix is structural: add a second body to the corridor by dropping one of your wide midfielders inside, which costs you width in your own attacking phase and is worth it. The version of that change most sides reach for is a formation switch announced from the technical area, which takes several minutes to settle. The faster version is a single instruction to one player, which takes one stoppage.

### Failure mode two: seam two is being opened by switches

The symptom is repeated receptions on your weak side between full-back and centre-back, usually followed by a cutback rather than a shot. The cause is a hand-off that is never called because the two defenders involved cannot see each other.

The prepared answer has two stages. First, move the ownership call to a third party, which is the goalkeeper or the deepest midfielder, both of whom can see the far side clearly. A call from outside the pair resolves ambiguity faster than a call from inside it. Second, if that does not hold, drop the weak-side winger to make a temporary back five when the ball is on the opposite flank. This is a significant concession because it removes your outlet, and it is a better concession than a series of cutbacks arriving into your six-yard box.

### Failure mode three: your build-up keeps being turned over

The symptom is your centre-backs or full-backs losing the ball inside your own half under his press. The cause is usually the pivot standing inside the cover shadow, occasionally a goalkeeper who releases too early, and sometimes simply a mismatch in ability that no positional adjustment will solve.

The prepared answer escalates in three steps. The first is the lateral pivot movement described earlier, which restores the inside pass. The second is dropping a midfielder between the centre-backs so that the build-up starts with a numerical advantage against the first line, which is the standard answer and works well as long as your opponent does not push a second presser onto the dropping player. The third, when the first two fail, is to stop building from the back entirely for a period, use the goalkeeper's distribution into wide areas beyond the press, and rely on the second-ball owner. Step three is not surrender. It is a recognition that a turnover thirty metres from your goal is worth several times more to the opponent than a fifty-fifty in midfield, and that the trade favors you.

### The fallback nobody should use

Man-marking him with a dedicated centre-back for the remainder of the contest is the option most likely to be suggested from the bench and it is the one to refuse. It is emotionally satisfying, it looks decisive, and it hands his side exactly the structural distortion they are trying to manufacture. If the situation is desperate enough that a personal assignment seems necessary, give it to a midfielder rather than a defender, because a midfielder pulled out of position damages a smaller part of your structure and can be replaced by a teammate sliding across. Pulling a centre-back out of a back four to follow a forward into midfield is the movement that ends with a cross into an unprotected penalty area.

## What to Concede Deliberately

Every plan concedes something, and a plan that pretends otherwise gets found out in the seventieth minute when the players discover the cost for themselves. State the concessions in the meeting, so that when they appear the team recognizes them as the plan working rather than as the plan failing.

Concede possession in front of your block, permanently and without apology. Sides using this plan will spend long periods watching the ball move sideways at the edge of their shape, and both players and supporters will find it uncomfortable. The discomfort is the point. Every sideways pass is a pass that is not entering the pocket, and the moment your midfield line jumps to relieve the discomfort is the moment the corridor opens.

Concede the switch of play. Your block will be compact toward the ball, which by definition leaves the far side available, and attempting to defend both sides at once produces a stretched block that defends neither. Your answer to the switch is delay at the source and a late slide, not permanent coverage.

Concede the shot from distance in front of your block, within limits. A shot from outside the box with bodies in the way is a low-value outcome and it is a far better one than a reception at the edge of your six-yard box. The limit is that the plan does not concede clean sight of goal from central areas, which is why the pocket owner's positioning is non-negotiable and why the goalkeeper treats a pocket reception as a shot cue.

Concede the space behind, conditionally. The high line is a genuine trade and it will occasionally produce a run in behind that is not covered. If that happens twice in twenty minutes, the trade is not working with the personnel you have and you should drop the line by five metres and thicken the midfield rather than persist for the sake of consistency. The plan's principles survive that change; only the numbers move.

Do not concede the reception between your lines facing forward. That is the one outcome the entire structure exists to prevent, and if it is happening more than a couple of times per half then no other concession is buying you anything, because the thing you are paying for is not being delivered.
## How the Plan Degrades With a Weaker Squad

The plan above assumes a defensive unit that can hold a compact mid-block, step together, and communicate under pressure. Most teams reading this cannot do all three, and the honest version of a counter blueprint says which parts to drop first.

The part to keep, at every level, is ownership. It costs nothing athletically, it requires no particular quality of defender, and it is the component that delivers most of the value. A parks side that agrees before kick-off who owns the pocket, who owns the weak-side channel, and who owns the second ball will defend a between-the-lines forward better than a technically superior side that has never discussed it. This is the rare case where the highest-return preparation is also the cheapest, which is why the argument at the top of this article matters more at amateur level than at elite level rather than less.

The part to drop first is the line height. A high line requires synchronized stepping, which requires either a great deal of rehearsal or a back four that has played together for years. Without it, drop the line to around twenty-five metres from your own goal in settled phases, accept that the pocket in front of you is larger, and fill it with a second holding midfielder. You have moved from denying the reception to contesting it, which is a downgrade, and it is survivable.

The part to drop second is the ambition in your build-up. A side that cannot beat the first press should not attempt to. Go long into a wide area, aim for the throw-in, put your second-ball owner in position, and remove the highest-value turnovers from the contest entirely. The cost is possession, and possession is worth less to you than a turnover thirty metres from your own goal is worth to them.

The part to keep last, alongside ownership, is the instruction not to follow him when he drops deep. It requires nothing but discipline, and it prevents the most common amateur failure, which is a centre-back being drawn thirty-five metres from his own goal by a forward who has no intention of doing anything with the ball once he gets there.

### What if you cannot field a double pivot?

Use one holding midfielder and give him a permanent shadow. The ball-near central midfielder recovers onto the pocket rather than onto the ball whenever the opponent circulates, which produces a temporary pair at the moments it is needed. Less reliable than a genuine double pivot, and far better than one screen alone.

The trigger for that recovery is the same wide pass that turns the pocket owner, so both behaviors are installed by the same rehearsal rather than by two separate ones.

## Teaching Zone Ownership to a Side That Cannot Man-Mark

Youth and amateur coaches face a version of this problem that elite staffs do not, which is that their players may not yet have the spatial vocabulary to understand a zone at all. The instruction "own that space" means very little to a fourteen-year-old defender, and repeating it more loudly does not help. What works is converting the zone into something visible.

Use physical references first. Mark the corridor between the back line and the midfield line with cones or a line of the pitch, tell the pocket owner that his job is to keep his heels near it, and give him a single success criterion, which is that no opponent receives inside the marked area facing forward. Success criteria beat instructions with young players because they can be self-assessed. A player who knows what a good outcome looks like will find his own way to it faster than one who has been told where to stand.

Then convert ownership into a naming exercise. Before a session game, walk the group through the pitch and have players say aloud which zone they own and who owns the zone beside them. The saying is the point. A defender who has spoken the words "the pocket is his, the channel is mine" once in a calm environment is dramatically more likely to make the call under pressure than one who has only heard those words from a coach.

Finally, rehearse the hand-off in isolation before rehearsing it in a game. A simple constraint works: two defenders, one attacker who moves slowly between two marked zones, and a rule that the defenders concede a point every time both of them move at once or neither does. The rule makes the failure visible and it makes the correct behavior scoreable, which is the mechanism by which a habit forms. The session article linked earlier carries the progressions in full, including the small-squad and poor-pitch versions.

### Which age group can learn this?

Ownership language works from the point where players can hold two responsibilities at once, which for most groups is early adolescence. Younger groups get the simple version: one player owns the space in front of the defenders and stays there, everybody else defends the ball. Add complexity only when that holds.

## Rest Defense for Sides That Will Dominate the Ball

A significant portion of the teams preparing for this forward will have more possession than his side, and everything above has to be re-read through that lens, because a plan written for a defending team applies differently to a team that spends the contest attacking.

The core adjustment is that your defensive phase is short and your rest defense is long. You will not sit in a mid-block for seventy minutes; you will hold the ball, lose it, and defend in transition, over and over. Under those conditions the ownership rule applies to your attacking shape rather than to your defensive shape. Every time you commit players forward, the corridor in front of your centre-backs must already have an owner standing in it, and that owner is not permitted to join the attack no matter how attractive the overload looks.

The second adjustment concerns your full-backs. A side that dominates the ball pushes both full-backs high, which is the structure this forward's counter-attacking movement is designed to punish, since the space he attacks after a turnover is exactly the one a high full-back has left. The standing instruction is that one full-back stays, always, and that the choice of which one is decided by the location of the ball rather than by preference. If you are unwilling to accept that constraint, the alternative is a back three in possession, which achieves the same protection with different personnel.

The third adjustment is your counter-press, and here the plan diverges from conventional wisdom. Counter-pressing aggressively after a turnover is normally correct, and against a side built around a forward who receives between lines it is correct only if your counter-press wins the ball within about three seconds. If it does not, you have committed players forward into a structure that has already released the ball into the space behind them. The prepared instruction is a time limit: press hard for the count of three, then drop and reorganize regardless of how close you feel to winning it. Teams that press for six seconds against this profile concede the transition that produces the shot.
## Scouting Him Properly: Mapping Reception Zones From Two Matches

The analysis that supports this plan does not require a data budget, a tracking feed, or a team of analysts. It requires two full matches, a pause button, and a disciplined method, and it produces something a coach can deliver in a fifteen-minute meeting.

Watch the first match for reception zones only. Ignore the goals, ignore the highlights, and ignore the ball when he is not involved. Every time he receives, mark the location on a pitch diagram and note two things beside it: which of your defensive positions would have owned that location in your shape, and whether he received facing your goal, facing sideways, or facing away. Twenty to thirty receptions is enough to produce a pattern, and the pattern is nearly always clearer than a coach expects, because a forward with a positional method returns to the same reference points repeatedly.

Watch the second match for the two seconds before each reception. This is the harder pass and it carries the information the first pass cannot. What you are recording is what he did before the ball was played: where he started, when he began to move, which defender he was standing behind, and what happened in your equivalent structure at that moment. This is where the seams become visible, because a reception in the pocket looks like a defensive error until you notice that the movement started while the ball was thirty metres away on the far side.

The third piece of the dossier is his pressing, and it is recorded differently. Rather than mapping locations, record the trigger: what the opponent did that made him start his run. Common triggers include a pass back to a centre-back, a heavy first touch, and a goalkeeper receiving with his hips square. Knowing his triggers lets you eliminate them from your own build-up, which is a far more actionable output than a heat map of where he presses.

Turn those three artifacts into one page. The page has the reception map with your ownership names written directly onto the zones, the movement triggers listed as prose in your meeting notes, and the press triggers listed as prohibitions for your own build-up. That page is the meeting. Anything longer will not be retained by players and anything shorter will not change behavior.

The errors that mislead analysts on this profile are worth naming, because they are consistent. The first is scouting him from highlights, which shows finishes and hides the walk into the corridor that produced them, and therefore produces a plan aimed at the shot rather than at the reception. The second is scouting only matches in which he scored, which biases the sample toward opponents who defended the corridor badly and hides the shape that contained him. Deliberately include a match in which his side struggled, since the containment pattern is the most useful thing in your entire study. The third is treating the number of touches as a measure of his influence, which reverses the truth for a forward whose value is created by occupying two defenders while touching nothing.

The advantage of a saved plan against a recurring opponent is real: the second time you prepare for this profile, the reception map is already built and the meeting takes ten minutes instead of an hour.

### What do you actually write on the whiteboard?

Three things and nothing else: the pitch diagram with a name written in each zone he uses, the four trigger words your unit will call, and the two prohibitions for your own build-up. Players retain a name and a word. They do not retain a paragraph, and a whiteboard covered in arrows is a meeting that changes nothing.

## The Numbers That Help and the Numbers That Mislead

A short note on measurement, because this profile is the one most often misjudged by a spreadsheet.

The numbers that help are the ones that describe location and situation rather than volume. Where his shots come from, how many of them arrive from inside the width of the six-yard box, and what proportion of his receptions occur between the opposition midfield and defensive lines are all durable descriptors of a positional forward, and all of them can be approximated by hand from two matches. The proportion of his side's high turnovers that begin with his press is another, and it is the number that most often surprises a staff preparing for him for the first time.

The numbers that mislead are aerial duel counts, physical outputs, and any measure that rewards contest volume. A forward who wins few aerial duels may simply be a forward who does not enter them, and a defensive plan built around that observation will be a plan built around an event he has no intention of contesting. Distance covered is similarly hollow here, since the running that matters is short, timed, and mostly untracked by the summary numbers a coach is likely to see.

The eye-test check that has to accompany any of it is the one described in the scouting section: watch the two seconds before the reception. No available number captures the walk into the blind side, and the walk is the mechanism.
## The Arguments People Have About Him, Answered

Five positions dominate the discussion around this forward, and each of them contains something true. A counter blueprint has to engage them, because a coach whose players believe one of the false versions will have a plan undermined from inside his own dressing room.

### "He is a system forward rather than an elite one"

The strong version of this argument says that his output depends on playing in sides that create high-quality chances and win the ball high, and that in a weaker structure the same movements would produce far less. The observable part of that is true and it is true of every forward whose value is positional rather than physical. A player who creates advantage by occupying seams needs teammates who use the space the occupation opens; that is what positional value means.

The verdict is that the argument proves less than the people making it think. From a defending coach's point of view the question is not whether he would score as often somewhere else. It is whether his movement forces your structure to compromise, and it does, regardless of who else is on the pitch. Prepare for the mechanism. The counterfactual career is not your problem on Saturday.

### "He is more effective as a second striker than as a lone nine"

This one is closer to a genuine tactical distinction and it changes your plan, which is why it is worth resolving rather than dismissing. As a lone forward he has to occupy two centre-backs at moments when he would rather be dropping, which reduces the frequency of his best action. In a front two or in a role behind a target, he is released from the pinning job and can live in the corridor almost permanently, which is his highest-value state.

The defending implication is direct. If he plays with a partner who occupies your centre-backs, your pocket owner is on his own and the double pivot becomes essential rather than preferable. If he plays alone, your centre-backs have more freedom to step, because the space behind the step is less immediately threatened, and the hand-off from midfielder to centre-back is safer. Read the team sheet and adjust the aggression of the step accordingly.

### "His pressing matters more than his finishing"

The people who say this are usually analysts and the people who disagree are usually supporters, and the honest answer is that the two are the same quality expressed twice. Both actions are built on reading a moment before it fully exists: the press starts before the opponent's touch is heavy, and the shot is struck before the goalkeeper is set. Ranking them is a debate about which output is easier to see.

For a defending coach the ranking does matter, though, and it points at your build-up. The pressing damage is preventable through your own decisions, since you control where your goalkeeper releases the ball and where your pivot stands. The finishing damage is only preventable by denying receptions. Preventable threats deserve more preparation time than unavoidable ones, which is why the build-up clause sits inside this counter-plan rather than in a separate possession meeting.

### "He is easy to defend physically"

This is the belief that costs the most and it is the one this article was written to dismantle. A defending unit that arrives at this fixture confident because their centre-backs are bigger has misunderstood which contest is being played. Physical superiority pays out in duels, and this forward's method is designed to complete its decisive action before a duel exists, which means your advantage is being held in a currency the opponent does not accept.

The verdict is that physical qualities in your back line are close to neutral here, with one exception worth naming: recovery pace in the covering centre-back has real value, because it lowers the cost of a failed step and therefore lets your defenders step earlier and more decisively. That is athleticism used to enable organization rather than to replace it, which is the general form of the argument this cluster has been building since its first article.

### "His output depends on the quality of the side around him"

The final position overlaps with the first but arrives from a different direction, usually as a claim about the service he receives. Again there is a true part: a forward who receives between lines needs somebody capable of playing the pass that arrives there, and those passes are not common at every level.

The practical use of that truth is that it names your real target. If you cannot deny the reception, deny the passer. Marking the pocket is one solution and pressuring the player who can find the pocket is another, and the second is often cheaper. When your scouting shows that a single midfielder plays most of the passes into the corridor, giving that midfielder a dedicated pressure assignment can be more effective than adding a body to the pocket, because it attacks the supply rather than the destination. That decision belongs to the scouting page, not to a general principle, and it is exactly the kind of judgment the cluster hub article frames as the difference between defending a player and defending a mechanism.

## The Duel From the Defender's Point of View

A short section for players rather than coaches, because the individual experience of this fixture is unlike the experience of facing a physical forward and defenders should know that in advance.

The first thing to expect is a quiet contest that feels comfortable and is not. You may go twenty minutes without a physical exchange and conclude that you are winning the duel. You are not necessarily winning anything; you may simply be a player he has decided not to engage. The measure of your evening is not how many contests you won but how many times he received facing your goal, and a defender who does not know that will report a good performance after a defeat.

The second thing is that your most important actions are your first steps, taken before the ball is played. Against a physical forward the decisive moment is contact. Here the decisive moment is a half-second of anticipation, and the physical qualities you rely on elsewhere are largely spectators. Defenders who enjoy the fight often find this fixture frustrating, and the frustration itself is a risk, because a frustrated defender steps into a challenge he does not own and the structure opens behind him.

The third thing is that your talking is a defensive action with the same status as a tackle. That framing is unfamiliar to most players and it is worth saying plainly in the meeting. The call that hands him over to your midfielder prevents a shot as surely as a block does, and the missed call concedes one as surely as a mistimed tackle. A defender who talks constantly and never touches the forward has had an excellent evening.
## Three Sequences, Worked

Principles become plans when a staff can narrate them at the speed the contest happens. What follows are three sequences of the kind that decide these fixtures, each written twice: the version where the protocol is absent and the version where it holds.

### Sequence one: the wide circulation and the pocket entry

The ball goes from the opposing centre-back to the full-back, who plays it into a winger holding the touchline. The winger takes a touch inside and looks up. During those four seconds, the forward has walked from a position beside your left centre-back to a position two metres behind your deepest midfielder, in the centre of the corridor.

Without the protocol, your midfielder is square to the ball, tracking the winger's options and feeling that his positioning is fine because he is between the ball and the goal. The pass is played through the gap he cannot see, the forward receives on the half-turn, and your centre-back is now choosing between stepping into a player already facing him and dropping to protect the space behind. He drops, because dropping feels safer, and the shot is struck from twenty metres with your goalkeeper adjusting rather than set.

With the protocol, the wide pass is the trigger. Your midfielder turns his hips to the touchline and calls "mine, pocket" as he does it, which is heard by the ball-side centre-back, who drops one metre and squares the line. The forward is now standing in front of a defender who is facing him rather than behind one who is facing away. The pass into the corridor is no longer available, and the winger's options reduce to the outside or backward. Nothing dramatic occurred. A shot was removed from the contest by a body turn and a word.

### Sequence two: the switch and the weak-side channel

Possession circulates on your right for twenty seconds, your block shifts across, and your left full-back tucks in as he should. The forward, who has been on the far side of your structure, drifts toward the space between that full-back and your left centre-back while the ball travels back through midfield toward the opposite flank.

Without the protocol, the full-back sees the forward enter his area and steps to engage, assuming the centre-back is holding behind him. The centre-back sees his full-back step and holds the line, assuming the full-back has taken the assignment. The switch arrives, the forward receives in the space between the two of them, and the cutback finds a runner arriving at the penalty spot with your midfielders still recovering. Reviewing that concession afterward, both defenders will describe reasonable decisions and neither will accept responsibility, which is the signature of a boundary failure rather than an individual one.

With the protocol, the ownership call comes from a third party. Your deepest midfielder, who is facing the field and can see the far side, calls the switch before it is played and names the owner: "inside is centre-back". The centre-back takes the channel, the full-back holds width and delays the winger, and the midfield line slides late so that the cutback lane has a body in it. The switch still arrives. It arrives into a resolved situation, and the reception happens with a defender in front of the forward rather than beside him.

### Sequence three: the press and the turnover

Your goalkeeper rolls the ball to your right centre-back. The forward starts a curved run from a position slightly wide of your centre-back's right shoulder, closing the lane into your pivot as he comes. Your pivot is standing directly behind the curve of the run.

Without the protocol, your centre-back looks inside, sees his midfielder covered, and plays the only pass available, which is down the line to your full-back. The full-back receives facing the touchline with an opponent already arriving, plays a hurried ball forward, and it is intercepted. Six seconds later your defensive structure, which was set up to build rather than to defend, is facing a three-against-three with the forward running into the corridor his own press just vacated.

With the protocol, the pivot has been moving as the run curved and is now half a metre outside the shadow, visible to the centre-back. The centre-back plays inside, the pivot receives with the pressing forward beyond the ball, and your side is playing forward against a defensive structure that has committed its highest player to a pressing action that failed. This is the phase in which his aggression costs his own team, and it is available to you two or three times per contest if your pivot's movement is rehearsed.

### The common feature

In all three sequences, nothing physical decided the outcome. No defender ran faster, jumped higher, or won a contest. What separated the two versions was whether a boundary had an owner and whether the transfer was spoken. That is the whole argument of this blueprint reduced to its smallest form, and it is why the preparation for this fixture happens in a meeting room and on a training pitch rather than in a gym.
## The Verdict

Defend him with organization or do not bother defending him at all. That is the conclusion the mechanism forces, and it is unusual in this cluster, where most of the counter-plans have a legitimate individual component: a defender profile who genuinely reduces the threat, a duel that can be won often enough to matter. Against this forward there is no defender who solves the problem, because the problem is not located inside a duel. It is located in the boundaries between your players, and boundaries are fixed by agreement rather than by selection.

That makes him, by a reasonable reading, the hardest forward in this cluster to plan for at levels below the elite, and the easiest to contain at the very top. A professional staff with a full preparation week, a settled back line, and players who communicate can execute the ownership rule and reduce him to receptions with his back to goal. A side that has never discussed the corridor will be beaten by movements that look modest on video and that nobody can identify as anybody's mistake. The gap between those two outcomes is a meeting and two rehearsals, which is the most encouraging sentence in this article and also the most demanding one, because it removes every excuse rooted in personnel.

The narrower verdict for a coach deciding what to do this week. Play a double pivot if you have one. Hold a mid-block with a line around the halfway line if your defenders step together, and drop five metres if they do not. Give the pocket, the weak-side channel, the second ball, and the short drop explicit owners, and rehearse the hand-off with words rather than glances. Move your pivot out of the cover shadow in your own build-up and stop the turnovers that produce his best moments. Accept the circulation, accept the switch, accept the long shot, and refuse the one thing you cannot afford, which is a reception between your lines facing your goal.

The wider point closes the cluster's running argument about athleticism. The forwards in this group have been getting progressively less physically extreme and progressively harder to defend without organization, which is not a coincidence and not a ranking. It is a demonstration that the defensive resource a forward consumes is a function of threat type, and that a defending staff's job is to work out which resource is about to be spent rather than to assume the answer is speed and strength. The cluster's [complete guide to stopping elite strikers](/2020/01/01/stopping-elite-strikers-complete-guide/) holds the general version of that frame, and the plans for the other forwards in the group show how differently the same principle cashes out when the threat is depth, power, or acceleration.

One last thing to say to the coach who reads all of this and still wants to give a defender a personal assignment. The instinct is understandable, since a named assignment feels like control and a zonal protocol feels like hope. It is exactly backward. A named assignment gives one player an impossible job and gives the opponent a lever to distort your structure. A protocol gives eleven players a small job each and leaves your shape intact while they do it. Control comes from the second one, and it is available to any side willing to spend twenty minutes agreeing on who owns what.
## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you stop Julian Alvarez?

Stop him with ownership rather than with a marker. Agree before kick-off which player owns the pocket in front of your back line, which owns the weak-side channel, which owns the second ball, and which owns nothing when the forward drops deep. Transfer responsibility between owners with a spoken trigger rather than eye contact, since eye contact requires both defenders to stop watching the pass. Hold a compact mid-block rather than a deep one, because dropping enlarges the space he wants. Move your holding midfielder out of the cover shadow in your own build-up so his press cannot manufacture turnovers high up the pitch. Then accept the circulation in front of you without chasing it, and refuse only the reception between your lines facing your goal.

### Q: What is Julian Alvarez's biggest weakness?

Relative to the elite forwards he is compared with, his least productive situation is the one where he is asked to pin two centre-backs alone with no partner occupying them, and where the ball arrives long and contested rather than to feet. In that state his best actions are unavailable: he cannot drift into a seam because leaving the line concedes the whole defensive structure a free player, and he cannot receive early because the delivery is a contest rather than a pass. A defending side that keeps its line high, its distances short, and its service into the corridor closed reduces him to receptions with his back to goal in areas where nothing decisive follows. The limitation is situational rather than technical, which is why organization defends him better than any individual can.

### Q: How do you defend Alvarez when he drops between the lines?

Give the space an owner rather than giving the player a marker. Your deepest midfielder holds the corridor with his hips open to the touchline, turning on every wide pass so he is facing the pocket rather than the ball at the moment a pass could be played. If the forward receives anyway, the nearest centre-back steps as the ball leaves the passer's foot and calls the step, while the far centre-back covers behind and the full-backs narrow. The two failures to avoid are both about numbers: two defenders arriving at the same shoulder, which opens the space a second attacker enters, and nobody arriving because each assumed the other had it. When he drops far enough that following him would break your line, let him go and step your line up instead.

### Q: Is Alvarez more dangerous as a lone nine or a second striker?

He is more dangerous with a partner. A lone forward has to occupy two centre-backs, which keeps him near the line at the moments he would rather be dropping into the corridor, so the pinning job directly reduces the frequency of his best action. Alongside a partner who occupies the centre-backs, he is free to live between the lines almost permanently, and that is his highest-value state. The defending implication is practical rather than theoretical. If he plays with a partner, your pocket owner has no support and a double pivot becomes essential. If he plays alone, your centre-backs can step more aggressively into the corridor, because the space behind the step is less immediately threatened by a second runner.

### Q: How do you stop Alvarez's pressing from creating turnovers?

Attack the geometry of the press rather than trying to outrun it. His curved approach closes the inside lane first and invites the pass down the line, so the answer is to keep the inside lane open. Your holding midfielder moves laterally as the run curves, staying half a metre outside the cover shadow, which restores the inside option and turns the press into a two-against-one. Your goalkeeper holds the ball a beat longer to make the run commit, then releases into the space the presser has vacated. Your full-back receives already turned, with a midfielder inside him, so the invited pass becomes a press-breaking one. When those fail, remove the risk entirely for a period by going long into a wide area with your second-ball owner positioned.

### Q: Which marking scheme suits a mobile forward like Alvarez?

Zonal ownership with rehearsed hand-offs, not man-marking and not pure zonal indifference. Man-marking gives him the power to pull a defender wherever he wants, which is the structural damage his movement is designed to produce, and it converts one of your defenders into a passenger every time he drops deep. Pure zonal defending without hand-offs concedes free receptions at every boundary, because the boundaries are precisely where he positions himself. The workable middle is a scheme where each zone has exactly one named owner, ownership transfers by a spoken call at the moment the passer commits, and every step forward has a pre-agreed cover behind it. If a personal assignment becomes unavoidable late in a contest, give it to a midfielder rather than a centre-back.

### Q: How do you defend Alvarez's runs off the shoulder?

Step as a line on the passer's back-lift rather than reacting to the runner. A forward who times his movement well beats a line that steps on his movement, because by then the pass is already travelling and the line is moving into a decision that has been made. Stepping on the loading cue moves your line while the pass is still a plan. The requirement is that all four defenders step together, which is a rehearsal problem rather than a talent problem, and the goalkeeper starts on his front foot to take anything played through. If your back line cannot step in unison, do not attempt it; drop five metres, thicken the midfield in front of the corridor, and accept a larger pocket to defend.

### Q: Is Alvarez a threat in the air?

He is not a target for a contested delivery in the way a taller centre-forward is, and defending him as if he were wastes your best header of the ball on an event he is not trying to win. His dead-ball value comes from arriving late at the penalty spot for the second contact and from making sharp movements in front of a marker rather than above one. Defend that with a dedicated second-ball defender who is forbidden from attacking the first delivery, and with a marking scheme that accounts for a runner arriving from depth rather than one standing in the six-yard box. In open play, treat crosses toward the back post and cutbacks as the higher-probability threats rather than the flighted ball into the middle.

### Q: How do you handle Alvarez in a transition moment?

Delay rather than press, and force him wide rather than trying to win the ball. On the turnover, your nearest player closes the carrier's forward passing angle without committing, buying the two seconds your structure needs to reset, because a beaten presser removes himself from the recovery entirely. If the ball does reach him with your line broken, the recovering defender runs the angle that moves the shot to the side, not the angle that gets a foot in, since a run aimed at the ball concedes the strike at the moment of the touch. Behind all of that sits your rest defense: one holding midfielder permanently in the corridor while you attack, centre-backs split rather than tight, and one full-back who does not join.

### Q: What shape limits Alvarez's movement between defenders?

A back four with a double pivot is the most reliable answer for most sides, because one holding midfielder can face the pocket permanently while the other engages the ball, which turns the corridor into a defended area rather than an open door. The cost is midfield numbers on the flanks, and it is worth paying. A back three with wing-backs is the second option and sometimes the best available, since the middle centre-back can step into the corridor without breaking the line, which removes the hesitation that produces free receptions. Its cost is the space between wing-back and outside centre-back. A back four with a single pivot is the shape most likely to be punished, which matters because it is also the most common shape below elite level.

### Q: Does Alvarez's finishing change how deep you sit?

Yes, and in the opposite direction to most instincts. Because he strikes early and accurately from the edge of the penalty area, dropping deep hands him a larger area to receive in and shortens the distance between his first touch and a shot on goal. A deep block against this profile increases the number of dangerous receptions rather than reducing them. Hold a compact mid-block with thirty to thirty-five metres between your deepest defender and your highest presser, and judge your line height by what it does to the size of the pocket rather than by how safe it feels. If your defenders cannot hold that line safely, come down five metres and add a second body in front of the back four rather than dropping the whole structure.

### Q: How does a goalkeeper prepare for Alvarez's early shots?

Treat any reception in the pocket as a shot cue rather than a build-up cue, and get to the set position earlier than usual, holding it longer. The recurring concession against him is the goalkeeper who is still taking an adjustment step when the ball is struck, because a keeper in motion is falling backward at the instant he needs to be loaded. Being marginally out of position but set beats being perfectly positioned and unset. Starting depth also has to reflect the higher line the plan uses, so sweeping ability matters; if the goalkeeper is uncomfortable defending space behind, the line comes down and the corridor is defended with numbers instead. His third job is naming the unowned player, since he sees the ownership map better than anyone.

### Q: What do sides that kept Alvarez quiet have in common?

They compress the vertical distance between their midfield and defensive lines, they keep a permanent body facing the space in front of their centre-backs, and they refuse to follow him when he drops into areas where he can do no damage. They also tend to be calm in their own build-up, which removes the high turnovers his pressing is designed to manufacture. What they rarely have in common is a physically imposing centre-back or an outstanding individual defensive performance, because the containment happens between players rather than inside a duel. Watching a match in which he was contained is the single most useful piece of scouting available, and it is the one most staffs skip in favor of watching the matches in which he scored.

### Q: How should an amateur side handle a hard working forward like Alvarez?

Keep the parts of the plan that cost nothing athletically and drop the parts that require rehearsal you do not have. Agree ownership before kick-off, which is free and delivers most of the value: who owns the pocket, who owns the weak-side channel, who owns the second ball, and who follows him when he drops, which is nobody. Then drop the high line, since synchronized stepping needs a settled back four, and fill the larger pocket with a second holding midfielder. Simplify your own build-up rather than losing the ball thirty metres from your goal, using the goalkeeper's distribution into wide areas with your second-ball owner positioned. The discipline not to chase him is worth more at this level than any tactical sophistication you might add.

### Q: What do scouts note about Alvarez's off ball work?

Three things, and only one of them shows up in a highlights reel. The first is where he starts his movements, which is usually a step behind a midfielder's blind side rather than on a defender's shoulder. The second is when he starts them, which is while the ball is still two passes away and nobody is watching him. The third is his pressing triggers: the pass back to a centre-back, the heavy first touch, the goalkeeper receiving with square hips. Recording the trigger is more actionable than recording the location, because it lets a coach eliminate the cue from his own side's build-up. Scouts also note his reception body shape, since receiving on the half-turn with the front foot pointed forward is what converts a reception into a threat.

### Q: Does Alvarez suit a counter-attacking side more than a possession side?

He functions in both, and the defending implications differ enough to be worth separating. In a possession side he lives on the boundaries of a settled block, which makes your ownership protocol and your midfield discipline the deciding factors, since the receptions come in a low-tempo, high-repetition pattern. In a counter-attacking side his value shifts toward the space behind a committed opponent and toward the turnovers his pressing creates, which moves the burden onto your rest defense, your pivot's cover-shadow discipline, and your willingness to keep a full-back at home. A defending coach should therefore prepare for the side rather than for the individual, and let the team's method decide which half of this counter-plan gets the rehearsal time.
