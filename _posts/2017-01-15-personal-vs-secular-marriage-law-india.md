A couple in India who have decided to marry each other have not finished deciding. Indian law offers two different legal machines for becoming married, and the comparison of personal vs secular marriage law in India determines far more than the style of the ceremony. One machine is the personal law of the couple's community, which marries them through a religious or customary rite and then governs the marriage, its dissolution and much of the property that surrounds it. The other is a single secular statute that marries any two adults before a state officer, asks nothing about their religion, and in exchange for that indifference attaches consequences to the form of the wedding that reach into the couple's privacy, their membership of a joint family and the law that will distribute their estates when they die. Most couples choose between these machines by instinct, by family expectation or by necessity, without ever seeing the two side by side. This article puts them side by side.

The verdict this comparison reaches can be stated at the outset, because a decision article owes its reader the answer before the working. Neither route is uniformly better, and the popular framing in which the secular route is the progressive choice and the personal law route the traditional one misdescribes both. The secular statute is the only door open to every couple, and it is also the only door that requires thirty days of public notice before a couple may pass through it, the only one that severs a member of a Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh or Jain undivided family from that family when the marriage crosses outside those faiths, and the only one that moves the couple's succession out of their personal law into the Indian Succession Act, 1925. The route that asks nothing about religion charges the most for it. Whether the charge is worth paying depends on which of three costs the couple can least afford to bear: a month of publicity, a defined share carved out of joint family property, or a different law of inheritance for themselves and their children. That is the deciding factor, and every dimension examined below feeds into it.

## The decision and the two doors

The personal law side of the comparison is not one law but a family of them, and the frame within which they sit together is mapped in [the complete guide to personal laws in India](/2016/09/01/personal-laws-india-complete-guide/), which this article treats as its starting point rather than re-drawing. A Hindu couple, a term the statute extends to Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs, marry under the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, whose conditions, ceremonies and divorce code are set out in [the pillar on the Hindu Marriage Act 1955](/2016/10/01/hindu-marriage-act-1955-explained/) and are summarised here only so far as the comparison requires. A Muslim couple marry by nikah under uncodified personal law, with the wife's statutory divorce remedies in the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939 and a divorced woman's maintenance position recast by the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986. A Christian couple marry under the Indian Christian Marriage Act, 1872 and divorce under the Divorce Act, 1869. A Parsi couple marry and divorce under the Parsi Marriage and Divorce Act, 1936. A Sikh couple marry by Anand Karaj, a ceremony the Anand Marriage Act, 1909 validates, while their matrimonial remedies continue to run under the Hindu statute. Each of these laws is a complete route: it says who may marry, by what rite, on what terms the marriage may be ended, and it connects outward to the succession law of the same community.

The secular side is a single statute. The Special Marriage Act, 1954, Act 43 of 1954, in force from 1 January 1955, allows any two persons who satisfy its conditions to marry before a Marriage Officer in a civil ceremony, whatever their religions and whether or not they profess any religion at all. Its procedure, its litigation and its internal logic belong to [the pillar on the Special Marriage Act 1954](/2016/11/01/special-marriage-act-1954-explained/), which owns that statute's questions; what follows takes from it only the features that decide a choice between routes. The Act is best understood as a deliberate act of legislative design. Its predecessor of 1872 had offered a civil form only to persons who formally declared that they professed none of the named religions, so that a civil marriage was purchased by renouncing one's faith on paper. The 1954 Act abolished the renunciation. A couple may marry civilly and remain, for every purpose the statute does not expressly govern, exactly what they were: the Hindu remains a Hindu, the Muslim a Muslim, the atheist an atheist. What the statute took away in religious demand it replaced with civil demand, and the rest of this article is an account of that exchange, dimension by dimension.

The comparison matters because the two doors do not lead to the same room. A marriage is not a single event but the opening of a legal relationship that will be tested at predictable points: when the couple need to prove the marriage to a bank, an embassy or a revenue officer; when the marriage breaks down and one of them wants out; when a parent dies and property is divided; when one spouse dies and the estate is shared. At each of those points the route chosen at the wedding supplies the rule. Couples discover this in sequence and usually in the wrong order, meeting the succession consequence at a funeral and the severance consequence in a partition dispute, years after the thirty days of notice that struck them, at the registry, as the whole price of the civil form. An honest comparison therefore has to run the full length of the relationship, from eligibility to inheritance, and has to resist the temptation to score the routes on the wedding day alone.

## Why there are two doors at all

The coexistence of the two systems is not an accident of drafting but a settlement, reached twice, once by a colonial legislature and once by a republican one. The personal laws are the older layer. The British administration of India undertook, from the late eighteenth century, to decide the family disputes of Hindus and Muslims by their own laws of marriage, inheritance and religious usage, and the codifications that followed were community by community: a marriage Act for Christians in 1872, a divorce Act for them in 1869, a Parsi code in 1936, and for Hindus nothing comprehensive until the republic's own Hindu code of 1955 and 1956. The secular layer began as an escape hatch from that system. The Act of 1872, passed after a campaign in which the Brahmo Samaj's marriages had been left in legal doubt, offered a civil form to persons who would declare that they belonged to none of the listed faiths; it was, in substance, a statute for dissenters, and its declaration of unbelief was the fence that kept it from competing with the personal laws. The amendment of 1923 widened the hatch for marriages within the Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh and Jain faiths, and the Act of 1954 removed the fence altogether. The settlement the republic struck in that decade is visible in the dates: Parliament enacted a general civil marriage statute in 1954 and then, in 1955 and 1956, codified the majority community's personal law in four Acts, reforming it from within rather than replacing it. The secular route was designed as an option beside the personal laws, not as their successor, and its consequence chapter, with the severance and succession provisions inherited from the older civil marriage tradition, reflects a legislature that expected the route to be used at the margins, by the interfaith and the dissenting, rather than as the ordinary form of Indian marriage.

That expectation explains the usage pattern the brief for this article records and the verdict must reckon with: the secular route is used disproportionately by interfaith and inter-caste couples and by couples marrying against family opposition. These are the couples for whom the personal law door is shut, or opens into a room their families control. The pattern is not evidence that the route has failed; it is evidence that the route is used by exactly the population it was built for, and that the population's defining characteristic, a marriage somebody with power over them opposes, is the one the notice machinery serves worst. A comparison that scored the routes by their total usage would be measuring the wrong thing, since the personal laws carry the whole weight of a society's ordinary weddings, most of which were never candidates for a registry office. The routes are not competitors for a market. They are a system and its safety valve, and the question for any given couple is not which system is winning but whether the valve's price is lower than the cost of staying in the main line.

The framers of the Constitution had the same choice at the level of the state and made it the same way. A uniform civil code was placed in the Directive Principles rather than enacted or made enforceable, a decision the uniform civil code article in this series examines on its own record; what matters here is the institutional consequence for marriage law. Because no code displaced the personal laws, reform proceeded inside them, community by community, and the secular statute proceeded beside them as the individual's exit from the system rather than the system's replacement. That dual track is why the comparison in this article is permanently live rather than a transitional curiosity: the two doors were not a stage on the way to one door, but a considered allocation, in which the community keeps its law for those who want it and the individual keeps a civil alternative for those who do not, cannot, or dare not. Every later amendment has respected the allocation. The Hindu code was reformed repeatedly without touching the secular statute's availability; the secular statute was amended in 1976 to soften its consequences for couples within the four faiths, without narrowing its door; the Christian divorce law was equalised in 2001 within its own code rather than by folding Christians into a general one. A couple choosing a route are therefore not betting on which system history will vindicate. They are using a settlement that has held, in its essentials, since the first decade of the republic, and whose terms this article has tried to state exactly.

## Personal vs secular marriage law, dimension by dimension

The ten dimensions that decide the choice are eligibility, age, ceremony, notice, registration and proof, divorce, mutual consent, maintenance, succession and joint family membership, with bigamy running across several of them. They are taken in the order in which a couple meets them.

The first dimension is who may marry at all. Every personal law is, in this respect, a members-only door. The Hindu Marriage Act requires both parties to be within its extended definition of Hindu, so that a Hindu and a Christian cannot marry under it at all, however willing both families are. The Parsi statute requires both parties to be Parsi Zoroastrians and to marry by the Ashirvad ceremony. The Christian statute is engaged where at least one party is a Christian, but the marriage it solemnises is a Christian marriage in form. Muslim law permits a marriage between two Muslims as a matter of course, and classical doctrine has traditionally treated a Muslim man's marriage to a Jewish or Christian woman as irregular rather than void in Sunni law and as void in Shia law, a distinction hedged with conditions that gives an interfaith couple no route they can rely on in a registrar's office or a courtroom. The secular statute has no membership condition of any kind. Its Section 4 asks about the parties' existing marriages, their capacity to consent, their ages and their relationship to each other, and about nothing else. For a couple of the same community, the eligibility dimension is neutral, because both doors stand open. For a couple across communities, it is the whole decision, because only one door exists.

Within eligibility sits a subtler difference: the prohibited degrees. The Hindu statute bars marriage between sapindas and between persons within the degrees of prohibited relationship, but allows custom governing either party to override both bars, which is why marriages between cross cousins and between a man and his niece, barred by the bare text, are valid across large parts of the country where custom sanctions them. The secular statute substitutes its own First Schedule, a list of thirty seven relationships for each sex, and its custom proviso runs in favour of a custom governing at least one of the parties. The Parsi statute has its own Schedule of prohibited degrees, and Muslim law its own rules of prohibited affinity and fosterage. A couple close in blood will therefore sometimes find that the personal law of their community, read with its customs, permits a marriage the secular schedule forbids, and sometimes the reverse, and this is one of the few dimensions on which the answer changes from family to family rather than from route to route.

### Are the minimum ages the same on both routes?

Under the two principal statutes they are identical: the Hindu Marriage Act and the Special Marriage Act each require the bridegroom to have completed twenty one years and the bride eighteen, and the Parsi statute fixes the same pair of ages. Below those ages the child marriage statute overlays every route alike.

The overlay deserves a sentence of its own, because it is the point at which the comparison between personal laws stops mattering. The Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006, Act 6 of 2007, defines a child as a male under twenty one or a female under eighteen and makes a marriage to which either party is a child voidable at the option of the party who was a child, and void altogether in the aggravated cases it lists, of enticement from a lawful guardian, of force or deceit, and of sale or trafficking. Classical Muslim law ties the capacity to marry to puberty rather than to a birthday, which is the point of sharpest friction between a personal law and the general statute, but the general statute is drafted to prevail: it criminalises the solemnisation and the promotion of a child marriage on every route, religious or civil. A couple, or more realistically a family, shopping between routes for a lower age will not find one. What the routes differ on is not the floor but the consequence of marrying near it, since a party who marries at eighteen under the personal law and repents of it has the child marriage statute's voidability remedy, while the secular route's own conditions simply refuse the marriage at the counter.

### What ceremony does each route require?

The personal law route requires the community's own rite: saptapadi for Hindus where custom includes it, offer and acceptance for a nikah, the Ashirvad for Parsis, a licensed minister or Registrar for Christians. The secular route requires no rite, only a signed declaration, three witnesses, and the statute's words of consent spoken before the Marriage Officer.

The difference is not merely aesthetic, because a rite can be performed defectively and a declaration cannot. A Hindu marriage can be attacked, sometimes decades later and sometimes by a party who finds the attack convenient, on the ground that the essential ceremonies of the parties' custom were never performed, and the litigation then turns on priests, photographs, relatives and recollection. The statute answers with presumptions, and the courts presume a valid marriage from prolonged cohabitation and general repute, but the presumption is a shield in a lawsuit rather than a substitute for certainty at the counter. The secular ceremony, by contrast, is a state act recorded in a book the state keeps. There is no rite to get wrong, no priest whose authority can be questioned, and no factual inquiry into what happened on the day beyond the register itself. Couples who marry against opposition, and who must expect the validity of the marriage itself to be the first thing challenged, should weigh this dimension heavily: the personal law route asks them to prove a religious event, the secular route asks the state to produce its own record.

The ceremony dimension also carries the dimension of meaning, which a legal comparison should not pretend away. For most couples the religious rite is not a formality wrapped around the legal act; it is the act, the thing their families witness and their community recognises, and a registry office exchange of declarations, however sufficient in law, does not supply it. The secular statute accommodates this more readily than its critics allow, since nothing prevents a couple from marrying civilly and celebrating religiously, in either order, provided the legal conditions of whichever route they rely on are satisfied; but a couple who rely on the religious ceremony alone for their legal status take the personal law route with its proof burdens, and a couple who rely on the civil ceremony alone take the secular route with its notice. What they cannot do is take the ceremony of one route and the legal incidents of the other, because the incidents follow the form.

## The formation file: what each route asks the couple to produce

The routes also differ in what they ask the couple to bring, say and sign, and the difference in paperwork is a difference in kind. The secular route is a documentary proceeding from the first step. The notice itself must state each party's name, occupation, marital condition, age, dwelling place, place of residence and length of residence in the district, because those particulars are what the public is being invited to check. Before the ceremony the parties and three witnesses must sign, in the Marriage Officer's presence, the declaration the Third Schedule prescribes, in which each party declares their condition as unmarried, widowed or divorced, affirms that they have completed the statutory age, and affirms that they are not related to the other party within the degrees of prohibited relationship, acknowledging that a false statement in the declaration is an offence. The ceremony itself may be solemnised in any form the parties choose, at the officer's office or at another place within a reasonable distance of it that the officer permits, but it is not complete until each party says to the other, in the presence of the officer and the witnesses, the words the statute sets down: I take thee to be my lawful wife, or husband. Fees on the route are prescribed by state rules and are nominal; the real currency of the route is time and exposure, not money. The file the state creates is the point of the exercise, which is why the certificate it yields is conclusive.

The personal law route asks for no file at all at the point of marriage. No statute requires a Hindu couple to notify anyone, produce documents or sign anything before their ceremony, and the validity of what they do turns on the rite and on the Section 5 conditions, not on any record of it. The documents arrive later and sideways: when the marriage is registered under state rules, the registrar will ordinarily ask for proof of age and address and for some evidence that a ceremony took place, and the couple discover that the informality of the wedding day has to be reconstructed on paper for every official purpose afterwards, from the first passport application to the first succession certificate. The Christian route sits between the two in documentary weight, since the Indian Christian Marriage Act works through licensed ministers and Marriage Registrars, with notice to the person who will solemnise and a certificate signed at the ceremony and transmitted for record; the Parsi route likewise ends in a certificate signed by the officiating priest, the parties and their witnesses and sent to the Registrar of Marriages. The Muslim route is the least documentary of all at formation, since the nikah is a contract concluded by offer and acceptance before witnesses, with the nikahnama as its customary record where one is drawn up, and no state involvement of any kind. A couple who value a thin file will find the personal law routes congenial; a couple who value a file that answers questions by itself will find the secular route's paperwork is not bureaucracy for its own sake but the purchase price of the certificate's conclusive force.

One practical asymmetry follows from the documentary difference and is worth stating because couples trip over it constantly. The secular route checks marital condition before the wedding, through the notice and objection machinery, so that a subsisting first marriage is designed to surface at the office rather than in a courtroom. The personal law routes check nothing in advance, so that a subsisting first marriage surfaces only when someone complains, and then it surfaces as a void marriage, a bigamy prosecution or a maintenance defence, years into the second union and often after children and property have accumulated around it. Neither design is irrational. Pre-clearance is the state's method where the state is the solemniser; post-facto enforcement is the community method where the community solemnises and the state reserves its force for breaches. But a party with a complicated marital past, a divorce obtained abroad, a first marriage whose dissolution is doubtful, is safer on the route that forces the question to the surface before the ceremony than on the route that lets it sleep, and advisers who know both systems route such clients accordingly.

Cost, finally, is a dimension couples ask about first and should weight last, because on both routes the state's charges are trivial beside the charges the comparison has been counting. The secular route's fees, for notice, solemnisation and a certified copy of the certificate, are prescribed by state rules and are nominal in every state; the personal law route charges no state fee for the marriage itself, and such registration fee as follows is equally nominal. The real costs sit off the receipt. On the personal law side they are social and evidentiary: the ceremony itself, which families fund at whatever scale their standing demands and which the law neither requires nor caps, and the contingent cost, years later, of proving the marriage by evidence if it is ever denied. On the secular side they are temporal and circumstantial: a month of residence, a month of notice, days taken from work for the office's hours, and, where an objection is filed or the three month window is missed, the whole sequence run again. Neither ledger contains a figure that should decide the choice, and a couple who choose by cost are choosing by the smallest numbers in the comparison while the largest ones, of privacy, property and inheritance, go unexamined.

## Notice: the dimension on which the comparison turns

Every personal law route can be completed in private. A couple who satisfy the conditions of their law may marry on a day of their choosing, before their families and their priest, with no antecedent communication to the state and no opportunity for any stranger to intervene. The secular route inverts this completely, and the inversion is the statute's best-known feature and its heaviest charge. Under Sections 5 and 6 of the Special Marriage Act, the parties must give written notice to the Marriage Officer of a district in which at least one of them has resided for thirty days. The notice is copied into a Marriage Notice Book that any person may inspect without fee, and a copy is affixed to a conspicuous place in the officer's office; where a party resides in another district, a copy travels there and is affixed again. Under Section 7, any person at all may object before the thirty days expire that the marriage would contravene one of the Section 4 conditions. Under Section 8, an objection stops the ceremony until the officer has inquired, with the powers of a civil court, and decided, subject to an appeal to the district court. If the marriage is not solemnised within three calendar months of the notice, Section 14 lapses the whole proceeding and the couple must begin again in public.

### What exactly does the thirty day public notice publish?

It publishes the couple. The notice states each party's name, occupation, marital condition and dwelling place, and it is exposed in two ways at once: entered in a book open to free inspection at the Marriage Officer's office, and pinned up in that office, with a further copy pinned up in the home district of any party who lives elsewhere.

The design has a defensible rationale, and the stance this series takes on contested questions requires it to be stated fairly. A state that marries people without any religious intermediary takes on itself the duty the community once performed: checking that neither party has a spouse living, is under age, or within the prohibited degrees. Publication is the checking mechanism of a society in which records were local and memory was the database; an existing wife, a parent of an underage bride, a village that knew the truth, could come forward within the month. The defenders of the notice, including the Law Commission in its examinations of the civil marriage laws, have treated publicity as the price of a form that dispenses with every other gatekeeper. The answer the comparison has to give is that the mechanism no longer fits the society it polices. Records have not become so complete that a bigamous party is reliably caught by a notice board, but families have remained quite capable of using a notice board to find a couple who have left home to marry, and the class of couples the statute most distinctively serves, the interfaith and inter-caste couples and those marrying against family opposition, is precisely the class for whom a month of advance publicity is not a formality but a danger window. A High Court has read the publication requirement down to an option the couple may elect, in litigation the Special Marriage Act pillar examines; whatever the eventual settlement of that question, the need for the reading down is itself the evidence that the dimension is mispriced.

The speed comparison, which is the form in which most couples first meet this dimension, is short. The personal law route is as fast as a ceremony can be arranged: a couple may be married within days of deciding, and such registration as their state requires follows the event. The secular route cannot be completed in under a month and a day, requires a month of prior residence in the district by at least one party, and can be pushed by a single objection, however unfounded, through an inquiry and an appeal, at the end of which the three month lapse clock may force the couple to start again. Where the objection is tactical, made to delay rather than to defeat, the statute's only sanction is a costs order capped at a figure the Act itself fixed at one thousand rupees. On speed and on privacy alike, then, the personal law route wins outright, and it wins by the largest margin on any dimension in this comparison. It is the counterweight to everything the secular route wins elsewhere, and it is why the verdict at the end of this article is a weighing exercise rather than a coronation.

Two features of the objection machinery soften the picture slightly, and a fair comparison records them. The first is that an objection is legally worthless unless it engages one of the Section 4 conditions: that a party has a spouse living, lacks capacity, is under age, or stands within the prohibited degrees. A parent's objection that the match is unsuitable, unwanted or across caste is not an objection the officer may act on, and an officer who refuses a marriage on such a ground will be reversed on the appeal the Act provides to the district court. The machinery can delay a lawful marriage; it cannot lawfully defeat one. The second is that the inquiry is time boxed, and the officer who exceeds his thirty days, or the objector who acts in bad faith, acts against the statute's grain, though the costs ceiling for bad faith remains the derisory figure noticed earlier. What neither feature touches is the publicity itself, which is complete before any objection is filed: the notice has been pinned up and copied to the home district from the first day, and it is the publication, not the objection, that reaches the family the couple may have left. The thirty days of residence the Act requires of at least one party in the district of notice interacts with this in a way migrant couples feel sharply. A couple who have moved to a city for work and safety will ordinarily give notice where they live, which is also where nobody knows them; but where one of them continues to reside, in the statutory sense, in the district of the family home, the travelling copy of the notice advertises the marriage in the one place it was meant not to be known. The statute's geography is the geography of a settled population, and the couples who use it hardest are frequently the couples who have unsettled themselves on purpose.

The reform direction that the litigation and the commentary converge on should be recorded here, because it identifies which part of the notice is defensible and which is not. The defensible part is verification: the officer's duty to be satisfied, on documents and on inquiry, that the Section 4 conditions hold, so that the state's ceremony is not lent to a bigamous or an underage union. The indefensible part is publicity as the method of verification, in an age when the officer can check age and marital status against documents the parties produce and records the state itself maintains, and when the only persons reliably reached by a notice board are the persons the couple have a constitutional right to marry without consulting. The Allahabad reading, which converts publication from a command into the couple's election, is best understood as the courts performing this separation ahead of the legislature: keep the check, drop the advertisement. A comparison written after that reading cannot describe the notice as a single indivisible feature, because the law in at least one large jurisdiction no longer treats it as one; it must describe a verification regime that is sound and a publication regime that survives, where it survives, on the strength of a drafting assumption from 1954 about how communities police marriages, an assumption the society has outgrown in every respect except the power of families to harm the couples who defy them.

## Registration and proof: what each route leaves behind

If the notice dimension is about what happens before the wedding, the registration dimension is about what the couple hold afterwards. Here the advantage reverses. The secular route ends with a certificate entered in the Marriage Certificate Book, and Section 13 of the Act makes that certificate conclusive evidence of the marriage. A certified copy is admissible without more, and the spouse of a person married under the Act can prove the relationship for a passport, a visa application, an employer's record, a bank nomination, an insurance claim or a succession proceeding without reconstructing anything. The personal law route ends with a ceremony, and such proof as exists is built afterwards and unevenly. Section 8 of the Hindu Marriage Act empowers states to make rules for registering Hindu marriages, and the Supreme Court in Seema v. Ashwani Kumar in 2006 directed that marriages of all communities be made compulsorily registrable, a direction the states have implemented through rules of varying reach and rigour. Registration under those rules records that a ceremony was reported; it does not cure a marriage that the personal law treats as void, and a couple who never registered remain married if the ceremony was valid, while a couple who registered a defective ceremony remain unmarried if it was not. The register is evidence, and useful evidence, but it is not the marriage.

Between the two systems sits a provision couples routinely misunderstand. Chapter III of the Special Marriage Act, Sections 15 to 18, allows a marriage celebrated in another form to be registered under the secular statute itself, on conditions that include a ceremony performed, the parties living together as spouses since, neither having another spouse living, both having completed twenty one years, and thirty days of residence in the district. Section 18 then deems the registered marriage to be a marriage solemnised under the Act, which means the registration imports the Act's consequence chapter with it: severance and the succession switch arrive with the certificate, subject to the same exemption for marriages within the Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh and Jain faiths. This is not the same act as registering a religious marriage under a state's evidentiary rules, though the two are confused constantly at registry counters. One records a marriage the personal law made; the other re-founds the marriage on the secular statute and changes the law of the couple's estates in the process. A couple who want only better paper should take the first. A couple who want the secular statute's regime, or who need its certificate for a purpose the state rules cannot serve, take the second, and should take it knowing what else it carries.

The proof dimension has a forward edge as well. Marriages are proved most often at moments of stress: at a death, in a benefits claim contested by relatives, in a maintenance case where the respondent's first defence is that no marriage ever took place. The secular certificate answers all of these at the threshold. The personal law couple answer them by evidence, and usually win, because the presumptions favour the spouse who lived as one; but the answer costs a proceeding, and the proceeding costs time and money at the moment the survivor can least afford either. Advocates advising a couple whose families are hostile, or whose property arrangements are complex, treat the certificate not as a bureaucratic souvenir but as the cheapest litigation insurance in family law, and on this dimension the secular route's advantage is as clear as its disadvantage on notice.

## Getting out: divorce, ground by ground

A couple choosing a route are also choosing, though they rarely think of it, the exit law of the marriage, because matrimonial relief follows the statute of the marriage: a marriage solemnised under one Act is dissolved under that Act, and the secular statute's divorce court has no jurisdiction over a marriage it did not create, any more than the Hindu statute's has over a nikah. The general maintenance provision of the criminal procedure code and the protection statute for domestic violence operate across routes, but the decree that ends the marriage must come from the law that made it. The comparison of exit law is therefore a comparison of five different codes, and it does not reduce to a tie.

Between the two principal statutes, the grounds are broadly parallel, and deliberately so. Section 13 of the Hindu Marriage Act and Section 27 of the Special Marriage Act were aligned by the Marriage Laws (Amendment) Act, 1976, which put cruelty and desertion into both codes as full divorce grounds and gave both the same architecture: a list of fault and status grounds open to either spouse, covering the respondent's adultery-equivalent conduct, cruelty, desertion for two years, unsoundness of mind, venereal disease, and disappearance for seven years; a further pair of grounds where an earlier decree of judicial separation or restitution has not been acted on for a year; and additional grounds reserved to the wife, of which the most used are that the husband has been guilty of rape, sodomy or bestiality and that a maintenance order in her favour has not been followed by resumed cohabitation. Leprosy stood in both lists for decades and was removed from the matrimonial statutes by the Personal Laws (Amendment) Act, 2019. The residual differences are small but real. The secular list includes a ground the Hindu list lacks, a respondent undergoing a sentence of imprisonment for seven years or more, and the Hindu list keeps two grounds with no secular analogue, renunciation of the world by entering a religious order and the respondent's ceasing to be a Hindu by conversion to another religion, both unintelligible in a statute that has no religious world to leave. A petitioner choosing between the two codes for a ground will almost always find it in both; the choice of route is not, between these two, a choice about divorce.

Beyond the codified pair, the exit laws diverge substantially, and this is where the brief's caution that grounds differ for other communities does its work. A Christian marriage is dissolved under the Divorce Act, 1869, whose original scheme was the most restrictive in the country: a husband could divorce for adultery alone, while a wife had to prove adultery compounded with cruelty, desertion, bigamy or another matrimonial offence. The Indian Divorce (Amendment) Act, 2001, Act 51 of 2001, substituted a new Section 10 with a common list of grounds open to either spouse and inserted Section 10A for divorce by mutual consent, bringing Christian exit law broadly into line with the other codes a century and a third late. A Parsi marriage is dissolved under Part III of the Parsi statute on its own list of grounds, in Parsi Matrimonial Courts that sit with community delegates, a forum no other route uses. A Muslim marriage presents the deepest asymmetry in the whole comparison. The husband retains the classical power of talaq, cut down by the Supreme Court in Shayara Bano v. Union of India in 2017, which set aside instant triple talaq by a majority of three to two, and by the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019, which made the instant form void and a criminal offence; the wife has no corresponding unilateral power and must instead use the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939, whose Section 2 gives her a list of grounds that includes the husband's disappearance for four years, his failure to maintain her for two years, his imprisonment for seven years, his cruelty and his impotence, or must obtain a consensual dissolution by khula or mubaraat. The result is that on the exit dimension the personal law routes are not one thing: the Hindu route and the secular route stand together, the Christian route has been reformed into their company, the Parsi route is a parallel code of its own, and the Muslim route distributes the power to end a marriage between the spouses more unequally than any other.

### How does divorce by mutual consent work on each route?

Almost identically under the two principal statutes. Section 13B of the Hindu Marriage Act and Section 28 of the Special Marriage Act each require a joint petition after a year or more of living separately, and each requires a second motion between six and eighteen months later, before the decree passes.

The shared architecture extends to its pressure points. Both statutes bar a divorce petition in the first year of marriage except on leave for exceptional hardship or depravity. Both second-motion windows have been read by the Supreme Court in Amardeep Singh v. Harveen Kaur in 2017 as directory rather than mandatory, so that the six month interval may be waived where the statutory conditions for waiver are made out, and the Constitution Bench in Shilpa Sailesh v. Varun Sreenivasan in 2023 confirmed that the Court may itself dissolve a marriage that has broken down irretrievably under Article 142, without sending the parties back to complete a waiting period the relationship has outlived. Neither statute, however, contains irretrievable breakdown as a ground a district court may apply, and that gap, which the Hindu Marriage Act pillar examines in its own article, is common to both routes. Mutual consent under the Muslim route follows a different logic, since a dissolution by agreement takes the form of khula or mubaraat under personal law rather than a statutory petition, and under the Christian code it is the inserted Section 10A that performs the same office as Sections 13B and 28. For the couple comparing the two principal routes, the honest summary is that exit by agreement is a draw; exit against opposition is a draw between the two codified routes and a materially worse position for a wife on the Muslim route; and exit from a marriage that has simply ended in fact, with no fault to plead, is unavailable as a matter of right on every route in the country.

The forum completes the exit comparison, because a ground is worth what the court that hears it makes of it. Both principal codes send the petition to the district court, and where family courts have been established under the Family Courts Act, 1984, to the family court, whose procedure is meant to be less adversarial and quicker than the ordinary civil trial; the practical experience of delay, adjournment and contested evidence is, on both routes, the experience of the same court system wearing the same arrears, and no couple should choose a wedding form in the hope of a faster divorce. The Parsi route's forum is genuinely different, a matrimonial court of the community sitting with delegates drawn from it, which gives Parsi divorce a character no other route reproduces. The Muslim wife's suit under the Act of 1939 likewise goes to the ordinary civil court of her residence. Forum therefore scores as a draw between the principal routes, with one exception noticed in the maintenance dimension: a spouse's choice of court within the codified system is generous, and the Hindu statute expressly lets a wife petition where she resides, a solicitude the case law under both codes has spent more time on than any difference between their grounds.

Nullity, the decree that a marriage was never valid or may be annulled, tracks formation law and so belongs to the comparison even though couples meet it rarely. The two principal statutes divide defective marriages the same way. A marriage that breaches the monogamy condition, the prohibited degrees or the sapinda bar is void under Section 11 of the Hindu Marriage Act, and a marriage that breaches the corresponding conditions of the secular statute, or that was procured by a party with a spouse living, is void under its Section 24. A marriage is voidable, valid until annulled, where consent was obtained by force or fraud, where a party was incapable of valid consent or of procreation, or where the respondent was pregnant by another person at the wedding, in the matching provisions of Section 12 of the Hindu statute and Section 25 of the secular one. Muslim law works with its own older categories, of the void marriage that is no marriage at all and the irregular marriage that a defect in form, such as the absence of proper witnesses, renders curable, categories that produce results near enough to the codified pair in the worst cases and different enough in the middle cases to reward separate advice. The comparative point is the one the ceremony dimension made from the other side: because the secular marriage is formed by a state act on pre-checked conditions, the class of marriages vulnerable to nullity is narrower on that route, consisting almost entirely of marriages that should never have passed the officer's inquiry, while the personal law route, which checks nothing in advance, leaves the whole field of nullity open to be fought over afterwards. A party who wants certainty that the marriage cannot be unpicked will pay for it in advance publicity; a party who wants privacy will accept the standing possibility, usually remote and usually theoretical, that the marriage's foundations can be litigated. The statutes will not let a couple have both, and the choice between certainty in advance and privacy in advance is the comparison's recurring bargain in its purest form.

Two lesser remedies complete the exit picture and behave the same way across the codified routes. Restitution of conjugal rights, the decree ordering a spouse who has withdrawn from the other's society to return, stands in Section 9 of the Hindu Marriage Act and Section 22 of the Special Marriage Act in materially identical terms, and in the Divorce Act for Christian marriages; its constitutionality was upheld for the Hindu statute in Saroj Rani v. Sudarshan Kumar Chadha, and the provision remains contested in principle wherever it appears, which is to say on every codified route at once. Judicial separation, the decree that suspends cohabitation without dissolving the bond, likewise appears in both principal statutes and in the Parsi code. Neither remedy gives either route an edge; they are part of the shared inheritance of the codified family laws, drafted from common models and amended in common exercises, and a comparison that scored them would be manufacturing a difference.

## Maintenance while the marriage lasts, and after

On maintenance the routes divide into two layers, and the layer that matters most in practice does not depend on the route at all. Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, carried into Section 144 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, gives a wife who is unable to maintain herself, and the other dependants it names, a summary claim against a person of sufficient means who neglects or refuses to maintain her, and it applies to every couple in the country whatever their religion and whatever form their marriage took. The Supreme Court's decision in Mohd. Ahmed Khan v. Shah Bano Begum in 1985, holding that the provision applied to a divorced Muslim woman, set off the legislative response of 1986 that the Shah Bano article in this series owns; the Constitution Bench in Danial Latifi v. Union of India in 2001 upheld the 1986 Act by reading its requirement of a fair and reasonable provision as one made within the iddat period but extending beyond it, and the later case law has treated the two claims, under the personal statute and under the general code, as cumulative rather than exclusive. A divorced woman's floor of support is therefore set by a route-neutral provision and its case law, and no couple should choose a wedding form in the belief that it buys or forfeits that floor.

The statute-specific layer, by contrast, does differ, and it contains one of the comparison's quieter surprises. Sections 24 and 25 of the Hindu Marriage Act give either spouse, wife or husband, a claim to maintenance pendente lite and to permanent alimony within the matrimonial proceeding itself. Sections 36 and 37 of the Special Marriage Act, performing the same office, are framed for the wife alone: the secular statute's interim and permanent maintenance provisions run one way, where the Hindu statute's run both. The Parsi statute likewise provides alimony, and the Divorce Act provides for a wife's alimony within its scheme. A husband without independent means, married under the secular statute, who needs support during or after a divorce has no claim under the Act that married him and must look to the general law, which gives him nothing comparable, since Section 125 is framed for wives, children and parents. The point is narrow and rarely decisive, but it is exactly the kind of asymmetry a comparison exists to catch: the statute drafted without reference to religion kept, in its maintenance chapter, a one-directional assumption the Hindu code had discarded. Mehr, the sum a Muslim husband undertakes to pay on marriage, and the 1986 Act's provision stand on the personal law side of the ledger as entitlements no secular marriage generates, though their practical value depends on amounts fixed at the wedding and on enforcement afterwards.

## The marriage in mid-course: children, legitimacy and the protected home

Between the wedding and the funeral, a marriage generates legal questions that the route answers in the couple's stead, and the mid-course comparison is closer than the formation comparison. The first concerns children. Both principal statutes protect the legitimacy of children of marriages that turn out to be void or are annulled as voidable: Section 16 of the Hindu Marriage Act and Section 26 of the Special Marriage Act, the latter substituted by the same amending Act of 1976, each provide that children of such a marriage are legitimate notwithstanding the decree, with rights in the property of their parents. The Parsi statute carries an equivalent protection for children of an invalid marriage. The significance for the comparison is that a defect in the wedding, which on the personal law route may be discovered a generation later, does not visit itself on the children of either route; the statutes converge here because the policy admits of no divergence. Custody and maintenance of children within matrimonial proceedings are likewise provided for in both principal codes, in Section 26 of the Hindu statute and Section 38 of the secular one, on the same welfare principle that governs custody everywhere in Indian law.

The second mid-course question is the matrimonial home, and here the operative statute is neither route's. The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 protects a woman in a domestic relationship, a category that reaches relationships in the nature of marriage as well as marriages in form, and gives her a right to reside in the shared household and to protection, residence and monetary relief orders from a magistrate, whatever law she married under and whether or not the relationship was a valid marriage at all. The delivered article on that Act owns its working; for this comparison the point is structural. The legislature, when it came to protect women inside relationships, deliberately refused to let the protection turn on the form of the relationship, and the consequence is that the most practically important mid-course rights, to safety and to a roof, are equalised across routes by a statute that ignores the routes. Where the comparison's dimensions differ, they differ on formation, proof, exit and property; where a legislature has intervened to protect the vulnerable spouse directly, it has flattened the routes into one. A couple weighing routes should know which of their future rights are route-dependent and which are not, and the honest map is that the route decides how they marry, how they prove it, how they leave it and what their children inherit, but not whether the law will protect a spouse inside it.

Names, faith and daily status belong to the same mid-course map, and on each the secular route's neutrality is genuine. A marriage under the Special Marriage Act changes neither party's religion, caste or community membership for any purpose the Act does not name; it does not require or record any change of name; and the wife's legal identity, domicile and capacity to hold and dispose of property are exactly what they were, as they are under every personal law route in this comparison, the older doctrines of coverture having no place in any of them. The two named exceptions, severance and the succession switch, are exceptions precisely because the statute otherwise leaves the parties' legal persons untouched. On the personal law side the equivalents are equally clear, save for one historical shadow: a marriage under the Hindu code confirms the parties within a legal community whose incidents, of coparcenary, of succession, of the ceremonies of their children, continue to unfold around them, which is comfort to most and constraint to a few, and is in either case the thing the couple's families understand themselves to be choosing. The secular route's corresponding offer is thinner and colder: the state will marry you and remember it, and will otherwise leave you alone, except where Sections 19 and 21 reach in. Whether that thinness is a defect or the entire point depends on the couple, which is the verdict of this article in miniature.

Adoption, which a couple meet later in the marriage, shows the same layered pattern and rewards a sentence. A Hindu couple adopt under the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956, which governs them by religion, and their marriage's form does not alter that: a Hindu married at a registry office remains a Hindu for the adoption statute, because Section 21A's world and the adoption statute's world are keyed to faith, not to the wedding. Beside it, the secular adoption route under the Juvenile Justice legislation and the Central Adoption Resource Authority is open to persons of any religion or none, so that here too India runs a personal track and a general track in parallel, and a couple's choice between them is governed by considerations, of the child's status and the parents' faith, that echo the marriage comparison without repeating it. The delivered article on adoption owns that machinery; the point for this one is that the two-door structure of Indian family law is not a peculiarity of marriage but its organising habit, and a couple who understand the marriage doors have learned the shape of the rooms beyond them as well.

## Succession and the joint family: the consequences discovered at funerals

The last two dimensions are the ones couples meet last and price least, and they are where the comparison's deciding factor gets its weight. On the personal law route, marriage changes nothing about the legal world the parties inhabit. A Hindu who marries under the Hindu Marriage Act remains a member of their undivided family exactly as before, keeps an undivided coparcenary interest that enlarges and diminishes with births and deaths in the family, and leaves an estate that will be divided under the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, the statute whose Section 6 was recast by [the Hindu Succession Amendment of 2005](/2016/10/15/hindu-succession-amendment-2005/) to make daughters coparceners by birth on the same terms as sons, a position the Supreme Court confirmed in Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma. A Muslim who marries by nikah leaves an estate governed by Muslim personal law, with its fixed fractional shares for the heirs it names and its restriction of testamentary disposition to one third of the estate after debts and funeral expenses. A Christian or a Parsi who marries under their own statute is within the Indian Succession Act's world, the Christian wholly and the Parsi under that Act's special chapter for Parsi intestates. The wedding form and the succession law are, on this route, two chapters of one book.

On the secular route the book changes mid-story, and the change is effected by three sections of the Special Marriage Act that operate silently at the moment of the ceremony. Section 19 provides that the marriage under the Act of a member of an undivided family who professes the Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh or Jaina religion shall be deemed to effect that member's severance from the family. The severance is automatic. It requires no document, no election and no knowledge on the couple's part, and its effect under the Mitakshara system is to convert the member's undivided and fluctuating coparcenary interest into the defined share they could have claimed in a partition made on the wedding day. Section 20 holds the baseline that the marriage itself forfeits no right of succession, borrowing the position of the Caste Disabilities Removal Act, 1850. Section 21 then provides that succession to the property of a person whose marriage is solemnised under the Act, and to the property of the issue of that marriage, shall be regulated by the Indian Succession Act, 1925, applied as though that Act's special chapter for Parsi intestates had been omitted. The Hindu son who marries a Christian colleague under the Act therefore leaves, if he dies intestate, an estate in which his widow takes a third and his lineal descendants two thirds between them, and in which his mother, an heir of the first rank under the Hindu scheme, takes nothing while the widow and children live. His will, if he makes one, is made and construed under the 1925 Act's testamentary code rather than under any personal law of wills. The provision reaches his children as well, which is why the succession dimension is properly described not as a detail of the wedding but as a choice of the law of two generations of estates.

Parliament softened both provisions in 1976, and the shape of the softening is the analytical heart of this dimension. Section 21A, inserted by the Marriage Laws (Amendment) Act, 1976 on the recommendation of the Law Commission's Fifty Ninth Report, disapplies Sections 19 and 21 where both parties to the secular marriage profess the Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh or Jaina religion. Two Hindus who marry at a registry office because they want no rite, or because their families oppose a match across caste, keep their joint family membership and their Hindu law of succession intact; the severance and the succession switch are reserved for marriages in which at least one party stands outside the four named faiths. The exemption was defended as removing a penalty on couples for whom the civil form was a preference about ceremony. Its distributive effect is sharper than its defence: the consequences are lifted precisely where the couple had another route available, since two Hindus could always have married by rite, and retained precisely where the couple had none, since the interfaith pair cannot marry under any personal law. The Law Commission's Two Hundred and Twelfth Report of 2008 mapped the resulting classification of civil marriages into those the exemption covers and those it does not, and any couple using the secular route should establish which side of the Section 21A line they stand on before they price the route, because the answer changes the comparison more than any other single fact about them.

Whether the succession switch is a cost at all depends on the family, and the comparison should say so plainly. For a Muslim party, the Indian Succession Act's unrestricted testamentary freedom replaces a personal law that confines bequests to a third of the estate, and a person who wants to leave property unequally, or outside the circle of Quranic sharers, may find the secular route's estate law affirmatively preferable. For a Hindu daughter in a family that has not internalised the 2005 amendment, marriage under the Act to a man outside the four faiths severs her from the coparcenary the amendment made her a member of by birth, converting a birthright share that would have grown with the family estate into a fixed fraction of its wedding day value. For the elderly parents of either party, the switch can move or extinguish an expected share without their ever being consulted. None of this is discoverable from the ceremony, none of it is mentioned at the registry, and no officer of the state is under any duty to explain it. It is, of all the dimensions, the one on which advice taken before the wedding is worth most and taken after the funeral is worth least.

## Bigamy, and the conversion alternative honestly stated

Bigamy is the dimension on which the routes come closest to a single rule, because the criminal law stands behind all of them. Section 494 of the Indian Penal Code, carried into the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, punishes whoever, having a husband or wife living, marries again in any case in which the second marriage is void by reason of its taking place during the life of the first spouse, and Section 495 aggravates the offence where the first marriage is concealed. The Hindu Marriage Act declares a marriage with a living spouse void and expressly attracts those provisions; the Special Marriage Act deems a person who procures a marriage under it while a spouse is living to have committed the offence, and makes the procured marriage void. Two qualifications give the dimension its comparative interest. The first is that the offence requires the second marriage to be a marriage in the eye of the law, solemnised with the ceremonies the relevant law treats as essential, so that a bigamous relationship dressed in a defective ceremony may fail as a prosecution even where it succeeds as a betrayal; the secular route's state ceremony, which cannot be defectively performed, removes that escape for its own users. The second is that Muslim personal law permits a Muslim man up to four wives, so that a second nikah by a married Muslim man is not bigamy at all within his own law, which makes the Muslim route the single exception to the dimension's common rule and one of the standing asymmetries of the personal law system.

It is the conversion alternative that requires the comparison's most careful handling, because it is the route interfaith couples are most often whispered towards. The suggestion is that one partner convert to the other's faith and marry under the shared personal law, avoiding the notice, the severance and the succession switch at a stroke. The legal record against the manoeuvre, where it is a device rather than a conviction, is settled and severe. In Sarla Mudgal v. Union of India in 1995 the Supreme Court held that a Hindu husband who embraced Islam and married again without dissolving his first marriage had contracted a void second marriage and committed bigamy, that conversion does not dissolve a marriage solemnised under the Hindu Marriage Act, and that the apostate husband could not use his new personal law to defeat the monogamy of his old one; Lily Thomas v. Union of India in 2000 declined to review that position and it stands. The Parsi statute anticipated the device expressly, providing that no Parsi, whether or not they have changed their religion, may marry in the lifetime of a spouse except after lawful divorce, on pain of the Penal Code's bigamy provisions, a clause the Law Commission has held up as the model the other laws lack. Around these holdings the states have built a second wall: freedom of religion statutes in states including Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Himachal Pradesh and Gujarat regulate conversion connected with marriage directly, requiring prior declaration to the District Magistrate in several of them, prohibiting conversion by marriage or for the sole purpose of marriage, and in several cases exposing a marriage that follows a prohibited conversion to being declared void, with challenges to those statutes pending and interim orders confining their operation. A couple who convert out of genuine belief and marry within the faith they have adopted stand in a different position from a couple who convert as a conveyancing device, and the law's suspicion falls on the second; but the suspicion is investigated, not presumed away, and the exposure it creates, criminal, civil and personal, is a real term in the comparison. This article describes that exposure because a comparison that concealed it would fail the couples it exists to serve; it offers no view on which couples should run it.

The proof paradox inside the bigamy offence deserves a final word, because it reverses the routes' usual positions one last time. Since the offence requires the second union to be a marriage solemnised with the ceremonies the governing law treats as essential, a proposition the Supreme Court settled in Bhaurao Shankar Lokhande v. State of Maharashtra, the bigamous party who goes through a truncated or invented ceremony may defeat the prosecution altogether, and the first spouse is left to a nullity suit against a union the criminal law cannot reach. The paradox is structural on the personal law side, where ceremonies are customary and variable and the prosecution must prove the essential ones were performed. On the secular side it cannot arise in the same way: a second marriage under the Act is either registered in the state's own book, in which case its formation is proved by the book and its voidness follows from the first marriage, or it was never solemnised at all, in which case there is no second marriage to attack. The state ceremony, whose publicity is the secular route's great burden, is also the reason its bigamy law has no twilight. Couples will not choose a route for this reason, and the comparison does not ask them to; but a legal system choosing how to police monogamy would notice that the route which announces marriages is also the route on which a second marriage cannot hide.

## The choice itself: the liberty both routes presuppose

Underneath every dimension runs a proposition the courts have stated so often, and in such similar terms, that it can be treated as settled ground for the comparison. An adult's choice of a partner is an incident of the liberty and dignity protected by Article 21 of the Constitution, and it is not subject to the approval of the family, the community or the state. The Supreme Court said it for inter-caste and inter-religious marriages in Lata Singh v. State of Uttar Pradesh, directing the administration and the police to protect couples marrying of their own will and to proceed against those who threatened them. It said it again in Shakti Vahini v. Union of India, condemning the assemblies that sit in judgment on such marriages and laying down preventive, remedial and punitive measures the state governments are bound to implement, from special cells to safe houses. It said it in its fullest form in Shafin Jahan v. Asokan K.M., restoring a marriage that a High Court had annulled at a father's instance, and holding that the choice of a partner, within the confines of law, is an exercise of the freedom of expression and of personal liberty into which neither parent nor court may intrude. These holdings are route-neutral, and that neutrality is their significance for a comparison. They protect the couple who marry by rite in a village temple against the panchayat that objects, and they protect the couple at the Marriage Officer's counter against the family that arrives with an objection, with the same hand.

What the constitutional floor does not do is equalise the routes built on top of it, and the comparison would fail in honesty if it let the rhetoric of choice conceal the machinery of difference. The right to choose a partner does not include a right to choose the legal incidents of the marriage, which Parliament fixes by statute and which differ, as this article has shown, in publicity, proof, exit and property. Nor does the case law dissolve the one genuine dilemma the comparison exposes, which is that the route offering the strongest state protection of the choice, in the form of an unimpeachable state record, is also the route that publicises the choice in advance to the very people the protection may be needed against. The courts have answered that dilemma from the liberty side whenever it has reached them, by protecting the couple and, in the Allahabad reading of the notice provisions, by cutting the publicity down to an election; but the statutory text couples will meet at the counter joins the two, protection and exposure, in a single procedure. A couple reading this comparison should therefore take the case law for what it is: a guarantee that the law will stand behind whichever door they choose, not a reason to stop comparing the doors.

## The verdict: name the cost you can least afford

The table's rows point in different directions, which is why any single recommendation offered to every couple would be wrong, and why the series thesis for a comparison article, a verdict with the deciding factor named, has to be executed at the level of the couple rather than the statute book. The deciding factor is this: which of the secular route's three charges can this couple least afford to bear. The first charge is privacy, a month of public notice falling due at the moment of maximum family tension. The second is family property, a severance that crystallises a share and can be a wound or a weapon depending on the family. The third is the estate, a change of succession law for the couple and their children that outlives everyone at the wedding. A couple who can bear all three, or who are exempted from the second and third by Section 21A, should generally take the secular route for its certificate, its unchallengeable formation and its open door. A couple for whom any one of the three is unbearable, and who have a personal law door open to them, should generally take that door and repair its weaknesses afterwards, by registering the marriage under their state's rules, by making wills, and by keeping the evidence of their ceremony as carefully as the secular statute would have kept it for them.

Applied by reader type, the verdict runs as follows. A couple of the same faith, with families content and a community around them, gain nothing from the secular route except a certificate, and pay for it with a month of publicity and, where one of them marries out of the four named faiths, with consequences they do not need; the personal law route serves them better, and it is not a failure of modernity to say so. A couple within the Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh or Jain faiths who want a civil ceremony, whether from conviction, economy or a wish to marry quietly, stand in the comparison's most favoured position: the 1976 exemption shields them from severance and from the succession switch, so that only the privacy charge remains, and a privacy charge borne by a couple whose families know of the marriage is a fee rather than a risk. An interfaith couple have no genuine choice to make, because only one door is open, and their task is not choosing but pricing: they should marry under the secular statute with their eyes open to the severance, should consider whether the crystallised share is worth claiming rather than abandoning, and should make wills under the succession law the statute has chosen for them, since a will is the instrument by which the Indian Succession Act's scheme can be bent back towards the distribution the family would have expected. A couple marrying against active family opposition face the comparison in its hardest form, because the personal law route, where it exists for them, offers privacy they may desperately need, while the secular route offers a certificate they may desperately need afterwards; the courts have held in Lata Singh v. State of Uttar Pradesh and in the Shakti Vahini directions that the choice of a partner is an incident of the liberty and dignity the Constitution protects, that neither family nor community may interfere with a marriage between consenting adults, and that the state owes such couples protection, and a couple in danger should claim that protection rather than surrender the route that fits them, but they should claim it knowing the notice window is when they will need it. What is said here is the general position of the statutes; it is not advice on any couple's case, and the interaction of a notice, a family settlement and a will in a particular family is exactly the kind of question on which advice on the individual facts should be taken, because limitation periods and forum rules are unforgiving.

One hybrid deserves its own sentence in the verdict, because it is the arrangement most couples who ask this comparison need. A couple may marry under their personal law, with its privacy, its rite and its intact property positions, and then register that marriage under their state's registration rules, acquiring a state-issued certificate of the fact without changing the law of the marriage in any particular. This is not the secular route and must not be confused with the Chapter III registration that re-founds a marriage on the secular statute: it is the personal law route with its one weakness, proof, repaired. Its limits are the limits of the state rules themselves, which vary in reach, in what they demand as evidence of the ceremony, and in how readily their certificates are accepted by authorities outside the state; a couple whose lives will be lived across jurisdictions, or against the opposition of people who will one day contest the marriage, may nonetheless need the secular certificate's conclusive force. But for the ordinary couple of one community, marrying with their families around them and wanting a document for the bank, the passport office and the revenue record, registration of the religious marriage captures most of the secular route's practical benefit at none of its price, and the persistence of low registration in many states is a failure of administration and awareness rather than of legal design.

The pricing exercise, reduced to the questions a couple should sit down with, is short enough to state. First, is a personal law door open to both of you at all: if it is not, the secular route is your route and the remaining questions are about managing its costs rather than choosing between costs. Second, if it is open, does either of you stand to lose a joint family position or a succession expectation that matters, and does the exemption for couples within the four faiths cover you: if it does not and the loss is real, the personal law route, or the secular route travelled with wills and advice, is where the arithmetic points. Third, how dangerous is publicity in your actual circumstances: not publicity in the abstract, but the named people who would learn of the marriage from a notice book and what they could and would do with the knowledge. Fourth, how complicated are your facts: a prior marriage, a doubtful divorce, a relationship near the degrees, a party near the age floor, any of which argues for the route that investigates first. A couple who answer those four questions in order have performed the whole comparison, and the dimensions exist to inform the answers rather than to substitute for them.

For the advocate, the comparison reduces to a discipline: audit the consequence chapter before the notice is filed, not after the funeral. Establish the Section 21A position, value the severed share, check the prohibited degrees against both the personal law's customs and the First Schedule, and never let a client register an existing marriage under Chapter III in the belief that it is a clerical act. For the aspirant, the comparison is a standard examination theme precisely because it resists summary, and working through [the ten dimensions as a structured deck on ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) before attempting an answer is a reliable way to hold the contrasts, above all the three silent sections, in their correct statutory places.

## Three couples, three answers

Abstract dimensions decide real cases only when they are run against facts, and three worked examples show the deciding factor doing its work. Take first a Hindu couple of different castes, both employed in the city where they live, whose families disapprove but are not dangerous. Both routes are open. On the personal law route they can marry within the week, in a ceremony whose validity no one can sensibly contest if the customary rites of either family are properly performed, and register the marriage under their state's rules afterwards; their coparcenary and succession positions are untouched, and their families' disapproval has no legal lever to pull. On the secular route they gain a conclusive certificate and lose a month to the notice book, and because both profess the Hindu faith the 1976 exemption shields them from severance and the succession switch; the privacy charge is the whole price, and it is payable to an audience of office clerks and strangers rather than to anyone who can act on it. The comparison's answer is that either route serves them, and the choice is genuinely one of taste: the ceremony their community recognises, or the certificate the state guarantees. Couples in this position who agonise over the law are agonising over the wrong thing, and an adviser serves them by saying so.

Take next a Hindu woman and a Muslim man, colleagues who wish to marry without either converting. The comparison collapses to a single door. No personal law will marry them in a form a court will reliably uphold; the conversion alternative carries the exposure the bigamy section described, resting on a change of faith neither of them wishes to make; the secular statute is the only route, and the analysis turns from choosing to pricing. His position is simple: he is outside the four faiths, so Section 19 does not touch him, and Section 21 moves his succession, and hers, and their children's, into the Indian Succession Act, whose testamentary freedom he may welcome. Her position is the expensive one: if she is a member of an undivided Hindu family, Section 19 severs her from it on the wedding day, crystallising the coparcenary share the 2005 amendment gave her by birth, and Section 21 displaces the Hindu Succession Act for her estate, so that her mother, who would have taken as a Class I heir, may take nothing if she dies leaving a husband and children. None of this is a reason to refuse the route, which is the only lawful one open to them; all of it is a reason to walk through it with advice, to decide whether the severed share should be claimed, settled or released, and to make wills that restore by testament the distribution the displaced statute would have produced. The couple who marry at the registry in ignorance of Sections 19 and 21 have not made a mistake about their wedding. They have made an unadvised disposition of two estates.

Take last a Christian couple, one of whom was married before and divorced under the Divorce Act, marrying again in the church of their community. Their route is not in doubt, since both are Christian and their families approve, but the example isolates a dimension the others conceal: the second marriage's dependence on the first marriage's ending. Under every route in this comparison, a party whose earlier marriage subsists cannot marry again, and the validity of the second marriage turns on the validity and finality of the first divorce, a decree of a competent court, its appeal period run or its appeal disposed of. The personal law routes leave the proof of that history to the parties and to any future challenger; the secular route, had this couple chosen it, would have examined the history in advance through the notice and the declaration each party signs of their condition as divorced. The example generalises. Wherever a couple's facts contain a legal complication, a prior marriage, a doubtful divorce, a relationship near the prohibited degrees, a party near the age floor, the route that investigates before the ceremony protects them better than the route that investigates after it, and this consideration, independent of religion, pushes complicated cases towards the secular statute and simple cases towards whichever form the couple prefer. It is the last refinement the verdict needs: price the three costs, and then ask whether your facts need a registrar's questions more than they fear a registrar's notice board.

## Where the general verdict does not hold

Five situations bend the general verdict and should be named. The first is territorial. In Goa a civil code of Portuguese origin, continued in force after liberation, governs the marriages of all communities on substantially common terms, so that the choice this article compares does not present itself there in the same form; the Goan couple's marriage is a registered civil act with religious solemnisation attached to it, and the comparison's dimensions collapse into one system. The second is the couple one of whom is an Indian citizen marrying abroad or to a foreign national in circumstances governed by the Foreign Marriage Act, 1969, where the secular route's Indian machinery is replaced by a diplomatic one and the personal law analysis changes with the place of celebration. The third is the Sikh couple, for whom the routes interleave: their ceremony is validated by their own statute of 1909, their registration may since the amendment of 2012 be taken under that statute rather than the Hindu Marriage Act, and yet their divorce and maintenance run under the Hindu code, so that they use the personal law route in two of its registers at once. The fourth is the couple for whom the secular statute's supposed charges are benefits in disguise: the son whose family would dissipate his share, and who finds in Section 19 a partition he could not have sued for without scandal; the testator whom personal law confines to a third of the estate, and who finds in Section 21 a freedom of bequest worth more than the notice costs; the spouse who will one day have to prove the marriage against hostile in-laws, and who finds in Section 13 the answer to a lawsuit not yet filed. A comparison that cannot recognise its own verdict reversing on the facts has not understood the material, and the reversals all run the same way: the secular route's costs are fixed and knowable, while the personal law route's costs are contingent and arrive unannounced, so that a couple who fear the contingent more than the fixed will rationally pay the fixed price even where the table scores it against them. The fifth situation is the couple within a Scheduled Tribe, for whom the Hindu Marriage Act does not apply at all unless the Central Government has directed its application to their community: their marriages are governed by custom, with custom's flexibility and custom's uncertainty of proof, and for such a couple the secular statute is not one door of two but the only codified door there is, a position many tribal couples discover only when a succession, a pension or a maintenance claim requires them to prove a marriage their custom never wrote down.

## A uniform code is debated; a secular option exists

Arguments about the future of family law in India are frequently conducted as though the alternative to the personal law system were a blank page on which a uniform civil code might one day be written. The premise is false, and the comparison in this article is the proof. A secular option exists, and has existed since 1954, is open to every adult in the country, and is used by a small minority of couples, not because it is hidden but because its price schedule, examined dimension by dimension above, suits only some of the couples who are free to choose it. That fact does not settle the code question in either direction: the existence of an opt-in secular statute is one thing, and the replacement of the personal laws by a single compulsory code is another, and the arguments that divide the country on the second are not answered by the first. It does discipline the debate, because any account of what a uniform code would change has to reckon with the revealed preference of couples who were offered uniformity as an option and mostly declined it, and with the reasons, of privacy, property and inheritance, that this article has set out. The full argument belongs to [the debate on a uniform civil code in India](/2017/01/01/uniform-civil-code-debate-india/), which owns it; what the comparison contributes is the standing reminder that the secular route is not a proposal. It is a statute, with a notice book, a severance clause and a certificate, and couples are choosing it or declining it every week on the terms this article has described.

The closing verdict is therefore the opening one, with its reasoning visible. The route that asks nothing about religion charges the most for it, and the charge is itemised: thirty days of publicity, a severed share, a substituted law of inheritance. Couples who can pay it, or who stand within the exemption that waives two thirds of it, receive in exchange the most secure and most portable marital status Indian law issues. Couples who cannot pay it, and who have a personal law of their own to marry under, are not choosing the worse law; they are choosing the law whose costs fall where they can bear them. The comparison's last word is the one its deciding factor dictates: do not ask which route is better. Ask which of the three costs your family can least afford, and marry under the statute that does not charge it. Couples who run the three costs honestly, and read their own facts for the complications that reward a registrar's questions in advance, will find that the comparison, properly worked, decides itself more often than the volume of commentary on it would lead anyone to expect.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the difference between marrying under personal law and secular law?

Marrying under personal law means marrying under the statute or uncodified law of the parties' own religion: the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 for Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs, the Indian Christian Marriage Act, 1872 and the Divorce Act, 1869 for Christians, the Parsi Marriage and Divorce Act, 1936 for Parsis, and uncodified Muslim law for Muslims. The ceremony is the community's own rite, no notice to the state precedes it, and the marriage carries the incidents of that system, including its law of succession and, for Hindus, membership of the joint family. Marrying under secular law means marrying under the Special Marriage Act, 1954 before a Marriage Officer, in a civil ceremony open to any two persons regardless of religion. That route requires thirty days of public notice, produces a certificate that is conclusive evidence of the marriage, severs a party within the Hindu undivided family from it, and places succession under the Indian Succession Act, subject to the exemption for couples where both profess the Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh or Jain religions.

### Q: Which marriage route is faster and which is more private?

The personal law route is both faster and more private at the point of marriage. A couple who satisfy their personal law's conditions may marry on any day their rite allows, with no waiting period, no notice and no state involvement, though state registration of the marriage may follow afterwards under local rules. The secular route has a built in minimum of thirty days between notice and ceremony, because Section 5 of the Special Marriage Act requires written notice to the Marriage Officer of the district where at least one party has resided for thirty days, and Sections 6 to 8 require the notice to be entered in a book open to inspection, a copy to be sent to the other party's home district, and any objection to be inquired into. If the marriage is not solemnised within three calendar months, a fresh notice is needed. The privacy difference is therefore structural: the personal law routes publicise the wedding only to those the couple invite, while the secular route publicises it in advance to anyone who reads the notice book.

### Q: Do divorce grounds differ between the two marriage routes?

Between the two principal codified routes, remarkably little. Section 13 of the Hindu Marriage Act and Section 27 of the Special Marriage Act have carried substantially the same catalogue of fault grounds since the amending Act of 1976 aligned them, covering voluntary sexual intercourse outside the marriage, cruelty, desertion, unsoundness of mind, venereal disease, disappearance for seven years, and non resumption of cohabitation or non compliance after decrees of judicial separation or restitution. The differences sit at the edges. The secular grounds include imprisonment for seven years or more, which the Hindu list lacks; the Hindu list includes renunciation of the world, which the secular list lacks. Either spouse may petition under both statutes, and each gives the wife additional grounds, including a husband's bigamous earlier marriage and non resumption after a maintenance decree. Outside that pair, the routes diverge genuinely: the Indian Divorce Act was equalised only by the amendment of 2001, Muslim divorce runs through talaq, the wife's delegated and judicial routes and the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939, and Parsi divorce has its own code and courts.

### Q: What are the succession consequences of each marriage route?

On the personal law route, succession follows the community's own law undisturbed: a Hindu's estate under the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, a Muslim's under uncodified law, a Christian's or a Parsi's under the Indian Succession Act, 1925. The secular route changes the answer for one class of couple. Section 21 of the Special Marriage Act provides that succession to the property of a person married under that Act, and to the property of the issue of such a marriage, is regulated by the Indian Succession Act, omitting its Parsi chapter, so that a Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh or Jain who marries at the registry office leaves the Hindu Succession Act behind unless Section 21A applies. Section 21A, inserted in 1976, exempts marriages where both parties profess one of those four religions, leaving their succession under the Hindu Succession Act. The practical bite of Section 21 falls on parents: under the Indian Succession Act a widow and children take first and a mother takes nothing where her child dies intestate leaving a spouse and descendants, whereas the Hindu statute places the mother in the first class of heirs.

### Q: Which route applies to an interfaith couple?

The secular route is the only route designed for them. Personal laws marry within their communities: the Hindu Marriage Act requires both parties to be Hindu in the extended statutory sense, which includes Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs; the Christian and Parsi statutes likewise marry within their faiths; a nikah between a Muslim and a person outside the faiths Muslim law recognises has an uncertain status in the courts. The Special Marriage Act, by contrast, asks no question about religion at all. Its Section 4 conditions concern age, monogamy, capacity and prohibited degrees, and nothing else, so an interfaith couple marries under it as a matter of course, keeping their respective faiths, since the Act neither requires nor records any change of religion. The costs of that openness are the ones this article prices: the thirty day public notice, Section 19 severance for a party in a Hindu undivided family, and succession under the Indian Succession Act by Section 21, none of which the interfaith couple can escape by any drafting, since the exemption in Section 21A is confined to couples where both parties profess the Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh or Jain religions.

### Q: Is bigamy treated the same under both routes?

The prohibition is the same and the machinery differs. Every route in the comparison is monogamous in law: the Hindu Marriage Act makes a subsisting marriage a bar and the second marriage void, the Special Marriage Act opens its conditions with the requirement that neither party has a spouse living, the Parsi statute penalises a bigamous marriage expressly even after conversion, and the Indian Divorce Act and Muslim law's limited polygyny stand apart, Muslim law being the one system in the comparison under which a man's second marriage during the first is not void. The criminal offence, formerly Section 494 of the Indian Penal Code and carried into the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, applies across routes, and the Supreme Court held in Sarla Mudgal and confirmed in Lily Thomas that a conversion does not dissolve a subsisting marriage or license a second one. What differs is detection. The secular route investigates marital condition before the wedding through the notice and objection machinery; the personal law routes investigate nothing in advance, so bigamy surfaces later, through a complaint, a voidness suit or a maintenance claim, and the prosecution must prove the second union was solemnised with the essential ceremonies.

### Q: Which marriage route better protects a woman's rights?

Neither route protects her across the board, and the honest answer is dimension by dimension. On proof and exit, the secular route is stronger: its certificate is conclusive evidence of the marriage, which defeats the commonest defence in maintenance litigation, the denial that any marriage took place, and its divorce grounds have always been available to the wife on the same terms as the husband. On property, the personal law route is frequently stronger for a Hindu woman: marrying under the Hindu Marriage Act leaves her coparcenary share under the Hindu Succession Act untouched, while the secular route severs her from the undivided family and displaces the statute under which her mother would inherit from her. On maintenance during the marriage the secular statute is weaker, since its Sections 36 and 37 speak only of the wife, where the Hindu statute lets either spouse claim; after the marriage both systems reach the same summary remedy under the general criminal procedure. On safety, the domestic violence statute protects her regardless of route. A woman choosing between the routes is trading protections, not accumulating them.

### Q: Should a couple choose the secular marriage route?

The comparison's verdict is that the question is incomplete until the couple name which cost they can least afford. Choose the secular route where it is the only lawful door, as it is for an interfaith couple who will not convert; where the protection of a public record outweighs the price of publicity, as for a couple marrying against dangerous opposition who have first secured their safety; or where the parties' faiths bring them within the exemption of Section 21A, so that the severance and succession charges fall away. Prefer the personal law route where a ceremony matters to the parties or their families, where privacy during the engagement cannot be risked, or where a Hindu woman's coparcenary share and her family's succession expectations are real assets that Section 19 and Section 21 would destroy without advice. In every case the decision should be made after reading the consequence provisions, Sections 19, 20, 21 and 21A of the Special Marriage Act, with the same care as the ceremony provisions, because the wedding lasts a day and the consequences are permanent.

### Q: What documents and witnesses does each route require?

The routes differ in what they ask the couple to produce, and the difference reveals what each route is. The secular route is documentary from the first step: the notice must state each party's name, occupation, marital condition, age, dwelling place, place of residence and length of residence, and before the ceremony the parties and three witnesses sign the Third Schedule declaration, affirming the statutory age, the absence of a living spouse, and freedom from the prohibited degrees, in the knowledge that a false declaration is an offence. The personal law route asks for no documents at the wedding at all, since the validity of a rite based marriage turns on the rite and the Section 5 conditions, not on paper. Documents arrive later and sideways, when state registration or a passport or a bank demands proof of a ceremony performed weeks or years earlier, and the couple discover that the informality of the wedding day has to be reconstructed in writing for every official purpose afterwards. The lesson for couples is to register whatever they do.

### Q: What changes for an inter-caste couple of the same faith who marry under the secular statute?

For an inter-caste couple within the Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh or Jain faiths, almost nothing of substance changes, and that is the secular route's most underappreciated use. Because both parties profess one of the four religions, Section 21A of the Special Marriage Act exempts them from the severance and succession consequences of Sections 19 and 21: they are not severed from their undivided families, and succession to their property remains under the Hindu Succession Act. The thirty day notice applies in full, so the route costs the same month of publicity as it costs anyone else, and the couple gain the same conclusive certificate. What the route does for them is narrow but real: it moves the marriage out of the family's sphere of control and into the state's, so that caste opposition has no legal lever to pull, since no objection on caste grounds is one the officer may act on. Where the families accept a temple wedding, the personal law route is cheaper in privacy; where the family controls the community's officiants or the couple want a state record beyond the family's reach, the secular statute serves them as cleanly as it serves an interfaith couple.

### Q: Does registering a religious marriage under the secular statute change succession and joint family rights?

Only through the route the registration takes, and the distinction is the most commonly misunderstood point in the comparison. A marriage may come onto the secular statute's books in two ways. Chapter III of the Special Marriage Act allows a marriage celebrated in any other form to be registered under it, and Section 18 then deems it a marriage solemnised under the Act, importing the whole consequence chapter, including severance under Section 19 and succession under Section 21, subject to the Section 21A exemption. That is a change of law dressed as paperwork, and a couple who register a temple wedding under Chapter III thinking they have merely recorded it have in fact converted its legal incidents. The other way is registration under state rules made for marriages celebrated in the community's own form, as distinct from registration under the Act itself; that records the fact of the marriage without changing its law. Couples should therefore ask which register they are being entered in, and advisers should treat a Chapter III registration exactly as if the couple had walked to the Marriage Officer before the ceremony rather than after it.

### Q: Do the prohibited degrees of relationship differ between the two routes?

In substance they overlap and in source they differ. The Hindu Marriage Act defines prohibited degrees in Section 3, with the sapinda bar extending five generations on the father's side and three on the mother's, subject to custom, and lists thirty seven relationships as prohibited on the mother's and father's sides where custom does not permit them. The Special Marriage Act's Section 2 and its First Schedule carry a list of degrees drawn from the same family of concepts but framed independently, and the Act bars marriages within its schedule unless the custom governing at least one party permits the union, with the schedule also listing relationships the Act treats as permitting on certain conditions. Custom is therefore an escape clause on both routes, and a couple related in a way their custom sanctions, as in cross cousin marrying communities of the south, may marry on either route. Muslim law's prohibitions differ structurally again, built from the Qur'anic categories of consanguinity, affinity and fosterage rather than from a statutory schedule. The practical rule is simple: ask your own community's rule first, because where custom permits, both the codified routes permit, and where it does not, neither does.

### Q: Can a couple divorce under a different statute from the one they married under?

Ordinarily no, and the exceptions prove the rule. A marriage solemnised under the Hindu Marriage Act is dissolved under that Act; one solemnised under the Special Marriage Act, under its Sections 27 and 28. The statute of the marriage governs the statute of the divorce, because grounds, forum and reliefs are incidents of the marriage the statute created. The exceptions arise where the law changed around the parties rather than where they chose it: a marriage celebrated under uncodified Hindu law before 1955 falls to be divorced under the Hindu Marriage Act, and a marriage registered under Chapter III of the Special Marriage Act becomes an Act marriage by the Section 18 deeming and is divorced under the Act's provisions. The conversion cases are the apparent exception that is not one: a Hindu married under the Hindu Marriage Act who converts and seeks to exit the first marriage does not gain a new statute, as Sarla Mudgal and Lily Thomas establish, and must dissolve the first marriage under its own law before any second union is lawful. Couples should therefore choose the wedding statute as if they were choosing the divorce statute too, because they are.

### Q: Which route is safer if the ceremony is later challenged as defectively performed?

The secular route, by the margin the comparison's proof dimension predicts. A marriage solemnised before a Marriage Officer under the Special Marriage Act is complete when the parties speak the prescribed words before the officer and witnesses and Section 13 makes the resulting certificate conclusive evidence of the marriage. There is no rite whose essentials can be disputed, no custom to prove, and no priest whose testimony is needed. On the personal law side the safety of the ceremony depends on how completely the rite was performed and on how provable that performance is. For Hindus the Supreme Court has required the ceremonies customarily necessary, with the saptapadi where the customary form includes it, and a challenge to a marriage whose rite was truncated or defective can succeed, as where a marriage was registered with no underlying ceremony performed. Muslim, Christian and Parsi ceremonies have their own formal essentials, and the Parsi Ashirvad's requirements are strict where the Nikah's contract form is forgiving. A couple who anticipate a challenge from family, a rival claimant or the state should prefer the route whose ceremony is the least contestable, and there is none less contestable than a state official's book.

### Q: Does family or community opposition affect the validity of a marriage under either route?

No, on either route, and the Constitution is emphatic on the point. An adult's choice of a partner is an exercise of the liberty and dignity protected by Article 21, not subject to approval by family, community or any assembly claiming to judge it, as the Supreme Court held in Lata Singh v. State of Uttar Pradesh, in the preventive, remedial and punitive framework of Shakti Vahini v. Union of India, and in restoring a marriage annulled at a father's instance in Shafin Jahan v. Asokan K.M. Opposition therefore cannot make a valid marriage invalid on either route. What it can do, and this is the comparison's wound, is operate differently on the machinery of each. On the personal law routes opposition has no procedural lever at all: there is no office to approach and no objection to file, and the family must resort to the courts or to the unlawful. On the secular route the notice machinery hands the opponent a lever, the objection, which cannot defeat a marriage that satisfies Section 4 but can delay it and, worse, alerts the opponent at the publication stage. The constitutional floor is equal; the procedural terrain is not.

### Q: Which route produces stronger proof of marriage for passports, visas and official records?

The secular route, and the gap widens the further the document must travel. The certificate under Section 13 of the Special Marriage Act is conclusive evidence of the marriage, entered in the Marriage Certificate Book by the officer who solemnised it, and it answers questions of identity, age and marital condition on its face, because the notice and declaration preceding it fixed those particulars. For passports, consular offices, immigration authorities and employers' records, a state certificate of this form is the document the system is designed to read. On the personal law side the marriage's proof is only as strong as its record: a certificate of registration under state rules is useful within the state, but it records a ceremony rather than constituting the marriage, and its acceptance outside the issuing state, and outside the country, depends on the receiving authority's confidence in a system it did not create. An unregistered personal law marriage, lawful as it remains, is proved by evidence of the ceremony, and a consulate or pension office asked to rely on affidavits years later is entitled to be slower. The hierarchy is a gradient of portability, and the state certificate travels best.

### Q: Does a marriage under the secular route survive if one partner later converts to another religion?

Yes, as a marriage under the Act, because conversion changes the parties' faiths and not the statute of their marriage. The Special Marriage Act's incidents are attached to the marriage by its sections, not by the parties' religion at any given moment: severance under Section 19 occurred at solemnisation, the succession regime of Sections 21 and 21A was fixed with it, and the divorce provisions of Sections 27 and 28 govern whatever the parties subsequently believe. One edge case sits at the exemption's boundary. Where both parties professed one of the four faiths at the wedding and thus enjoyed Section 21A's protection, and one party later leaves that faith, the statute gives no clear answer on whether the exemption travels with the conversion, and the question falls to be litigated on the Act's text. The comparison with the conversion-for-bigamy cases sharpens the answer rather than clouding it: in those cases the conversion was an attempt to escape a subsisting marriage's consequences, and the courts refused the escape; here the marriage's own statute is unmoved by conversion, because the Act never asked about religion. The route that asks nothing about religion has no reason to ask again.

### Q: Do both routes offer restitution of conjugal rights and judicial separation?

Both principal codified routes do, in near identical terms, and the minor faith statutes each have their equivalents. Section 9 of the Hindu Marriage Act and Section 22 of the Special Marriage Act provide restitution of conjugal rights, the decree by which a spouse who has withdrawn from the other's society without reasonable excuse may be directed to return, and the two provisions differ more in numbering than in substance. Judicial separation, the decree that suspends cohabitation without dissolving the marriage, is Section 10 of the Hindu Act and Section 23 of the Special Marriage Act, and the grounds for it track the divorce grounds, so that a party who can prove cruelty or desertion can seek the lesser relief instead. The remedy of restitution is the most debated in family law, defended as a soft remedy and criticised as an anachronism, and the Supreme Court has upheld its constitutionality in the Hindu Act's context; the comparison records it as a draw. Couples should know that both routes offer the same staircase of relief, from restitution through separation to divorce, and that choosing a route does not choose a philosophy of the marriage's end.

### Q: Which route should a couple choose if they plan to live in joint family property?

The personal law route, unless the couple priced and accepted what the secular route does to the family's property structure. A Hindu who marries under that Act and continues in the joint family continues as before: coparcenary membership survives the wedding, the 2005 amendment's equal shares are undisturbed, and residence in the family property raises no question of law. The secular route alters the position on the wedding day. Section 19 of the Special Marriage Act severs a member of an undivided family from it, on the fiction of a partition immediately before the marriage, and the party's share is crystallised. Residence in the family home afterwards becomes a matter of arrangement rather than of right. Section 21A's exemption changes the answer for couples where both profess the Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh or Jain faiths, but for an interfaith couple marrying under the Act the severance is unavoidable. A couple whose life will be lived under the family roof should therefore treat the property consequence as the deciding factor it is, and either marry under the personal law or take advice on claiming, settling or releasing the severed share before the wedding.

### Q: Why do so few couples use the secular route when it is open to everyone?

Because the route's users are a defined population, not the population at large, and the comparison's history explains why. The secular statute was designed as a safety valve beside the personal laws, not as their replacement: it serves the couples the personal law door does not serve, the interfaith couple, the couple whose community's officiants are closed to them, the couple whose families control the community's forms. Those couples are numerous yet small as a share of all marriages, because most Indian marriages are arranged or approved within the community. Two further features keep the number down. The thirty day notice, with its publicity, deters exactly the couples the route was built for, the ones marrying against opposition; and the consequence provisions, the severance and the succession switch, deter the couples who have something to lose by leaving their personal law's property system. The result is a route that is used disproportionately by the opposed and the interfaith, used sparingly by everyone else, and misunderstood by many who would benefit from it. Scoring the routes by volume measures the wrong thing; the secular statute's small share of weddings is a feature of its design, not evidence of failure.
