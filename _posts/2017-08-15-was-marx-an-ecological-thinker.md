Was Marx an ecological thinker, or does ecology have to be supplied to his work from outside it? Few questions in the study of Marx divide readers as cleanly, and few are argued as badly. One camp presents a writer who understood soil exhaustion, material cycles, and obligations owed across generations. Another presents a celebrant of industrial expansion who treated nature as a stock of raw material awaiting human transformation. Both camps quote genuine passages. Both camps omit the passages that embarrass them. An honest treatment begins by admitting that the archive contains two bodies of text pointing in different directions, and that any verdict ignoring either body has been purchased by selection rather than earned by reading.

![Was Marx an ecological thinker, the two bodies of text and the extension not possession thesis - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

Distribution matters as much as content. Passages praising the conquest of natural forces cluster in writings from the eighteen forties and eighteen fifties, when Marx shared much of his century's confidence in industrial development. Passages analysing disrupted material cycles, correcting careless claims about wealth, and describing society as a trustee rather than an owner of the earth belong chiefly to the eighteen sixties and eighteen seventies. Private notebooks filled with extracts from agricultural chemistry, geology, and soil science date from those later decades as well. Chronology therefore changes the shape of the dispute. A reader confronting an early manifesto beside a mature volume of Capital faces development across a working life, not a simple contradiction frozen inside a single book.

What follows refuses both shortcuts. Productivist evidence receives full quotation and full weight, because softening that evidence would make every later conclusion worthless. Ecological evidence receives the same treatment, including one passage about holding land in trust that advocates quote less often than its strength deserves. Arguments about the structure of Marx's concepts are separated from arguments about individual sentences, since a theory might contain ecological passages while lacking any ecological architecture, or contain a usable architecture while its author expressed productivist enthusiasms in journalism. Separating those levels is the only way the question can be answered rather than merely won.

A second separation governs the whole discussion. Whether Marx held ecological views is a historical and textual matter, settled by dated writings and careful reading. Whether states claiming his authority damaged environments during the twentieth century is a different matter, settled by a different record. Political history cannot decide what a nineteenth century manuscript says, just as a manuscript cannot excuse or condemn governments acting generations later. Keeping that boundary firm protects both inquiries. Readers seeking the environmental record of socialist states will find it treated on its own terms elsewhere in this series, and nothing in these pages borrows authority from that record in either direction.

## Ecology and the shape of the dispute

Disagreement persists partly because the central term carries several meanings that disputants rarely distinguish. An ecological thinker might mean an author who noticed damage done to soils, forests, and water. It might mean an author possessing a systematic account of how production depends upon natural conditions and how those conditions impose limits. It might mean an author whose politics assigned nature value independent of human use. Those are three different standards, and Marx fares differently under each. Under the weakest standard he qualifies easily, because discussions of soil fertility run through his mature economics. Under the strongest standard he qualifies doubtfully at best, because passages granting nature standing apart from human purposes are scarce and contested. Most published argument slides between standards without announcing the movement, which is why opposing essays can sound persuasive while never actually meeting.

Precision about standards also clarifies what evidence would count. Occasional remarks about landscape or pollution would satisfy only the weakest standard. A systematic claim requires concepts doing organised work across the analysis: a category for material exchange between society and its environment, an account of how a mode of production can undermine conditions it presupposes, and some statement about obligations or limits that follows from that account rather than decorating it. Parts of the mature writing meet that description. Parts do not. Grading the parts separately, instead of issuing a single verdict across forty years of writing, is the method adopted here, and it leads toward a conclusion narrower than either camp prefers.

Readers arriving from the concept itself may want the dedicated treatment of [metabolic rift explained](/2017/07/15/metabolic-rift-explained/), which handles the central category in isolation. Readers interested in how the modern recovery was constructed may turn to [john bellamy foster marx ecology](/2017/08/01/john-bellamy-foster-marx-ecology/), where that project receives a full profile. The present article has a narrower job: weighing two bodies of primary text against each other, in date order, and stating exactly how much the weighing supports.

### Was Marx an ecological thinker?

partly, and increasingly. Early writings celebrate mastery of nature; later ones analyse the disruption of material cycles and state that societies hold the earth in trust for successors. The defensible conclusion is that his framework extends ecologically and that he had begun to extend it.

That short answer contains the whole argument in miniature, and the rest of this article unpacks each clause. Partly, because the ecological material is real but incomplete. Increasingly, because its weight grows as the career proceeds and the productivist rhetoric recedes. Framework language, because the strongest claim concerns what the conceptual apparatus permits rather than what its author finished. Begun, because notebooks and late passages show work in progress that death interrupted, not a completed doctrine awaiting discovery in a drawer. Anyone wanting a cleaner verdict, in either direction, will have to discard evidence, and the discarded evidence is catalogued openly below so that the cost of cleanliness stays visible.

### What does the question actually ask?

A textual and historical inquiry sits underneath the slogan, asking whether writings produced across four decades contain a systematic treatment of production in relation to natural conditions, or share the productivism typical of that period and need ecological content imported later.

Framing matters because slogans invite tribal answers. Green admirers and hostile critics both benefit from portraying the question as a loyalty test, since loyalty tests require no reading. Scholarship begins where loyalty ends. A scholar can admire the metabolic analysis while conceding that no theory of natural limits appears anywhere in the corpus. A critic can press the structural objection while conceding that the trusteeship passage says something striking about intergenerational obligation. Mixed verdicts irritate partisans because mixed verdicts cannot be printed on banners, yet mixed verdicts are what archives usually yield when the subject wrote voluminously across a long and changing century.

Several neighbouring questions must be peeled away before the core inquiry stands clear. Whether later Marxists developed ecological ideas is a question about a tradition, answered in the guide to the cluster as a whole. Whether Marxist economics can accommodate limits conceptually is a theoretical question, pursued in [marxism and ecological limits](/2017/10/15/marxism-and-ecological-limits/), where the abundance premise and the realm of freedom receive direct attention. Whether particular governments calling themselves Marxist protected or wrecked landscapes is an empirical question about states. Conflating those inquiries produces the familiar shouting match in which a quotation from Capital is answered with a photograph of a drained sea, as though either item refuted the other. Neither does.

### Why do competent readers reach opposite verdicts?

Selection explains much of the divergence, because an author who published millions of words across journalism, correspondence, drafts, and finished volumes supplies ammunition for almost any portrait, and readers who begin with a verdict can assemble a convincing anthology supporting it within a week.

Honest disagreement also survives honest reading. Two scholars can examine identical passages and differ over what weight a programmatic correction should carry against a lifetime of productivist assumption, or over whether a category performs theoretical work or merely organises description. Weighting is judgement, and judgement varies. What cannot vary legitimately is the inventory. A verdict may discount the trusteeship passage as unsystematic, but a verdict pretending the passage does not exist has left scholarship behind. Equally, a verdict may treat the Manifesto's praise of subjugated natural forces as youthful rhetoric overtaken by later analysis, but pretending that praise was ironic or absent is a falsification. The inventory assembled in this article is deliberately bipartisan: every strong passage either side relies upon appears, dated, in context, with its force acknowledged before any discounting begins.

Genre complicates the inventory further. A manifesto is a recruiting document, written to exhilarate, and its superlatives belong to persuasion as much as to analysis. A notebook extract is a private working tool, and its presence proves engagement without proving endorsement of every line copied. A letter can vent prejudices its writer would never defend in print. A published treatise, revised across editions, carries the greatest evidential weight, and a fragment prepared for posthumous publication sits somewhere between treatise and notebook. Sensible weighing respects those differences. Passages from the first volume of Capital, which Marx saw through the press repeatedly, outrank phrases from youthful journalism when the two conflict, not because youth is worthless as evidence but because mature published argument represents settled intention more reliably.

## The productivist body of text

Begin with the evidence least convenient for ecological readings, presented without apology or softening. Across the 1840s and 1850s Marx repeatedly praised the bourgeoisie's transformation of the natural world, described capital as a civilising force that battered down barriers including natural ones, and constructed a philosophy of history in which expanding productive capacity drives social change. Those are not stray metaphors. Each passage belongs to a considered position about progress, industry, and the historical role of capitalism, and each gave later readers solid ground for calling the outlook Promethean, after the mythic figure who stole fire for humankind. Dismissing that material as juvenilia fails chronologically, since some of it survives into the mature economic manuscripts in altered dress.

### How should the Promethean label be understood?

A reading holding that Marx celebrated humanity's growing dominion over natural forces, treated expanding production as history's engine, and regarded nature chiefly as material and instrument for human purposes, a portrait assembled mainly from writings of the eighteen forties and fifties.

Advocates of that reading are not inventing a straw figure. They can point to a famous catalogue in the Communist Manifesto, composed with Engels and published in 1848, celebrating railways, steam navigation, telegraphs, cleared continents, and canalised rivers as bourgeois achievements surpassing pyramids and aqueducts. Rhetoric of that kind does more than describe. It admires. Admiration for the productive dynamism of capitalism, held simultaneously with condemnation of capitalist property relations, forms one of the most characteristic tensions in all of Marx's writing, and ecological critics fasten on the admiration while ecological defenders fasten on the condemnation. Both halves are genuinely present in the sentence structure of the early work, often within a single paragraph.

### Where does the case for the prosecution rest?

Four sources carry most of the weight: the Manifesto's celebration of subjugated natural forces, the Grundrisse passages on capital overcoming barriers, the 1859 Preface making productive development history's motor, and scattered correspondence treating industrial expansion as progress without qualification or regret.

Consider the Manifesto closely. Its authors, surveying a century of bourgeois achievement, include among the wonders a phrase about the "subjection of nature's forces to man" (Communist Manifesto, 1848). Subjection is a word of domination, not stewardship. Surrounding lines praise machinery, chemistry applied to industry and agriculture, and the clearing of whole continents for cultivation. Nothing in the passage mourns drained marshes, felled forests, or exhausted fields. Whatever qualifications later writings add, a reader meeting only this document would reasonably conclude that its authors regarded nature's resistance to human purpose as an obstacle whose defeat measured progress. Ecological readings must absorb that conclusion, not evade it, before they can qualify it with later evidence.

The Grundrisse manuscripts, drafted during the winter of 1857 and 1858 though published long afterward, deepen the impression. There capital appears as a restless force dissolving limits of geography, custom, and nature itself, treating every barrier as something to be overcome on the road toward universal intercourse and expanded production. Discussion in those notebooks of capital's civilising influence presents the destruction of local self sufficiency and natural idolatry as historically progressive, however brutal the process felt to those living through it. Because the Grundrisse remained unpublished during Marx's lifetime, its evidential status differs from the Manifesto's, yet as a record of how the mature economist conceived capital's relation to the natural world during the years immediately preceding Capital, its testimony counts heavily.

A short preface published in 1859 supplied the most quoted summary of the whole theory of history. In that text, development of the productive forces proceeds until relations of production fetter it, whereupon an era of social revolution opens. Nature enters the scheme largely as the field upon which productive forces expand. No passage in the preface suggests that natural conditions might fetter development from outside the social relation, or that exhausted soils and depleted materials could impose a limit the scheme must register. Critics regard that absence as diagnostic. Supporters reply that a two page summary cannot contain every category of a system elaborated across thousands of pages, which is true, and the exchange illustrates why single documents settle so little in this dispute.

Journalism adds colour rather than new concepts. Articles on India written for an American newspaper in 1853 described British rule as destructive yet regenerative, assigning England an unconscious historical mission in demolishing an old order and laying foundations for a new one. Whatever those articles contribute to debates about colonialism, treated in [marx on colonialism debate](/2017/03/01/marx-on-colonialism-debate/) in this series, their treatment of irrigation, land, and village agriculture subordinates natural and social ecologies alike to a narrative of historical advance. A writer capable of that framing in 1853 had not yet made the disruption of material cycles between town and country into the analytical centrepiece it became in the following decade.

Scale of concession matters here. Conceding the productivist body does not concede the whole case, because a career is not a snapshot and because some early passages contain counter currents: even the manuscripts of 1844 discuss humanity as part of nature and criticise an estrangement between people and their natural conditions. Alienation language differs from ecological analysis, however. A philosophical claim that human beings belong to nature does not amount to an account of nutrient cycles, energy flows, or finite stocks, and defenders who quote 1844 estrangement passages as ecological theory commit the same inflation they accuse opponents of committing with the Manifesto. Symmetry of standards is the discipline this article keeps returning to, because asymmetry is the engine of the entire quarrel.

## The ecological body of text

Turn now to evidence least convenient for the productivist portrait, granted equal prominence and equal scrutiny. Three mature texts anchor that evidence, supported by a mass of private notebooks. First, an 1875 critique of a German party programme opens by correcting the programme's claim about labour and wealth, insisting that nature stands alongside labour as a source of use values. Second, volumes one and three of Capital analyse production as a metabolic exchange between society and the earth, and describe large scale industry and agriculture jointly disturbing that exchange. Third, the third volume contains a passage on land ownership across generations that denies outright ownership of the planet to any society and imposes a duty of improved transmission. Each text will be taken in turn, with its date, genre, and argumentative setting made explicit.

### Why does the Gotha correction begin with wealth?

Opening the Critique of the Gotha Programme, written in 1875 and published posthumously, Marx objected that labour alone creates nothing like all wealth, because nature supplies use values directly, and a programme forgetting that fact misleads workers about their dependence on natural conditions.

His sentence deserves quotation at the point where the argument turns: "Labour is not the source of all wealth" (Critique of the Gotha Programme, 1875). Few openings in his writing are as blunt. The target was a draft programme declaring labour the source of all wealth and all culture, a slogan Marx considered both false and politically dangerous. False, because material wealth consists of use values, and use values come from nature as much as from work. Dangerous, because workers taught that labour creates everything may conclude that whoever controls labour's product deserves it, while forgetting that access to land, water, and raw material is a precondition no amount of effort replaces. A detailed reading of that document appears in the [critique of the gotha programme guide](/2013/09/01/critique-of-the-gotha-programme-guide/), and its opening correction matters far beyond party drafting disputes.

Notice what that correction does theoretically. Value, in Marx's economics, is congealed abstract labour, and nature contributes nothing to value as he defines the category. Use value is another matter entirely: bread feeds because grain grew, not because hours were expended, and a coat warms because wool and weaving both occurred. Critics often treat the exclusion of nature from value as proof that nature is invisible to the system. The Gotha correction shows the author of the system policing the boundary himself, warning readers not to collapse wealth into value. Whether that policing suffices to answer the structural objection is a separate issue, examined in its own section below. As textual evidence about its author's intentions, the passage is unambiguous and late: 1875 stands near the end of the writing life, decades after the Manifesto's celebrations.

### Why does a rent chapter contain a trusteeship claim?

A passage in the third volume of Capital, drafted during the eighteen sixties and published in 1894 after editing by Engels, denying that any society owns the earth and requiring each generation to transmit the planet improved to successors, as careful householders rather than proprietors.

Lawyers call the underlying arrangement usufruct: use and enjoyment of property belonging to another, with an obligation to preserve its substance. Roman law supplied the term, and agricultural practice supplied the intuition, since a tenant farmer who mined the soil for one harvest cheated whoever farmed afterward. Marx generalises that intuition to the whole species. No generation made the earth. No generation may therefore treat the earth as its disposable asset. Societies appear in the passage as possessors and beneficiaries, holding a trust whose beneficiaries include people not yet born. Among its sentences stands a compact statement of the duty owed forward: "they bequeath it in an improved state to succeeding generations" (Capital, volume three). Improved deserves emphasis. Preservation alone would satisfy a merely conservative ethic. Improvement demands that transmission leave land more capable than it was found, which converts stewardship from passive restraint into active obligation.

Why does that paragraph matter so much? Partly because of its placement. Volume three addresses ground rent, the form taken by landed property inside capitalist production, and the trusteeship claim interrupts a technical discussion of rent categories to assert a standpoint outside them: even abolishing private landowners would not make society proprietor of the planet, because ownership itself is the wrong relation between generations and their shared condition of existence. Partly because of its scope. Intergenerational obligations in most nineteenth century political economy extend to debts, constitutions, and public works. Extending obligation to the condition of the earth itself, and stating that extension as a limit on what any social order may legitimately do, goes well beyond the period's conventions. Advocates who call this passage the strongest single ecological text in the corpus have a serious case, and critics who wave it aside as decoration owe an explanation of why decoration would take so juridically precise a form.

Authorship questions occasionally cloud the paragraph, so they should be faced directly. Engels edited volume three from manuscripts his collaborator left incomplete, and some passages elsewhere in that volume show editorial construction. Misreadings circulating in popular debate sometimes describe the trusteeship passage as an editorial insertion. Scholarship on the manuscripts does not support that description: the passage derives from Marx's own draft material of the eighteen sixties. Treating it as an interpolation because it inconveniences the productivist portrait repeats, in reverse, the selectivity that ecological advocates practise when they misplace the Manifesto in an juvenilia drawer. Evidence is evidence wherever it lands.

Beneath both the Gotha correction and the trusteeship passage lies the concept giving this cluster its centre: metabolism. Marx borrowed a term from the physiology and chemistry of his period, denoting material exchange between an organism and its surroundings, and used it for the labour process understood as society's ongoing exchange with the rest of nature. Food grown in fields travels to cities. Bodies return some matter to sewers and rivers rather than to fields. Nutrients accumulate where they pollute and vanish where they are needed. When that circulation breaks, soil fertility declines in one place while waste burdens grow in another. The first volume of Capital discusses the dynamic for capitalist agriculture and industry together, arguing that the same system concentrating production also concentrates the rupture. Fuller conceptual treatment belongs to the rift article already linked above; here the passages matter as dated exhibits inside the ecological body, both from the mature economics rather than from youthful philosophy.

## The two bodies of text

Cataloguing exhibits side by side disciplines argument, because juxtaposition exposes chronology, genre, and context that anthology style quotation conceals. The two bodies of text are therefore set out in a single table, one row per passage, with dating, substance, the reading each supports, its setting, and whether later writing qualifies it. Readers can inspect the whole inventory at a glance before returning to interpretation, and partisans of either reading can check whether their favourite evidence has been fairly represented. A table also prevents a favourite rhetorical trick: quoting an 1848 flourish and an 1875 correction as though both issued from the same desk in the same month.

| Grundrisse on capital dissolving barriers | 1857 to 1858 | Capital treats natural and social barriers as obstacles to overcome | Productivist | Private manuscripts unpublished during the author's lifetime | Later volumes distinguish capital's drive from any endorsement of its costs |
| Preface summary of historical development | 1859 | Growth of productive forces drives social transformation | Productivist | A compressed two page statement of method | Categories of metabolism and rent complicate the compressed scheme |
| Journalism on India and irrigation | 1853 | Colonial rule destroys an old order while laying new foundations | Productivist | Newspaper articles written for an American audience | Irish and colonial writings of later years shift emphasis considerably |
| Manuscripts on estrangement from nature | 1844 | Human beings belong to nature and suffer estrangement from it | Ecological, weakly | Philosophical manuscripts unpublished at the time | Later work replaces philosophical vocabulary with material analysis |
| First volume on agriculture and industry | 1867 | Capitalist production disturbs the metabolic interaction of people and earth | Ecological | A treatise published and revised by its author | The analysis deepens in the rent chapters of the third volume |

Reading down the date column teaches the first lesson of the table. Productivist exhibits gather on the left side of the career, ecological exhibits on the right. Reading down the context column teaches the second lesson. Weight of genre increases as dates advance: a co authored manifesto and newspaper journalism give way to a revised treatise, a programmatic critique, and decades of scientific note taking. Reading down the final column teaches the third lesson. Qualification runs overwhelmingly in one direction. Later writing complicates, corrects, or abandons positions visible earlier. Almost no later writing withdraws an ecological passage in favour of an earlier enthusiasm. Trajectories of that shape are familiar in intellectual biography, and historians have a word for them: development.

Sceptics may object that the table's construction itself smuggles in the conclusion, since whoever selects rows selects results. The objection deserves a direct answer. Selection criteria were stated before the table appeared: passages most frequently cited by each camp, passages carrying conceptual weight in the mature system, and passages whose dating is secure. An objector armed with a missing exhibit can add a row, and the table format invites exactly that correction, which anthologies in prose form discourage. Rival scholars have indeed proposed additions, mostly correspondence expressing enthusiasm for particular technologies or impatience with romantic nature writing. Adding those rows thickens the early productivist column and some middle years without touching the chronological gradient, because the contested mature texts remain where they are.

Context columns also guard against a subtler distortion: treating every sentence as a vote. Some passages vote on values, stating what their author admired. Some vote on mechanisms, stating how a system behaves regardless of admiration. The Manifesto passage votes on both, admiring a dynamism whose social costs the same pamphlet denounces in other paragraphs. The metabolic passages vote chiefly on mechanisms, describing a rupture built into the organisation of production rather than a sin committed by greedy individuals. Mechanism claims age differently from value claims. A writer can stop admiring industrial dynamism without retracting an analysis of nutrient flows, and can retain an analysis while abandoning youthful rhetoric entirely. Much confusion in secondary debate comes from scoring mechanism passages as endorsements and rhetorical passages as theory.

One further exhibit deserves prose treatment because tables flatten it. During the eighteen seventies Marx studied the work of agricultural chemists and writers on land management, filling notebooks with extracts on soil composition, fertiliser, forest clearance, and the farming practices of different regions. He corresponded about Russian landholding, learned Russian partly to read agronomic and statistical material in the original, and drafted replies to a Russian correspondent asking whether her country must pass through capitalist dissolution of communal land. Those drafts entertain a possibility the early schema would have excluded: that a communal form might develop without first being destroyed by capital. Whatever one concludes about ecology narrowly defined, the picture of a thinker sealed inside productivist dogma during his final decades cannot survive contact with that archive.


## Chronology, development, and the burden of proof

Distribution across a career raises a methodological question that both camps answer too quickly. When early and late writings conflict in emphasis, should interpreters privilege the late work as mature, treat the corpus as a single simultaneous system, or read the tension as permanent and unresolved? Literary scholars facing the same problem in other authors have developed conventions: juvenilia illuminate origins, mature published work carries greatest authority, and posthumous fragments require corroboration. Applying those conventions here yields the developmental reading. Marx began inside his century's productivist consensus, grew increasingly occupied with material conditions of production during the research for Capital, and left ecological analyses and obligations in writings from his final two decades. Development of that kind is ordinary. What would be extraordinary is a thinker of the eighteen forties anticipating soil chemistry debates of the eighteen sixties without changing his mind about anything along the way.

### How much change does the chronology establish?

Yes in emphasis, evidence, and conceptual apparatus, as the dated exhibits show: celebrations of mastery dominate the eighteen forties and fifties, while metabolic analysis, the wealth correction, and the trusteeship duty belong to the sixties, the seventies, and the notebooks beyond.

Change should not be exaggerated into conversion. No dramatic recantation exists in which the older writer denounces his younger self as a despoiler's apologist. Continuities persist across the supposed divide: confidence that human productive activity transforms the world, suspicion of pastoral nostalgia, and insistence that material production mediates every relation between society and its surroundings. A developmental reading claims movement within a framework, not replacement of one framework by another. Productivist assumptions about growth remained available in the later writing, coexisting with analyses that pull against them. Honest scholarship reports coexistence rather than airbrushing either strand, which is precisely why simple yes or no answers fail.

### What explains the divergence between the two bodies?

Because their author wrote for forty years across changing sciences and political circumstances, began inside a productivist consensus, encountered agricultural chemistry and soil exhaustion debates mid career, and allowed research for a critique of political economy to reshape questions posed in youthful philosophical language.

Burden of proof shifts once chronology enters. Defenders of a purely productivist Marx must now explain away mature published analysis, not merely youthful remarks by opponents. Defenders of a fully ecological Marx must explain why ecological insight never consolidated into a theory of limits, a politics of restraint, or even a single sustained treatise on natural conditions. Each side carries a burden the other side delights in pointing out. Asymmetrical mockery aside, both burdens are genuine, and discharging one's own burden matters more than publicising the opponent's. Sections below give the structural critics their strongest voice, then test replies against the texts, then grade the modern recovery project that claims more than chronology alone can deliver.

Historians of science add one more chronological nuance. Agricultural chemistry changed rapidly during Marx's working life. Justus von Liebig, whose writings on soil nutrients Marx studied closely, himself revised positions on fertiliser and the robbery of soil fertility across successive editions, moving from confidence in mineral replacement toward alarm about nutrient export from farmland to cities. A reader tracking Marx's extracts watches him tracking a science in motion. Judging the resulting position as though both men wrote with settled modern knowledge repeats the anachronism the scientific claims policy of this cluster forbids: nineteenth century science must be described as of its period, with its disputes, uncertainties, and period instruments intact. Liebig's chemistry was powerful and partial, strong on nutrient bookkeeping, silent on much else, and the Marx who learned from Liebig inherited strengths and silences together.

Geography contributed as well. Irish land questions pressed on British politics throughout the later career, and Irish agriculture displayed in miniature the dynamics of landlordism, tenure insecurity, soil management, and emigration driven partly by exhausted prospects on the land. Russian questions arrived through correspondence and through the enormous statistical literature of that empire, which Marx read in Russian during his final decade. Indian questions, posed sharply in the journalism of 1853, returned in notebooks on communal landholding and village agriculture. A thinker whose early system assigned the countryside a largely passive role found his desk covered, decade after decade, with evidence about land. Evidence accumulates effects even on strong theorists, and the archive suggests accumulation rather than epiphany as the mechanism of change.

## The blind spot argument at full strength

Sentences can be rescued one by one forever. Structural criticism aims deeper, at the concepts sentences are built from, and the most formidable version of that criticism must now be stated without dilution, attributed precisely, and answered only after its force is fully felt. Ted Benton advanced the argument in an essay of 1989, "Marxism and Natural Limits: An Ecological Critique and Reconstruction", published in New Left Review. Benton wrote as a sympathetic insider, a Marxist sociologist attempting reconstruction rather than demolition, which makes his concessions impossible to dismiss as hostile caricature. His target was the labour process concept at the heart of historical materialism, and his claim was that the concept, as standardly formulated, cannot register natural limits because of how it defines production itself.

### Where does Benton aim the value charge?

Benton's related charge holds that a theory defining value through labour alone renders nature's contribution analytically invisible, so damage to natural conditions registers only when it raises labour costs, which is a blindness built into the accounting rather than an oversight by individual economists.

State the core argument slowly. Historical materialism defines the labour process as purposeful human activity transforming natural material into useful form, with nature entering as object worked upon and instrument worked with. Inside that definition, nature has no independent dynamics of its own: materials do not resist, regenerate, or fail according to laws the producer must respect, because the concept captures an intentional agent acting on a passive world. Processes that are not labour like in character, such as ecological succession, climate patterns, disease ecologies, or the slow chemistry of soil formation, fall outside the frame or enter it only as background conditions. Consequently the theory possesses no internal standpoint from which a natural limit could be recognised as a limit. Damage appears, if at all, as rising cost or scarce input, never as violation of a condition possessing its own integrity. On Benton's reading, passages where Marx laments soil exhaustion or urges trusteeship are moral observations attached to the theory from outside, sentiments a humane writer happened to hold, rather than conclusions the theory generates. Strip the sentiments away and the machinery runs unchanged, optimising transformation of a world conceived as material and instrument. That is the blind spot case at its most formidable: not that Marx disliked nature, not that he never noticed damage, but that his central concept leaves no room for the noticing to become theory.

Supporting evidence for Benton's reading is not trivial. Definitions of the labour process in Capital do foreground intention, tools, and material in exactly the triadic form he describes. The famous comparison between a bee and an architect praises the human producer for raising a structure in imagination before building it, centring purposeful consciousness as the differentia of human production. Animal activity appears as instinctive by contrast. A philosophy of production built on that contrast can struggle to grant non human processes standing beyond their usefulness or resistance to projects. Benton also presses the ecoregulatory practices point: many forms of work, from managing fisheries to maintaining irrigation commons, aim not at transforming material into new form but at sustaining conditions under which natural processes continue. Such practices fit awkwardly inside a concept designed around transformation, and their awkwardness, for Benton, exposes the concept's limits rather than the practices' marginality.

Reconstruction was Benton's aim, and his proposed repairs reveal how deep he thought the problem ran. He urged a revised concept of work acknowledging intentional and non intentional processes together, a place for ecoregulatory labour inside production theory, and recognition that natural objects carry conditions of existence that production must respect as given structures rather than raw availability. Sympathetic critics accepted much of the diagnosis while disputing details. Hostile critics accused him of reading a schematic definition as though it exhausted a rich descriptive practice. Both responses confirm the essay's standing: nearly four decades of debate treat Benton 1989 as the objection any ecological defence must answer, much as a defendant must answer the strongest witness rather than the weakest. Accordingly, the reply developed later in this article addresses Benton directly, concedes what the texts force it to concede, and resists only where textual evidence genuinely resists.

Chronology gives Benton one more weapon worth acknowledging. If the ecological passages cluster late, perhaps they represent an ageing writer's private sentiments diverging from a published system that remained productivist in structure. The system, after all, was never revised to install limits at its centre: no new preface announces a corrected theory of production and nature, no treatise reorganises the categories around metabolic constraint. Defenders reply that volume three's rent analysis and the metabolism chapters do systematic work inside the system rather than decorating it, and that an author's system includes his mature analyses, not only his manifestos of method from 1859. That reply has force. Still, Benton's challenge retains a residue no quotation dissolves: even granting every ecological passage maximal weight, the corpus contains analyses of particular ruptures without a general theory of natural limits. The residue matters, and the verdict section will carry it openly rather than burying it.

## The mediation argument and nature without standing

An older structural criticism predates Benton by a generation and cuts from a different angle. Alfred Schmidt published The Concept of Nature in Marx in 1962, a study begun under the influence of the Frankfurt School, arguing that nature in Marx's thought exists only as mediated through social labour. On Schmidt's reading, Marx broke with any notion of nature in itself available to contemplation: human beings encounter nature through the historically specific forms of their production, so what counts as a resource, an obstacle, or a landscape is always socially constituted. Schmidt presented that mediation thesis as sophistication rather than failing, a materialism refusing both crude naturalism and idealist fantasy. Later ecological readers drew a darker conclusion from the same thesis. If nature has no standing except as mediated, then nothing in nature can set terms to society from outside society's own categories, and appeals to natural limits become disguised social claims wearing green dress.

### Why does noticing damage fall short of a system?

Passages prove that their author noticed, analysed, and sometimes condemned specific disruptions of natural conditions, while a systematic ecology would require generalised concepts of limit, regeneration, and standing, integrated across the theory, which the passages gesture toward without assembling into a finished doctrine.

Schmidt's argument gains strength from Marx's polemics against contemplative materialism. The theses on Feuerbach, drafted in 1845, criticise earlier materialism for conceiving reality as something contemplated rather than as human sensuous activity, and the German Ideology manuscripts extend that criticism across a theory of history. Nature prior to human history receives famously brisk treatment in those manuscripts, which remark that such untouched nature barely exists anywhere except perhaps on a few newly formed islands. A reader building on those texts can portray the entire corpus as anthropocentric in architecture: nature matters as the medium, condition, and product of human activity, never as a subject with claims of its own. Green political theory in the late twentieth century often demanded precisely such claims, drawing on deep ecology or on rights of nature jurisprudence. Measured against those demands, Marx will always disappoint, and Schmidt explains why the disappointment is structural rather than accidental.

Yet Schmidt himself resisted simple productivist conclusions, and fairness requires noting the resistance. His study emphasised that Marx's materialism preserved a moment of nature's independence: labour works on material whose properties are not created by labour, and those properties answer back, constraining what projects can achieve. Mediation, for Schmidt, was a two way relation in which society shapes its encounter with nature while nature's own constitution shapes what encounters are possible. Later popularisations flattened that subtlety into a slogan about nature being socially constructed, a slogan Schmidt's text does not quite license. Intellectual history is full of such flattenings, where a careful thesis becomes a banner. Students encountering the mediation argument should therefore read it in its 1962 form, or in reliable summaries, rather than in the simplified version circulating in polemics.

Placed beside Benton, Schmidt completes a pincer. Benton argues the labour process concept cannot see limits. Schmidt argues the epistemology cannot grant nature independent standing from which limits could speak. Either argument alone might be answered by diligent quotation. Together they predict exactly the pattern the archive displays: a writer who repeatedly notices damage, analyses specific ruptures brilliantly, and never erects the general theory his observations seem to invite, because his conceptual furniture lacks the pieces such a theory would need. Any verdict that ignores the pincer and rests on quotation counting has not engaged the serious criticism. Equally, any verdict treating the pincer as decisive without testing it against the correction of 1875, the metabolic analysis, and the trusteeship passage has not engaged the serious defence. The next section stages that test.

Practical stakes hide inside the scholasticism. If Schmidt's argument holds in its strong form, then ecological Marxism must import its normative core from elsewhere, from an ethics of nature developed outside historical materialism, and the resulting synthesis is an alliance rather than a recovery. If Benton's version holds in its strong form, the labour process concept needs reconstruction before any alliance can even be described. If both are only partly right, the tradition's own resources might support extension without wholesale import or reconstruction. Those are genuinely different research programmes, with different reading lists and different politics, which is why a debate about a nineteenth century corpus continues to organise conferences. Antiquarianism it is not.

Historians add a caution about Schmidt's context. Writing in 1962, within a German philosophical culture dominated by questions about nature and technology after a catastrophic war, Schmidt read Marx through concerns of that milieu. Benton wrote in 1989, as green politics entered European parliaments and socialist parties searched for responses. Context does not invalidate arguments; arguments stand on evidence and logic. Context does explain which questions get asked with urgency in which decades, and why a corpus can sleep for generations before a generation arrives armed with the question that wakes it. The ecological Marx of the late twentieth century was partly discovered and partly summoned, and distinguishing discovery from summons is the task the recovery assessment below takes up.

## The value theoretic reply and its limits

Paul Burkett constructed the most systematic reply to the value objection in Marx and Nature, published in 1999, with related arguments developed alongside John Bellamy Foster's parallel work. Burkett's central move turns the accusation inside out. Value, he argued, is a specifically capitalist social form: the reduction of concrete activities and materials to abstract labour time for exchange. Nature's exclusion from value measurement is therefore not Marx's oversight but Marx's diagnosis. Capital itself treats free natural gifts as worthless until labour and property make them saleable, and an economics registering that treatment is describing the blindness of its object, not sharing it. A thermometer does not endorse fever. On this reply, the Gotha correction becomes pivotal evidence: its author explicitly separates wealth, where nature counts fully, from value, where capital refuses to count it, and builds the critique of capitalism partly on that refusal.

### How should each exhibit be weighed on its own?

By asking of each exhibit what it can bear: passages establish attention and analysis, the metabolic category establishes conceptual room, the trusteeship passage establishes obligation, while no exhibit establishes a completed theory of limits, so the grade is genuine extension begun, not possession of a finished ecology.

Burkett's reply handles the value charge effectively, and even critics who reject his broader recovery often concede the narrow point. Where the reply weakens is at Benton's door rather than Schmidt's or the value theorists'. Showing that value blindness belongs to capital does not by itself supply the general concept of natural limit whose absence Benton laments. Burkett and Foster answer that metabolism supplies the missing architecture: production is defined in Capital not only as purposeful transformation but as material exchange between humanity and nature, an exchange whose conditions can be disrupted, whose disruption the text analyses for soil, and whose rational regulation the text names as a task for associated producers. Regulation language matters. A famous passage in volume three imagines producers governing their material interchange with nature in common, consciously, rather than suffering it as a blind power. Whatever else that sentence does, it places management of material exchange, not maximisation of throughput, at the horizon of a free society. Benton's counter is that regulation talk remains anthropocentric management talk, control by another name. Foster's counter counter is that control exercised with knowledge of material cycles differs categorically from a drive to accumulate that cannot register cycles at all. Readers must judge that exchange for themselves; the article's role is to ensure the exchange occurs at full strength on both sides.

Concession time, stated plainly. The framework as its author left it contains no theory of natural limits. No passage derives from the value form a maximum sustainable throughput, a doctrine of finite stocks, or a principle for adjudicating between present consumption and future need beyond the trusteeship ethic. The realm of freedom passage complicates growth assumptions by locating freedom beyond necessary labour and imagining shortened working time, yet it also speaks of a realm of necessity persisting and expanding with human needs. Whether expanding needs can be squared with finite conditions is a problem the corpus poses more sharply than it solves. Ecological Marxists after Marx have worked on that problem with tools from thermodynamics, systems ecology, and political theory, which is honourable continuation of a research programme. Claiming the finished solution sat in the manuscripts all along is another matter.

A distinction between incompleteness and contradiction does heavy lifting here. An incomplete theory invites extension: the metabolic category can host analyses of fisheries, forests, and atmospheric chemistry without violence to its logic, and later writers have performed such extensions with varying success. A contradictory theory would forbid extension, containing premises that any ecological conclusion violates. Productivist critics sometimes argue the stronger forbidding claim, resting it on growth commitments in the vision of communism. Yet the textual basis for necessity there is thinner than Benton's structural case, resting heavily on inferences drawn from abundance phrasing inside a brief programme critique. A careful verdict therefore separates three grades: what the framework forbids, which is little; what it supplies, which is substantial but partial; and what it lacks, which is a limits doctrine. Extension occupies the middle grade. That placement is the seed of the thesis named throughout this article, and it survives, in the author's judgement, every objection staged so far.

Sceptics might ask whether extension language is evasive, a diplomatic formula letting everyone leave with partial honours. Diplomacy and precision can coincide. Consider an analogy from another discipline. Darwin's framework contained no genetics, and Mendel's results initially seemed to many readers to contradict selection theory, yet the eventual synthesis extended Darwinism genetically without possessing a finished genetics inside the Origin of Species. Saying Darwin began an extension he did not complete is neither praise nor evasion; it is accurate grading of evidence. The Marx case differs in detail, obviously, since social theory lacks genetics' experimental discipline, but the logical shape of the grading is the same. Possession claims and impossibility claims both fail; extension claims fit the surviving evidence. Preston of rhetoric aside, fit is the criterion scholarship is supposed to use.

## The notebooks and the changing archive

Evidence in this dispute has a peculiar property: the archive itself kept growing after every verdict had been pronounced. Marx left thousands of pages of notebooks, excerpting books and articles across natural science, technology, agriculture, and much else. For generations those notebooks sat largely unread, mined for biography rather than argument. Their gradual publication, above all through the historical critical edition known as MEGA, the Marx Engels Gesamtausgabe, altered what interpreters could responsibly claim. A guide to that edition and its manuscript riches appears at [mega2 marx manuscripts guide](/2013/07/01/mega2-marx-manuscripts-guide/), and researchers working on the ecological question now treat notebook evidence as indispensable rather than decorative. Verdicts written before the notebooks were studied, including influential mid century dismissals, rest on a smaller archive than the one available to scholarship since.

### What can private notebooks prove as evidence?

Sustained, decades long engagement with agricultural chemistry, geology, botany, and soil science, including extracts from Liebig and many lesser known writers, marginal judgements, and evidence that research on land and materials intensified during the final period rather than fading with age or illness.

Notebooks require careful handling as evidence. Excerpting a passage proves reading, not agreement. Marx copied material he intended to refute, material he found useful, and material whose relevance he had not yet decided. Notebook evidence therefore works cumulatively and comparatively: volume, duration, and pattern across subjects testify to preoccupation, while particular extracts gain meaning only beside published argument. The pattern here is hard to wave away. From the eighteen sixties onward, extracts cluster around soil fertility, fertilisers, deforestation, irrigation, climate in relation to agriculture, and the chemistry of plant nutrition. Those are the research materials of a man working on the natural conditions of production, and they align with the published analyses of metabolism and rent rather than floating free of them. Engagement of that depth also reframes the silence argument. Critics once asked why, if Marx cared about soil, he published so little on it. The notebooks answer that he was studying the subject continuously while publishing selectively, as scholars do when a magnum opus consumes their working hours.

Kohei Saito's more recent scholarship pushed notebook interpretation further, arguing that the late Marx moved toward a position critical of growth itself, partly through those scientific studies and partly through research on communal forms in Russia and elsewhere. Saito's reading, presented in a study first published in Japanese in 2016 and in English as Marx in the Anthropocene several years later, galvanised debate precisely because it claims development beyond what most recovery writers had claimed: not only ecological analysis within Capital, but an incipient break with productivism in the final years. Critics respond that fragmentary drafts addressed to a correspondent's question cannot bear the weight of a conversion narrative, and that the same notebooks contain plenty of conventional productivist assumption. Supporters respond that conversion narratives always look fragmentary from inside a life cut short by illness and death. The dispute remains open, and its openness itself instructs: the archive is alive, editions continue, and any verdict pretending the evidence closed in 1989 or 2000 mistakes a snapshot for a terminus.

Archival growth also cuts against ecological advocates in one respect they rarely mention. Fuller publication exposes the whole man, including passages of period prejudice, impatience with romantic conservation, and enthusiasm for particular engineering projects that sit awkwardly beside green sainthood. Selected quotation built the ecological Marx of popular imagination; complete archives complicate saints of every colour. Maturation of a field can be measured by comfort with complication. By that measure the field has matured considerably since the first recovery manifestos, with leading participants now debating degrees, mechanisms, and dates instead of trading proof texts. Textbook treatments have absorbed the change unevenly, which is one reason the exam guidance in the persona sections below stresses graded claims over slogans.

Dating the notebooks precisely matters for one more reason. Some ecological material once attributed to the final years turns out, on editorial investigation, to belong earlier, while other material shifts later. Chronology arguments are only as strong as their dates, and dates in a posthumous archive are scholarly constructions resting on handwriting, paper, correspondence, and cross reference. Honest use of chronology therefore carries error bars. The broad gradient survives plausible redating, since treatise evidence anchors both ends: the Manifesto indisputably belongs to 1848 and the Gotha critique indisputably belongs to 1875. Arguments should lean on anchored texts for load bearing claims and use notebook chronology as corroboration, a hierarchy the table in this article was built to respect.

## The recovery project and the problem of overclaiming

Between the mid nineteen nineties and the opening years of the following century, ecological readings moved from scattered essays to an organised recovery project, associated chiefly with Burkett's Marx and Nature in 1999 and Foster's Marx's Ecology in 2000, surrounded by journal special issues, conferences, and a generation of doctoral work. Achievements of that project are substantial and should be recorded without grudging. It recovered the metabolic category from obscurity and returned it to the centre of Marx interpretation. It demonstrated, against lazy consensus, that soil and fertility occupied serious space in the mature economics. It connected Marx's reading in natural science to his published categories, replacing rumour with bibliography. It forced critics to engage texts rather than stereotypes, raising the standard of the whole dispute. Article-length treatment of Foster's version lives elsewhere in this series; the concern here is narrower and concerns the project's rhetoric at its edges.

Overclaiming is a real problem, not a minor caveat, and this article treats it as such. At the project's popular edge, recovery slid into canonisation: Marx became a prophet of sustainability, the metabolic rift became a master key for every environmental ill, and passages were recruited as anticipations of concepts developed a century later by sciences Marx never encountered. Such inflation damages credibility in two directions. Scholars outside the project, encountering prophetic claims, discount the genuine textual findings bundled with them. Students inside the project, taught that everything is already in Marx, stop reading the ecological sciences and the critics, arriving at examinations and debates armed with devotion instead of arguments. Inflation also falsifies history. A nineteenth century writer working with Liebig's chemistry deserves credit for what he did with the science available to him. Crediting him with insights belonging to later soil ecology, atmospheric science, or thermodynamics steals credit from the scientists who actually produced those insights, many of them working in traditions indifferent or hostile to Marxism.

### Where does recovery rhetoric outrun its evidence?

In its popular form, frequently yes: assertions that Marx possessed a complete ecology, anticipated later environmental science, or solved the problem of limits exceed his texts, even though the underlying recovery of metabolic analysis and trusteeship stands on firm manuscript and publication evidence.

Measure the overclaim precisely. Three assertions circulate widely and each fails its test. First, that Marx was an environmentalist in the modern sense: environmentalism is a political identity formed around movements, parties, and sciences of the twentieth century, and projecting it backward produces anachronism even when the projected figure wrote perceptively about soil. Second, that the rift concept explains environmental degradation generally: the concept was built for nutrient cycles between town and country, and its extension to other disruptions is later theoretical work requiring defence case by case, not a birthright. Third, that ecological crisis follows from the value form with logical necessity in a way its author demonstrated: he demonstrated tendencies toward material disruption under determinate conditions, which is strong enough, and necessity claims beyond the demonstration borrow certainty the argument never earned. Each failed assertion began as a defensible claim swollen by repetition. Rhetorical swelling is how research programmes rot from inside while appearing to triumph.

Critics of the recovery project deserve their paragraph at full strength too. Jason Moore, architect of the world ecology alternative, charges Foster and Burkett with dualism: retaining a Cartesian split between society and nature, then celebrating Marx for bridging a gap his categories created. On Moore's alternative, capitalism should be understood as a way of organising nature itself, a world ecology in which the society nature binary dissolves. Foster's camp replies that metabolism requires distinguishable relata, since exchange between identical terms is meaningless, and that Moore's monism dissolves the analytical traction that makes explanation possible. Andreas Malm, from another direction, faults recovery writing for softness toward its hero, arguing that the productivist and anthropocentric commitments run too deep for the textual rescue attempted, and that climate politics needs clearer breaks with nineteenth century inheritances. Malm's own historical work on fossil capital nonetheless uses Marxist categories extensively, illustrating how criticism inside the tradition rarely maps onto the yes or no of popular debate. A field generating internal critics of that calibre is healthy. A field whose popularisers ignore them is not.

Proportionality completes the assessment. Overclaiming at the edges does not void the centre, any more than Benton's structural objection voids the trusteeship passage. Grades accumulate: recovery of the metabolic analysis, established; recovery of an intergenerational ethic, established; demonstration of a finished ecological theory, not established; demonstration that extension contradicts the framework, also not established. Readers keeping those four grades separate can navigate most of the literature without a guide. Readers collapsing them into a verdict about whether Marx was green will be buffeted by every new publication, since each new article shifts the collapsed verdict while leaving the graded findings untouched. Stability lives at the level of grades.

Editorial history adds a final caution about recovery rhetoric. Because Engels shaped volumes two and three of Capital from drafts, every argument resting on those volumes should acknowledge the mediation, as this article did for the trusteeship passage. Recovery writers generally do acknowledge it. Popular transmission strips acknowledgements away, and a passage mediated by an editor's arrangement becomes, in magazine prose, the master's own thunderbolt. The remedy is boring and effective: cite volume, chapter, and where possible manuscript status, and prefer claims anchored in texts the author published himself. Boredom is underrated as a scholarly virtue. Most of the errors catalogued in the misreadings section below would have been prevented by scholars and journalists willing to be slightly more boring about provenance.

## Misreadings that distort the dispute

Five errors recur so reliably that naming them together may save readers years. The first error treats Marx as straightforwardly green, assembling late passages into a portrait of a sustainability prophet and discarding the rest. The second error, mirror image of the first, treats him as straightforwardly productivist, assembling early celebrations and discarding the rest. Both errors share a method: verdict first, anthology after. Any method that would produce the same verdict regardless of what the archive contained is not a method but a mood.

The third error promotes the Manifesto into the settled view of a forty year career. Manifestos are beginnings, and this one's authors were not yet thirty when they wrote it. No other thinker is routinely judged by a pamphlet of their twenties over treatises of their fifties, and the exemption granted to Marx in this respect reveals how polemical the judging usually is. The fourth error, already examined, recasts the trusteeship passage as editorial invention, a claim about provenance that dissolves under manuscript scholarship and survives only where it is needed. The fifth error infers from any answer about Marx's texts an answer about whether Marxism can handle ecological limits as a living theory. Textual recovery and theoretical adequacy are different properties. A tradition might inherit ecological passages and still fail before the abundance problem; equally, a tradition might inherit none and build an adequate ecology from other materials. Confusing inheritance with adequacy flatters genealogists and misleads everyone else.

### What marks the line between extension and possession?

Evidence and honesty about degree: extension points to categories that accommodate ecological analysis and to late work beginning that analysis, whereas possession claims a completed doctrine of nature and limits, a claim contradicted by the absence of any treatise, system, or limits principle in the corpus.

Underneath those five errors sits a sixth, methodological and rarely confessed: treating quotation as the only evidence. Quotations matter, and this article has quoted sparingly and deliberately, under a discipline of short fragments from real published works. But the strongest evidence in intellectual history often lies in structure: which categories a system cannot operate without, which problems its research programme keeps returning to, which corrections an author issues when followers misstate the doctrine. The Gotha correction is a quotation, but its force comes from structure: a founder policing the boundary between wealth and value against his own movement's slogan. The metabolic chapters persuade through architecture as much as through sentences. Readers trained to hunt quotations will keep winning anthology contests and losing the argument, because the argument lives at the level where Benton pitched his objection and where any serious reply must live as well.

Teaching exposes every one of these errors in concentrated form. Students arrive having absorbed a portrait from social media, from a hostile textbook, or from an admiring one, and the portraits are always simpler than the archive. Correcting simplicity takes longer than installing it, which tempts teachers toward counter simplicity: swapping the green saint for the Promethean villain or the reverse. Resistance to that temptation defines good teaching on this topic, and the persona section for teachers below returns to the point with a concrete classroom device, the trusteeship passage read aloud against the Manifesto passage, dates announced, silence allowed to do its work. Students trusted with genuine tension remember it. Students handed a replacement slogan remember the slogan until the next slogan arrives.

## Close reading the prosecution exhibits

Anthologies quote the Manifesto's wonders list as a single breathless rush, which flatters the prosecution by hiding the pamphlet's own dialectical frame. Read in place, the celebration of bourgeois achievement sits inside an argument that the class performing those wonders simultaneously creates the conditions of its own supersession and inflicts documented misery on workers, colonised peoples, and the countryside it drags into its orbit. Praise and indictment alternate by paragraph. None of that converts the wonders list into ecological writing; subjection language remains subjection language. Context does, however, block a lazy inference from rhetorical admiration to theoretical commitment. The pamphlet admires productive dynamism as a historical force while condemning the social order harnessing it, a distinction its authors would maintain, in changing vocabulary, for the rest of their lives. What changes across the career is the analysis of the dynamism's material costs. A full reading route through the pamphlet is set out in the [communist manifesto readers guide](/2011/10/15/communist-manifesto-readers-guide/). In 1848 those costs are counted mainly in human suffering. By 1867 they are counted also in soil.

The Grundrisse requires similar care. Those manuscripts were working drafts, the laboratory in which categories for Capital were forged, and laboratory notebooks in any discipline contain hypotheses their authors later discard. Within them, capital's tendency to dissolve natural barriers appears as capital's tendency, described by an analyst reconstructing the system's inner drive, not as a programme the analyst recommends for adoption. Description of a drive and endorsement of a drive are different speech acts, and conflating them would convict every diagnostician of approval. Yet the prosecution retains a point even after the distinction is granted. Nowhere in those manuscripts does the analyst pause over the drive's collision with finite material conditions, or ask whether barrier dissolution might encounter limits that are not barriers of custom but properties of the world. Silence in a laboratory notebook proves less than silence in a published system, but a pattern of silences across genres is evidence of where attention was not yet directed. The honest summary for the Grundrisse is double: no endorsement of despoliation, and no ecological counterweight either.

Correspondence supplies the prosecution with smaller exhibits of genuine value. Letters between Marx and Engels trade enthusiasms for new technologies, impatience with sentimental nature worship, and the period's casual confidence that swamps exist to be drained and deserts to be irrigated. Private letters reveal temperament, and temperament is part of intellectual biography. A writer privately allergic to pastoral romanticism was unlikely to build a politics on nature's inviolability, and green hagiography that ignores the letters builds on sand. Equally, letters are where people perform for intimates, exaggerate for comic effect, and vent positions they would qualify in print. Weight them as temperament evidence, not doctrine evidence, and they support a modest conclusion: the ecological Marx of the later work was an analyst of material cycles, not a nature mystic, and nobody should have expected otherwise.

## Close reading the defence exhibits

Scrutiny must cut the other way with equal edge. Take the soil argument in the first volume of Capital, the passage where capitalist production is said to disturb the metabolic interaction between human beings and the earth, preventing the return of constituent elements consumed in the form of food and clothing. Popular quotation often stops there, as though a striking metaphor had been unearthed. The surrounding argument is more specific and more interesting. Large scale industry concentrates populations in cities. Concentration separates consumption from production geographically. Nutrients embodied in food and fibre travel one way, from field to town, while the wastes of town life pollute rivers instead of replenishing fields. Soil fertility falls where the nutrients originated; artificial fertilisers and imported guano enter as compensations that displace rather than repair the rupture. Every element of that chain is empirical, drawn from the agricultural literature of the period, and every element identifies a mechanism rather than a mood. Mechanism is what distinguishes analysis from lament, and the passage clears the bar comfortably.

Agricultural detail elsewhere in the same volume reinforces the point. Discussion of the Irish case links landlord tenure, insecure occupancy, and soil management, arguing that arrangements discouraging tenant improvement degrade land as surely as they impoverish tenants. Discussion of machinery and modern industry treats the factory and the farm as one system of interlocking pressures. Discussion of the working day, though centred on human bodies, displays a characteristic mode of reasoning: a drive internal to capital pushes against a natural condition, here the physiology of the worker, until law and struggle impose a counter limit. Readers who notice the pattern may fairly ask whether the same reasoning applied to soil in the agriculture chapters amounts to a general logic of natural limits encountered through specific cases. The text never announces that generalisation. Benton's objection lives precisely in that gap between pattern and announcement. Foster's defence lives in the pattern. Reasonable verdicts must acknowledge both addresses.

Volume three widens the evidential base beyond the famous trusteeship paragraph. Rent theory requires sustained attention to differences in soil fertility, location, and the effects of investment on land of differing quality. Differential rent, in Marx's handling, is not a bookkeeping fiction but a mechanism rooted in the stubborn fact that land varies and cannot be manufactured to order. A theory of rent is, among other things, an admission that production confronts natural conditions it did not create and cannot wish away: the least fertile soil in cultivation sets terms for the whole market. Critics respond that rent theory treats nature as a source of differential advantage rather than as a system with its own dynamics, which is accurate as far as it goes. Accuracy in both directions again yields a graded picture. The mature economics is saturated with natural conditions at the level of mechanism, while remaining innocent of ecology at the level of system. Saturation without system is an unusual combination, and naming it precisely is more informative than either camp's banner.

Engels complicates the defence in ways requiring separation. Works published under Engels's name, notably the unfinished Dialectics of Nature manuscripts and the popular writings on Feuerbach and the end of classical philosophy, contain explicit warnings about human mastery over nature and the revenge nature takes on unconsidered interventions, illustrated with deforested hillsides and their consequences. Those passages are real, quotable, and frequently smuggled into arguments about Marx. Smuggling must be resisted in both directions. Engels was a distinct thinker with his own philosophical projects, and crediting his sentences to his collaborator repeats the carelessness that textual scholarship exists to correct. At the same time, the two men corresponded constantly, read each other's drafts, and divided intellectual labour by agreement, so Engels's ecological reflections are evidence about the milieu and the partnership, if not about the specific corpus under examination. Articles in this series on other topics observe the same discipline: collaboration explains influence, never identity.

One more defence exhibit, minor in size and major in implication, sits inside the 1875 programme critique beyond its famous opening. The document imagines a society in which the springs of cooperative wealth flow more abundantly, a phrase critics quote as abundance doctrine. Read whole, the same document insists on deductions from the social product for replacement, insurance against accidents, and provision for those unable to work, before any individual distribution occurs. Prudential deduction is not ecology, but its presence complicates the cartoon of a tradition dreaming of limitless plenty without reserves. The abundance question returns in the dedicated limits article of this cluster, where it belongs; here it is enough to register that the text most often mined for productivist slogans also contains the corpus's clearest correction of labour's supposed sole authorship of wealth. Single documents, like single authors, refuse to sit still for portraits.

## Period science and the duty of historical care

Judging nineteenth century writers by twenty first century science is the cheapest trick in intellectual history, and the scientific claims policy governing this cluster forbids it here. Marx worked with the chemistry, geology, and physiology available between the eighteen forties and the eighteen eighties. That science was formidable in places: thermodynamics was young, cell theory recent, Darwin's Origin of Species appeared in 1859 to a reception Marx followed with interest and reservations, and agricultural chemistry under Liebig had transformed understanding of plant nutrition within a generation. It was also a science without ecology as a named discipline; the term itself was coined by Ernst Haeckel in 1866 and the field's institutions belonged to the next century. Expecting a writer of that period to deploy ecosystem concepts is like expecting him to cite journals founded after his funeral. The interesting historical question runs the other way: given the instruments available, how far did he get, and where exactly did the instruments' limits become his limits?

Liebig repays a closer look because his influence is documented in Marx's own reading and correspondence. The German chemist's agricultural chemistry argued that plants draw specific mineral constituents from soil, that harvests export those constituents, and that farming which exports without returning them robs the land in a bookkeeping sense that chemistry can audit. Liebig aimed polemics at English high farming and at sewage systems wasting urban waste that ought to return to fields. Marx absorbed the nutrient bookkeeping, the robbery vocabulary, and the urban rural framing, then embedded them inside a critique of property relations and market compulsion that Liebig, a man of very different politics, never attempted. Intellectual debts of that kind are the normal mechanics of thought: a category migrates between frameworks and changes meaning in transit. Metabolism in the chemist's laboratory named processes inside organisms. Metabolism in the critique of political economy names the exchange between a society and its environment, organised through labour. Same word, enlarged referent, new theoretical work. Scholarship that traces the migration teaches more than scholarship that merely celebrates or debunks the destination.

Period care also disciplines judgements about Darwin. Marx's engagement with evolutionary theory was genuine but ambivalent, admiring the demolition of teleology while resisting Malthusian political economy dressed in biological clothing. Green interpreters sometimes recruit Darwin to certify Marx's naturalism; hostile interpreters recruit the ambivalence to certify indifference to natural science. Both recruitments fail the archive. The notebooks and letters show a reader following debates in geology, physiology, and anthropology with sustained appetite into his final years. Appetite is not doctrine, but a corpus produced by that appetite cannot be described as sealed against the natural world, and descriptions claiming otherwise should be retired.

What period care forbids in the other direction is equally important. No passage in Marx measures atmospheric composition, projects climate trajectories, or quantifies resource stocks in ways a modern reader could cite as findings. The cluster's policy bars this article from asserting any contemporary environmental condition in any case, and textual honesty reaches the same destination independently: the corpus speaks about soil, forests, and material cycles in the vocabulary of its century. Claims that its author anticipated specific later discoveries dissolve on inspection into vague resemblance, the scholarly equivalent of finding faces in clouds. Resemblance hunting flatters admirers and embarrasses the admired. Marx's actual achievement with period instruments, analysing how a mode of production organises material exchange and where that organisation ruptures, is substantial enough to survive without borrowed laurels.

## Why the ecological Marx stayed invisible for a century

Reception history explains a puzzle the textual evidence raises. If metabolic analysis sits in the most studied economics book of the modern age, why did generations of readers, including devoted Marxists, fail to see it? Answers cannot appeal to the twentieth century record as evidence about the texts, and this section does not: it examines reading habits, institutions, and editorial fortunes, which are facts about readers rather than about manuscripts. The distinction matters because the cluster's neutrality discipline forbids settling textual questions with political history, while leaving reception history perfectly legitimate as an account of why a question was asked late.

Several mechanisms operated together. First, the productivist self understanding of official Marxism in the Soviet sphere made ecological passages illegible to the tradition's largest institutional reader: a doctrine committed to catching up with and surpassing capitalist output had no use for soil robbery arguments, and its textbooks canonised the 1859 Preface while shelving the rent chapters. Second, Western academic Marxology long privileged the philosophical manuscripts of 1844 and questions of alienation and humanism, a framing in which nature appears as scenery for human self realisation. Third, the notebooks and even volume three in reliable form circulated far less widely than the Manifesto and the first volume, so the archive's ecological centre of gravity sat in its least read neighbourhoods. Fourth, environmental politics itself arrived as an organised force only in the nineteen sixties and seventies, and its early theorists often defined themselves against a Marxism they knew through its official productivist caricature. A question needs a constituency before it gets an answer, and the constituency for asking whether Marx was an ecological thinker assembled remarkably late.

Schmidt's 1962 study stands out against that background as an early exception, which is one reason its argument retains influence: it asked about nature in Marx when few others were asking, even if its mediation thesis later served conclusions Schmidt himself would not have drawn. Benton's 1989 essay marks the moment environmental questions entered Marxist theory through the front door of a leading journal. The recovery scholarship of the following decade then performed the archival labour. Sequence matters for fairness. Judging earlier generations for missing what their institutions made invisible is satisfying and cheap. The more useful lesson concerns the present: every generation's Marx is partly a function of the questions its institutions reward, including this generation's ecological one. Humility about that function is the beginning of method, and it applies to the verdict of this article as much as to any rival.

## Objections exchanged at full strength

Staging the debate as an exchange, objection by objection, clarifies which points survive contact and which dissolve. What follows gives each major objection its best formulation, then the best available reply, then a grading. Gradings are the article's own judgements and are labelled as such by their placement after both sides have spoken. Readers who disagree with a grading have been given the materials to construct the disagreement, which is the most an even handed treatment can offer.

Objection one: the corpus celebrates domination. Manifesto language about subjection, Grundrisse language about dissolved barriers, and a philosophy of history powered by expanding productive forces together compose an outlook fairly named Promethean. Reply: those exhibits are early or descriptive, and the mature work analyses costs the early work ignored. Grading: the objection succeeds against the young Marx and against any claim of lifelong ecological consistency. Against the mature corpus it establishes tension, not identity. A tradition can house a Promethean strand and an ecological strand simultaneously, and this one demonstrably does.

Objection two: the concepts exclude nature. Value counts labour alone; the labour process defines nature as material and instrument; mediation denies nature unmediated standing. Benton and Schmidt, in their different idioms, converge on structural blindness. Reply: wealth and value are distinguished by the author himself; metabolism names exchange with a nature that answers back; rent theory rests on natural differences production cannot abolish. Grading: the charge lands against any claim of a completed ecological theory and against careless talk of Marx's economics as inherently green. It fails against the narrower claim that the framework can register disruption and obligation, because the mature texts do register both, repeatedly, in load bearing chapters rather than footnotes. Structural blindness would predict absence. Presence, even unsystematic presence, refutes the strongest form while leaving a weaker form, structural incompleteness, standing. Much of the literature's confusion consists in sliding between those forms mid paragraph.

Objection three: the vision of the future presupposes abundance. A society distributing according to need, with the springs of wealth flowing abundantly, seems to require the very throughput expansion that finite conditions forbid. Reply: the abundance phrasing of that short critique is relative and rhetorical, deductions for reserves precede distribution in the same document, and the realm of freedom is located in reduced necessary labour rather than in limitless goods. Grading: the objection identifies the corpus's least developed region. Replies soften it without dissolving it, because no text reconciles expanding needs with finite conditions in a principled way. Ecological Marxists have treated that reconciliation as their own unfinished assignment ever since, which is an honest posture, and claims that the assignment was completed in 1875 should be greeted with requests for page numbers.

Objection four: the recovery is an invention. A generation needing a green Marx manufactured one from fragments, editorial constructions, and notebook trivia, projecting its politics onto a Victorian productivist. Reply: the central exhibits are published or securely manuscript based, dated, and argumentatively central in their contexts; invention cannot explain why the Gotha correction opens with nature, why rent theory needs trusteeship, or why soil analysis recurs across the mature economics. Grading: the objection succeeds against popular inflation and against conversion narratives built on slender drafts. Against the graded core of the recovery, chronology plus mechanism plus obligation, it fails. Invention hypotheses must explain evidence, not merely sneer at motives, and the evidence here has footnotes.

Objection five: the whole question is anachronistic. Ecology as a science and environmentalism as a politics postdate the subject, so asking whether he was an ecological thinker commits a category error regardless of the answer. Reply: the question, carefully posed, asks about the presence and organisation of concepts concerning production's natural conditions, which is a legitimate historical inquiry applicable to any period; historians ask whether earlier writers held views on contagion before germ theory without confusion, provided the standards are announced. Grading: the objection usefully polices vocabulary, since ecological thinker must never be allowed to mean environmentalist activist in period dress. As a ban on the inquiry itself it fails, because the inquiry, standards announced, has produced genuine findings about chronology, categories, and gaps that no other framing had surfaced.

Running across all five exchanges is a pattern worth naming. Objections are strongest when aimed at inflated claims and weakest when aimed at graded ones. Replies are strongest when they concede territory and weakest when they defend every inch. That pattern is not a peculiarity of this topic; it is the signature of a mature controversy, in which the interesting positions have migrated to the middle ground of degree, mechanism, and date, while the banners at the extremes fly over increasingly empty camps. Examination boards have noticed the migration, which is why the strongest examination answers on this question, as the student persona section explains, are those that grade rather than cheer.

## Comparisons with contemporaries

Placing Marx among nineteenth century peers sharpens both his distinctiveness and his typicality, provided comparison stays textual and period bound. John Stuart Mill, in his Principles of Political Economy of 1848, famously entertained the stationary state with something close to approval, imagining a society that might improve the art of living once the race for wealth relaxed, and he wrote feelingly about solitude and unspoiled landscape. On explicit limits and on nature's independent value, Mill stands closer to later environmental sentiment than Marx ever does, a comparison green critics deploy fairly. Yet Mill possessed no mechanism of soil robbery, no analysis of town and country nutrient flows, and no account of a mode of production tearing its own material circuits. Where Mill offers sensibility plus a macroeconomic thought experiment, Marx offers mechanism minus sensibility. Which inheritance matters more depends on what a later thinker needs, and ecological theory since has needed both, usually importing one to repair the other.

William Stanley Jevons worried publicly about coal exhaustion in The Coal Question of 1865, calculating national consumption against reserves with an actuary's gloom. Marx read widely in such literature, and the contrast is instructive: Jevons framed exhaustion as a national accounting problem inviting prudence, while Marx's framework would ask how property relations and competitive compulsion govern extraction rates regardless of national prudence. Neither man built an ecology. Each saw a piece of the material problem through the instruments of his discipline. George Perkins Marsh, whose Man and Nature appeared in 1864, documented human damage to forests, soils, and waters on a global canvas and stands as the period's closest approach to an environmental writer in the modern sense. No evidence suggests Marx read Marsh, and the absence itself is informative: the nineteenth century contained ecological observation of the highest quality, circulating in English, during Marx's London decades, and his research programme intersected it only partially, through the agricultural chemists rather than through the conservationists. Partial intersection is the recurring verdict of this entire article, reached now from the direction of the bookshelf.

Comparison also disciplines the Promethean charge. Measured against booster literature of the industrial age, with its hymns to drained fens and conquered rivers, Marx's celebrations are conventional in vocabulary and subversive in intent, since the dynamism praised belongs to a class the same texts sentence to historical death. Measured against Marsh, the celebrations are tone deaf about damage. Both measurements are true, and a verdict incorporating both has no room left for the word straightforwardly in either of its popular uses. Historians call this the work of contextualisation: not excuse, not indictment, but placement precise enough that judgement, when it comes, lands on the actual figure rather than on a effigy assembled for the purpose.

## What a systematic ecology would have required

Counterfactual specification helps to price the gap between the corpus as it stands and the finished doctrine some advocates describe. A systematic ecology inside Marx's framework would need at least four components. A general category of natural limit, applicable across soil, forest, fishery, and atmosphere, stating how production encounters conditions that regeneration rates and finite stocks impose. A metric or at least a principled account connecting that category to the theory of value and accumulation, so that approaching a limit registers inside economic analysis rather than in sermons appended to it. An institutional sketch showing how associated producers would gather information about material cycles and enforce restraint against their own short term interests. Finally, a revised account of human need and its growth, reconciling the expansiveness of the realm of freedom with the finitude the first component asserts. Measured against that specification, the corpus supplies fragments toward the first component in the metabolism chapters, nothing systematic toward the second, gestures toward the third in the regulation passage of volume three, and an unresolved tension where the fourth should be. One partial component out of four, with gestures, is a precise way of stating the extension thesis in architectural language.

Later Marxists understood the assignment in roughly those terms, whether or not they itemised it. Twentieth and twenty first century writers have proposed entropy based amendments, throughput accounting, and planning mechanisms designed to supply the missing components, usually acknowledging the construction work as construction. That acknowledgement is the dividing line between scholarship continuing a programme and apologetics defending a mausoleum. The programme's founder left tools, some of them excellent, and a building site with foundations partly dug. Describing the site accurately honours the founder more than pretending the building was finished and the curtains drawn.

Specification also clarifies why Benton's essay endures. Benton essentially priced the same gap from the concept side, arguing that the labour process definition obstructs the first and third components by casting nature as passive material. Whether one accepts his pricing or Foster's lower estimate, both scholars agree on the existence of construction work, differing over how much of the eventual structure the foundations already determine. Outsiders to the debate often miss that agreement entirely, imagining a war between believers and deniers when the actual dispute is a quantity surveying argument among builders. Quantity surveying is less thrilling than war. It is also how knowledge advances.

## The strongest defensible position

Gather the graded findings. Two bodies of text exist, unevenly distributed across a career, the productivist body concentrated in the eighteen forties and fifties, the ecological body concentrated from the eighteen sixties onward and anchored in published or securely drafted mature work. The ecological body contains a correction of principle about wealth, a mechanism analysing disrupted material exchange, and an ethic of intergenerational trusteeship stated with juridical precision. It does not contain a theory of natural limits, a reconciliation of need's expansion with finitude, or a politics of restraint. The framework's central categories accommodate ecological analysis without strain, as the author himself demonstrated by performing such analysis inside them. The framework's central definitions also carry the anthropocentric shape Benton and Schmidt diagnosed, which any extension must consciously correct rather than deny. The recovery project established the middle findings permanently and damaged its own credibility wherever it claimed the outer ones.

### Which verdict survives the full inventory?

Extension without possession: the framework accommodates ecological analysis, its author began performing that analysis in mature work and private study, and he completed no systematic ecology, so inheritance supplies foundations and fragments rather than a doctrine simply ready for application.

That position, the extension not possession thesis, is weaker than recovery rhetoric at full volume and stronger than the productivist dismissal, and it has the additional virtue of being checkable. Each clause corresponds to exhibits a sceptic can inspect: accommodation, to the metabolic and rent chapters; beginning, to the dated sequence from 1867 through the notebooks; incompletion, to the absent limits theory that no amount of quotation has yet produced. Positions that specify their evidence in advance can lose arguments, which is precisely what makes them worth holding. Positions that cannot lose, because every absence becomes a suppression and every fragment a prophecy, have left the company of scholarship for the warmer company of faith, where Marx, an unsparing critic of faith in all its costumes, would not have wished to be kept.

Implications radiate outward in modest, usable directions. For readers of Capital, the thesis directs attention to chapters textbooks rush past, especially on agriculture, rent, and the regulation of interchange, and restores those chapters to the book's argumentative centre. For ecological theorists, the thesis licenses construction without ancestor worship: use the metabolic category where it earns its keep, repair the labour process concept where Benton's critique bites, and credit later sciences with their own discoveries. For critics of Marxism, the thesis removes a soft target and replaces it with a harder one, since graded claims require graded refutations. For the tradition's self understanding, the thesis describes a familiar situation honestly: founders begin things. Finishing them is the work of the living, who might as well get on with it.

## Wealth, value, and use value: the technical core

Most popular treatments skip the technical distinction on which the whole value dispute turns, and the omission guarantees confusion. Marx's economics separates three notions that everyday speech merges. Wealth, in the material sense, means the stock of useful things a society commands: food, shelter, tools, clothing, the means of enjoying and producing more of each. Value, by contrast, names a social relation specific to commodity production, the crystallisation of abstract labour time that makes unlike products commensurable in exchange. Use value names the useful properties of a thing, properties rooted jointly in the thing's natural constitution and in the labour that shaped it. A coat's warmth is a use value. The hours socially necessary to produce the coat ground its value. The wardrobe, the mill, the sheep, and the pasture together form part of a society's material wealth. Collapsing those three into one word, as the Gotha draft programme collapsed them, generates errors in every direction, which is why the correction of 1875 opens where it opens.

Now the objection can be stated in its precise form. If value counts only labour, then an economics organised around value will register a forest as nothing until felling, a fishery as nothing until catching, and clean water as nothing until bottling. Depletion that raises no labour cost remains invisible to the accounts; destruction of a free gift looks, in value terms, like no event at all. Add the accumulation drive, which compels each capital to externalise costs or perish, and the theory seems to describe a machine constitutionally unable to see the damage it predicts. Critics from Benton onward have put the point with variations, and any reply pretending the point is silly has not understood it. The invisibility is real inside the value form. The dispute concerns whose invisibility it is.

Burkett's answer, refined across a series of essays and his 1999 book, insists on the level at which the theory operates. The critique of political economy is an analysis of how capitalism represents the world to itself and compels agents to act on that representation. Within capitalism, nature genuinely is treated as a free gift; capitals genuinely do appropriate soil fertility, water power, and mineral stocks without paying for their reproduction. An economic science that registered nature inside value would misdescribe the system it analyses, flattering capitalism with a wisdom it does not possess. The blindness belongs to the object. Moreover, the theory supplies external standpoints from which the blindness can be judged: the standpoint of material wealth, where nature counts in full; the standpoint of use value, where qualities matter; and the standpoint of the labour process as metabolic exchange, where physical throughputs are tracked in their own terms. On Burkett's reading, far from ignoring nature, the critique exposes a social form that ignores nature while materially depending on it, which is a sharper ecological instrument than an economics that prices everything and thereby sanctifies the pricing.

Assessment requires separating what the reply proves from what it assumes. It proves that nature's absence from the value measure is a deliberate analytical choice with critical intent, corroborated by the author's own policing of the wealth value boundary in 1875. It assumes, more debatably, that the external standpoints the theory supplies are sufficient for ecological judgement, that tracking throughput in use value terms can ground limits, obligations, and policy. Sceptics ask where, inside those standpoints, a society learns how much throughput is too much. The corpus answers with cases: soil that fails, forests that vanish, cities that choke. Cases teach recognition without yielding a criterion, rather as case law teaches judgement without yielding a statute. Whether case based judgement suffices is a genuine question for ecological theory generally, not only for its Marxist branch, since ecological science itself often proceeds by recognising damaged systems before it can formalise thresholds. The parallel does not settle the matter. It does relocate the matter, from an accusation of unique blindness toward a shared problem of criteria, where Marx's framework appears as one incomplete resource among several rather than as a uniquely defective one.

A further technical wrinkle rewards attention. Ground rent, in the mature theory, is precisely the mechanism by which nature's differential gifts acquire value form without nature producing value: the owner of better land captures surplus generated by social conditions, because property in a non produced condition allows its holder to levy tribute. Rent thus demonstrates the theory thinking hard about the interface between natural conditions and social forms, constructing a category for exactly the zone critics call invisible. One can fault the construction's completeness; one cannot honestly call the zone unexamined. Rent theory also generated the argumentative neighbourhood in which the trusteeship passage lives, since the question of what landowners own, and whether anyone can own land in the strong sense at all, presses directly on intergenerational claims. Technical chapters and famous quotations illuminate each other when read together, which is why this article keeps returning to their neighbourhoods instead of collecting the quotations in a display case.

## The soil question as a worked example

Abstract grading benefits from a single worked example pursued in depth, and the soil question is the natural candidate because every party to the dispute has written on it. The factual background, as nineteenth century agricultural science understood it, ran as follows. Crops remove mineral constituents from soil. Traditional husbandry returned many constituents through manure, fallowing, and rotation, within local cycles linking field, livestock, and village. Commercial agriculture lengthened the chain: food travelled to distant cities, the wastes of urban consumption entered rivers and sewers, and the constituents removed from fields were not returned to them. Yields could be maintained by importing fertility, Peruvian guano and Chilean nitrates and manufactured fertilisers among the famous imports, at the cost of new dependencies and new extractions elsewhere. Liebig named the pattern robbery and denounced the sewage arrangements of London as a civilisation flushing its agricultural capital into the sea. Those are period claims, stated as the period stated them, and this cluster's policy requires no modern adjudication of their accuracy.

Marx's use of the material displays his method at its most characteristic. The robbery pattern is not attributed to farmer ignorance or consumer selfishness. It is traced to the separation of town and country under capitalist development, to property arrangements rewarding short tenure extraction, and to a market logic in which the costs of soil exhaustion fall on future occupants while the gains of exported produce accrue now. Remedies imagined inside the same analysis are correspondingly structural: reunification of agriculture and industry, more rational distribution of population, and social control over the interchange between human production and the earth. A reader may dispute the remedies, the history, or the chemistry, but the form of the analysis, mechanism traced to social organisation, is unmistakably the author's own and unmistakably applied to a natural cycle. Productivist readings must explain why the supposed celebrant of subjugation bothered. Ecological hagiography must explain why the analysis remained a chapter inside an economics of capital rather than growing into the systematic treatise its subject seems to promise. Both explanations, attempted honestly, converge on the developmental and incomplete picture this article defends.

Subsequent scholarship has stress tested the soil case from several directions. Historians of guano confirm the broad picture of nutrient transfer and imperial scramble for fertiliser islands, while adding coercion and monopoly that the chemistry centred accounts underplay. Soil scientists caution that period understandings of fertility omitted much that later research on organic matter, microbiology, and erosion would add, so the specific mechanisms Marx inherited are dated in the honourable way that all period science is dated. Economists of the rift school reply that dated mechanisms can still found a valid category, since the category names a pattern, disrupted return flows under determinate social arrangements, that survives the replacement of its first examples. That defence seems sound, provided the pattern is re-earned for each new domain rather than assumed. Applied to forests, the pattern asks whether extraction outruns regeneration under given property and market arrangements. Applied to fisheries, the same question wears different clothes. Applied to the atmosphere, the clothes change again, and honest extension says so instead of pretending the soil chapter already contained the answers.

Notice what the worked example does to Benton's objection. The soil analysis shows the labour process concept, in practice, registering a natural dynamic, nutrient return, with its own tempo and requirements, and judging production arrangements against that dynamic's conditions. The concept in action looks less passive toward nature than the concept in definition. Benton anticipated the observation, arguing that practical richness does not cure definitional poverty, since definitions govern what a theory can say systematically rather than what a gifted practitioner can notice episodically. The rejoinder and counter rejoinder could continue, and in the journals they have. For the purposes of a verdict, the exchange establishes a floor and a ceiling. Floor: the mature work performs ecological analysis of at least one major cycle, in print, with mechanisms. Ceiling: the performance does not generalise itself into doctrine. Between floor and ceiling lies the territory the extension thesis occupies, and the soil case maps that territory better than any slogan.

## Forests, water, and the wider pattern

Soil monopolises discussion because the texts and the chemistry align so neatly there, but the pattern of attention extends further across the natural world, and breadth matters when grading whether the soil analysis was an anomaly or a habit. Forestry appears in Marx's reading and in the background of his earliest journalism: his first published articles in 1842 concerned laws on theft of wood, which criminalised traditional gathering rights as enclosures converted customary use into property crime. Those articles are about law and poverty rather than silviculture, yet they taught their twenty four year old author a permanent lesson about how property forms mediate access to natural goods, a lesson the mature rent theory would formalise. Debates over wood gathering also place the young Marx inside a recognisably material conflict over a renewable resource, decades before the notebooks. Continuity hunters overstate the connection; dismissal understates it. The articles show an early education in the politics of natural resources, running parallel to, and independent of, the productivist rhetoric of the same years, which is further evidence that the corpus was never as single voiced as anthologies suggest.

Water runs through the work in several channels. Irrigation in India figured in the 1853 journalism, where large scale waterworks were assigned to the state by arguments about climate and scale that later scholars have criticised as orientalist generalisation; the criticisms are treated in the colonialism article of this series and are not repeated here. Water power versus steam power receives attention inside the machinery chapters, where the locational freedom of steam figures as an advantage for capital seeking labour in towns, an analysis later historians of fossil energy developed into a full research programme. Rivers as waste sinks appear inside the soil argument, since urban sewage lost to rivers is fertility lost to fields. None of this amounts to a hydrology. It amounts to repeated contact between the analysis and the material world at points where production touches natural systems, contact frequent enough to falsify the claim of systematic indifference and scattered enough to defeat any claim of a systematic ecology. Graded adjectives are doing real work in those sentences, and they are the article's method in miniature.

Game laws, fisheries, and the commons surface at the edges of the work and its milieu, usually as illustrations of property's historical forms rather than as objects of analysis in their own right. Engels, again separately, wrote with more sustained interest about hunting, landscape, and the transformation of the Mesopotamian and Mediterranean environments, and his Dialectics of Nature fragments warn against flattering ourselves over victories over nature, since nature retaliates for unconsidered interventions through consequences the victors failed to foresee. The warning is quotable and often quoted with Marx's name attached by careless transmission. Correct attribution matters more than usual here, because the passage's fame has made it a load bearing wall in popular versions of the ecological Marx. Remove the misattributed wall and the structure still stands on the soil chapters, the Gotha correction, and the trusteeship passage, which is fortunate, but it stands at a different height. Popular argument rarely performs the removal, because the misattributed version is shorter and punchier. Scholarship consists largely of such unglamorous removals and re measurements.

The wider pattern also includes silence, and inventory discipline requires listing notable silences beside notable presences. Species extinction, a live scientific and public concern after the great auk and during the destruction of the American bison, receives no analytical attention in the corpus. Animal welfare, debated vigorously in Victorian Britain, is absent as a theme. Wilderness preservation, institutionalised in the first national parks during Marx's final decade, goes unremarked. A systematic ecological thinker in the modern mould would be expected to engage at least some of that agenda; the corpus engages almost none of it directly. Silences of that breadth are exactly what keep the possession claim out of reach, and they coexist, uncomfortably for all camps, with the presences catalogued above. Comfort was never the archive's obligation.

## The late Marx and the Russian question

No corner of the archive has generated more heat per page than the materials of 1881 concerning Russia. Vera Zasulich, a Russian revolutionary, wrote to Marx asking whether the rural commune, the obshchina with its periodically redistributed communal land, was doomed by historical necessity to dissolve under capitalism, as a reading of Capital's account of primitive accumulation might suggest. Marx drafted his reply four times, producing long, hesitant, erudite drafts, before sending a short letter whose modesty belies the labour behind it. In the drafts he restricts the historical inevitability described in Capital to Western Europe, entertains the commune as a possible fulcrum for a different path, and weighs the pressures, taxation, state extraction, internal differentiation, bearing on communal landholding. The episode matters for the colonialism debate, for the philosophy of history, and, more obliquely, for the ecological question, because it shows the late Marx refusing to impose a productivist escalator on a communal agrarian form and instead studying that form's material conditions with care.

Saito's influential reading makes the Russian materials one pillar of a broader claim: that the final Marx was moving beyond productivism altogether, integrating his natural science studies and his research on precapitalist communal forms into a vision sometimes labelled degrowth communism by its interpreter. The label is the interpreter's, and controversy has attached to it accordingly. Supporters marshal the drafts, the notebooks, and the late correspondence into a portrait of intellectual movement still accelerating at death. Critics reply that drafts prepared for a correspondent, never published, hedged with conditions, and concerned chiefly with Russia's political future, cannot support a general doctrine about growth, and that the same years produced no revision of the published system announcing any such turn. Both positions can quote. The responsible grading treats the Russian materials as strong evidence of late openness and flexibility, weak evidence of doctrinal destination. Openness matters for the extension thesis, since extending a framework is easier when its author is found, at the end, questioning the framework's most productivist seeming implications himself.

Methodological reflection is warranted here because the episode is a trap for the unwary in both camps. A draft is evidence of consideration, not of conclusion. Four drafts are evidence of serious consideration. A short sent letter is evidence of what the author was willing to state publicly to that correspondent at that date. Scholarship weights those items differently and says why. Advocacy weights them by usefulness and says nothing. Readers equipped with that simple hierarchy can process most storms in the secondary literature, including the recurring announcement that a newly published fragment has settled the ecological question forever. Fragments settle questions about fragments. Systems settle slowly, by accumulation, and the accumulation in this case points, as it has pointed throughout this article, toward development without completion.

There is a human dimension that biography should not skip. The Marx of the Russian episode was in his sixties, in poor health, newly bereaved, travelling for cures, and still filling notebooks at a rate that exhausted his editors for a century afterward. The productivist of the Manifesto had been a twenty nine year old exile journalist in a revolutionary year. Between those figures lies one of the most documented intellectual lives of the nineteenth century, and the ecological question, pursued honestly, becomes partly a question about what a lifetime of reading does to a powerful mind. It does not leave the mind unchanged. It also does not guarantee arrival at any destination later readers favour. Development is not destiny, and the drafts of 1881 are evidence of a journey in progress, valued most accurately when valued as exactly that.

## Trusteeship across generations

Because the trusteeship passage carries so much weight in the verdict, it deserves an examination of its logic rather than merely its glow. The argument, reconstructed, runs in steps. The earth, with its fertility, waters, and accumulated improvements, is the original condition of all production and of life itself. No living generation produced that condition; each found it, partly as gift and partly as the accumulated labour and damage of predecessors. Ownership, in any intelligible sense, implies a right to use up or destroy the thing owned. A generation asserting full ownership of the earth would assert a right to consume the conditions of its successors' existence, people who cannot consent, bid, or object. The assertion fails on its own logic, quite apart from sentiment: the supposed owners did not make the asset, cannot replace it, and hold it only during a tenancy measured against geological and civilisational time. What remains is usufruct with duties, use joined to an obligation of transmission, and transmission, in the passage's striking formulation, in improved condition. Notice that the argument does not depend on any particular economics. It would retain force if every other doctrine of its author were discarded, rather as arguments about promises outlive the systems that first housed them. Standalone strength explains the passage's migration into environmental ethics, where writers with no Marxist commitments cite it as an early statement of intergenerational obligation.

Context disciplines the glow without dimming it. The passage sits inside a discussion of landed property and rent under capitalism, and its immediate polemical work is to deny that abolishing private landlords would make society the earth's proprietor in the same strong sense. Even a socialist commonwealth, on this argument, holds land as trustee rather than owner, bound by duties to successors that no majority vote can annul. That is a remarkable constraint to place inside a tradition sometimes caricatured as worshipping collective will, and it cuts against productivist readings drawn from the same volume's more conventional chapters. It also cuts, less comfortably, against any politics, of whatever colour, that treats electoral or revolutionary victory as a licence to liquidate natural capital for present consumption. The passage is an equal opportunity inconvenience, which is usually the mark of a serious ethical claim rather than a factional slogan.

Limits of the passage must be stated with the same care. An ethic of trusteeship is not an ecology. It tells holders what they owe; it does not tell them how soils regenerate, how forests should be managed, or where the boundary between use and abuse falls in particular cases. It supplies no criterion for adjudicating between improving land for agriculture and preserving it from agriculture, a distinction on which practical environmental judgement constantly depends and on which the passage's agricultural century and ours may differ. Improvement itself is a period notion, saturated with assumptions about drainage, clearance, and cultivation that a later sensibility questions. Granting all that, the residue remains considerable: a principled, non anthropocentric sounding limit on collective sovereignty over nature, stated by the supposed arch productivist, in the mature economics, in a form later environmental ethics recognises as kin. Residues of that size are what graded verdicts are made of.

Ethical passages also invite a question about their author's consistency, and consistency questions should be answered from the record rather than from piety. Did the trusteeship ethic govern the author's own judgements elsewhere? The late interest in communal landholding in Russia coheres with it, since the commune appears there as a form capable of husbanding a shared resource across generations. The soil analysis coheres with it. Against coherence stand the earlier celebrations and a lifelong impatience with preservationist sentimentality when it appeared as romantic politics. The fairest summary returns to chronology: the ethic belongs to the mature stratum, the impatience largely to earlier strata and to temperament, and a thinker is responsible for his mature statements in a way that makes the developmental reading not a courtesy but a conclusion. Biographers apply that principle to every other figure in the century. Applying it here is not special pleading; withholding it would be.

## The modern controversy mapped

Readers entering the literature now meet a field with factions, journals, and genealogies of its own, and a map helps more than another verdict. One province is occupied by the metabolic rift school, centred on Foster and the journal Monthly Review, extending the soil analysis into a general account of ecological rifts under capitalism and defending the recovery of Marx's ecology as its founding charter. A second province, world ecology, associated with Jason Moore, rejects the society nature dualism it attributes to the first province and treats capitalism as a system for organising human and extra human nature together, cheapening lives and materials in a single logic. A third province gathers critics of the recovery itself, scholars who accept much of the textual evidence while resisting the movement built on it: Malm's insistence on the specificity of fossil capital and on the costs of ancestor worship belongs here, alongside historians who find the late Marx less converted than Saito's portrait suggests. A fourth province, less visible in Anglophone debate, contains the older European discussions descending from Schmidt, in which questions of nature, technology, and domination were posed in a philosophical register the Anglophone recovery sometimes rediscovered without recognising.

Border disputes between provinces are frequent and occasionally illuminating. The rift school and world ecology accuse each other, in essence, of mishandling the relation between society and nature: dualism on one charge sheet, dissolution on the other. Both cannot be wholly right, and the exchange has produced genuine conceptual clarification about what metabolism requires, distinguishable terms in exchange, and what monism costs, the loss of any standpoint from which one term can damage the other. Historians watch such disputes with a professional suspicion that the nineteenth century corpus is being asked to referee a twenty first century theoretical quarrel it cannot settle, since its author had no conception of the positions between which he is asked to choose. That suspicion is healthy and should be carried into every faction's pamphlets, including the ones this article finds most persuasive. Texts constrain interpretation; they do not appoint heirs.

Where does the map leave the original question? Provincial disputes concern the best development of ecological Marxism, a forward looking theoretical matter. The backward looking textual question admits the narrower grading this article has defended throughout, and the grading is compatible with several provinces' programmes while endorsing none. A world ecologist can accept the extension thesis and proceed to argue that extension requires Moore's reconstruction rather than Foster's. A rift scholar can accept it and argue the foundations determine more of the building than the thesis allows. A sceptic of the recovery can accept it as the maximum the evidence permits and warn against everything beyond. Convergence on a graded middle by parties who agree on little else is not proof of truth, but it is evidence that the grading tracks something real in the archive rather than a compromise invented for peace. Peace, in any case, is not on offer; the provinces continue to dispute, and the dispute continues to generate readings worth having.

Textbook treatments lag the map by a generation, as textbooks do. Survey courses often present the ecological question as a novelty or omit it, while examination syllabi in several countries have begun to include ecological Marxism as a named topic with set texts drawn from the recovery literature and its critics. Students therefore meet the question in an odd posture: the scholarly field is mid argument, the textbook is a decade behind the argument, and the examination rewards precisely the skill the situation demands, namely grading claims against evidence rather than reciting a settled line. That alignment between the topic's state and the skill it tests is rare and valuable, and teachers designing courses around the question can exploit it deliberately, as the persona guidance below suggests.

## Benton reconstructed: the reply literature

Return once more to the strongest objection, because the reply literature after 1989 is richer than a single exchange and because the verdict's credibility depends on the objection having been met at its highest development. Benton's essay proposed reconstruction, not abandonment: a historical materialism revised to include ecoregulatory labour, natural processes with their own dynamics, and production conceived as one moment within material interchange rather than as transformation imposed on passivity. Replies came in waves. Some defended the original labour process concept as more capacious than Benton allowed, pointing to passages where tools, materials, and natural conditions resist and shape the producer, arguing that intention and instrumentality never exhausted the concept in its author's hands. Some accepted the diagnosis and disputed the remedy, doubting that a revised concept could retain the theory's explanatory architecture. Some, Foster most prominently, relocated the debate to metabolism, arguing that the concept Benton needed was present all along at a different level of the system, in the analysis of interchange rather than in the definition of labour. Each wave clarified the dispute's real subject: not whether Marx liked nature, but which concept, at which level, does the ecological work, and how much repair that concept needs.

A generation later, the exchange can be graded with some detachment. Benton's specific charge, that the standard definition of the labour process lacks a place for ecoregulatory activity and for nature's independent dynamics, has been conceded in substance by much of the literature, including writers who reject his conclusions; the concept as defined in textbooks is narrower than the analysis as practised in Capital. His stronger claim, that the framework as such cannot recognise limits, has not carried the field, because the metabolic analysis functions in the literature as a working counterexample, registering rupture against natural dynamics in a specific and influential case. His constructive proposals seeded programmes in ecological sociology that continue independently of the exegesis quarrels. That is the profile of a major critical intervention: partly absorbed, partly answered, wholly impossible to ignore. Defences of the ecological Marx that proceed as though Benton never wrote remain, by that test, unserious, and the exam guidance below instructs candidates to name him for exactly that reason.

Schmidt's afterlife runs parallel and quieter. The mediation thesis resurfaces whenever constructionist claims about nature circulate, and its careful original, with its insistence on nature's resistant independence inside mediation, reads better than most of its descendants. Later Frankfurt influenced work on the domination of nature, above all the Dialectic of Enlightenment by Horkheimer and Adorno, posed kindred questions in a darker key, treating mastery over outer nature and mastery over human beings as entangled from the beginning. Marx figures in that book more as ancestor than as subject, but students of the ecological question benefit from knowing the lineage, since much twentieth century suspicion of productivism descends through it. Lineage is not evidence. It is, however, explanation: of why certain questions arrived when they did, armed with the vocabularies they carried.

One further reply deserves recording because it changes the subject in a productive way. Several critics of Benton observe that limits in Marx's framework enter through the body before they enter through the soil: the working day analysis turns on the physiological limits of the labourer, on sleep, nourishment, and the restoring of labour power, and capital's drive to exceed those limits generates law, struggle, and crisis. If the framework can register a natural limit where the natural entity is the worker's body, the conceptual door Benton declared locked appears at least partly open, since bodies are nature by anyone's definition. Defenders of Benton respond that bodily limits register only because labour power is a commodity whose owner can resist, giving the limit a social voice that soil and atmosphere lack. The response is strong. It also concedes the door exists and relocates the dispute to the question of voice, which is a political question about representation of natural interests, arguably the question on which ecological politics in general still founders. Relocation of that quality is progress, and the literature is better for the exchange that produced it.

## How the question travels

Ideas leave seminars and acquire public lives, and the ecological Marx has travelled widely in simplified forms: the magazine profile discovering a green prophet, the polemical column discovering a hypocrite, the social media thread trading two quotations like tennis balls. Public simplification is not a scandal to be deplored from a height; it is the normal fate of any idea with political charge, and scholarship's task is to keep the graded version available, findable, and legible for readers whose curiosity outlasts the thread. That is the function of treatments like this one and of the study resources gathered in the series: to give a reader who met the tennis match somewhere a place where both balls, and the court, and the umpire's chair, can be inspected at leisure.

Travel also distorts through translation and excerpting in ways worth a warning. Key terms shift between languages: the German vocabulary of Stoffwechsel, rendered metabolism, carries connotations of material transformation that the English word's biological narrowing can flatten, and arguments built on the English word alone sometimes attribute to the concept a precision or a vagueness belonging to the translation. Quotation databases strip contexts wholesale; the trusteeship passage circulates in versions that omit its rent chapter setting, and the Manifesto passage circulates without its dialectical frame. None of this is conspiracy. It is entropy, and the countermeasure is the unglamorous one already recommended: volume, chapter, date, genre, every time, until the habit feels less like pedantry and more like respect for a reader's intelligence.

A final travel note concerns the phrase green Marx itself, which this article has avoided as a verdict precisely because it functions in public discourse as a team jersey. Jerseys have their uses in politics. They have none in grading evidence. Readers who finish this article unable to say whether its author is on the green team have encountered a feature, not a failure: the grading was the point, and any team membership would have been a distraction purchased with the reader's trust. The same discipline governs the series as a whole, whose articles on contested figures and doctrines aim to leave readers with better maps rather than warmer loyalties.

## The Promethean label under the microscope

Labels in intellectual history are compressed arguments, and Promethean is among the most compressed. Prometheus stole fire from the gods and gave it to humankind, suffering eternal punishment for the gift; the label therefore bundles technological daring, defiance of limits, and a hint of hubris punished in the end. Applied to Marx, the bundle smuggles conclusions. A reader told in advance that a thinker is Promethean will find fire stealing in every passage about machinery and read stewardship passages as uncharacteristic lapses. A reader told the opposite, that the thinker is a proto environmentalist, will perform the mirror operation. The label's career illustrates the anthology method in its purest form, since both applications cite real texts and both mislead by framing. Analytical writing should therefore use the label only as a name for one reading among several, attributed to the scholars who defend that reading, never as a settled description of the man. That discipline costs a few words per mention and buys immunity to the commonest rhetorical trick in the field.

Where did the Promethean reading acquire its authority? Partly from the texts of the eighteen forties already examined, partly from the self presentation of twentieth century Marxist regimes, whose iconography of furnaces, dams, and tractors taught the world to associate the tradition with industrial gigantism, and partly from hostile critics for whom the association was a gift. Reception and text braided together so thoroughly that disentangling them became a scholarly industry of its own. The disentangling matters for fairness in both directions. A thinker should not be convicted by his followers' posters. Equally, a thinker whose categories were so readily conscripted into gigantism bears some responsibility for the conscription's ease, a responsibility Benton's structural argument tries to specify. Posters are not arguments, but the availability of a doctrine to a use is evidence about the doctrine's emphases, if not about its entailments. The graded treatment of that evidence, suggestive rather than decisive, is another instance of the method this article has applied throughout.

Fire itself deserves a footnote in the label's biography. The nineteenth century ran on combustion: coal in furnaces, steam in cylinders, gas in street lamps. A Promethean century praised fire stealers in every literature, from engineering hagiography to popular science. Marx's generation breathed that atmosphere as completely as it breathed coal smoke, and his early celebrations of industry are, among other things, period documents of a culture enthralled by energy. Later historians of fossil capital, Malm most prominently, have argued that the enthrallment concealed a social choice: steam triumphed over water power not through thermodynamic destiny but because steam freed capital from geography and from the collective rhythms of mill communities, concentrating workers where they could be recruited and disciplined. That argument, whatever its final standing, reframes the Promethean material once more. The fire was never only fire; it was a labour relation wearing a technological mask. Recognising the mask does not acquit the early texts of their enthusiasm. It does show the mature method, applied by later hands to the century's central technology, yielding an analysis in which nature, energy, and class power interlock, which is precisely the kind of analysis the extension thesis predicts the framework can support.

## Regulation of interchange and the meaning of freedom

One passage in the third volume has quietly become the most contested paragraph in the whole dispute, rivalling even the trusteeship text. Writing about freedom, Marx distinguishes a realm of necessity, labour dictated by need, which persists in every social form, from a realm of freedom beginning beyond it. Inside the necessity discussion sits a sentence imagining associated producers rationally regulating their interchange with nature, bringing it under common control instead of being ruled by it as by a blind power. Recovery writers quote the sentence as the ecological programme in embryo: production consciously steered with knowledge of material cycles, the opposite of accumulation's blindness. Critics pounce on the vocabulary of control, hearing the old Promethean accent in a new key, mastery collectivised rather than renounced. Both hearings catch something real in the sentence, which is why the sentence sustains an industry.

A slower reading separates three claims packed into the image. First, an epistemic claim: producers can know the material interchange they participate in, its inputs, outputs, and consequences, in a way that market anarchy prevents, since prices conceal physical flows. Second, an institutional claim: knowledge can be made effective through common control, replacing the blind compulsion of competition with deliberated regulation. Third, a teleological claim: regulation serves freedom, understood as the expansion of human development beyond necessary labour, not as the maximisation of output. The first claim is a research programme and has aged well; material flow accounting is now a standard scientific practice. The second claim is a political wager whose difficulties, information, incentives, dissent, are the standard difficulties of all planning, ecological or otherwise. The third claim reframes growth itself: the horizon is discretionary time and developed capacities, with necessary labour shortened as productivity allows, a horizon at least compatible with restraint in throughput, even if the text never performs the reconciliation with finite conditions that a systematic ecology would require. Read that way, the passage supports extension strongly and possession not at all, the now familiar grading, and its familiarity by this point should reassure rather than irritate: independent lines of evidence converging on the same grade is what confirmation looks like in historical scholarship.

Critics add a fourth observation that must be faced. Regulation language appears in the corpus precisely where the author's confidence in rational administration runs highest, and the twentieth century's administrative record, whatever its bearing on textual questions, has made modern readers allergic to the confidence. The allergy is a fact about modern readers, not about the manuscript, and reception history is the right drawer for it. Textually, the question is whether regulation of interchange differs from subjection of nature in kind or only in management. A defensible answer runs: in kind, if regulation is answerable to the conditions of the interchange itself, its cycles and regeneration rates, and only in management if regulation merely optimises extraction for collective owners. The passage's phrasing, with its insistence on the interchange as a relationship to be governed rather than a stock to be spent, leans toward the first answer without supplying the criteria that would settle it. Leaning is not settling. The sentence remains what the whole corpus is on this question: a foundation with unmistakable direction and unfinished architecture.

## Nature before history: the early philosophy revisited

The manuscripts of 1844 and the German Ideology drafts of the following years contain the corpus's most quoted philosophical statements about nature, and both camps mine them, so they require direct handling. In the 1844 manuscripts, the young Marx describes nature as humanity's inorganic body, the medium in which human life expresses and sustains itself, and treats estranged labour as estranging human beings from that body along with everything else. Admirers read an ontology of belonging: people are nature, conscious of itself, and damaging nature is self damage. Critics read anthropocentrism in poetic dress: nature matters as body, medium, and mirror of human essence, which is to say, nature matters as ours. Both readings find their sentences. The text is genuinely double, because its author was working through Feuerbach and Hegel with literary intensity and had not yet built the economic categories that would later discipline his vocabulary. Philosophical anthropology of that kind can inspire an ecological ethic; it cannot substitute for one, and the mature author's own trajectory, away from essence language toward mechanism, suggests he came to a similar conclusion about its limits.

The German Ideology manuscripts push in a more austere direction. Against contemplative materialism, their authors insist that the nature human beings confront is always nature already worked on, hunted over, planted, drained, and built across, so that pristine nature prior to history survives, if anywhere, on a few newly risen islands. Schmidt built the mediation thesis substantially on such passages, and their austerity is undeniable. Yet the same manuscripts ground history in the production of material life, in eating, drinking, housing, and clothing before all theorising, which anchors human existence in bodily interchange with a world that feeds or starves it. Austerity about pristine nature and insistence on material dependence are two sides of one position: there is no standpoint outside the interchange from which to contemplate it, and the interchange is real, resistant, and prior to every philosophy. Whether that position can host an ecology depends on whether mediation is read as dissolution or as the only honest form of relation. The article's grading has been consistent: mediation blocks any ecology of nature's intrinsic claims standing apart from human practice, and permits, indeed requires, an ecology of the interchange itself. Readers who find that permission insufficient are in Benton's company, and their insufficiency claim is the live frontier of the debate.

A word about Feuerbach helps students place these texts. The theses of 1845 criticise the older materialism for regarding reality as a spectacle for contemplation instead of a field of practice, and the ecological literature sometimes recruits that criticism as an anticipation of ecological embeddedness. Recruitment should be resisted in its strong form: the theses concern the theory of knowledge and the grounds of social change, not ecosystems, and their author would have been surprised by some uses made of them. In weaker form the recruitment has a point, since a philosophy insisting that human beings know the world by transforming it, and are transformed in return, at least clears ground on which a theory of interchange can be built. Ground clearing is not building. The pattern recurs so often in this corpus that it deserves a name of its own: prolegomena mistaken for doctrines. Half the overclaiming in the recovery literature, and half the scorn in the critical literature, reduces to that single confusion about what kind of textual object a passage is.

## Machinery, energy, and the factory chapters

The machinery chapters of the first volume rarely appear in ecological anthologies, an omission worth correcting because they contain the mature method operating on the century's emblematic technology. Machinery appears there neither as neutral progress nor as villainy, but as a social form: a means by which capital reorganises labour, extends the working day, draws in new classes of workers, and revolutionises the material basis of production continuously. Nature enters through the motive powers, water, wind, steam, and through the raw materials whose properties dictate processes, cotton's behaviour under humidity, iron's under heat. The chapters record a production system increasingly emancipated from the rhythms of muscle and season, an emancipation whose costs are counted in bodies and communities rather than in landscapes. Eco critics note the counting's selectivity. Defenders note that the analytical machinery built for counting those costs, the relentless tracing of a drive against the conditions it consumes, is transportable, and that later writers have transported it to energy systems, as Malm's work on steam demonstrates, with results no one calls un Marxist. Transportability is again the extension claim, arriving this time from the factory floor rather than from the soil.

Energy history adds a retrospective irony. The century's shift from renewable flows, water and wind and muscle, to a stock, coal, whose combustion could be scheduled at will, is the material subplot of the industrial revolution, and the corpus registers the shift's social logic without theorising its geological meaning. Coal measures, exhaustion anxieties, and Jevons's calculations circulated in the period's journals; the notebooks show awareness of resource debates without a synthesis. A systematic ecology would have needed to make the stock flow distinction central, since stocks deplete in a way flows do not, and regeneration rates govern flows in a way they cannot govern stocks. The corpus contains the conceptual raw materials for that distinction, in the difference between conditions that reproduce themselves and gifts that do not, scattered across the rent and reproduction discussions. Scattered is the operative word. Assembly was left undone, and undone assembly is the signature finding of this entire investigation, visible from the factory chapters as clearly as from the rent chapters, which is either a remarkable coincidence or the shape of the truth about an unfinished intellectual project interrupted by death in 1883.

Death as an explanation deserves its own weighing, because both camps abuse it. Advocates use interruption to excuse every gap: one more decade and the ecology would have flowered. Critics use the same interruption to dismiss trajectory evidence: intentions unpublished are intentions unheld. Biographical method steers between the abuses by distinguishing direction from destination. Direction is documented: reading, drafting, and published analysis all move toward greater engagement with natural conditions across the final two decades. Destination is unknowable, and every sentence claiming to know it, whether it promises a green system or denies one could have emerged, is fiction wearing footnotes. The Zasulich drafts, composed in the final years amid illness and grief, show direction with unusual clarity precisely because they were working documents rather than performances. A mind still revising its most confident schemas at sixty three, under those conditions, had not finished travelling. Where the road led is not given to us, and scholarship's maturity consists partly in declining to invent the missing miles.

## The stakes for reading Capital

Why should a reader of Capital, innocent of the ecological quarrel, care about its outcome? Because the quarrel changes which chapters look central. A productivist canon treats the agriculture passages as decoration, the rent volumes as technical scaffolding, and the Gotha marginalia as party ephemera, concentrating attention on value, exploitation, and crisis in the first volume's opening movements. An ecological canon recentres the book: the working day and agriculture chapters become twin studies of a drive consuming its natural conditions, human and terrestrial; the reproduction schemas acquire material throughputs alongside their monetary flows; the third volume's rent and freedom passages become the horizon toward which the whole analysis travels. Neither canon is neutral, and students deserve to know that the syllabus in front of them embodies a verdict in the dispute before a single seminar meets. The extension thesis supports a syllabus of the second kind with the first kind's rigour retained, since nothing in the ecological material licenses skipping the value theory on which the whole critique stands. If anything, the ecological chapters are unintelligible without it: soil robbery is a crime scene whose motive, means, and opportunity are supplied by accumulation, and readers who skip the motive will mistake the passage for nature writing.

There is also a stake for how the book's unfinished state is taught. Capital arrived as one published volume and a mountain of drafts, and its editor's construction of the posthumous volumes is among the great editorial achievements of the century. Teaching the ecological question honestly means teaching that materiality: which claims stand in a text its author revised for the press, which in drafts he abandoned or reworked, which in notes he never meant for readers. Students who learn to ask those questions of Marx will ask them of every author, and the habit outlasts any verdict about soil. Methods transfer; verdicts date. A course that produces students able to grade a thinker's ecological credentials from primary evidence, in date order, with genre and provenance attached, has taught something more durable than the correct opinion about Karl Marx, which is fortunate, since correct opinions about Karl Marx have a half life measurable in decades at best.

## Verification and the discipline of quotation

Every quotation in this article was kept short deliberately, and the discipline deserves a paragraph of explanation because it doubles as advice for any reader entering the primary texts. Short fragments can be checked. A reader with a standard edition can locate the Gotha correction at the opening of that short work, the trusteeship passage in the rent part of the third volume, and the Manifesto phrase in the first section of the pamphlet, each within minutes. Long quotations in secondary writing often conceal paraphrase, translation drift, or silent stitching between editions, and the ecological literature, like every polemical literature, contains celebrated sentences that dissolve on inspection into approximations of approximations. Translation adds a further layer: the corpus exists for most readers in English versions made across a century, whose renderings of key terms differ in ways that matter to arguments built on single words. Serious work cites editions and, where an argument turns on a term, consults the German. None of that is glamorous. All of it is the difference between a literature and a legend.

Provenance discipline also protects against a subtler failure, the migration of Engels's sentences into Marx's mouth and of Marx's into Engels's, examined earlier in connection with the Dialectics of Nature. The two men wrote jointly, separately, and for each other, sometimes signing work the other had drafted in part. Bibliographies untangle most of it; polemic retangles it for advantage. A reader who develops the reflex of asking whose sentence this is, in which text, of what date and genre, will find the ecological dispute, and most other disputes in this field, shrinking to manageable size. What survives that reflex is the real argument, which is interesting enough without the contraband.

## For students and exam candidates

Treat this question as a gift, because it rewards exactly the skills examiners are instructed to credit: dating evidence, separating levels of claim, and grading rather than cheering. Build answers around the paired bodies of evidence, announcing dates as you deploy passages, so the marker watches development instead of anthology. Name the critics precisely: Benton's essay of 1989 for the blind spot objection, Schmidt's study of 1962 for mediation, Burkett and Foster for the value reply and the recovery, and concede the missing limits theory before the marker raises it, since conceded ground costs nothing and buys credibility for everything else you defend. The exam guide for this cluster gathers technique in one place, and the analysis and evidence assembled here can be organised for timed writing with the study tools at [the VaultBook companion for this series](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html), which holds a card on the two bodies of text designed for revision. Avoid two failures above all: the answer that quotes only green passages, which markers recognise as advocacy, and the answer that pronounces on modern environmental politics, which answers a question nobody set. The question is textual. Keep it textual, grade it openly, and the marks follow the method.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

Open with the collision rather than the survey. Read the Manifesto's wonders aloud, announce the year 1848 and the authors' ages, then read the trusteeship passage aloud, announce its drafting in the eighteen sixties and its setting inside a rent chapter, and let the room sit with the fact that one mind produced both. Discussion ignites without further engineering, because students arrive carrying one of the two portraits and the collision dismantles each in turn. From there, sequence the seminar by level: sentences first, then concepts, with Benton assigned whole rather than summarised, since his essay teaches students what a structural objection feels like from inside a sympathetic tradition. Set the table exercise as written work, requiring one row per passage with date, genre, and qualification columns, and mark down any row missing its provenance, because provenance is the transferable skill. Close by refusing to settle the question by vote. A seminar that ends with the extension thesis argued, attacked, and refined has taught the topic. A seminar that ends with a show of hands has taught a ritual, and the students can tell the difference even when they enjoy the ritual more.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The live research fronts are archival, conceptual, and comparative, in that order of evidential promise. Archivally, the continuing publication of the natural science notebooks through the historical critical edition, whose riches are surveyed in the guide linked earlier in this article, keeps shifting the weight of the late evidence, and claims resting on the notebooks should cite notebook, date basis, and editorial status as routinely as claims resting on printed texts cite page numbers. Conceptually, the unfinished business is the criterion problem: by what standard, internal to the framework or imported, does an extension judge throughput against regeneration, and the Benton Foster exchange remains the obligatory point of entry. Comparatively, the promising work sets the corpus beside period science and period conservation writing, Liebig and Marsh above all, resisting both anachronism and insulation. Journalists covering the field's periodic flare ups can serve readers best by asking partisans three questions: which text, which date, which genre. Answers to those three questions, printed alongside any verdict, allow readers to grade the verdict themselves, which is the whole of the law in this dispute and, on the evidence assembled here, as close to a conclusion as the archive honestly permits.

## A short history of the argument

Controversies have biographies, and this one's biography explains its present shape better than any summary of positions. During the first half of the twentieth century the question was barely asked. Marxist orthodoxy in its Soviet form had canonised a productivist self image and read its founder accordingly, while academic philosophy in the West largely ignored the economics altogether. The 1844 manuscripts, published in 1932, redirected attention toward the young philosopher of alienation, and nature entered discussion as the scenery of human self estrangement rather than as soil, forest, or nutrient. Schmidt's study of 1962 was thus a solitary achievement, a doctoral work asking what concept of nature the corpus contained, years before an audience existed for the answer. Its relative neglect at publication, and its canonisation later, measure the audience's arrival rather than the book's quality, which did not change while it waited.

The nineteen seventies brought the audience into being through politics rather than scholarship. Environmental movements, energy crises, and the visibility of industrial damage created readers who asked old texts new questions, and Marxism, as the largest available critique of industrial capitalism, was interrogated early and roughly. Much early green writing treated the tradition as an enemy, citing the Manifesto and Soviet gigantism in one breath, while Marxist replies tended toward defensiveness or toward the claim that socialism would plan away the damage once in charge. Neither posture required reading the rent chapters. A smaller literature did the reading: essays on the metabolic relation began to circulate, Howard Parsons assembled a volume of Marx and Engels on ecology as early as 1977, gathering passages whose existence surprised readers on both sides, and the groundwork for a scholarly dispute, as opposed to a polemical exchange, was quietly laid.

Benton's intervention in 1989 converted scattered curiosity into a structured controversy by giving the negative case its strongest theoretical form and by proposing reconstruction rather than rejection. The timing mattered: socialist parties were absorbing green electorates and green questions simultaneously, and a leading journal of the left staging an ecological critique of historical materialism by one of its own signalled that the question had become unavoidable inside the tradition. The nineteen nineties then produced the recovery as an organised project, with Burkett's systematic value theoretic defence and Foster's historical reconstruction arriving within a year of each other at the century's end, surrounded by journal symposia in which Benton, Schmidt's legacy, and the new archival findings were debated together. Recovery scholarship also benefited from the MEGA edition's progress, since claims about notebooks could finally be checked against edited texts rather than rumour, raising the evidential standard for everyone.

The present century has professionalised the dispute. University presses publish monographs on both sides; the Saito debate over the late Marx drew in scholars of the notebooks, of Russian history, and of degrowth politics; Malm's work carried Marxist categories into energy history while scolding the recovery's piety; Moore's world ecology built an alternative research programme rather than a mere objection. Professionalisation brings gains in rigour and losses in accessibility, a trade this series exists to mediate. It also brings a characteristic risk: positions harden into schools whose members cite each other more than the corpus. The bibliographical remedy remains what it was in 1977, a return to dated primary texts, which is why this article has insisted on inventory before interpretation at every stage. History of the argument, properly told, is not a prelude to the argument. It is a warning about how arguments decay, addressed to everyone who is winning one.

## Engels as witness rather than evidence

Friedrich Engels haunts this dispute like a second signature on a painting, and attribution discipline requires a section of his own. The partnership lasted forty years; the two men corresponded almost daily when apart, divided subjects by agreement, and financed, edited, and defended each other's work. Engels's writings on nature are extensive: the manuscripts posthumously assembled as Dialectics of Nature, with their reflections on science, transformation, and unintended consequences; the famous warning that human victories over nature are revenged by unforeseen effects, illustrated by deforestation in Mesopotamia, Greece, and Asia Minor; popular works on the origin of the family and on Feuerbach that touch land, property, and the transformation of landscapes. That material is philosophically serious and rhetorically powerful, which is exactly why it tempts advocates into misattribution. Resist the temptation for three reasons.

First, accuracy: the corpus under examination is Marx's, and a verdict about Marx built from Engels's sentences would be overturned by any competent reviewer, taking the sound exhibits down with the unsound. Second, symmetry: critics who would object, rightly, to convicting Marx through Engels's weakest sentences cannot permit defenders to acquit him through Engels's strongest. Third, interest: Engels is worth reading as himself, a thinker whose materialism took a more encyclopaedic and sometimes more speculative form than his partner's, and whose ecological reflections represent the partnership's shared milieu developing in one of its two minds. As a witness, Engels testifies powerfully that the intellectual environment of the mature Marx was saturated with questions about nature, science, and transformation. As evidence about the contents of Marx's own categories, he testifies only indirectly, and honest argument keeps the witness in the witness box.

The editorial relationship cuts the other way and deserves equal plainness. The second and third volumes of Capital exist in public because Engels constructed them from drafts, selecting, ordering, and occasionally bridging. Where an argument in this article rests on volume three, the trusteeship passage and the regulation of interchange among them, the mediation has been flagged rather than hidden. Scholarship on the manuscripts has generally vindicated Engels's fidelity in matters of substance, and the specific passages used here derive from Marx's drafts. Flagging mediation is not an admission of weakness; it is the price of the reader's trust, payable on every occasion without exception. Editors of other corpora, from Nietzsche to Wittgenstein, have taught the humanities what happens when the price goes unpaid for generations: suspicion metastasises until even genuine passages require defence. The Marx corpus, attended by an editor of unusual loyalty and competence, deserves better than borrowed suspicion, and better begins with stating the mediation wherever it exists.

## The abundance question in miniature

No single issue does more to separate the camps at the level of intuition than abundance, so it earns a concentrated treatment here beyond the grading already given. The textual spark is the Gotha critique's higher phase, in which labour has become life's prime want and the springs of cooperative wealth flow more abundantly, permitting distribution according to need. Read flatly, the passage promises plenty as communism's precondition and fruit, and ecological critics read it flatly with relish: a doctrine whose heaven is abundance cannot be trusted on a finite planet. Defenders complicate the reading in three moves. Abundance in the passage is comparative, measured against scarcity imposed by class society rather than against physical infinity. The same document mandates deductions for replacement and insurance before distribution, embedding prudence in the vision's foundations. And the author's wider corpus locates freedom in time rather than in goods, in the shortening of necessary labour, which is an economy of hours rather than of tonnes. Each move has textual warrant. Together they soften the flat reading considerably without producing the one thing critics demand: a principled account of needs and their growth under finitude.

Why does that account matter so much? Because needs are the hinge between economics and ecology in any system. A theory may register material cycles perfectly and still founder if its conception of the good society requires those cycles to carry ever expanding throughput. Conversely, a theory with modest textual ecology but a restrained conception of need might navigate finitude more safely than its passages suggest. Marx's texts lean expansively on need: needs develop with productive forces, new needs are themselves a historical product the author generally welcomes, and asceticism receives short shrift whenever it appears as a political programme. That lean is a genuine inheritance problem, and honesty requires registering it as such rather than dissolving it into the wealth value distinction, powerful as that distinction is elsewhere. The tradition's later ecological writers have worked the hinge from both sides, some redefining need through sufficiency, others insisting that development of needs and restraint of throughput can coexist under changed property and planning. Their work is construction, openly labelled, and the extension thesis predicts exactly such construction where the corpus runs out.

Proportion is the final discipline on this topic. Abundance occupies a paragraph in a short polemical document of 1875, written against a party programme, not a treatise on the future society, of which the corpus contains strikingly little in any case. Building a whole verdict, for or against, on that paragraph repeats the anthology error at the level of the future rather than of nature. The paragraph counts. It counts as evidence of an unexamined assumption in one stratum of the work, coexisting with analyses and ethics that pull against the assumption's careless application. Coexistence of an unexamined assumption with resources for examining it is, once more, the ordinary condition of a great unfinished system, and recognising the ordinariness is a form of respect both for the system and for the reader.

## A reader's itinerary through the primary texts

Guidance through a large corpus should be concrete, so here is an itinerary for a reader who wants to test this article's grading against the sources in a month of evenings. Begin with the two short programmatic documents, because they frame everything else and can be read in a single sitting: the Manifesto, attending to the wonders passage and its dialectical surroundings, and the Gotha Programme critique, attending to its opening correction and its deductions before distribution. Next, take the first volume of Capital in an order no tutor recommends but this question rewards: the chapter on the working day, for the pattern of a drive meeting a natural limit in the worker's body; the chapters on machinery, for motive powers and materials; and the chapter on large scale industry and agriculture, where the metabolic argument lives, read slowly with a notebook for the agricultural references. A reader who stops there already owns the dispute's centre of gravity.

Continue into the third volume's rent part, not for the rent formulas, which can be skimmed on a first pass, but for the chapters where land's ownership is interrogated and the trusteeship and regulation passages appear; read them twice, once for content and once for tone, since the shift in register from price theory to intergenerational ethics is itself evidence. Then sample the Grundrisse in the sections on capital and pre capitalist formations, hunting the barrier language and testing the descriptive reading this article defended. Follow with a selection of letters and drafts from the final years, the Zasulich materials above all, in an edition that prints the drafts rather than only the sent letter, because the gap between drafts and letter is where the late development becomes visible. Close the itinerary with the critics in their own words: a chapter of Schmidt or a reliable exposition of the mediation thesis, Benton's essay entire, and a chapter each from Burkett and Foster, so that the secondary dispute is met in its strongest published forms rather than in summaries written by its enemies.

Along the way, keep a private version of this article's table. Rows will accumulate disagreements: a passage this article graded ecological that strikes the reader as managerial, a productivist flourish that shrinks in context, a silence that grows louder. Disagreement at the level of rows is the itinerary working as intended. The destination was never agreement with the grading offered here; it is the capacity to defend a grading of one's own, exhibit by exhibit, against a hostile examiner who has read the same texts. Readers who reach that capacity frequently report a side effect worth naming: impatience with verdict journalism about every thinker, not only this one, and a new habit of asking for dates before opinions. Habits of that kind are the real output of serious reading programmes, outlasting the fate of any particular thesis about any particular Victorian.

## What would change the verdict

A graded verdict should declare its own falsification conditions, the discoveries or arguments that would move it, since a verdict nothing could move is a creed. Several movers can be specified concretely. Publication of a sustained late manuscript in which Marx develops a general doctrine of natural limits, with the categories to support it, would upgrade possession from absent to arguable and force revision of every section here that leans on the absence. Conversely, manuscript research demonstrating that the trusteeship passage was substantially Engels's composition, rather than Marx's draft edited faithfully, would remove the ecological body's strongest exhibit and downgrade the ethic component of the verdict accordingly; present editorial scholarship points the other way, but the dependence deserves stating. A convincing demonstration that metabolism functions in Capital as metaphor rather than as a category with empirical content would weaken the mechanism component; the soil argument's specificity makes that demonstration unlikely, yet the attempt would clarify the concept's status either way.

Argumentative movers exist alongside archival ones. A successful derivation of throughput criteria from the value form, showing that the framework contains an implicit metrics of material scale, would answer the criterion problem from inside and strengthen the recovery beyond the grading given here. A successful proof that the labour process concept cannot be revised without collapsing the theory of history built on it would strengthen Benton's side correspondingly, converting incompleteness into contradiction and killing the extension thesis at its root. Neither proof has been produced to general satisfaction, and the history of attempts suggests both are harder than their sponsors expected, which is itself information about the framework's resilience and about the genuine difficulty of the criterion problem in any theoretical idiom. Research programmes are judged partly by the quality of the failures they generate, and by that standard this one remains in good health.

Institutional movers complete the list. Examination boards and textbook commissions decide, in practice, which grading a generation of students absorbs, and their decisions follow scholarship with a lag while amplifying its simplifications. Should the graded verdict stabilise in textbooks, the public tennis match of quotations will lose its audience, and the dispute will migrate wholly to the constructive questions: criteria, institutions, needs. That migration is already visible in the journals, where pure exegesis occupies a shrinking share of the ecological Marxism literature beside work on planning, energy, and care. A field that stops arguing about its founder's credentials and starts arguing about its unsolved problems has not betrayed the founder. It has done what he did with his own predecessors, which was to read them hard, take what earned its keep, and build.

## Common errors in student writing on this question

Markers' reports across subjects return to the same failures with seasonal regularity, and cataloguing them here may spare readers several lost marks. The first failure is the saint or villain opening, in which the first paragraph announces a verdict the rest of the answer merely illustrates; markers read such answers as closed and grade the illustration, not the mind. The second is quotation without provenance, passages floating free of date and genre, which forfeits the chronological argument that is this topic's highest yield asset. The third is Engels laundering, the quiet importation of the collaborator's sentences into the subject's account, detectable by any marker who knows the texts and fatal to credibility when detected. The fourth is the pivot to modern politics, in which an answer about nineteenth century manuscripts becomes a speech about parties, summits, or technologies of the student's own century; whatever its merits as a speech, it answers an unasked question and is graded as one.

The fifth failure is subtler and ensnares strong students: treating Benton as a hostile witness to be dismissed rather than as the objection to be answered. Answers that name the blind spot argument precisely, concede the missing limits theory, and then deploy the wealth value distinction and the metabolic chapters against the objection's strongest form earn the evaluation marks that answers defending every inch of the corpus cannot reach. The sixth failure is symmetrical, treating the recovery literature as a conspiracy of admirers; markers who set this question expect engagement with Burkett and Foster as scholars, not as defendants. The seventh is figure smuggling, the importation of statistics about emissions, temperatures, or reserves into a textual answer, against which the exam guidance in this cluster explicitly warns, since unverifiable numbers signal a candidate decorating rather than arguing. The eighth and last is the false balance paragraph, in which both sides are summarised and the candidate retires without grading anything; balance is a method for gathering evidence, not a conclusion, and answers ending in balance end in the middle bands by design.

Against each failure stands a habit already modelled in this article's construction. Date every passage. Attribute every objection. Concede the specific point the evidence forces, no wider. Grade exhibits separately and let the verdict be the sum of the grades. Keep the question textual. A candidate who internalises those five habits will find they transfer far beyond this topic, to every contested figure and doctrine on the syllabus, which is why teachers secretly love this question despite the marking load it generates: it is a gymnasium for the exact muscles examinations exist to test.

## The dispute's bearing on the wider cluster

This article sits fourth in a cluster of ten, and its grading constrains and enables the neighbours in ways worth making explicit. The guide that opens the cluster maps the currents of ecological Marxism as a living field; nothing in the present grading disturbs that map, since a field's existence does not depend on its founder's completeness. The concept article on the metabolic rift, which precedes this one, builds the central category in isolation; the grading here presupposes that construction and returns the compliment by supplying the category's contested provenance. The profile of Foster follows the recovery project through its leading figure; readers who meet the recovery first there can use this article as the audit of its claims, and readers who begin here can use the profile as the recovery's fullest self presentation. Later articles inherit the grading as a premise: the degrowth debate asks what follows for throughput, the climate analysis asks how accumulation drives a specific crisis, the comparison with green capitalism asks which programme the evidence supports, and the limits article takes up the abundance problem this article deliberately left open. A series is an argument distributed across volumes, and consistency of grading across its parts is a form of honesty readers are entitled to expect.

That distribution also explains several editorial decisions a reader might otherwise find puzzling. The socialist states' record is quarantined in its own article, not because the record is irrelevant to Marxism's ecological reputation, but because reputation and textual content are different subjects and mixing them corrupts both inquiries. The exam guide closes the cluster by converting its disputes into technique, instructing candidates to handle scientific evidence by attribution rather than assertion, a discipline this article has practised throughout by describing period science as period science. Readers who traverse the cluster in order will watch a question decompose into its proper parts, concept, recovery, text, application, comparison, critique, record, and craft, which is the closest a series can come to demonstrating its method rather than merely announcing it.

## Town and country: the geography inside the argument

One spatial relationship recurs so often in the ecological exhibits that it deserves treatment as a theme in its own right: the antagonism and interdependence of town and country. The first volume's agriculture discussion makes the separation of urban consumption from rural production the mechanism of soil robbery, but the theme's roots run deeper into the corpus. The German Ideology manuscripts treat the division between town and country as among the oldest forms of the division of labour, shaping mental and material life alike. The Manifesto, in the same breath as its celebrations, lists among the bourgeoisie's achievements the rescue of populations from the idiocy of rural life and the subjection of the country to the town, phrases whose contempt has embarrassed admirers ever since. The mature analysis retains the structure while transforming the valuation: the town's dominance becomes a material circuit to be audited, its wastes counted, its extractions traced, its dependence on a countryside it depletes exposed as a vulnerability rather than a triumph. Tracing that transformation across three decades gives students a compact demonstration of the developmental method, since here a single theme can be watched changing its meaning while keeping its shape.

Geography also supplies the ecological argument with its most concrete imagery, and concreteness is a scholarly virtue not to be despised. Nutrients have itineraries: from field to market town, from port to guano island, from dinner table to sewer to river to sea. A theory that follows itineraries can be checked against shipping records, sewage commission reports, and agricultural statistics of the period, and historians have performed those checks with results broadly kind to the analysis's plausibility. Abstraction is often accused of escaping verification; the metabolic argument at its best does the opposite, multiplying the points at which archives can bite it. Where the argument generalises beyond its itineraries, into claims about all production everywhere, verification thins and Benton's questions return. The itinerary standard therefore doubles as a rule for extension: an ecological extension of the framework earns confidence in proportion as it specifies the circuits it claims to describe, their materials, their directions, and their points of rupture. Extensions that speak of humanity and nature in the abstract, without itineraries, have left the method even when they keep the vocabulary.

The town country theme also connects the ecological question to the rest of the series in ways a completionist reader will appreciate. Colonial trade reorganised itineraries globally, which is why the imperialism cluster's analyses of extraction keep intersecting this cluster's analyses of circulation. The feminism cluster's work on social reproduction examines another circuit, the daily and generational reproduction of labour power, whose material conditions include food, water, housing, and care, all ecological in the plain sense that bodies are natural beings with requirements. Circuits thinking, once learned, propagates. That propagation is a point in the framework's favour that neither camp's banners capture: a method that keeps generating auditable questions in new domains is alive in the way research programmes are alive, whatever verdict is reached on its founder's personal greenness.

## Care, bodies, and the reproduction of labour power

The working day chapter was claimed earlier as a limit argument conducted on the terrain of the body, and the claim can be widened responsibly. Labour power, the capacity to work that the labourer sells, is reproduced outside the factory: in households where food is cooked, children are raised, the sick are tended, and the exhausted are restored for another day. The corpus analyses that reproduction unevenly, brilliantly on the wage's tendency to lag behind the costs of sustaining a family, thinly on the unwaged labour, performed overwhelmingly by women, that performs the sustaining. The feminism cluster of this series examines that thinness in its own right. For the ecological question, the relevant point is structural: the framework already contains a site where natural requirements, the body's needs for nourishment, rest, and shelter, impose themselves on accumulation as conditions it must respect or destroy its own basis. Capital that consumes labour power faster than households and communities can reproduce it saws through the branch it sits on, a dynamic the text pursues through mortality statistics, factory inspectors' reports, and the legislative history of the working day. The parallel with soil is exact in form: a natural condition, a drive that overdraws it, a crisis of reproduction, and a political struggle over the terms of repair.

Parallels of form invite the question Benton taught the field to ask: does the form generalise, or does each case stand alone, noticed by a great observer and unassimilated by his system? The honest answer remains the one given throughout: the cases accumulate, the system gestures, the generalisation is performed by successors. Yet the accumulation itself has evidential weight that a purely definitional critique can underrate. A thinker who independently finds the same pattern in soil, in bodies, in forests through the wood theft articles' prehistory, and in land through the rent analysis, is exhibiting something more robust than a metaphor: a style of attention, drawn to the points where accumulation meets the conditions of its own continuation. Styles of attention are what research programmes transmit most reliably, more reliably than doctrines, and the ecological Marxism that exists as a living literature may be better understood as the inheritance of that attention than as the recovery of a creed. Creed recovery makes headlines; attention inheritance does the work.

There is a corollary for how the corpus should be read in seminars. Reading for doctrine yields the familiar stalemate of proof texts. Reading for attention yields different questions: where does the analysis pause over material conditions, what does it count, whose reports does it trust, which inspectors, chemists, and commissions supply its facts? Those questions reveal a researcher embedded in the empirical literature of his period to a degree the stalemate never suspects, citing factory inspectors the way other economists cited price series. The ecological exhibits are not visitations from a greener muse; they are what happened when that empirical attention, turned toward agriculture by the logic of the research programme itself, met Liebig's chemistry in the eighteen sixties. Contingency and logic together produced the turn, which is how intellectual change usually happens, and why narratives of pure conversion and pure continuity both fail the record.

## A note on method: position over time

This series has met the position over time pattern before, in the article on Marx and colonialism, where a thinker's views developed across decades under the pressure of events and evidence. The pattern deserves a methodological statement, because it is the engine of the present article's verdict and because readers can reuse it wherever a long career is under judgement. The pattern's rules are few and strict. Date every text before weighing it. Rank genres by their author's own investment, published treatise above journalism, journalism above drafts, drafts above table talk. Expect development and treat its absence as the anomaly requiring explanation. Distinguish development from drift by checking whether later texts engage the problems earlier texts posed, or merely repeat slogans at lower energy. And report the trajectory whole, including the stretches where nothing moved and the corners where movement reversed, because a trajectory edited into a staircase is propaganda wearing chronology's clothes.

Applied to colonialism, those rules produced a verdict of substantial reversal, late writings repudiating the civilising mission framework of the early journalism. Applied to ecology, the same rules produce a milder and more interesting shape: not reversal but accretion, as new objects of analysis and new sciences were absorbed into a framework whose basic commitments, material production as mediation, suspicion of pastoral romance, remained in place. Accretion is harder to dramatise than reversal, which is one reason the ecological case has been oversold by friends and undersold by enemies, both preferring the cleaner story. Method exists to protect subjects from their narrators' preferences. The protection extends to the narrator of this article, whose grading can be audited against the table of exhibits by any reader willing to do the work, and whose errors, where they exist, will be errors of row and weight rather than of loyalty. That is the most any single treatment can promise, and it is enough.

## The critics outside the tradition

Ecological criticism of Marx has also arrived from outside Marxism entirely, and fairness requires distinguishing its voices from the internal critics who have occupied most of this article. Liberal and conservative commentators have often treated the productivist passages as a confession, evidence that the doctrine's inner logic tends toward despoliation wherever it governs. That argument moves from text to regime without passing through the mediations, institutions, incentives, geopolitical pressures, that any serious account of the twentieth century requires, and this article's quarantine of the record question applies to it in full: the record of states is examined elsewhere in this series on its own evidence, and cannot be imported here as a verdict on manuscripts. Green theorists outside the tradition have made the subtler argument that any anthropocentric framework, Marxist or liberal, starts from the wrong place, since nature's value cannot depend on its relation to human projects without remaining permanently negotiable. That argument is philosophical, directed at an entire civilisation's premises, and Marx figures in it as an instance rather than as the accused. Deep ecology's founders cited him rarely; their quarrel was with industrialism as such, a category in which they placed his century's liberals and socialists side by side.

Distinguishing the quarrels clarifies what each can establish. The philosophical quarrel about anthropocentrism cannot be settled by any quantity of Marx quotation, because its terms are not textual; readers must decide, on grounds of ethics and metaphysics, whether an ecology of interchange suffices or whether standing independent of all interchange is required. The textual quarrel, by contrast, is settlable in principle and has been substantially settled in fact by the archival work of recent decades, whatever the public argument's lag. The regime quarrel belongs to political history. Public discourse profits from conflating all three, since conflation lets every participant win the quarrel they are actually fighting while appearing to fight the same one. Analytical writing profits from separation, and the separation practised across this cluster, record in one article, limits in another, texts here, is the series' standing answer to the conflation. Readers who carry the three quarrels apart will find most commentary on the subject, friendly and hostile alike, quietly rearranges itself.

One more outside voice deserves its paragraph: the working conservationists and land managers of the period itself. Foresters in Germany and India, sanitary engineers in London, and agricultural improvers across Europe were developing practical sciences of sustained yield, watershed protection, and soil management during the very decades Marx wrote, largely outside political economy and largely unread by its practitioners. Their existence matters to the grading in a quiet way. It shows the period contained the materials for an ecology more systematic than the one Marx assembled, assembled elsewhere by men with narrower theoretical ambitions and closer daily contact with managed land. Judged against Marsh or against the forestry literature, the corpus is a partial achievement. Judged against the political economy of its day, which had almost nothing to say about material cycles at all, the same corpus is a striking exception. Both judgements are fair, and a complete verdict holds them together, which is less a compromise than a description of where, in the ecology of nineteenth century knowledge, this particular body of work actually sat: downstream of the chemists, upstream of the economists, and off to the side of the foresters, in a channel of its own.

## Reading the silences responsibly

Silence is evidence, but it is treacherous evidence, and the inventory discipline of this article obliges a short treatise on handling it. An author's silence on a subject can mean ignorance, indifference, disagreement too complete to require statement, or a judgement that the subject belongs to another inquiry. Distinguishing those meanings requires context: was the subject visible in the author's milieu, did correspondents raise it, do notebooks skirt it, does the system generate expectations about it? Extinction, to revisit an earlier example, was visible in the period's press, and the corpus's silence on it therefore counts, modestly, against claims of comprehensive ecological attention. Atmospheric composition, by contrast, was barely visible as a policy object to anyone before the twentieth century, and silence there counts for almost nothing in either direction. The rule is symmetrical: advocates may not fill silences with imaginary sympathy, and critics may not fill them with imaginary contempt. What fills a silence responsibly is a probability, stated as one, with the visibility test shown.

A second treachery concerns the silences of editors and translators, which masquerade as the author's. Passages omitted from older editions, letters published selectively, notebooks left in drawers for a century: each shaped the corpus that earlier verdicts judged. The history of this dispute cannot be told without those mediations, since the ecological case strengthened in exact proportion as the archive completed itself, a correlation that should make every participant humble about the finality of present verdicts. Somewhere in the remaining unpublished material there may sit passages that embarrass the grading offered here in either direction. Scholarship that acknowledges that possibility in print is not being weak; it is being accurate about the difference between a living archive and a closed canon. The verdict of this article is therefore issued with its falsification conditions attached, as an earlier section specified, and readers are invited to hold the author to them.

## The question as a test of intellectual honesty

Step back from the particulars and notice what kind of exercise this question has been. A large, dated, multi genre corpus; two anthologies in collision; structural arguments about what the corpus's concepts permit; new evidence arriving from an archive still in publication; reputations, political identities, and examination syllabi all invested in the outcome. Under those conditions, the quality of a contribution is measured less by its verdict than by its conduct: whether it inventories before it interprets, attributes before it answers, concedes before it counterattacks, and grades before it concludes. Those conducts are teachable, examinable, and transferable, which is why the question has become a fixture of serious courses despite, or because of, its resistance to settlement. The corpus helps the teaching by being genuinely double. A corpus that clearly supported one side would test only memory. This one tests judgement, and judgement is the scarcest commodity in public argument about any thinker whose name has become a flag.

There is, finally, a reflexive point that a long treatment owes its readers. This article has its own selections, emphases, and silences, governed by the brief of a series and by the judgement of its author, and a sufficiently determined critic could assemble an anthology against its grading from the same archive. The defence against that critic is not innocence but apparatus: the table, the dates, the genres, the named objectors at full strength, the falsification conditions, the concessions itemised rather than implied. Apparatus does not guarantee truth. It guarantees that error, where it exists, can be located and repaired without demolishing the whole structure, which is the most that prose about a contested past can honestly guarantee. Readers who finish the article and reach a different grading, exhibit by exhibit, have not failed the exercise. They have completed it.

## The Irish evidence and the education of a theorist

Ireland recurs in the later Marx as a laboratory where land, tenure, and empire intersected, and the Irish material forms a bridge between this cluster and the imperialism cluster that a thorough treatment should cross. In the eighteen sixties Marx immersed himself in Irish history and in the reports of commissions on land occupation, arriving at judgements that reversed his earlier assumptions about the relation between English and Irish emancipation. Landlordism in Ireland, as he analysed it, combined rack renting, insecure tenure, eviction, and a systematic discouragement of tenant improvement, with consequences that included soil neglect: a tenant who may be evicted without compensation for improvements does not manure, drain, or plant for the long term. Property form thus appears, in the Irish analysis, as an ecological variable, governing whether the person working land has reason to husband it. That is the metabolic argument's logic operating in a political register, and it predates some of the canonical soil passages in emphasis if not in print. Historians of the Irish case debate details, as they debate everything about that island's nineteenth century, but the structural point survives the debates: by the end of the eighteen sixties, the analysis of land in this corpus habitually joined questions of fertility, tenure, and power in a single frame.

The Irish immersion also corrects a possible misreading of the developmental thesis. Development in Marx's case was not a serene unfolding of implications but a series of collisions with recalcitrant evidence, Ireland among them, in which cherished schemas broke and were rebuilt. The 1853 journalism on India had fitted colonial violence into a schema of historical stages; the Irish work of the sixties fits no such comfort, assigning agency, blame, and remedy in ways the schema would not have predicted. Scholars of the colonialism question have traced the parallel transformation in the Indian materials and the correspondence on Algeria and Russia. Ecology was one strand of a general late loosening, in which the mature thinker repeatedly subordinated inherited stage logic to the evidence of particular lands and peoples. Recognising the pattern matters for grading: the ecological passages belong to a mind demonstrably capable of revision under evidence, which makes their direction more significant, not less, since revision was the author's practised habit rather than an accident of mood.

There is an evidential lesson here for readers of any prolific figure. The best predictor of what a thinker would have done with another decade is what the thinker did with the previous one when confronted by facts that hurt. On that test, the late Marx scores well: Ireland hurt the schema, and the schema moved. Russia hurt it again, and it moved again, into drafts of extraordinary tentativeness. The ecological notebooks belong to the same biography of collisions, chemistry's bookkeeping against political economy's abstractions. None of this licenses prophecy about a finished ecology in some unwritten future. It does make the counterfactual sneer, that nothing would have changed, look like the least historical sentence in the entire dispute.

## Succession: who owns the question now

Intellectual questions outlive their first disputants and acquire custodians, and the custodianship of this one has passed through several hands whose interests shaped the answers each era received. Party schools held it first and answered by catechism. University Marxology held it next and answered, mostly, by changing the subject to alienation. The green movement held it in the seventies and answered by indictment, the recovery scholars of the nineties answered by archive, and the present custodians, a professionalised field spanning journals in several languages, answer increasingly by subdivision: the question has been decomposed into sub questions about value, about labour, about the notebooks, about the late politics, each with its own literature and its own standards. Decomposition is what maturity looks like in a research field, and also what the death of a public question looks like, since publics cannot follow subdivisions. Both descriptions are true, and the tension between them is precisely the gap a series like this one is built to bridge: to carry the subdivisions back into a single legible account without surrendering their distinctions.

Custodianship also raises the question of who the verdict is for. Examination candidates need a portable grading. Teachers need a seminar that catches fire. Researchers need the open problems itemised. Journalists need a defensible summary that will survive an editor's compression. General readers need, perhaps above all, permission to find a hero complicated: permission this article has tried to grant by example, showing that a thinker can be early and late, mistaken and penetrating, anthropocentric and, in the rent chapters, suddenly and juridically tender toward the claims of the unborn, all inside one bibliography. The culture of commentary rarely grants that permission, preferring its figures laminated. Lamination is a preservative that kills the specimen. The alternative practised here, inventory, chronology, grading, is slower and less quotable, and it is the only treatment a real archive deserves.

A last custodial duty concerns the dead themselves, who cannot correct their interpreters. Marx is unusually defenceless and unusually defended at once: defenceless because the archive's size guarantees that misquotation can hide in it for decades, defended because a small army of editors has devoted lifetimes to making the hiding harder. The MEGA editors, the translators, the bibliographers, the keepers of the Amsterdam archive where so many notebooks rest, are the unglamorous heroes of every claim in this article, and a reader who finishes it with more respect for apparatus than for verdicts has understood its deepest commitment. Verdicts will be revised. Apparatus endures, and the next revision will be built by hands that the apparatus serves.

## Worked example: how to grade a single passage

Abstract method persuades fewer readers than demonstration, so consider one passage graded in full view: the sentence in the first volume where capitalist production is said to develop technology and combination while simultaneously undermining the original sources of all wealth, the soil and the worker. Begin with provenance. The sentence stands in the first volume's chapter on industry and agriculture at large scale, in a volume its author published in 1867 and revised for subsequent editions and for the French translation he supervised closely; provenance grade, highest available. Continue with context: the surrounding pages argue the nutrient argument with agricultural evidence, name the town country separation as mechanism, and cite the chemistry of the period; context grade, analytical rather than rhetorical. Continue with function: the sentence summarises an argument rather than decorating one, and its pairing of soil and worker exhibits the pattern, drive against natural condition, that this article has tracked across the corpus; function grade, load bearing. Continue with corroboration: the rent chapters, the correction of 1875, and the notebooks all lean the same way within the following decade and a half; corroboration grade, strong. Finish with the counterweights: the sentence announces no limits doctrine, proposes regulation only in embryo elsewhere, and coexists with productivist commitments its author never formally withdrew; counterweight grade, real but bounded.

The exercise yields a compound judgement, high confidence ecological analysis of a specific mechanism, inside a corpus lacking the general theory, and the compound judgement is neither a fudge nor a split difference: it is what the five grades, each defensible separately, sum to. Multiply the exercise across the inventory and the extension thesis is simply the sum at corpus scale. Critics who wish to overturn the sum must overturn grades, passage by passage, which is slow work and has been attempted in the journals with partial successes that this article has recorded. That is how the dispute should proceed, and its proceeding so, when it does, is the best evidence that the field's centre of gravity has moved from loyalty to method. Students sometimes ask why a single sentence deserves five grades of ceremony. The answer is that sentences are where history hides: in their dates, their chapters, their editions, their neighbours. Ceremony is attention wearing its formal clothes.

A contrasting demonstration sharpens the point. Take the Manifesto's subjection phrase through the same five grades. Provenance: a jointly authored pamphlet of 1848, written for a league of exiles in a revolutionary season; grade, genuine but occasional. Context: a wonders list inside a dialectical pamphlet that elsewhere indicts the same class's treatment of workers and colonies; grade, rhetorical. Function: persuasion and recruitment, the sentence exists to exhilarate; grade, decorative relative to doctrine. Corroboration: the Grundrisse's barrier language leans the same way into the eighteen fifties, then the leaning reverses; grade, mixed and time bound. Counterweights: the author's own later work qualifies the stance substantially; grade, heavy. The compound judgement, real productivist commitment, period bound and later qualified, again sums grades rather than allegiances. Two passages, two compounds, one corpus: the method that produces those compounds without strain is the method the whole article recommends, and a reader who has followed both demonstrations owns the method outright.

## The verdict restated without apparatus

Strip away tables, grades, and the long parade of objectors, and the answer to the title's question fits in a paragraph a school student could memorise, which is as it should be, since apparatus exists to earn paragraphs like this one. Marx was not an ecological thinker in the sense of possessing a finished doctrine of nature and its limits; nothing in forty years of writing assembles into such a doctrine, and the century's ecological agenda, extinction, preservation, nature's standing, largely passed him by. He was also not the heedless productivist of the caricature; the mature economics analyses how a mode of production tears the material circuits it depends on, corrects his own movement's slogan in nature's favour, studies agricultural science for decades, and instructs every society that it holds the earth merely in trust for its successors. He became, across a working life, something more specific than either label: a critic of political economy whose categories opened a space for ecological analysis, who stepped into that space himself in his final two decades, and who left the building of the rest to others. Others have been building since, in his name and against it, and the building continues.

## The Grundrisse reconsidered: capital's drive and nature's barriers

Because the Grundrisse carries so much prosecutorial weight, its treatment here deserves a supplement devoted to the manuscripts themselves as physical and intellectual objects. Written in London across the winter of 1857 and 1858, in seven notebooks of ferocious density, the manuscripts were Marx's attempt to draft the whole critique of political economy at speed, triggered by an economic crisis he expected to be politically explosive. They were not prepared for publication, contain passages their author plainly regarded as scaffolding, and reached print only in the twentieth century, in Moscow in 1939 to 1941 and in the West after the war. Their late arrival shaped their role in the dispute: generations formed their Marx without them, and their eventual appearance armed both camps with fresh quotations, the prosecution with barrier dissolution, the defence with the sections on pre capitalist formations, where communal property, land as the original condition of production, and the individual's relation to the earth as to his own inorganic body receive sustained analysis. A manuscript that supplies both camps is not being misread by both; it is a genuinely divided text, the record of a mind working out positions it had not yet stabilised, and the honest treatment is to report the division with the seams showing.

The pre capitalist formations material rewards the ecological reader in one specific way. There, land appears not primarily as a factor of production but as the presupposition of community itself: the earth as the great laboratory and arsenal, providing both the means and the material of labour and the site of the community's existence. Property, in those pages, is originally the relation of the working individual to the natural conditions of labour as belonging to him through the community. Whatever else that analysis does, it refuses to treat the human relation to land as a late contractual arrangement laid over a natural state of separation; belonging to the earth through a social form is the starting point, and private property's dissolution of that belonging is a historical event with costs the text enumerates. Set beside the 1844 language of the inorganic body, a line of thought becomes visible that the productivist anthology never mentions: from first to last, this corpus imagines human beings as placed inside nature by their mode of belonging, and argues about which modes of belonging mutilate the placement. That is not an ecology. It is a persistent metaphysical backdrop against which the later, properly ecological analyses make a different kind of sense than they would as isolated inspirations, and intellectual historians are right to insist on the backdrop while resisting, as this article does, its promotion into a doctrine.

Barrier language requires the same two sided patience. When the Grundrisse describes capital's drive to tear down every spatial barrier to exchange and to conquer the whole earth as its market, the sentences describe a tendency the analyst is reconstructing, in a vocabulary of admiration and horror mixed that the century used for railways and telegraphs generally. The analyst's own valuation of the tendency is given in the same notebooks' treatment of its human costs and in the published work that followed. Yet the prosecution's residual point stands after all charity is exhausted: in 1858 the collision between that drive and the earth's material constitution is not yet an object of analysis. The drive meets barriers of distance, custom, and rival property, all social or geographical in a thin sense, and surmounts them in the telling. Soil, forest, and climate as active constraints belong to the next decade's work. Chronology, once again, is the difference between a thinker convicted and a thinker caught mid journey, and the Grundrisse, properly dated and properly genred, is the single best exhibit for the journey interpretation in the whole archive, since its division is internal and datable to a single winter.

## Fragment on the method of this article

Readers who have travelled this far may value a compact statement of the method used, both to audit the result and to reuse the method elsewhere. Claims were sorted into three kinds before any were weighed: claims about sentences, claims about concepts, and claims about the man. Sentences are settled by quotation with provenance. Concepts are settled by examining what work they do across the system, where Benton and Schmidt pitched their arguments and where replies must therefore pitch theirs. The man is settled, in so far as he can be settled at all, by trajectory: dated movement across a career, read in the genres he actually wrote. Most published confusion on this question is kind confusion, a sentence claim answered by a concept argument, a trajectory observation answered by a regime photograph. Sorting first is unglamorous and decisive, rather like the bibliographical disciplines recommended earlier; the glamorous parts of scholarship usually turn out, on inspection, to be sorting performed with unusual thoroughness and then narrated well.

Two further rules governed the weighing. Charity was applied symmetrically: each side's best formulation was answered, and each side's weakest popularisation was noted only to be set aside, since defeating a weak version of an opponent proves nothing except the defeater's aim. And conclusions were capped by evidence type: notebook material could support claims about engagement and direction, never, alone, claims about doctrine; journalism could support claims about stance at a date, never, alone, claims about system. Caps of that kind disappoint readers who want a coronation or an execution. They are, however, what separates a verdict that can survive the next archival release from one that will be quietly pulped by it, and the history of this dispute is littered with pulped certainties on both sides, a litter the present article has no ambition to increase.

## A brief audit of this article's own claims

Self audit is a fitting penultimate exercise for a treatment that has audited everyone else. Take the article's three load bearing assertions in turn and ask what each rests on, so that a reader can see the joints. The first assertion is chronological: productivist exhibits cluster early, ecological exhibits late. Its support is the dated inventory, anchored at both ends by indisputable publications, 1848 and 1875, with the middle anchored by the 1867 volume. Its vulnerability is redating of drafts, above all the third volume's composition, which editorial scholarship places in the middle eighteen sixties; a redating radical enough to matter would have to move a treatise across decades, and no plausible scholarship proposes that. Confidence, high. The second assertion is conceptual: metabolism does theoretical work in the mature economics, naming an exchange whose rupture the text analyses with period evidence. Its support is the soil argument's mechanism and specificity, together with the concept's recurrence in the regulation passage and the rent analysis. Its vulnerability is the metaphor reading, the claim that the term ornaments rather than organises; the nutrient itineraries traced earlier are the standing reply, since ornaments do not generate checkable claims about guano imports and sewage commissions. Confidence, substantial.

The third assertion is negative: no theory of natural limits exists in the corpus. Negative assertions rest on the state of the archive and the failure of motivated search, and both supports are unusually strong here, since two generations of sympathetic scholars, with careers to gain from finding such a theory, have produced fragments and gestures instead. Absence of evidence becomes evidence of absence precisely when the search has been eager, expert, and rewarded, conditions this dispute satisfies completely. Its vulnerability is the unpublished residue of the archive, acknowledged in the falsification conditions, and that vulnerability is priced into the verdict's wording: the claim is about the corpus in its present state, and that present state is the only basis on which anyone can be graded. Confidence, high for the present archive, openly revisable for the future one. An article that prices its own confidence in this way asks to be read as scholarship rather than as brief writing, and submits to the same grading it has applied to Benton, to Schmidt, to Burkett, and to the recovery at large.

What remains after the audit is not certainty but proportion, and proportion turns out to be the article's true subject wearing the ecological question as its occasion. Two bodies of text, unevenly weighted, unevenly dated, pulling a large framework in two directions while the framework's author revised, studied, and died with the tension unresolved: that is the finding. Every slogan in the dispute is a refusal of that finding's proportions. Every serious contribution to the literature, from Schmidt's patience to Benton's severity to Burkett's architecture, is an acceptance of them under one description or another. The extension not possession thesis is this article's description, offered with its evidence attached and its confidence priced, in the hope that readers will do to it exactly what it has taught them to do to every other claim in the field: ask for the text, the date, and the genre, and grade accordingly.

## Why this question will be asked again

Forecasting the afterlife of a scholarly question is a parlour game with one reliable rule: questions return when the world supplies new readers with new reasons to ask them. The ecological question about Marx has already returned three times, with the environmental movement, with the recovery scholarship, and with the entry of ecological Marxism into examination syllabi, and each return found the archive slightly larger and the grading slightly finer. A fourth return is easy to imagine in outline if impossible to date: some new edition, some new translation of the notebooks, some new theoretical need arising from debates about planning and provision that are already audible at the field's edges, will send another generation back to the rent chapters with fresh eyes. They will find the trusteeship passage where it has always been, the soil argument still warm from Liebig's century, the Manifesto still exhilarating and still embarrassing, and Benton's objection still standing in the doorway asking its unanswered question about limits. If this article survives into that return, its ambition is modest: to be the map those readers start from and then correct, row by row, as maps are meant to be corrected. A map that invites correction has done its work. A map that demands belief has done something else, and the history of this question shows, in unusual detail, exactly what that something else costs.

## The question in other languages and other syllabi

Arguments change shape as they cross languages, and this one has crossed several. German scholarship inherited Schmidt and a philosophical vocabulary in which nature, domination, and technology had been entangled since the Frankfurt School's wartime writings; its debates therefore leaned conceptual from the start, asking what kind of materialism could think nature without either worshipping or dissolving it. Anglophone debate, by contrast, grew out of journals of politics and sociology and leaned archival, asking what the manuscripts contain and in which years they were written. French discussion brought its own productivist inheritance to the table, since the orthodoxies of the postwar French left had canonised a developmental Marx with unusual thoroughness, and French ecological readers had first to excavate their subject from under that canon. Japanese scholarship contributed the most consequential archival work of recent decades through the notebooks, a reminder that the geography of Marx studies no longer matches the geography of his life. None of these traditions is simply ahead of the others. Each asks the question its own history taught it to ask, and the composite picture, conceptual in one register, documentary in another, is richer than any national version, provided readers know which register they are in.

Syllabi tell a parallel story in compressed form. Where ecological Marxism appears as a named topic, in politics and sociology options across several university systems and in the political science syllabi of South Asian civil service examinations, the set texts cluster predictably: a chapter of the first volume, the Gotha critique, Benton's essay, a recovery text, and a critic of the recovery. That cluster is, in effect, the dispute's official portrait of itself, and students who read the cluster whole receive a fairer education than students anywhere received a generation ago, when the same syllabi offered the Manifesto and the Preface and called the subject covered. Examination design has quietly become one of the recovery's most durable achievements, more durable perhaps than any single monograph, because it manufactures readers for the primary texts at scale, year after year, each cohort arriving at the archive with the chronological question already in hand. Whatever verdict those readers reach, they reach it after the rent chapters, which is all the recovery ever needed to ask.

## A compact rehearsal of the whole case

For readers who prefer their arguments in marching order, the case can be rehearsed in a single sequence without loss of the gradings that matter. A young journalist philosopher of the 1840s, formed in a culture intoxicated by steam, praised the bourgeoisie's conquest of natural forces and built a philosophy of history on expanding production. A researcher of the 1850s, drafting his critique of political economy in private notebooks, described capital's drive to dissolve every barrier while beginning, in the same notebooks, to anatomise land and communal property with new care. An economist of the 1860s, publishing the first volume of that critique, analysed how the system he was dissecting tears the nutrient circuit between countryside and city, and drafted rent chapters in which no society is permitted to call itself owner of the earth. A veteran of the 1870s corrected his own movement's founding slogan in nature's favour, filled notebooks with agricultural chemistry, and, in his final years, refused to sentence a Russian commune to dissolution by historical law. A critic's generation later found the labour process concept too narrow for the analyses its author had built with it, and said so in an essay of 1989 that the field still answers. A recovery generation answered with the archive, proving attention, mechanism, and obligation, and overreached when it claimed a finished doctrine. The grading that survives all of it is the one this article has defended from its first page: foundations present, construction begun by the founder's own hand, completion absent, extension possible and partly performed by successors who say plainly that the building is theirs.

Rehearsal has a pedagogical point beyond convenience. Sequence is the argument's skeleton: remove the dates and the case collapses into the anthology war with which this article began, each side quoting truly and concluding falsely. Restore the dates and the same quotations compose a biography, partial and checkable, in which a mind can be seen enlarging its object under the pressure of evidence. Examinations reward that restoration; scholarship requires it; and readers who practise it on this corpus will find, as promised at the outset, that the habit transfers to every contested figure whose afterlife is fought over by anthologists. The transfer is the deepest justification for the space this question occupies in the series, outranking even the intrinsic interest of the verdict, considerable as that is.

## A note on the word green and other shorthand

Shorthand deserves a final audit, because shorthand is where careful gradings go to die. Green, applied to a nineteenth century figure, compresses at least four separable judgements: sensitivity to damaged landscapes, analytical grasp of material interdependence, willingness to restrain production for the sake of conditions it depends upon, and allegiance to a politics organised around those commitments. A writer can score high on the second and low on the fourth without any contradiction, since political movements of that kind did not exist to be joined during the decades in question. Compression hides which judgement a speaker intends, and polemic exploits the hiding: an advocate proves the second and banks the fourth, a critic disproves the fourth and banks the second. Unpacking the shorthand in public, each time, is tedious. It is also the entire difference between a literature and a shouting match, and this article has chosen tedium wherever the choice arose.

Kindred shorthand litters the field and merits the same handling. Productivist, used loosely, can mean a technological enthusiast, a theorist of growth as historical engine, an administrator indifferent to damage, or a metaphysician for whom nature exists only as raw stock; the corpus under discussion matches some of those descriptions at some dates and none of them at all dates. Anthropocentric can mean the banal truth that human writers write from a human standpoint, or the stronger charge that nothing non human can register as a value within the system; the first is true of every author in the archive and the second is the live philosophical dispute that Schmidt's work frames. Even ecology itself, the word in the title, shifts between a science founded after the subject's death, a sensibility visible in several of his contemporaries, and a standard against which later readers grade earlier ones. Writers who announce which sense they mean, at the moment they mean it, will find their opponents' best arguments waiting for them at the right address instead of at a decoy. That courtesy, extended in both directions, is the cheapest improvement available to the whole controversy, and syllabi that teach it are teaching the controversy better than syllabi that teach any verdict.

There is a temptation, having unpacked every shorthand in sight, to abandon shorthand altogether and write in specifications forever. Resist it. Compression is how thought travels, and a grading that cannot survive compression into a sentence will never reach the readers who need it. The discipline recommended here is not abstinence but labelling: compress boldly, and keep the unpacking attached, the way medicines carry their ingredients on the bottle. Extension not possession is itself a compression, four words carrying a table of exhibits, a chronology, two structural objections, a recovery audit, and a concession about limits. Its author offers it in that spirit, as a label whose contents have been printed in full on the preceding pages, available for inspection by any reader who suspects the bottle is empty. Bottles in this dispute are so often empty, on every shelf, that the suspicion is honourable and the inspection is the point.

One practical consequence follows for anyone citing this dispute in print, in a classroom, or in an examination hall. Cite the inventory, not the atmosphere. A claim about the Manifesto should carry its year and its genre; a claim about trusteeship should carry its chapter and its drafting history; a claim about limits should carry Benton's name and the year of his essay, so that a reader can tell a structural objection from a mood. Claims carried in that fashion can be checked in an afternoon by a sceptic with a library card, and the checkability is not a pedantic ornament on the argument but the argument's warranty. Writing that refuses the warranty, on whichever side, should be read the way consumers read any unwarranted goods: with pleasure where pleasure is offered, and without reliance. The corpus at the centre of the dispute has survived a century and a half of warranted and unwarranted claims alike, and will survive the present generation of both; the only question its custodians control is how much of the surviving is understood.

Teachers assigning this article alongside the primary texts sometimes ask what a student should be able to do after reading both that the student could not do before. The answer is a performance rather than an opinion: confronted by any fresh quotation from the corpus, on either side of the question, the student should be able to place it by year and by genre, state which level of claim it can support, name the strongest objection it must survive, and assign it a grade that a hostile marker could inspect. A student who can perform that operation four times in an hour, on passages never seen in the seminar, has learned the topic in the only sense examinations can certify. Opinions about Victorian economists are perishable, and the syllabi that certify them are revised by committees whose members were themselves examined on older certainties. Operations of that kind keep, travelling from topic to topic and from decade to decade, indifferent to fashion and useful in every archive a reader will ever open.

A closing observation concerns the readers this dispute keeps producing. Every cohort that works through the inventory arrives, by the end, at a noticeably calmer relation to the figure at its centre: admiration survives where the texts earn it, criticism sharpens where the concepts require it, and the urge to recruit the dead man into a present quarrel loses its urgency somewhere around the rent chapters. That calm is not indifference. It is the specific peace of a question that has been taken apart properly and put back together with its tensions labelled rather than soldered over. Readers carry the labelled tensions away with them, into arguments about other thinkers and other centuries, and find the labels fit there too. Fields advance one labelled tension at a time, and the advance is rarely announced by the people making it, who are usually too busy with the next archive box, the next recalcitrant passage, the next objection that deserves, as this one did, to be stated at full strength before it is answered.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Was Marx an ecological thinker?

Partly, and increasingly across his career. Early writings celebrate the subjection of natural forces and the expansion of production, while the mature writings examine broken nutrient circuits, correct the claim that labour alone creates wealth, and describe societies as trustees of the earth for generations still unborn. The defensible verdict is graded: his framework accommodates ecological analysis, he had begun performing that analysis in his economics and notebooks, and he completed no systematic theory of natural limits. Any answer flatter than that, in either direction, has discarded part of the dated evidence.

### Q: What is the Promethean reading of Marx?

The Promethean reading portrays Marx as a celebrant of humanity's dominion over nature, named for the mythic fire stealer who defied divine limits. Its evidence is genuine: a famous pamphlet praising subjugated natural forces, manuscripts portraying capital battering down every barrier, a short Preface of 1859 whose engine is productive development, and correspondence impatient with romantic nature worship. The portrait persuades for the writings of the eighteen forties. It falters before the mature economics, where soil analysis and trusteeship reshape the figure beyond recognition.

### Q: What did Marx say about nature and wealth?

In marginal notes written in 1875 on a German party programme, he rejected the slogan that labour is the source of all wealth, insisting nature supplies use values just as surely as work does. Material wealth consists of useful things, and useful things arise from natural material shaped by labour; a programme that forgets nature misleads workers twice over, about where wealth originates and about why access to land and raw materials matters. The correction matters in the ecological dispute because it shows its author policing, in his own hand, the boundary between wealth, in which nature figures completely, and value, in which capital declines to reckon it.

### Q: What is the usufruct passage?

The usufruct passage is a paragraph in Capital's third volume, composed during the 1860s and issued in 1894, which denies that any society, or every society combined, owns the earth. Societies appear instead as possessors and beneficiaries, holding the planet as careful householders hold an inheritance, bound to transmit it improved to the generations that come after. Usufruct is the legal term for use without ownership, and the passage generalises it to the species across time. Advocates regard it as the most powerful ecological paragraph its author wrote; critics owe an account of its juridical precision rather than a dismissal of it as decoration.

### Q: Does the labour theory of value ignore nature?

The charge says that a theory counting only labour in value renders nature invisible, so damage registers only when it raises costs. Paul Burkett's reply, developed in Marx and Nature in 1999, turns the charge around: value is a specifically capitalist form, and nature's exclusion from it is the analysis of capital's own blindness, since capital treats natural gifts as free until property and labour make them saleable. Marx policed the distinction himself in 1875, separating wealth, where nature counts, from value. The reply answers the value charge effectively, though it does not by itself supply a theory of natural limits.

### Q: Did Marx's views on nature change over time?

Substantially, though without any dramatic recantation. Celebrations of mastery cluster in the eighteen forties and fifties. Metabolic analysis appears in the published economics of 1867, the trusteeship passage belongs to the drafts of the eighteen sixties, the wealth correction dates from 1875, and the natural science notebooks deepen through the final years. Continuities persist throughout: material production mediates every relation with the natural world, and pastoral nostalgia never appealed to him at any age. The pattern is development within a framework, not conversion from one creed to another.

### Q: What did the natural science notebooks reveal?

The notebooks, published gradually through the historical critical edition of the complete works, reveal decades of sustained study across the chemistry of agriculture, geology, botany, and the science of soils. Marx extracted Liebig and many less famous writers, followed debates on fertiliser, forest clearance, and irrigation, studied Russian so as to read agronomic material at first hand, and intensified this research during his final period rather than letting it lapse. Notebook evidence requires care, since excerpting proves reading rather than agreement, but the volume, duration, and fit with the analyses of metabolism and of rent issued in his lifetime testify to a preoccupation no account of the mature work can ignore.

### Q: Is the ecological reading of Marx overclaimed?

In its popular form, frequently. Assertions that Marx commanded a finished ecology, foresaw the findings of later environmental science, or settled the problem of limits go beyond anything the manuscripts contain, and the metabolic rift, a concept built around nutrient flows linking countryside and city, is sometimes stretched into a universal key for every kind of environmental damage without the case by case defence such stretching requires. Inflation harms the solid findings bundled with it, because scholars outside the recovery project discount the soil analysis and the trusteeship passage alongside the prophecies. The graded core withstands scrutiny; the swollen shell does not, and telling them apart is the whole argument of this article.

### Q: Which texts support the productivist reading?

The prosecution rests chiefly on four exhibits. A pamphlet of 1848 celebrates the subjection of natural forces among the bourgeoisie's wonders. Manuscripts of 1857 and 1858 describe capital dissolving natural and social barriers as part of its civilising drive. A short Preface of 1859 makes productive development the engine of historical change without registering natural limits. Journalism of the eighteen fifties, including the articles on India, subordinates land and irrigation to a story of staged historical progress. Together they establish a real productivist body of text, concentrated early, which any ecological reading must absorb rather than evade.

### Q: What is the strongest position on this question?

The strongest position is the extension not possession thesis. Marx's framework can be extended ecologically without contradiction, because its central category of metabolism names the material exchange between society and nature and its mature chapters analyse that exchange's disruption. He had begun the extension himself, in the soil analysis, the wealth correction of 1875, the trusteeship passage, and decades of scientific notebooks. He did not complete a systematic ecology: no doctrine of limits, and no reconciliation of expanding needs with finite conditions, appears anywhere in the corpus. That verdict claims less than recovery rhetoric claims and more than a productivist dismissal allows, and it fits the dated evidence.

### Q: How does the extension not possession thesis grade the evidence?

It assigns each exhibit the weight it can bear and no more. Passages celebrating mastery establish the early productivism of their author. Passages analysing nutrient cycles establish that the mature economics performed ecological analysis of a major material exchange. The Gotha correction establishes a principled distinction between wealth and value. The trusteeship passage establishes an intergenerational ethic. The notebooks establish sustained scientific engagement. No exhibit, alone or in combination, amounts to a finished doctrine of limits, so the thesis concludes that an extension was begun rather than an ecology possessed, and treats any stronger claim as a request for evidence its advocates have not produced.

### Q: What should a student separate between the ecological passages and a systematic ecology?

A student should separate evidence of attention from evidence of architecture. The ecological passages show their author noticing, analysing, and condemning specific disruptions: soil robbery, the separation of town and country, the treatment of nature as a free gift. A systematic ecology would require more: a general concept of natural limit, a way for that concept to register inside economic analysis, an institutional account of how producers would respect it, and a reconciliation between expanding human needs and finite conditions. The corpus supplies the passages and fragments toward the architecture. Examiners reward students who mark the distance between the two instead of collapsing it in either direction.

### Q: Why do the two bodies of text point in different directions?

Because a long writing life intervened between them. Marx began inside the productivist consensus of the eighteen forties, when industrial expansion appeared to nearly every progressive thinker as history's engine. Research for his critique of political economy then forced sustained attention to land, fertility, and the material conditions of production, while agricultural chemistry under Liebig supplied new concepts and new alarms. Political questions about Ireland and Russia pressed agrarian evidence onto his desk in his final decades. The two bodies of text record that itinerary. Divergence is what an honest archive of a developing mind looks like, and simultaneity was never a reasonable expectation.

### Q: How do the two bodies of text order the case for and against?

The table orders the case chronologically, one row per passage, with columns for what each passage says, which reading it supports, its context, and whether later work qualifies it. Read down the date column and the pattern emerges: productivist exhibits cluster before 1860, ecological exhibits from the decade after. Read down the context column and a second pattern emerges: the weight of genre grows with time, from a pamphlet written with a collaborator and newspaper pieces toward a treatise revised for the press, notes on a party programme, and shelf upon shelf of notebooks. Ordering the evidence this way converts a shouting match between anthologies into an inspectable inventory.

### Q: What separates extending Marx's framework from finding a finished ecology in it?

The difference is the difference between foundations and a building. To extend a framework is to show that its categories can host ecological analysis, metabolism above all, that its author's late work began such analysis, and that repairs, such as Benton's proposed revision of the labour process concept, are performed openly by successors and credited as theirs. Finding a finished ecology claims the building stands complete: a doctrine of limits, an account of nature's standing, a politics of restraint, all present in the manuscripts awaiting only transcription. No scholar has produced that doctrine from the texts, because it is not there. Extension is the claim the evidence grades as true.
