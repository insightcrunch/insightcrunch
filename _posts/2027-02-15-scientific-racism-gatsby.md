Ten pages into The Great Gatsby, before Gatsby himself has said a word, a wealthy man at a dinner table leans forward and announces that the white race is about to be submerged. This is where scientific racism and Tom's books enter the novel. Tom Buchanan is not raving. He is citing a book. He believes he is passing along settled knowledge, the kind that has been "proved," and he expects the table to be grateful for the education. The moment is easy to skim past as one more sign that Tom is a boor. Read it that way and you miss the point Fitzgerald built into it. The pseudo-science behind Tom's dinner-table lecture is not a throwaway character note. It is the novel pausing to show you a real ideology that gripped respectable America in the 1920s, an ideology that borrowed the prestige of the laboratory to make bigotry sound like fact, and it matters enormously to how you read Tom, the Buchanans, and the world of privilege the whole book anatomizes.

The reason this frame changes your reading is simple. If Tom's outburst is only personal nastiness, then it stays contained inside one unpleasant man. If it is the era's dominant race theory speaking through him, then his ugliness is not private eccentricity but the voice of a whole class that considered its own supremacy a scientific finding. That is the difference between a character flaw and a diagnosis of a society. Fitzgerald wrote the second thing. Recovering the real books behind Tom's half-remembered lecture lets you see it.

## The scene: Tom cites a book about the decline of his own race

The passage arrives during Nick's first dinner at the Buchanan estate in East Egg. The conversation has been drifting, and Nick, to fill a silence, asks whether they can talk about crops. Tom seizes the opening for something far larger. "Civilization's going to pieces," he breaks out. He has become, he says, a terrible pessimist about things, and he asks Nick whether he has read "The Rise of the Coloured Empires by this man Goddard." When Nick admits he has not, Tom presses on with the enthusiasm of a convert. "Well, it's a fine book, and everybody ought to read it. The idea is if we don't look out the white race will be" submerged. Then the crucial claim: "It's all scientific stuff; it's been proved."

That last sentence is the hinge of the whole moment. Tom is not offering an opinion he expects to defend. He is reporting a fact he believes lies beyond argument. When Daisy teases him, saying "Tom's getting very profound" and that "he reads deep books with long words in them," Tom does not laugh along. He insists, glancing at her impatiently, that "these books are all scientific," and that "This fellow has worked out the whole thing." Then he delivers the conclusion the book has supposedly earned him: "It's up to us, who are the dominant race, to watch out or these other races will have control of things."

A moment later he adds the racial label that gives the theory its era-specific shape. "This idea is that we're Nordics," he says, and with a small condescending nod he sorts the people at his own table into the master category, before crediting his group with having "produced all the things that go to make civilization," then "science and art, and all that." Nick watches the performance and registers something the reader is meant to catch: "There was something pathetic in his concentration, as if his complacency, more acute than of old, was not enough to him any more."

That single word, pathetic, is Fitzgerald's thumb on the scale. Nick does not argue with Tom or refute the book. He simply lets us see a large, secure, physically dominant man who needs a race theory to feel safe, and the neediness leaks through the bluster. To read the scene fully you have to know two things Nick's narration assumes and never explains: what the book actually was, and why an ordinary rich American in 1922 would treat its conclusions as proved.

## What was scientific racism in the 1920s?

### What was scientific racism in the 1920s?

Scientific racism was the practice of using the language and authority of biology, anthropology, and statistics to argue that human groups formed a fixed hierarchy of ability and worth. In the 1920s it was not a fringe belief. It was taught in universities, published by respectable houses, and cited by lawmakers as settled fact.

The movement had a long nineteenth-century pedigree in skull measurement, so-called craniometry, and in misapplied readings of evolution, but it reached a peak of cultural authority in the United States in the years just before and around the novel's composition. Its core assertions ran roughly as follows. Humanity, its theorists claimed, divided into distinct biological races. These races could be ranked by intelligence, moral character, and capacity for self-government. The ranking was hereditary and permanent, so that no amount of education or environment could lift a lower group. And the higher groups, having built what the theorists called civilization, were now endangered by the faster reproduction and rising ambition of the lower ones. Every one of those claims is false. Modern genetics recognizes no such biological ranking, and the category of race as these writers used it does not correspond to any coherent division of human heredity. But falseness did not stop the ideas from carrying the prestige of science, because they arrived wearing its costume: charts, measurements, footnotes, and confident predictions.

What made the doctrine dangerous was precisely its respectability. A crude prejudice can be dismissed as ignorance. A prejudice that comes bound between hard covers, endorsed by men with academic titles and issued by a serious publisher, is far harder for an ordinary reader to resist, because it does not feel like prejudice. It feels like being informed. Tom's phrase "it's been proved" captures the entire seduction. He is not defending a hatred. He believes he is reciting a result.

## The real books behind "Goddard": Stoddard, Grant, and the Nordic myth

Fitzgerald did not invent the book Tom cites. He fictionalized it, and only thinly. "The Rise of the Coloured Empires by this man Goddard" points unmistakably at a real bestseller of the era, Lothrop Stoddard's "The Rising Tide of Color Against White World-Supremacy," published in 1920. The echo is deliberate and pointed. Stoddard's title imagines exactly the scenario Tom fears, a global color line in which the white world, having dominated the globe, now faces the demographic and political awakening of the peoples it ruled. Tom's paraphrase, that the white race will be "utterly submerged" unless "we" watch out, compresses Stoddard's thesis into a dinner-table warning.

The surname Fitzgerald chose deepens the joke and the accusation. "Goddard" is close to Stoddard by a single letter, but it also summons Henry H. Goddard, an American psychologist who popularized intelligence testing in a hereditarian key and whose studies were used to argue that feeblemindedness ran in families and threatened the national stock. By blending the two names, Fitzgerald points not at one crank but at a whole apparatus, the popular race-panic bestseller on one side and the academic machinery of intelligence testing and heredity studies on the other. The fictional author is a composite because the ideology was a composite.

There is a third figure standing behind the scene, and he supplies the word Tom reaches for with such pride. When Tom announces "we're Nordics," he is borrowing from Madison Grant, whose 1916 book "The Passing of the Great Race" sorted Europeans into three so-called races, Nordic, Alpine, and Mediterranean, and placed the Nordic at the top as the builder of civilization and the natural ruler. Grant's framework is the direct source of Tom's self-flattering taxonomy and of his claim that his group "produced all the things that go to make civilization." The novel has Tom fuse Stoddard's global color line with Grant's internal European hierarchy, which is roughly how the ideas circulated in the popular mind, as a single vague conviction that the best people were losing ground to everyone else.

### Is the race book Tom mentions invented or based on real tracts?

It is based on real tracts, not invented. "The Rise of the Coloured Empires by this man Goddard" is a light fictionalization of Lothrop Stoddard's 1920 bestseller "The Rising Tide of Color," with the Nordic language drawn from Madison Grant's "The Passing of the Great Race" and the surname echoing the eugenicist Henry Goddard.

One detail sharpens the point and is worth carrying into any essay on the subject. Stoddard's book was published by Charles Scribner's Sons, the very house that would publish The Great Gatsby in 1925. Fitzgerald was needling his own publisher's list. The pseudo-science he mocks was not an exotic import; it sat on the same catalog that carried his novel. That proximity tells you how mainstream the ideology had become. This was not a doctrine whispered on the fringe. It was a commercial success from a prestige publisher, the kind of book a Yale man like Tom would encounter as respectable current thinking.

## How did racism borrow the authority of science?

### How did racism borrow the authority of science?

Racism borrowed scientific authority by adopting its forms: measurement, classification, statistics, and confident prediction. Writers presented ranked racial hierarchies as empirical findings rather than prejudices, cited academic credentials, and framed conclusions as proved. The costume of objectivity let old bigotries pass as neutral knowledge, which is exactly why they spread so far.

The mechanism deserves a closer look, because it is the intellectual engine of the whole phenomenon and the thing Fitzgerald is exposing. Prejudice on its own is a feeling, and feelings can be questioned. What the era's race theorists did was launder the feeling through the vocabulary of the laboratory until it came out looking like a result. They measured skulls and published the numbers. They administered intelligence tests to immigrants and army recruits and reported the scores as if they proved innate group differences rather than differences in language, schooling, and familiarity with the test. They drew family trees of so-called degenerate lineages and treated the charts as demonstrations of heredity. Each of these moves took a subjective judgment, that certain people were inferior, and dressed it in an objective procedure, so that the conclusion seemed to emerge from the data rather than from the prejudice that had shaped the data in the first place.

The result was a doctrine that felt unarguable to the people who held it. You cannot easily debate a chart. Tom's certainty, his flat assurance that the matter has "been proved," is the natural voice of someone who has mistaken a costume for a fact. He has never weighed the evidence, because in his world the evidence is not something a gentleman weighs. It is something a gentleman has been handed. The borrowed authority did the believing for him. That is the pattern this article names and returns to, the borrowed-authority pattern, in which prejudice hires the prestige of science, presents its conclusions as findings, and thereby escapes the scrutiny that a naked opinion would invite. Once you can see the pattern, you can see how it works in Tom, and you can see why Fitzgerald took such care to expose it rather than simply denounce it.

## Eugenics: the movement behind the books

The books Tom half-remembers did not float free of institutions. They were the popular literature of a broad and organized movement called eugenics, which proposed to improve the human population by encouraging reproduction among the supposedly fit and discouraging or preventing it among the supposedly unfit. In the United States of the 1910s and 1920s eugenics was not a whispered cause. It had research centers, university courses, popular exhibits at state fairs, and enthusiastic support from scientists, physicians, and reformers who considered themselves progressive. The Eugenics Record Office collected family pedigrees and lobbied for policy. Textbooks in biology explained the movement's claims to schoolchildren. Fairs held "better baby" and "fitter family" contests that judged human beings the way livestock were judged.

Two consequences of that movement matter for reading Gatsby, because they show the ideology moving from the page into law and life. The first is immigration policy. The race theories Tom recites supplied intellectual cover for the drive to restrict newcomers by national origin, and in 1924, the very year Fitzgerald was finishing the novel, Congress passed the Johnson-Reed Immigration Act, which set quotas designed to favor northern and western Europeans and to sharply cut arrivals from southern and eastern Europe while barring most Asian immigration outright. The lawmakers who built those quotas cited the same pseudo-scientific hierarchy Tom parrots, treating certain European groups as biologically superior stock worth preserving. The reader who wants the full picture of that climate should turn to the wider story of [immigration and nativism in Gatsby's America](/2027/02/08/immigration-nativism-gatsby/), which this article's narrower focus on the race books deliberately leaves to that piece.

The second consequence is even harsher. Many American states passed laws permitting the forced sterilization of people judged unfit, and the practice was upheld and expanded through the decade. The doctrine that certain lineages should not reproduce, an abstraction on Grant's pages, became surgery performed on real bodies without consent. Fitzgerald does not stage any of this in the novel, and the book is not a treatise on eugenics. But knowing that Tom's dinner-table creed shared its intellectual roots with sterilization laws and national-origin quotas changes the weight of the scene. The ideology he finds so reassuring was, in the same years, being written into statute and cut into flesh. His complacency sits on top of a machinery of real harm, and Fitzgerald's readers in 1925 would have felt the connection more sharply than a modern reader who has forgotten how respectable this thinking once was.

### Did educated Americans actually believe this pseudo-science?

Yes, widely. Scientific racism in the 1920s was mainstream among the educated. It was taught at leading universities, published by prestige houses, endorsed by credentialed scientists, and cited in Congress. Tom Buchanan, a wealthy Yale graduate, believing it as settled fact is historically accurate rather than an exaggeration of one man's ignorance.

The namable claim the decoder supports is the borrowed-authority pattern: in Gatsby, scientific racism is not raw hatred but hatred that has hired the prestige of science, and Fitzgerald exposes it not by refuting the books, which would flatter them with the dignity of debate, but by showing a foolish man reciting them badly. The exposure works through character, not lecture. That is the move worth understanding next.

## How Fitzgerald exposes the ideology through Tom's clumsiness

The most important thing to notice about the dinner scene is how the ideology is discredited. Fitzgerald never has Nick stand up and deliver a refutation. He never inserts a wise character to explain that the race theories are false. Instead he lets Tom carry them, and he lets Tom carry them badly, so that the theory sinks under the weight of the man reciting it. This is the novel's method throughout, and it is far more effective than argument would be. To argue with the books is to concede that they might be worth arguing with. To hand them to a bully who cannot keep them straight is to strip them of the one thing they craved, respectability.

Watch how the clumsiness accumulates. Tom cannot get the author's name right in a way the text quietly signals through the odd, wrong-sounding "Goddard." He cannot summarize the argument without collapsing into vague menace, "these other races will have control of things," which is not a thesis but a fear. He credits himself and his friends with producing "all the things that go to make civilization," reaching lazily for "science and art, and all that," and the trailing "and all that" gives him away completely, because a man who genuinely valued science and art would not wave at them so lazily. He needs Daisy's mocking prompt, "he reads deep books with long words in them," to reveal that the long words are the point for him. The theory flatters him, and he wants the flattery, but he has not done the reading closely enough to defend it. He has absorbed a conclusion and skipped the argument, which is exactly what borrowed authority allows.

### How does Tom's clumsiness undercut the ideology he repeats?

His clumsiness undercuts it by draining its dignity. Tom garbles the author's name, cannot state the argument without dissolving into vague menace, and treats science and art as things "and all that." A doctrine that survives only in the mouth of a man who has not understood it cannot claim the seriousness it demands.

Nick's narration seals the demolition with two touches. First there is the word pathetic, applied not to the ideas but to Tom's need for them, so that the reader's contempt is redirected from the fearsome content of the theory toward the small frightened man who leans on it. Second there is the physical framing of Tom throughout the novel, the enormous body, the cruel hands, the habit of leaning on people, which makes his fear of being "submerged" or losing "control" ring absurd. Here is the most physically dominant man in the book, secure in inherited millions, terrified that he is losing his grip. The gap between his actual power and his imagined vulnerability is where Fitzgerald plants the irony. A man who has everything, panicking that it will be taken by people who have almost nothing, is not a figure of menace to be debated. He is a figure of self-exposure to be watched. ## The Plaza outburst: the same ideology, cornered

The dinner-table lecture is not the last time Tom reaches for race panic. In the novel's climactic confrontation at the Plaza Hotel, when Gatsby and Daisy's affair is finally forced into the open, Tom's defense of his marriage swerves without warning into the same territory. Cornered, losing the argument on its actual terms, he reaches for the largest weapon he owns. "Nowadays people begin by sneering at family life and family institutions," he says, "and next they'll throw everything overboard and have intermarriage between black and white." Nick's narration lands the judgment immediately: "Flushed with his impassioned gibberish, he saw himself standing alone on the last barrier of civilization."

The word gibberish is Fitzgerald refusing to dignify the outburst, and the phrase "the last barrier of civilization" is pure Stoddard and Grant, the exact self-image the race books offered their readers, the beleaguered defender of a civilization under siege. What the scene reveals is the function of the ideology in Tom's psychology. It is not something he believes coolly at a dinner table and forgets. It is the club he grabs whenever he feels his position threatened. A man is taking his wife, so he pivots to the defense of the white race, as if the two were the same emergency. Jordan's dry murmur, "We're all white here," punctures the performance by pointing out that his supposed civilizational crisis has no basis in the room. And Nick's summary of the whole display, that "the transition from libertine to prig was so complete," catches the hypocrisy at its root, a man who keeps a mistress in the city lecturing others on the sanctity of family and race.

Set the two scenes side by side and the pattern is unmistakable. At dinner the ideology is leisure-time self-congratulation. At the Plaza it is a cornered man's last resort. In both, the race theory serves not truth but Tom, propping up a threatened ego with the borrowed authority of pseudo-science. That consistency is Fitzgerald's argument, made through structure rather than statement: the doctrine that presents itself as objective civilizational defense is, in practice, the reflex of privilege whenever privilege feels afraid.

### Does the novel endorse or expose the ideology Tom repeats?

The novel exposes it, decisively. Fitzgerald frames Tom's race theory with words like pathetic and gibberish, gives it to a hypocrite who garbles it, and stages it as the reflex of a frightened man defending his advantages. Nothing in the narration endorses the ideas. The exposure runs through irony and character rather than through direct rebuttal.

## What did era race science claim about civilization and decline?

The organizing fear of the whole doctrine, and the note Tom strikes first, is decline. "Civilization's going to pieces," he begins, and that sentence is the emotional core of the entire genre of race panic. The theorists did not simply rank groups. They told a story, and the story was always one of a golden group in danger. A superior stock, they claimed, had built everything of value, science, art, law, the state itself, and that stock was now being outbred, diluted, and displaced by inferior populations who reproduced faster and had begun to demand power. The clock was running out. Unless the superior group acted to preserve itself, through immigration limits, marriage restrictions, and the other levers of eugenics, civilization would collapse into the hands of people incapable of sustaining it.

This narrative of decline is worth naming precisely because it explains the strange emotional temperature of Tom's speech. He is rich, safe, and dominant, yet he talks like a man watching the barbarians reach the gates. The decline story supplied that feeling. It converted the ordinary anxieties of a changing society, new immigrants, shifting cities, women claiming new freedoms, into a cosmic emergency in which the listener was cast as the heroic last defender. That is a flattering role, and it is a frightening one, and the combination is addictive. It gives a man both a villain to fear and a noble part to play. Fitzgerald understood the appeal exactly, which is why he gives Tom the grand phrase "the last barrier of civilization" and then, in the same breath, calls the speech gibberish. The decline story is the pseudo-science's most powerful product, and it is also its clearest tell, because a theory that always concludes with its believer as the heroic defender of everything good should invite suspicion rather than assent.

## The intelligence tests behind the panic

The surname Goddard opens onto a strand of the era's pseudo-science that the popular race books drew on constantly, the mass intelligence testing that gave the whole movement its air of hard data. In the second decade of the twentieth century, American psychologists adapted intelligence tests and began administering them at scale, most dramatically to nearly two million army recruits during the First World War. The men who designed and promoted these tests read the aggregate scores as evidence of innate group differences, concluding that recruits of northern European background were more intelligent than those from southern and eastern Europe, and that both ranked above Black Americans. The conclusion was worthless, because the tests measured schooling, literacy, and cultural familiarity far more than any fixed capacity, and recruits who had spent longer in American schools naturally scored higher. But the numbers were real numbers, generated by a real procedure, and they were published and publicized, and that was enough to convince a public primed to believe them.

Henry Goddard belonged to this world. He was among the psychologists who brought hereditarian intelligence testing to the United States and applied it to immigrants and to studies of allegedly degenerate family lines, arguing that low intelligence was inherited and posed a danger to the national stock. When Fitzgerald reached for a surname that would fuse the popular race panic with its academic underpinnings, Goddard was an apt choice, close enough to Stoddard to signal the bestseller and evocative enough of the testing movement to gesture at the laboratory behind it. Tom's insistence that the material is "all scientific" and that "this fellow has worked out the whole thing" is the voice of a man who has absorbed the testers' confidence secondhand. He has never seen a test or a score, but he has caught the aura the numbers produced, the sense that the matter had been measured and settled by experts. The tests supplied the borrowed authority, and men like Tom supplied the belief.

## The self-made man the theory feared: scientific racism and Gatsby

There is a deeper irony threaded through the novel that only the historical context brings into focus. The race theory Tom recites was not only about skin color and continents. In its Nordic form, as Grant framed it, it was also an anxiety about the wrong kind of European rising, about old established stock being swamped by newcomers and upstarts who did not belong at the top of society. And the novel's central figure is precisely such an upstart. Jay Gatsby, born James Gatz to poor farmers in North Dakota, has manufactured himself into a wealthy man through means Tom finds unspeakable, and Tom's hostility toward him carries the exact charge the race theory encoded, the panic of the entrenched at the ambitious stranger who has climbed into their world.

Tom makes the connection explicit in the Plaza scene. His sneer that Gatsby is "Mr. Nobody from Nowhere" pivots immediately into the warning about intermarriage and the last barrier of civilization, as though a self-made bootlegger courting his wife and the collapse of the racial order were manifestations of a single threat. In Tom's mind they are. The Nordic theory taught him to see any challenge to established hierarchy as a symptom of civilizational decline, and Gatsby is a living challenge to it. This is why the scientific-racism context matters far beyond the two scenes where race is named. It supplies the ideological lens through which Tom views everything, including Gatsby, and it reveals that his snobbery and his racism are not separate prejudices but the same conviction that some people are born to rule and others are born to serve. The doctrine that ranks races also ranks classes, and Tom applies it seamlessly to both.

Reading the novel this way sharpens the tragedy of Gatsby's aspiration. The American dream Gatsby embodies, the faith that a man can remake himself and rise, runs directly against the era's fashionable science, which insisted that a man's worth was fixed at birth by blood and could not be earned. Gatsby stakes his life on the belief that he can become someone new. Tom, armed with Goddard and Grant, believes such transformation is a fraud against nature. When Tom destroys Gatsby, he is enforcing the hierarchy the race books defend, and the novel's grief for Gatsby is partly grief for the dream that hierarchy exists to crush.

## Reading Tom's diction line by line

The precision of Fitzgerald's exposure lives at the level of individual words, and slowing down to read Tom's diction repays the effort. Consider the verb in his opening, that civilization is "going to pieces." It is a curiously passive and vague image, an object falling apart on its own, and it sets the tone for everything that follows, a doom without an agent that Tom cannot quite specify. Then comes "utterly submerged," a phrase of drowning that imagines the white race not defeated in a struggle but simply covered over, erased by sheer volume. The word carries no fight in it, only inundation, which tells you the fear is demographic, a terror of numbers rather than of any actual conflict.

Notice next the possessive machinery of the speech. Tom speaks in a relentless "we" and "us," "it's up to us, who are the dominant race," and the pronoun does the ideological work, drawing a boundary around the deserving and consigning everyone else to "these other races," a phrase that lumps the whole of humanity outside his group into an undifferentiated mass. He does not name a single one of the peoples he fears. They exist for him only as a threatening plural, which is the essence of the prejudice, a refusal to see individuals where the theory has taught him to see only categories. When he arrives at "Nordics," he finally names his own group with pride, and the asymmetry is total: his side has a proud name and a heritage, the other side has only the label "other."

The most revealing phrase, though, is the throwaway "and all that." Tom credits his group with producing "all the things that go to make civilization," reaching lazily for "science and art, and all that," and the casual dismissiveness of the ending sabotages the boast completely. A man who truly revered science and art would not gesture at them so carelessly. The phrase reveals that Tom does not actually care about civilization's achievements. He cares about owning them, about belonging to the group that gets the credit. Civilization for him is not a body of work to be cherished but a trophy to be claimed, and the laziness of "and all that" exposes the hollowness at the center of his pride. Fitzgerald could have written a paragraph denouncing Tom's shallowness. Instead he wrote three words and let them do it.

## Why "submerged" matters: race panic and the novel's water

Tom's chosen image of catastrophe, being "submerged," is worth holding up against the rest of the novel, because water and drowning run through the book as quiet threats and finally deliver its central death. Gatsby dies in his swimming pool, floating on a mattress as the water carries a slow circle around him, and the imagery of a man overtaken by water closes the novel's action. Tom's fear of racial submersion and Gatsby's literal death by water are not the same thing, and it would overreach to claim Fitzgerald planned a strict symbolic equation between them. But the recurrence of drowning as the novel's figure for being overwhelmed and erased gives Tom's word an extra resonance a careful reader can feel.

There is a grim irony in the pattern. Tom fears submersion and survives untouched, floating above every consequence on his inherited wealth, while Gatsby, who threatens no one, is the one the water actually takes. The man terrified of drowning stays dry; the dreamer drowns. If Tom's racial panic imagines a flood coming for people like him, the novel answers by drowning the outsider instead and leaving the insider safe, which is a bleaker and truer picture of how power protects itself. The race theory promised Tom that he was endangered and heroic. The plot demonstrates that he was neither, that he was never at risk, and that the destruction he feared he visited instead upon a man who only wanted to rise. The word submerged, planted early, quietly prepares this reversal, so that the reader who remembers Tom's fear feels its emptiness when the water finally closes over the wrong man.

## What a 1925 reader would have recognized

Part of what modern readers lose is the immediacy the scene had for its first audience. A reader in 1925 did not need footnotes to catch Tom's reference. Stoddard's "The Rising Tide of Color" had been a widely reviewed and discussed bestseller only five years earlier, and Grant's "The Passing of the Great Race" had gone through multiple editions and shaped public debate. The Johnson-Reed Immigration Act, built on exactly this thinking, had passed the year before the novel appeared and dominated the news. Eugenics was a familiar word attached to respectable institutions. When Tom said the book had "been proved," a contemporary reader would have known both the book he meant and the widespread conviction that it had indeed been proved, and would have registered Fitzgerald's mockery against the grain of that conviction.

This immediacy means the scene was, for its first readers, more pointed and more daring than it can easily feel today. Fitzgerald was not describing a safely discredited past. He was needling a live ideology at the height of its prestige, one endorsed by his own publisher's catalog and written into current law, and he was doing it by making its adherent a fool and a hypocrite. The satire had teeth precisely because the target was fashionable rather than fringe. Recovering that context restores the scene's original charge. It was not a comfortable jab at obvious villainy but a sharp dissent from mainstream opinion, delivered so economically, through a name, a phrase, and a narrator's chosen adjectives, that it could pass a careless reader entirely while landing with full force on anyone paying attention. Reading it now with the history restored lets a modern reader feel the edge the first readers felt, and understand why the passage was a small act of nerve.

## Stoddard versus Grant: two race panics Tom blends into one

### What is the difference between the two books behind Tom's speech?

Stoddard's "The Rising Tide of Color" imagines a global threat, the white world overwhelmed by the larger populations of Asia and Africa. Grant's "The Passing of the Great Race" imagines an internal European threat, Nordics diluted by lesser European stock. Tom fuses the two, which is itself part of Fitzgerald's exposure of how sloppily the ideology was actually held.

The two bestsellers behind Tom's lecture were not identical, and the difference is worth grasping because Tom's failure to keep them apart is one more sign of how carelessly he holds his supposed knowledge. Stoddard's book, the one whose title Fitzgerald mirrors most closely, was fundamentally about geopolitics on a planetary scale. Its nightmare was the world's white minority, having colonized and dominated the globe, now facing the sheer demographic weight and rising political consciousness of the far more numerous peoples of Asia and Africa. Its color line was global, drawn between the white world and everyone else, and its fear was that empire and dominance abroad could not last against those numbers. When Tom says the white race will be submerged, he is speaking Stoddard's language of a small group drowned by a global majority.

Grant's book worked at a different scale entirely. His obsession was internal to Europe and to America's white population. He sorted Europeans into Nordic, Alpine, and Mediterranean types and warned that the superior Nordic strain, which he identified with America's founding stock, was being diluted by intermarriage and outbred by supposedly lesser European groups, especially the southern and eastern Europeans arriving in large numbers. Grant's fear was not of a distant global majority but of the immigrant next door, the Italian or the Pole or the Jew who, in his scheme, degraded the Nordic character of the nation from within. When Tom says "we're Nordics" and preens over having built civilization, he is speaking Grant's language of a hierarchy among white people, not Stoddard's language of a global color line.

Tom mashes these two distinct panics together without noticing the seam. He invokes Stoddard's planetary submersion and Grant's Nordic supremacy in the same breath, as though they were one seamless theory rather than two overlapping and partly incompatible ones. This is exactly how the ideology lived in the popular mind, as a single vague dread of losing ground to everyone below, and Tom's inability to distinguish its strands is not a small slip. It is Fitzgerald showing that Tom has not actually thought about any of this. He has swallowed a mood and called it science. A genuine student of the theories, however repugnant, would know that Stoddard and Grant feared different things. Tom knows only that he is afraid and that a book somewhere told him his fear was proved. The blending is the exposure, rendered so quietly that it reads as mere bluster until you know what the two real books actually argued.

## Myths and oversimplifications to avoid

Several misreadings recur whenever students and casual readers meet the scientific-racism scene, and clearing them away is most of the work of reading it well.

The first and most common is treating Tom's book as invented nonsense, a bit of comic exaggeration Fitzgerald made up to signal that Tom is stupid. This flattens the scene badly. The book is a real book, lightly disguised, and the ideas were real and mainstream. If you assume Fitzgerald invented the doctrine, you turn a precise historical indictment into a vague joke, and you lose the crucial fact that a novel published by Scribner was mocking a bestseller published by Scribner. The scene is sharper, not softer, once you know the sources are genuine.

The second misreading runs in the opposite direction. It takes the reality of the ideology so seriously that it reads the scene as Fitzgerald endorsing or at least entertaining the theory, or as evidence that the novel itself shares Tom's racism. This confuses depiction with approval. A novel that gives a repugnant idea to a repugnant character, frames it as pathetic and as gibberish, and lets a hypocrite garble it, is not endorsing the idea. It is exhibiting it. The question of Fitzgerald's own private views is a separate and murkier matter that the text does not settle in these scenes; what the text does is stage the ideology for judgment, and the judgment it invites is contempt.

A third error separates the scene from its era, reading Tom's outburst as timeless personal bigotry that could occur in any period. This misses the specificity Fitzgerald built in. The Nordic language, the fear of submersion, the appeal to what has been "proved," the very existence of a fashionable book on the subject, are all markers of a particular moment, roughly 1916 to the mid-1920s, when this pseudo-science enjoyed peak respectability. Detaching the scene from that moment costs you the historical argument the novel is making about its own society, that this was not one crank's obsession but the settled conviction of a ruling class.

### Is Tom's outburst just personal bigotry or something larger?

It is both, and the larger frame is the point. Tom's racism is personal, but Fitzgerald stages it as the voice of an entire privileged class reciting the era's fashionable pseudo-science. Reading it as one man's private flaw misses the novel's claim that scientific racism was mainstream conviction, not isolated ignorance.

A final oversimplification worth naming is the assumption that because the ideology is discredited today, it must have looked ridiculous then, so that Tom is simply behind the times. He is not behind the times. In 1922 he is exactly of his time, holding views that leading scientists, universities, and lawmakers shared. The discomfort the scene should produce is not the comfortable feeling of watching a backward man, but the sharper recognition that respectable knowledge can be monstrous, and that the costume of science is no guarantee of truth. That recognition is the scene's lasting value, and it is why the passage still unsettles readers a century later.

## The critical afterlife: how the race passages have been read

For much of the twentieth century, criticism of The Great Gatsby paid little sustained attention to the race material, treating Tom's outbursts as color in a portrait of a brute rather than as a subject in their own right. The novel was read chiefly as a story of the American dream, of love and money and time, and the scientific-racism scene registered mainly as evidence of Tom's unpleasantness. That reading is not wrong, but it left a rich seam untouched. In the later decades of the century, as scholars turned more attention to how American literature encodes race and whiteness, the passages drew closer study, and the fictional Goddard was traced firmly to Stoddard, Grant, and the eugenics movement, so that the historical specificity this article recovers became a standard part of informed reading.

That shift matters because it changed the scale of the scene. Read as one man's bigotry, the dinner-table lecture is a local detail. Read as the novel's registration of a mainstream ideology, it becomes part of the book's larger anatomy of a ruling class and its self-justifications. The later criticism did not invent this dimension; it recovered what a 1925 reader would have grasped and what the intervening decades had allowed to fade. The historical sources were always there, thinly disguised. What changed was the willingness of readers to take them seriously as more than a character's quirk, and to ask what it means that the novel's most secure and privileged figure is also its most committed racial theorist.

Approaching the passages through the questions a later critical tradition brought to them, about how whiteness operates as an unspoken norm and how the novel exposes it, is the work of the dedicated critical-lens articles rather than this one. ## Closing verdict

The scientific racism behind Tom's books is one of the most precisely engineered pieces of historical grounding in The Great Gatsby, and it rewards the reader who refuses to skim it. Fitzgerald took a real, respectable, bestselling ideology, the race panic of Stoddard and Grant and the eugenics movement that surrounded them, and handed it to a rich man who recites it badly, so that the doctrine collapses under the man's own vanity and fear. The novel's method is exposure through character rather than argument, and it is devastating precisely because it never stoops to debate. To argue with the books would grant them dignity. To let Tom mangle them, framed by Nick's pathetic and gibberish, is to show them for what they were, prejudice wearing the costume of science, the borrowed-authority pattern in its purest form.

The verdict that decides how to read the scene is this: Tom's race theory is not personal eccentricity but the voice of a class, and the novel's judgment of it is contempt delivered through irony rather than sermon. The deciding factor is the framing. Every marker of authorial control, the composite name Goddard, the word pathetic, the word gibberish, Jordan's deflating murmur, the physical absurdity of a dominant man fearing submersion, points the same way, toward exposure and away from endorsement. Read the scene as Fitzgerald built it and you gain not only a sharper understanding of Tom but a lasting caution that outlives the novel, that knowledge dressed in the authority of science can still be a lie, and that the surest tell of the lie is that it always ends with its believer cast as the hero. That caution is why the passage still matters, and why the reader who recovers the real books behind Tom's dinner-table lecture reads the whole novel more clearly.

There is a final reason the scene deserves the weight this reading gives it. The pseudo-science Tom recites did not stay on the page or die with its era. It fed immigration quotas, sterilization laws, and worse abuses in the decades that followed, and its basic move, dressing prejudice in the vocabulary of measurement and calling the result proved, has been repeated in every generation since under new names. Fitzgerald caught the pattern at its most respectable moment and pinned it to a character so exactly that the pinning still holds. When a reader today learns to notice how Tom mistakes a costume for a fact, how his certainty rests on authority he never examined, and how his fear always flatters him with a heroic role, that reader has acquired more than a footnote about the 1920s. They have acquired a way of seeing through the same maneuver whenever it returns, which it reliably does. A great novel teaches its readers to recognize things beyond its pages, and the scientific-racism scene, small and skippable as it looks, is one of the sharpest lessons in seeing that The Great Gatsby has to give.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How does scientific racism relate to Tom's books in The Great Gatsby?

Scientific racism is the ideology behind the book Tom cites at Nick's first dinner in East Egg, "The Rise of the Coloured Empires by this man Goddard." That fictional title lightly disguises real 1920s bestsellers, chiefly Lothrop Stoddard's "The Rising Tide of Color" and Madison Grant's "The Passing of the Great Race," which argued that a superior white or Nordic stock was being submerged by inferior groups. Tom treats these claims as proved fact. Fitzgerald uses the reference to ground his portrait of the Buchanans' world in a real, respectable, and monstrous body of pseudo-science, showing that Tom's racism is not private eccentricity but the settled conviction of a ruling class. The relationship is the novel's method of exposing an ideology through a character who recites it badly.

### Q: What was scientific racism in the 1920s?

Scientific racism was the use of biology, anthropology, and statistics to argue that human groups formed a fixed, hereditary hierarchy of intelligence and worth. In the 1920s it was mainstream rather than fringe, taught at universities, published by prestige houses, and cited by lawmakers. Its practitioners measured skulls, administered intelligence tests to immigrants, and drew family pedigrees, presenting the results as proof that certain races were superior and endangered by the reproduction of others. Every core claim is false; modern genetics recognizes no such biological ranking. But the doctrine carried the prestige of science because it wore its costume of charts, measurements, and confident prediction. That respectability is precisely what made it dangerous and what let a wealthy, educated man like Tom Buchanan accept it as settled knowledge.

### Q: What book does Tom cite in Gatsby and what does it claim?

Tom cites "The Rise of the Coloured Empires by this man Goddard," a fictional title that closely echoes Lothrop Stoddard's 1920 bestseller "The Rising Tide of Color Against White World-Supremacy." The claim Tom relays is that the white race will be "utterly submerged" unless dominant peoples act to protect their position against the rising populations and ambitions of other groups. He adds the Nordic framework drawn from Madison Grant, insisting that his own group produced civilization itself. Tom presents all of this as scientific fact that has "been proved." The real books made exactly these arguments and were commercial successes from respectable publishers, including Scribner, which also published Gatsby. The reference is not invented nonsense but a precise pointer at genuine ideology that gripped mainstream America.

### Q: How did racism borrow the authority of science in the era?

It borrowed authority by adopting the forms of science: measurement, classification, statistics, and confident prediction. Theorists measured skulls and published the numbers, administered intelligence tests and reported the scores as proof of innate group differences, and charted so-called degenerate family lines as evidence of heredity. Each move took a subjective prejudice, that certain people were inferior, and dressed it in an objective-looking procedure, so the conclusion seemed to emerge from data rather than bias. Academic titles and prestige publishers completed the disguise. The result felt unarguable, because a chart is harder to dispute than a naked opinion. Tom's flat certainty that his views have "been proved" is the natural voice of this borrowed authority, a man who has mistaken the costume of science for its substance and never weighed the evidence himself.

### Q: How does Tom's clumsiness undercut the ideology he repeats?

Tom garbles the very doctrine he is proud of, which drains it of the dignity it demands. He cannot state the author's name in a way that sounds right, signaled by the odd "Goddard." He cannot summarize the argument without collapsing into vague menace about "these other races" gaining "control of things." He credits his group with producing "all the things that go to make civilization," reaching lazily for "science and art, and all that," and the lazy "and all that" betrays that he does not truly value what he claims to defend. He needs Daisy's mocking prompt about deep books with long words to expose that the long words are the whole appeal. A doctrine that survives only in the mouth of a man who has not understood it forfeits the seriousness it claims, and Fitzgerald lets that collapse happen without a word of direct refutation.

### Q: Is the race book Tom mentions invented or based on real tracts?

It is based on real tracts. "The Rise of the Coloured Empires by this man Goddard" is a light fictionalization of Lothrop Stoddard's 1920 bestseller "The Rising Tide of Color Against White World-Supremacy," with the Nordic language taken from Madison Grant's "The Passing of the Great Race" and the surname echoing the eugenicist Henry Goddard. The disguise is thin by design, so that a contemporary reader would recognize the source. Fitzgerald did not need to invent a race theory because a fashionable one already sat on bookstore shelves, published, notably, by Scribner, the same house that issued Gatsby. Treating the book as made-up comedy flattens the scene. Its power comes from being an accurate pointer at genuine, respectable, mainstream pseudo-science of the moment Fitzgerald was writing.

### Q: Who was the real writer behind the fictional Goddard?

The fictional Goddard is a composite. The book and its thesis point most directly at Lothrop Stoddard, whose "The Rising Tide of Color" (1920) imagined the white world submerged by a rising color line, exactly Tom's fear. The surname sits one letter from Stoddard while also summoning Henry H. Goddard, an American psychologist who promoted hereditarian intelligence testing and warnings about feeblemindedness threatening the national stock. Behind Tom's "Nordics" stands a third figure, Madison Grant, author of "The Passing of the Great Race." Fitzgerald blended these names and ideas because the ideology itself was a blend, circulating in the popular mind as a single vague conviction rather than one man's system. The composite points not at a lone crank but at a whole apparatus of respectable race science.

### Q: What is Nordic theory and why does Tom invoke it?

Nordic theory, drawn largely from Madison Grant's "The Passing of the Great Race" (1916), divided Europeans into three supposed races, Nordic, Alpine, and Mediterranean, and ranked the Nordic at the top as the builder and rightful ruler of civilization. It flattered northern Europeans and cast southern and eastern Europeans as inferior stock. Tom invokes it when he announces "we're Nordics" and sorts the people at his table into the master category with a condescending nod. He reaches for the theory because it does exactly what he wants, it places him at the summit of humanity and credits his group with everything valuable. The vanity is the tell. A hierarchy that conveniently seats its believer at the top should invite suspicion, and Fitzgerald frames Tom's pride as pathetic rather than persuasive.

### Q: How does eugenics connect to the ideas in Tom's books?

Eugenics was the organized movement behind the popular race books, proposing to improve the population by encouraging reproduction among the supposedly fit and discouraging it among the supposedly unfit. It had research centers, university courses, and popular support in the 1910s and 1920s. The books Tom half-remembers were its bestselling literature, and its ideas moved from the page into law. The same hierarchy Tom recites supplied cover for the 1924 Johnson-Reed Immigration Act, which set national-origin quotas favoring northern Europeans, and for state laws permitting forced sterilization of people judged unfit. Fitzgerald does not stage eugenics directly, but knowing that Tom's dinner-table creed shared its roots with sterilization statutes and immigration quotas gives his complacency a darker weight, since the ideology he finds reassuring was being written into real policy and real harm.

### Q: Why did Fitzgerald give this ideology to Tom specifically?

Tom is the ideal vessel because he is the novel's embodiment of secure, inherited, physically dominant privilege, and giving the race panic to him exposes its true function. A theory about civilization under siege sounds absurd in the mouth of the most powerful man in the book, who has everything and fears losing it to people with almost nothing. That gap between his actual dominance and his imagined vulnerability is where the irony lives. Fitzgerald could have given the ideas to a poor or marginal character and made them a study in resentment. By placing them in Tom instead, he shows the doctrine as the reflex of a ruling class, a prop that threatened privilege reaches for whenever it feels afraid. The choice turns one man's bigotry into a diagnosis of the elite the novel anatomizes.

### Q: What is The Rise of the Coloured Empires a stand-in for?

It stands in for Lothrop Stoddard's "The Rising Tide of Color Against White World-Supremacy," a real 1920 bestseller that warned of the white world being overwhelmed by the rising populations and political awakening of colored peoples across the globe. Fitzgerald's fictional title mirrors Stoddard's, and Tom's paraphrase, that the white race will be "submerged," compresses its thesis. The stand-in also gathers in the broader genre, including Grant's Nordic hierarchy, so that the single fictional book represents the whole fashionable literature of race panic. The disguise is deliberately thin, letting contemporary readers recognize the target while giving Fitzgerald the freedom of fiction. That the real book came from Scribner, Gatsby's own publisher, adds a layer of pointed irony to Tom's earnest recommendation that everybody ought to read it.

### Q: How does Daisy react to Tom's racial theories at the dinner?

Daisy responds with mocking deflection rather than agreement or protest. She tells Nick that "Tom's getting very profound" and jokes that "he reads deep books with long words in them," a needling that exposes Tom's real motive, the flattery of seeming learned, more sharply than any direct challenge could. When Tom presses the theory, she whispers "we've got to beat them down" while winking, a performance of mock enthusiasm that refuses to take him seriously. Her irony does real work in the scene, puncturing Tom's solemnity and signaling that even his wife treats the lecture as a pose. At the same time her deflection is a form of avoidance rather than resistance; she mocks the delivery without confronting the content, which is characteristic of how the novel's charming surfaces glide over its cruelties.

### Q: Did educated Americans actually believe this pseudo-science?

Yes, and widely. Scientific racism in the 1920s was mainstream among the educated classes. It was taught at leading universities, published by prestige houses like Scribner, endorsed by credentialed scientists and physicians, and cited in Congress during debates over immigration. Eugenics exhibits appeared at state fairs, and biology textbooks explained the movement's claims to schoolchildren. A wealthy Yale graduate like Tom Buchanan treating race hierarchy as settled fact is historically accurate, not an exaggeration of one man's ignorance. This is precisely why the scene unsettles rather than merely amuses. Fitzgerald is not portraying a uniquely backward individual but the ordinary conviction of his class and era, which forces the reader to reckon with how respectable, credentialed knowledge could be both authoritative and monstrous at the same historical moment.

### Q: How does the scientific-racism scene connect to Tom's Plaza outburst?

The two scenes reveal the same ideology serving the same psychological function. At the East Egg dinner, race theory is leisure-time self-congratulation, Tom flattering himself with the era's fashionable pseudo-science. At the Plaza, cornered as Gatsby and Daisy's affair breaks open, Tom swerves into the identical territory, warning that people will "throw everything overboard and have intermarriage between black and white," and imagining himself "standing alone on the last barrier of civilization." Nick calls it gibberish. The pivot shows that the doctrine is not a cool conviction but a weapon Tom grabs whenever his position feels threatened. A man is taking his wife, so he reaches for the defense of the white race as if the two were one emergency. The consistency across both scenes is Fitzgerald's argument, made through structure, that the ideology is the reflex of frightened privilege.

### Q: What did era race science claim about civilization and decline?

The organizing claim was decline. Race theorists argued that a superior stock had built everything of value, science, art, law, and the state, and that this stock was now being outbred and displaced by inferior populations who reproduced faster and were beginning to demand power. Unless the superior group acted, through immigration limits, marriage restrictions, and eugenic measures, civilization would collapse. Tom strikes this note first with "Civilization's going to pieces." The decline story converted ordinary anxieties about a changing society into a cosmic emergency in which the believer played the heroic last defender. That flattering and frightening combination made it addictive. It also made it self-revealing, since a theory that always ends with its believer cast as civilization's savior should invite suspicion rather than assent, which is exactly the suspicion Fitzgerald's framing encourages.

The novel exposes it decisively. ### Q: How did this pseudo-science shape 1920s immigration policy?

The race theories Tom parrots supplied intellectual cover for restricting immigration by national origin. In 1924, the year Fitzgerald was finishing the novel, Congress passed the Johnson-Reed Immigration Act, which set quotas designed to favor northern and western Europeans while sharply cutting arrivals from southern and eastern Europe and barring most Asian immigration outright. Lawmakers cited the same hierarchy of superior and inferior stock that the eugenics movement and books like Grant's and Stoddard's promoted, treating certain groups as biologically preferable to preserve. The doctrine thus moved from bestseller to statute within Gatsby's own composition years. Fitzgerald does not dramatize the law, but the nativist climate it expressed is the air Tom's outbursts breathe, which is why recovering the historical sources deepens rather than decorates a reading of his racial panic.
