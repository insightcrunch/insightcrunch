Marx and Weber on class is the comparison every student of sociology meets early, and it is usually taught in a form that both thinkers would have disowned. The standard version runs in two sentences. Marx, it is said, divided society into two great classes defined by property, while Weber refined the picture by adding status and party, so that stratification became three dimensional where it had been one. The summary is tidy, memorable, and wrong at its foundation, because it presents Weber as correcting a thinker who had supposedly reduced social rank to wealth alone, and it presents the correction as a substitution of prestige for property. Weber made no such substitution. Read in his own text, Weber grounds class in the ownership and non ownership of property with a bluntness that rivals anything in Marx, and the genuine dispute between the two men lies somewhere more interesting than the textbook contrast suggests. It lies in where a class is constituted, in what a class can be expected to do, and in whether history has a direction. This article reconstructs both frameworks from their strongest statements, names the point of agreement that examination answers routinely erase, and then asks the only comparative question worth asking: which framework answers which question better, judged by a stated criterion rather than by allegiance.

![Marx and Weber on class compared, production against market situation, status, party and life chances - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-39.webp)

The stakes of getting the comparison right are not merely antiquarian. Class remains the working concept through which sociology explains unequal incomes, unequal health, unequal schooling, and unequal political weight, and the two classical frameworks still organise the research that measures those inequalities. The large occupational schemas used in mobility research descend from Weber through a line of scholars who refined his market based categories. The relational schemas used in the study of exploitation and workplace authority descend from Marx through scholars who rebuilt his production based categories for economies he never saw. When the two traditions are tested against the same evidence, the classical dispute becomes an empirical one, and the verdict is more instructive than either side's partisans suggest. A reader who understands what Marx and Weber actually claimed can also see why the modern contest ends so often in a draw on prediction and a genuine divergence on explanation. That is the destination of the argument that follows. The route runs through each framework in its own terms first, because a comparison conducted before both positions are stated fairly is not a comparison at all but a verdict in search of a hearing.

## The caricature and what it conceals

The caricature survives because it is genuinely useful to teachers working against a clock. A two column table with Marx on the left and Weber on the right fits on one slide, generates one examination paragraph, and gives students a contrast they can reproduce under pressure. Its cost is accuracy at exactly the points where the two theories touch. Consider what the caricature asks a student to believe. It asks that Marx had no vocabulary for honour, prestige, or political organisation, although his historical writings are crowded with all three, from the factions and clienteles of French politics to the careful attention he paid to the peasantry as a class defined partly by its mode of life. It asks that Weber discarded property as a crude economic criterion, although the essay in which he defines class states the opposite in a single sentence that this article will quote and examine at length. And it asks that the two schemes can be merged by simple addition, as though status and party were annexes built onto a Marxist ground floor, when in fact Weber's dimensions belong to a rival architecture with its own foundations, its own account of action, and its own refusal of historical direction.

Clearing away the caricature does not dissolve the differences. It sharpens them. Once the false contrast between property and prestige is removed, the real contrasts stand out in higher relief. Marx constitutes class inside production, in the relation between those who own the means of production and those who must sell their capacity to work. Weber constitutes class inside the market, in the differing chances that property and skills command when goods and services are exchanged. Marx builds into his concept an expectation that shared position ripens into shared organisation and shared action, so that a class becomes a historical force. Weber withholds that expectation entirely, treating collective action by a class as one possibility among several, dependent on conditions that have to be investigated case by case. Marx gives history a motor and a direction, however qualified by his own historical writing. Weber gives history no destination at all, only a long process of rationalisation whose outcomes he regarded with disciplined pessimism. These are deep differences, and none of them is the difference the slide describes.

### What is the difference between Marx and Weber on class?

Marx grounds class in the relations of production and expects class interests to generate collective action. Weber grounds class in market situation and life chances, treats collective action as contingent, and adds status and party as independent dimensions of stratification rather than as effects of class.

That paragraph is the whole comparison in miniature, and the rest of this article is its unfolding. Each clause of it deserves scrutiny, because each has been flattened in transmission. Grounding class in production is not the same as saying that income determines rank. Treating collective action as contingent is not the same as denying that classes ever act. Adding status and party as independent dimensions is not the same as denying that wealth buys honour, which Weber knew perfectly well and stated with some dryness. The sections that follow take the two frameworks in turn, restore the qualifications that the caricature strips away, and only then set them side by side in the grid that forms this article's findable artifact. Readers in a hurry can go straight to that grid, but the grid without the reconstruction invites exactly the misreading the article exists to correct, because its cells compress arguments that need their reasoning attached.

## Two thinkers who never debated

A preliminary correction concerns the relationship itself. Marx died in 1883, when Weber was nineteen years old and still a student. Weber's mature sociology was written a generation later, in a Germany transformed by industrialisation, organised labour, and a mass socialist party that claimed Marx as its theorist. Weber's engagement with Marx was therefore real but indirect and partly adversarial in a complicated way. He argued with the Marxism of his contemporaries, with historical materialism as a method, and with the ghost of Marx that haunted German social science, a presence he acknowledged in a famous and respectful aside about the intellectual honesty that required admitting how much every social scientist of his generation owed to Marx's problems even when rejecting Marx's solutions. Scholars dispute how directly the class essay answers Marx at all. The safer and better supported statement is that Weber worked in a field whose questions Marx had set, disagreed with the answers given by Marx's self declared heirs, and built an alternative whose points of contact with Marx are structural rather than polemical. Treating the essay on class, status, and party as a point by point rebuttal misdescribes its genre. It is a set of definitions offered for research, deliberately spare, and its silences about Marx are part of its design.

The texts on the two sides also differ in finish, and the asymmetry matters for fairness. Marx never completed a systematic statement of his class theory. The chapter on classes in the third volume of Capital breaks off after little more than a page, at the very moment the definition is promised, because the manuscript was left unfinished at his death and assembled by Engels from papers in varying states of completion. What readers call Marx's theory of class is a reconstruction from the analysis of capital in the first volume, from the historical studies of France, from the political pamphlets, and from programmatic statements scattered across forty years of writing. Weber's statement, by contrast, is a finished set of distinctions in the opening part of Economy and Society, the vast work published after his death in 1920 by his widow and his collaborators, and widely read in English through the anthology edited by Hans Gerth and C. Wright Mills in 1946, which carried the class, status, and party essay to the postwar audience that made it canonical. One side of the comparison is thus a theory embedded in an unfinished system, and the other is a typology embedded in an unfinished treatise. Humility about both is the correct scholarly posture, and any verdict that ignores the state of the texts is claiming more certainty than the evidence licenses.

There is a further asymmetry of intention. Marx wrote as a partisan of the class whose emancipation his theory predicted, and his categories carry an openly practical charge: to name a relation correctly was, for him, already a step toward transforming it. Weber wrote under a self imposed discipline of value freedom, insisting that the sociologist's categories should serve analysis rather than advocacy, and that the lecture hall was not a pulpit. This difference in the point of the enterprise explains some of the difference in the concepts. A category designed to identify the agent of historical change will be drawn so that the agent can recognise itself in it. A category designed to sort market positions for comparative research will be drawn for precision and portability across societies. Judging either category by the other's purpose produces the standard confusions: Marx accused of failing at neutral description, Weber accused of failing at critique. Both accusations contain a fragment of truth and a large measure of category error, and the sections on the modern successors will show each tradition quietly borrowing the other's virtues when it comes time to do empirical work.

## Marx: class as a relation inside production

The foundation of Marx's account is a relation, not a quantity. A class is not a band of people sharing an income level, a consumption style, or a neighbourhood. It is a position within the relations of production, defined by a group's relation to the means of production and by its relation to other groups occupying the complementary positions. The owners of factories, land, and capital confront those who own nothing but their capacity to work, and the confrontation is not incidental but constitutive: each position exists only in relation to the other, as buyer and seller of labour power, as appropriator and producer of surplus value. This relational criterion is what separates Marx's concept from every merely gradational scheme that slices a population into upper, middle, and lower by income. A gradation describes a distribution. A relation describes a mechanism, and the mechanism is exploitation, the extraction of surplus labour that the whole analysis of Capital exists to demonstrate. To lose the relational criterion is to lose the theory while keeping its vocabulary, a fate that befalls many summaries. The fuller statement of the Marxian side is given in this series in the [marxist theory of class guide](/2011/09/01/marxist-theory-of-class-guide/), and the present article draws on it where the comparison requires precision.

Because the criterion is relational, the number of classes in Marx is an empirical and historical question rather than a fixed count. Under capitalism the tendency of the analysis is toward two great poles, bourgeoisie and proletariat, because competition and accumulation progressively strip intermediate producers of independent standing. But Marx's historical writing recognises landlords, peasants, the petty bourgeoisie of shopkeepers and artisans, finance capitalists, and the lumpenproletariat, and his account of France in the middle of the nineteenth century is a study in the friction between the polar tendency and the crowded reality. The tension between the two class model of the theory and the many class narrative of the histories is not a defect to be explained away. It is the theory working as intended, distinguishing the deep structure of a mode of production from the layered inheritance of actual societies, in which older classes persist, newer ones form, and fractions within each great class pursue divergent interests. Critics who triumphantly point at the middle strata as a refutation of Marx are usually refuting the slide version, in which the tendency is mistaken for a description of every present moment.

The second element built into Marx's concept is the passage from position to action. A class that shares a position and an interest is, in his vocabulary, a class in itself; it becomes a class for itself when it organises, acquires consciousness of its situation, and acts collectively in pursuit of its interests. The distinction licenses a research question that runs through all his political writing: under what conditions does a mere category of people become a historical actor? His answers are specific and unsentimental. Shared conditions of life and work concentrate workers, communication and combination follow, trade unions and parties give the combination durable form, and political struggle educates the class in its own capacities. The peasantry of France, by contrast, failed his test for actorhood, dispersed across smallholdings, sharing a position without the interconnection that organisation requires, and therefore represented by others rather than representing themselves. Whatever one thinks of the verdict, the analytical structure is clear: class position generates interest, interest supplies a motive for organisation, and organisation, achieved or blocked, decides whether the class enters history as a subject or remains an object of other classes' politics.

### Did Marx reduce everything social to economics?

No. His historical writing attends to honour, tradition, political organisation, and the theatre of life, and his theory treats those things as real forces. His claim is narrower and stronger: over time, the relations of production set the limits within which honour, tradition, and politics operate, and explain why some arrangements persist while others collapse under their own contradictions.

The qualification matters because Weber's status dimension is often introduced as though it named a territory Marx had never visited. Marx knew that soldiers, priests, officials, and aristocrats commanded deference out of proportion to their market power, and he had a developed vocabulary for the ideological forms in which class rule dresses itself. What he did not grant those phenomena was independence in the last instance. Honour, in his account, tends to follow the contours of property and power, consolidating where wealth is old, evaporating where wealth collapses, and serving to legitimate the order that produces it. Whether that subordination is correct is an empirical and theoretical question, and it is exactly the question on which Weber's scheme takes the opposite side. Stating Marx's position as a denial of status is a misreading; stating it as a theory of status, in which prestige is real but derivative, is the accurate version, and it makes the eventual comparison sharper because both men are then answering the same question with different theories rather than talking past each other in different vocabularies.

A final element of the Marxian framework must be restored before the comparison can proceed, because the objection section will lean on it. Marx's class theory is dynamic in a specific sense: classes are not only positions in a structure but products of a process, and the structure itself changes as accumulation proceeds. The same development that concentrates capital also concentrates workers, raises the socialisation of production, and generates crises in which the relations of production fetter the forces they once developed. Class therefore has a historical dynamic written into it, a tendency for the central conflict to intensify and simplify until the order that rests on it is transformed. No element of this dynamic has a Weberian counterpart, and its presence or absence is the deepest single difference between the frameworks, deeper than the location of class itself. A theory with a direction and a theory without one will read the same society as a story at different stages of its plot and as a field of forces in temporary balance, respectively, and their practical counsels diverge accordingly.

## Weber: class as a position in the market

Weber's definition begins from the individual facing a market. A class exists, in his formulation, when a number of people share a specific causal component of their life chances, insofar as that component is represented exclusively by economic interests in the possession of goods and opportunities for income, and is represented under the conditions of commodity and labour markets. Every term in that compressed sentence does work. A class is a shared component of life chances, not a community and not an organisation. The component is economic, a matter of what one's property and skills can command. And the arena in which it operates is the market, the sphere of exchange, where owners of land, of capital, of qualifications, and of nothing but labour power meet and bargain from unequal positions. Where Marx locates class at the point of production, at the workbench and the factory gate, Weber locates it at the point of exchange, at the hiring desk and the counter. The relocation is the single most consequential move in his scheme, because everything else he says about class follows from it.

From the market location Weber derives his classes by the kinds of property and service that can be brought to it. Property classes are distinguished by the income their assets yield, positively privileged where rents, interest, and dividends flow in, negatively privileged where debts and obligations flow out. Acquisition classes are distinguished by the services they sell, entrepreneurs and professionals at the privileged pole, skilled and unskilled workers ranged by the market value of what they offer. Social classes, a third and looser term, name the plurality of class situations between which movement is easy and typical across a lifetime or a generation. The scheme is deliberately plural. Where Marx's analysis drives toward polarisation, Weber's distinctions multiply categories wherever the market differentiates positions, and he saw no theoretical scandal in the multiplication. A typology that sorts is doing its work when it sorts finely, provided the sorting predicts what it was built to predict, namely the distribution of life chances among the people sorted.

Life chances is the concept that binds the scheme together, and it deserves its own examination because examination answers lean on it heavily and define it loosely. The phrase names the probabilities that attach to a person's class situation: the probability of finding work and of keeping it, of rising income across a career, of surviving infancy and avoiding chronic illness, of obtaining schooling for one's children, of being housed decently, of reaching old age with security. These probabilities are not chosen and are only partly influenced by effort. They are dealt to individuals by their market position before any question of merit arises, and they cumulate, since each realised chance alters the hand from which the next is drawn. Weber's concept therefore captures something that income snapshots miss, the way advantage and disadvantage are structured as odds across a whole biography. It also explains why two people with identical incomes in a given year may occupy different class situations: the salaried professional whose credentials guarantee re-employment faces different odds from the casual worker whose identical pay this month rests on a contract that may not be renewed. Security, trajectory, and exposure to risk are class phenomena in Weber's sense even when the payslips match.

### What did Weber mean by life chances?

Life chances are the probabilities a market position deals to a person across a whole biography: the odds of steady work, rising income, decent housing, schooling for children, and security in illness and old age. They attach to the position before effort or merit enter, and they accumulate as each outcome reshapes the next set of odds.

Defining class through life chances also reveals Weber's methodological commitments. His sociology is built from the actions of individuals and the meanings they attach to them, and his categories are instruments for causal analysis rather than names of collective beings. A class, for Weber, is not a thing that acts, suffers, or decides. It is a pattern in the distribution of chances, visible to the analyst, which may or may not become visible to the people inside it. This is why his language stays cool where Marx's heats up. The concept is meant to serve comparative research across slave economies, feudal orders, and capitalist markets, and it is deliberately stripped of any implication that the people sharing a class situation share a destiny, a culture, or a cause. Whether that coolness is scientific virtue or analytical evasion is one of the questions the verdict section takes up, since the Marxian rejoinder is precisely that a concept of class unable to register exploitation has sorted the world while declining to explain what holds the sorting in place.

It is equally important to register what Weber's market definition retains from the older political economy both men inherited. The market is not, in his treatment, a neutral meeting place of equals. Property enters it before any bargain is struck, and the distribution of property decides who must bargain from need and who can bargain from surplus. A person with no property but labour power faces the market under a compulsion the property owner escapes: the need to sell continuously in order to live. Weber states this plainly, and his plainness is the bridge to the agreement examined in the next section. His disagreement with Marx is not about whether property divides the world. It is about where the division does its work, in exchange rather than in production, and about what may legitimately be inferred from it, chances rather than destinies.

## The agreement clause

Here is the sentence on which this article's namable claim rests. In the essay on class, status, and party, Weber writes that property and lack of property are the basic categories of all class situations. The sentence is thirteen words in the standard translation, and it dismantles the textbook contrast at a stroke. Weber does not replace property with prestige. He does not treat ownership as one lifestyle marker among others. He states, as the foundation of his own scheme, the very divide that the caricature assigns exclusively to Marx, between those who have property and those who do not. The point deserves the name this article gives it: the agreement clause. Any comparison that presents Weber as substituting status for property has failed to read the clause, and any examination answer that opens with that substitution begins by misrepresenting the thinker it claims to expound.

What follows from the agreement is a relocation of the genuine dispute. If both men ground class in property, their differences must be sought in three narrower places. The first is the site of constitution: Marx's property divide operates in production, where owners of means of production set non owners to work and appropriate the surplus, while Weber's operates in the market, where property of every kind, including credentials and skills treated as marketable assets, commands differing chances. The second is the expected sequel: Marx's divide generates interests that press toward organisation and conflict, while Weber's generates probabilities that may issue in anything from collective action to individual resignation. The third is the surrounding architecture: Marx subordinates honour and political power to the economic relation in the last instance, while Weber grants status and party their own logics and their own causal weight. A student who can state the agreement clause and then locate the dispute in those three places has already surpassed the standard answer, because the standard answer spends its length on a contrast its subject explicitly forecloses.

### Where exactly do Marx and Weber agree?

They agree that property, and its absence, form the divide from which class situations arise. Weber states the point as the foundation of his scheme, not as a concession extracted from it. Their disagreement begins after the agreement: over whether the divide works through production or through the market, and over what follows from it for action and history.

The agreement clause also disciplines the reading of Weber's pluralism. His many classes are differentiations within a world already divided by property, not alternatives to that division. The rentier, the entrepreneur, the credentialed professional, and the propertyless worker are not four free standing types; they are positions generated by kinds of property and kinds of marketable service in a structure whose basic categories remain ownership and non ownership. Conversely, the clause disciplines the reading of Marx. If Weber, the supposedly anti Marxist pluralist, begins from property, then Marx's insistence on property cannot be the crude reductionism of the caricature either, since the same starting point in Weber's hands supports a rich and differentiated analysis. The shared foundation suggests that the difference between the frameworks is a difference of development rather than of starting point, each thinker elaborating the property divide toward a different theoretical destination: Marx toward a theory of exploitation and historical transformation, Weber toward a theory of chances, honour, and organised power. Comparisons that grasp this produce better questions. Instead of asking which man noticed more dimensions, they ask which development explains more of what the researcher wants explained, which is the form the verdict section will give the contest.

## The seven-point Marx and Weber grid

The seven-point Marx and Weber grid gathers the comparison into the form this series requires of its artifacts: one table, consultable in a minute, whose cells compress arguments the surrounding sections defend at length. Read it after the reconstructions rather than before them, and treat each cell as a heading under which a paragraph of reasoning stands.

| Point of comparison | Marx | Weber |
|---|---|---|
| Ground of class | Relations of production: position relative to the means of production | Market situation: chances commanded by property and marketable services |
| Number of classes | A polar tendency under capitalism, with many classes and fractions in actual societies | Plural by design: property classes, acquisition classes, social classes |
| Source of class interest | Exploitation: the appropriation of surplus labour in production | Life chances: the probabilities a market position deals across a biography |
| Status of collective action | Expected: shared position ripens into organisation and class for itself | Contingent: communal action possible, never guaranteed by position alone |
| Prestige and power | Real but derivative: honour and rule follow property in the last instance | Independent dimensions: status honour and party organisation obey their own logics |
| Historical dynamic | Directional: accumulation, crisis, and transformation of the mode of production | Without destination: rationalisation and bureaucratisation proceed without a terminus |
| What each predicts | Polarisation, organisation, and conflict centred on production | Differentiation of chances, honour struggles, and competition among organised groups |
| Modern successor schema | Relational class analysis associated with Erik Olin Wright | Occupational class schemas associated with John Goldthorpe |

Seven contrasts, and each conceals a nuance the table cannot hold. The ground of class differs, yet both grounds are property. The number of classes differs, yet Marx's histories are as crowded as Weber's typologies. The source of interest differs most deeply, since exploitation is a relation between classes while life chances are an attribute of a position, and a theory of what classes do to each other is not interchangeable with a theory of what positions do for their occupants. The status of collective action differs as a matter of theoretical expectation, which is why the same strike reads as the theory confirming itself in one framework and as a contingent achievement requiring explanation in the other. Prestige and power mark the boundary of Marx's explanatory ambition, since his claim that honour follows property is a claim, contestable and contested, while Weber's independence thesis is equally a claim, and the evidence of honour systems that outlive the wealth that founded them can be mustered on Weber's side. The historical dynamic is the widest gulf: direction against directionlessness, a story with a plot against a process with only a trend. And the predictions row shows why the contest survived into empirical sociology, because the two frameworks do not merely interpret the same facts differently; they direct attention to different facts, and a discipline needs both directions of attention more often than it needs a winner.

A word on the final row is warranted here, with its full treatment reserved for the section on the modern contest. The successor schemas are not footnotes to the classical dispute; they are its continuation by other means, built by scholars who operationalised each framework for survey research and then submitted the results to comparison. Goldthorpe's occupational classes, constructed from employment relations and market and work situations, carry Weber's market location into the study of mobility. Wright's class locations, constructed from ownership, authority, and expertise within production, carry Marx's relational location into the study of income and attitudes. That the two schemas can be run against the same datasets is the best evidence that the classical comparison is a live research programme rather than a museum piece, and it is also the reason this article insists on a criterion based verdict: the contest has data, and data deserve better than tribal loyalty.

## Status: honour with laws of its own

Weber's second dimension answers a question his class concept deliberately leaves open: why do people who share a market position so often fail to recognise each other as fellows, while people across class lines unite in defence of a rank, a style of life, or a code of honour? His answer is the status group. Where class is rooted in the market, status is rooted in social honour, in the estimation of prestige that a group claims and others concede. Status groups cultivate distinctive styles of life, restrict social intercourse to their own circle, regulate marriage within it, and monopolise privileges and the goods that symbolise them, from forms of dress and address to access to offices and professions. A status order therefore stratifies a society along lines that cross its class structure at every angle. The impoverished aristocrat retains honour the wealthy parvenu cannot buy outright. The high caste labourer outranks the low caste merchant in honour while trailing him in income. For Weber these are not anomalies to be ironed out by a richer class concept. They are evidence that honour is a distributive system of its own, with its own currency and its own defences.

The independence of status is the point on which Weber's scheme most clearly refuses absorption into Marx's. A Marxian reading treats honour as superstructural, real in its effects but ultimately anchored in the distribution of property and power, so that prestige follows wealth the way a shadow follows a body, with a lag and with distortions. Weber's treatment reverses the explanatory priority in the cases that interest him most. Status honour, he argues, can stand in direct tension with the market, and status groups characteristically restrict the free play of market forces, monopolising opportunities, fixing conventions of consumption, and despising the naked pursuit of gain that the market rewards. The established professions that close entry by credential and code, the old families that treat new money as vulgar, the castes that assign honour by birth regardless of occupation: each is a status order limiting class formation from outside. Whether honour is ultimately anchored in property is, for Weber, a question to be settled case by case, and his cases frequently settle it against the economic reading. The honest summary is that Marx explains status by class in the long run and Weber explains class situations partly by status in the short and medium runs, and since most social life is lived in the short and medium runs, the practical difference between the frameworks is larger than the long run formulation suggests.

### How does status differ from class in Weber's scheme?

Class rests on market position and the life chances it deals, a matter of property and marketable services assessed without reference to honour. Status rests on social estimation, on prestige claimed and conceded, expressed through a shared style of life, restricted intercourse, and monopolised privileges. The two orders overlap often and coincide rarely, and each can obstruct the other.

Status also does explanatory work that the verdict section must weigh. It accounts for the political behaviour that class models chronically underpredict: voting and loyalty that follow confessional, ethnic, regional, and honour lines rather than income lines. It accounts for consumption as a social act, the purchase of distinction rather than of utility, which later sociology developed extensively. And it accounts for closure, the process by which groups monopolise opportunities by restricting eligibility, whether the boundary drawn is one of birth, credential, language, or respectability. Closure is arguably the Weberian concept with the widest working life in empirical sociology, applied to professions, to immigration regimes, to elite schooling, and to housing markets, and it belongs to the status side of the scheme rather than the class side. A comparison that confines Weber's contribution to a triad of nouns misses the machinery the nouns contain. Status is not a polite word for prestige left over after economics. It is a theory of how honour is organised, defended, and converted into advantage, and on subjects where that is the phenomenon, education hierarchies among them, the Marxian framework reaches for supplementary explanations while the Weberian framework is already home.

Marx is not defenceless on this territory, and fairness requires his counter resources to be stated in his own terms. He has the concept of the traditional and customary elements in the value of labour power, which lets honour and expectation enter the wage itself. He has the analysis of ideology, in which the prestige of dominant ideas and professions is traced to the order they legitimate. His historical narratives track status groups with a novelist's eye, the finance aristocracy, the bureaucratic caste, the peasantry bound by tradition to a name and a memory. The difference is one of theoretical bookkeeping. In Marx's accounts, status phenomena are entries in a ledger whose totals are struck elsewhere, in the relations of production. In Weber's, the ledger has several currencies and no master account. A researcher persuaded that the economy denominates every other sphere in the long run will find Marx's bookkeeping truer. A researcher struck by how long honour orders survive the economic arrangements that supposedly explain them, hereditary prestige persisting across revolutions and regime changes, will find Weber's refusal of a master account the more honest instrument. The grid records the divergence; it cannot settle it, because the settlement depends on time horizons and on which cases a tradition of research chooses as its tests.

## Party: organised power as a third order

The third term of Weber's triad is the one summaries handle worst, because party in his usage is not confined to political parties in the electoral sense. A party, for Weber, is any association whose orientation is toward the acquisition of social power and the influencing of communal action, whatever the content of its programme. Parties may represent class interests, status interests, or ideals pursued for their own sake; they may be ephemeral or permanent, charismatic or bureaucratic, devoted to patronage or to a cause. Their defining feature is organisation directed at power within a corporate group, a state, a church, a municipality, an apparatus of rule. The concept therefore spans the revolutionary organisation, the electoral machine, the interest association with a permanent staff, and the faction inside a bureaucracy. What unites them is not ideology but form: a staff, a discipline, a struggle for command of an organisation's decisions.

Placing party beside class and status completes Weber's refusal of a single distributive order. Power in his scheme is not a shadow cast by wealth, because organisation generates power of its own. A disciplined minority with a staff and a programme can rule a wealthy but unorganised majority, and the history of revolutionary parties, administrative apparatuses, and machine politics supplies his evidence. The bureaucratic officialdom of a modern state is his standing example of a stratum whose power rests neither on property nor on honour in the traditional sense but on position within an apparatus, on files, procedures, and expertise monopolised by office. This is the point at which Weber's scheme reaches toward a theory of domination that Marx's class concept touches through the state and ideology but does not centre. It is also the point at which the two frameworks generate their most instructive disagreement about politics: whether political organisation expresses class position, as the Marxian tradition expects, or constitutes an independent field in which class is one resource among several, as the Weberian tradition insists, with organisational skill, legitimacy, and control of apparatuses doing causal work of their own.

The comparison sharpens when the two traditions examine the same organisations. A trade union, in a Marxian account, is the elementary form of working class combination, arising from the class position itself as workers learn their common interest against employers. In a Weberian account, the same union is a party in his technical sense, an association with a staff, a treasury, and internal struggles for control, whose officials acquire interests of their own and whose behaviour follows the logic of organisation as much as the logic of class. The second reading does not refute the first; it explains the phenomena the first reading treats as betrayals or accidents, the conservatism of union bureaucracies, the divergence between members' interests and officials' strategies, the survival of organisations after their founding purposes fade. Weber's student Robert Michels generalised the point into an iron law of oligarchy drawn from studying a socialist party itself, and the fact that the law was formulated inside the Marxist movement's own house gives it a standing in this comparison that external criticism could not achieve. Organisation, the Weberian tradition concludes, is never a transparent vessel for class. It is a stratum forming machine of its own, and any class analysis that ignores it will be surprised by its own instruments.

None of this makes party a free floating force, and Weber is careful, as Marx would demand, about the resources organisation requires. Parties need money, and money follows class and status channels; parties recruit, and recruitment follows the grievances that class situations and status injuries supply. The Weberian claim is not that power floats above the economy but that it is not reducible to it, that the conversion of economic position into organised power is a contingent achievement with its own conditions and its own failures. That claim, modest as it sounds, is the hinge of the whole comparison, because Marx's framework stakes its historical wager on the conversion succeeding for one class in particular. The next section takes the wager up directly.

## Collective action: expectation against contingency

The deepest practical difference between the frameworks is a difference of expectation about what classes do. In Marx's construction, the movement from shared position to collective action is the theory's central storyline. Capital itself assembles the workers, concentrates them in factories and cities, disciplines them in cooperative production, and teaches them combination by forcing them to resist encroachments on wages and hours. Organisation is therefore not an accident that befalls a class but the maturation of the class itself, the form in which an objective interest becomes a subjective force. Failures of organisation are explained within the theory: immaturity, division by trade or nation, the dead weight of tradition, the bribery of a labour aristocracy by imperial rents. The expectation is defeasible in every particular case and unwavering in general. Given time and development, the proletariat becomes a class for itself, because the structure that exploits it also equips it.

Weber's construction contains no equivalent storyline, and its absence is deliberate. A shared class situation, he argues, creates at most a probable basis for communal action, and whether anything follows depends on conditions external to the situation itself: the transparency of the connection between position and outcome, the presence of intellectuals who formulate the grievance, the availability of organisation, the behaviour of status groups and parties that may cut across the class line. Workers may react to identical situations with revolt, with individual striving for exit, with millenarian hope, or with resignation, and the sociologist's task is to explain the variation rather than to predict a single outcome from the position alone. Class action is thus demoted from destiny to datum. What Marx treats as the theory's crown, Weber treats as one empirical possibility among several, no more guaranteed by a market situation than a shared lottery of birth guarantees a shared politics.

### Why does Weber doubt that classes act together?

A shared class situation supplies similar chances and grievances, but people read them through status loyalties, confessional ties, and national feeling. Collective action therefore depends on transparency of interests, on organisers, and on circumstances no market position guarantees, so Weber treats it as contingent where Marx treats it as the expected maturation of a class.

The empirical record since both men wrote can be quoted by each side, which is precisely Weber's point about contingency and precisely Marx's point about tendency. Episodes of rapid class organisation, mass strikes, and revolutionary parties confirm that class positions can ignite collective action on a scale no other identity has matched in industrial societies. Episodes of quiescence, of workers voting for parties of order, of class grievances dissolving into ethnic or confessional conflict, confirm that ignition is not automatic. The sophisticated positions within each tradition converge on conditional statements: organisation follows position when specified conditions obtain, a formulation that began as Weber's caution and became, in the best Marxian historiography, a description of how the expectation actually works in practice. E. P. Thompson's account of the making of a working class, in which class is a historical relationship that happens through experience and articulation rather than a structure that secretes consciousness, is the famous meeting point, Weberian in method even where Marxian in sympathy. The convergence is real, and the grid's cell on collective action should be read with it in mind: the difference survives as a difference in default expectation, in what each framework treats as requiring special explanation, action for Weber, inaction for Marx. Defaults matter because they steer research. A tradition surprised by inaction studies obstacles; a tradition surprised by action studies triggers; and the two literatures, read together, cover the phenomenon better than either covers it alone.

There is one more asymmetry to record before the objection is heard. Marx's expectation of collective action is attached to a normative investment Weber never makes. The proletariat is not merely predicted to organise; its organisation is the mechanism of a general emancipation, the point at which the exploited class's interest coincides with the abolition of class itself. This gives the Marxian expectation a structure no Weberian category replicates: the class is expected to act, and its action is expected to end the conditions that define it. Weber's framework, by design, contains no class whose victory dissolves the scheme, no final synthesis in which status and party wither. Stratification for him is a permanent feature of organised societies, changing its forms under rationalisation but never approaching abolition. Readers choosing between the frameworks are therefore choosing, among other things, between a theory in which class society is a chapter and a theory in which it is a condition. The choice is partly empirical and partly temperamental, and honesty in teaching requires saying so.

## History without a destination: rationalisation and bureaucracy

Marx's framework carries a philosophy of history inside it, restrained in his best work and unmistakable in its architecture. Modes of production rise, develop the forces within them, enter contradiction with their own relations, and give way; the sequence is not a timetable, and Marx resisted attempts to turn it into a universal path every society must tread, but the direction under capitalism is argued with full theoretical force: accumulation concentrates capital, socialises labour, generates crises of increasing scope, and prepares both the material conditions and the collective agent of a successor order. Class struggle is the mechanism by which the direction is travelled. A reader always knows, in a Marxian text, roughly where in the story the present stands.

Weber's long view is a process without a plot. Rationalisation, the spread of calculation, formal rules, and bureaucratic organisation through economy, law, administration, and culture, advances across centuries without pointing beyond itself. Its fruits are competence and predictability; its cost, in his sombre vocabulary, is disenchantment, the retreat of ultimate meanings from public life, and the construction of an administrative order that confines its inhabitants with the efficiency it serves them. Bureaucracy is the purest carrier of the process, and his analysis of it is the part of his work that most constrains both frameworks in this comparison. Bureaucratic administration, he argued, is technically superior to every alternative for precision, continuity, and calculability, which is why every modern order acquires it, capitalist and socialist alike, and why it survives the revolutions that change a society's rulers and property relations. The official's power rests on expertise and files rather than ownership, a stratification resource neither classical class scheme anticipated, and its permanence mocks any expectation that transforming property alone transforms domination.

This is the substance behind the extra question students ask when they sense that both frameworks strain against the modern administrative world. Weber's bureaucracy analysis limits Marx by identifying a structure of domination that the abolition of private capital does not abolish, and it limits Weberian class analysis itself by showing that the market is not the only arena in which life chances are dealt: the office, the credential, and the file deal them too, through hierarchies that his class and status concepts must stretch to cover. His own treatment partly concedes the point by ranking officials as a status group with a distinctive honour of office. The stretch is instructive. It shows a framework encountering its boundary honestly, and it gives the comparison one of its few asymmetrical verdicts: on the specific question of why administrative apparatuses persist and rule, Weber's wider scheme explains more than Marx's narrower one, a concession the Marxian tradition has repeatedly absorbed by developing its own theories of bureaucracy, usually by treating the apparatus as a stratum with interests rooted in its control of the surplus, which is a Marxian answer built on Weberian ground.

The absence of historical direction in Weber also has a methodological root that students should grasp, because it explains the shape of his class concept. His ideal types are measuring rods for reality, constructed by one sided accentuation, and they are not expected to be realised or to evolve into one another. A class situation does not mature into anything. It is a configuration that recurs, combines, and dissolves as markets shift. Marx's categories, by contrast, are moments of a developing totality, and their definitions carry their futures inside them: capital is unintelligible without accumulation, the proletariat without its growth and organisation. This is why the two bodies of theory feel different to read even when they describe the same factory. One is dissecting a specimen; the other is tracing a life. Neither procedure is a mistake. They are answers to different questions about what social science is for, and the verdict section will treat them as such rather than forcing a single crown onto a comparison that contains several distinct contests.

## The strongest Weberian objection

The objection must now be heard at full strength, in the form a serious Weberian would choose. It begins with the occupational structure of any advanced economy and asks the production based scheme a simple question: where do all these people go? Between the owners of substantial capital and the propertyless wage earners stretches the great mass of modern employment: managers who direct production they do not own, professionals whose credentials command scarcity rents, technicians, administrators, supervisors, public employees, the self employed, small employers who work beside their handful of staff. A relational scheme with two poles must either force this mass into one pole, where its incomes, security, and outlook plainly diverge from the pole's other members, or multiply intermediate positions until the elegant structure resembles the plural typology it was meant to replace. The Marxian literature has done the second, honestly and ingeniously, generating contradictory locations, semi autonomous positions, and fractions without end. The Weberian presses the point: each rescue concedes that market situation, the very criterion the rival scheme began from, is doing the classificatory work. A credential is a market asset. A manager's authority is priced in a labour market. What the relational scheme calls an intermediate position, the market scheme calls a class situation, and it gets there directly, without apology or scaffolding.

The objection then turns from classification to performance. Class schemes earn their keep by predicting the outcomes stratification research cares about: income and its growth, unemployment risk, health and mortality, children's attainment, mobility between generations, political attitudes. On these standard variables, the objection continues, market and occupational schemas perform at least as well as relational ones in head to head tests, and they do so with categories that coders can apply reliably from survey data, without adjudicating the theoretically loaded question of who exploits whom. Parsimony joins performance: a scheme that explains the same variance with fewer theoretical commitments is, by the ordinary rules of science, the scheme to prefer. Add the historical count: the predicted polarisation did not arrive on schedule, real incomes of wage earners rose across the twentieth century in the advanced economies, and the middle strata grew rather than withered. A framework whose central tendency failed its largest test, the objection concludes, survives by qualification, while the framework that expected differentiation all along simply watches the world resemble its typology.

A third strand of the objection is methodological and cuts deepest, because it concerns what kind of statement a class theory is. Marx's categories, the Weberian argues, are not neutral instruments but moments of a philosophy of history, and their apparent empirical content is protected by the philosophy when the facts turn awkward: delay becomes immaturity, contrary voting becomes false consciousness, the growth of middle strata becomes a transitional form. A theory that can absorb every outcome has explained none of them in advance. Weber's categories, whatever their limits, make no prophecy and therefore cannot cheat; they sort positions, state the chances attached, and leave the future open, which is what a science of society should do. The charge of unfalsifiability has been pressed against many grand theories, and the Marxian tradition has answers to it, some of them considered in the reply that follows. But the objection deserves its full force before any answer is attempted, because a comparison that weakens the strongest opposing case has decided nothing. On classification, on performance, on parsimony, and on method, the Weberian objection claims the field, and any verdict that ignores the claim is allegiance dressed as judgement.

## What the production view explains that the market view assumes

The reply begins by conceding what must be conceded, plainly and without varnish. On the standard outcome variables of stratification research, income determination, mobility chances, health gradients, and attitudinal patterns, market based schemas perform comparably to relational ones and sometimes marginally better, with cleaner coding and fewer auxiliary hypotheses. That concession is the price of honesty, and the brief for this comparison requires it to be paid. The relational scheme's claim to superiority does not lie on that ground, and its defenders damage it when they pretend otherwise. Where the production view claims its advantage is upstream of the variables the market scheme predicts so well. A market schema takes market positions as its starting point: it begins with people already endowed with property, credentials, and labour power, already facing one another as buyers and sellers, and it predicts, often brilliantly, what follows. The production view asks how the endowments got there and what happens between the parties once the bargain is struck and the factory door closes. Why does one side bring factories and the other side bring only hands? Why does the working day divide into a portion that replaces the wage and a portion that does not, and who appropriates the second portion? Why does the authority inside production take the form it takes, command flowing one way, obedience the other? These are questions about the origin and internal structure of market positions, and a framework that begins after them cannot answer them, because its categories start where the answers end.

Exploitation is the name of the largest of these upstream facts, and the reply's central claim is that a class theory without it describes a distribution while declining to explain it. The market schema can report, with great precision, that the chances of the propertyless are worse than the chances of the propertied. The production schema explains why the connection is not accidental: the worse chances of the propertyless are the obverse of a transfer, the surplus labour appropriated in production, which funds the returns that make the propertied chances better. Distribution, on this account, is not a pattern that settles over society like weather; it is produced, continuously, in a relation between classes, and the relation is the explanation of the pattern. A researcher satisfied with prediction will find this surplus metaphysics. A researcher who wants to know why the pattern reproduces itself, why the children of each class so reliably inherit its chances, and where the resources that fund advantage are generated, will find the market schema's silence on production to be the gap that matters. The two frameworks are therefore not answering the same question with different success. They are answering different questions, and the objection's performance comparison, valid on its own ground, does not touch the ground on which the production view stands.

The reply has a second element, about dynamics. Market situations are snapshots of endowments, and a theory built from them explains change by importing it: technology shifts, demand shifts, credentials inflate, and the schema re sorts the population into its new positions with undiminished elegance. The production view generates change from inside its categories, because accumulation is a process with consequences: it concentrates capitals, revolutionises techniques, creates and destroys occupations, and periodically convulses the whole structure in crisis. Whether the generated changes match the historical record in detail is arguable, and the objection has already had its say on the largest prediction. But a framework that explains why positions change at all holds an explanatory asset a classificatory scheme cannot replicate, and the asset shows in practice: when researchers confront a transformation of the class structure itself, the deskilling of a craft, the platform reorganisation of a trade, the financialisation of an economy, they reach for relational and processual language even when their datasets are coded in occupational classes. The wider theoretical map on which this division of labour sits, including the functionalist alternative that dissolves class into shared values and graded prestige, is charted in this series in the comparison of [marxism vs functionalism](/2021/08/01/marxism-vs-functionalism/), and it confirms the pattern: each tradition keeps the questions it can answer and borrows, quietly, for the rest.

On method, the reply concedes the danger and disputes the verdict. A theory with a historical wager can degenerate into prophecy defended by qualification, and parts of the Marxian tradition did exactly that. But the relational core of the theory, stripped of timetable, makes claims that can fail: that control of production confers systematic advantage beyond what market position alone predicts, that authority at work sorts people into locations with distinct interests, that ownership as such, separate from occupation and income, carries effects on wealth accumulation and political outlook. The modern successor examined next built those claims into a survey instrument precisely so they could be tested, and some of them survived testing while others did not, which is the biography of a research programme rather than of a dogma. The Weberian objection, fairly heard, convicts the philosophy of history in its prophetic form. It does not convict the relational analysis of production, and the distinction between those two targets is the most important judgement this comparison asks its reader to make.

## The middle ground both frameworks must cross

Every comparison of the two schemes is eventually tested against the same awkward population: the strata that sit between the great poles, too propertied or too credentialed for the proletariat of the theory's imagination, too dependent on salaries and markets for the bourgeoisie. Marx's own name for much of this territory was the petty bourgeoisie, the small producers and traders who own their means of production and work them themselves, and his analysis of their position was subtler than the polar model suggests: a class defined by a contradiction, exploiting no one systematically and exploited by capital through credit, rent, and competition, politically oscillating between the camps that flank it. The category has proved durable because the position is durable, renewed in each generation by new trades. Its fuller treatment in this series, in the article on the [petty bourgeoisie and middle class](/2011/12/01/petty-bourgeoisie-and-middle-class/), shows how much of the modern argument about the middle was already staged inside the classical texts.

The modern middle is larger and stranger than the classical petty bourgeoisie, because it is composed mainly of employees. Managers, professionals, experts, and administrators sell their labour power, which places them on one side of Marx's founding divide, while commanding authority, autonomy, credentials, and incomes that place them far from the divide's other pole in every lived respect. The two frameworks handle this population characteristically. The market scheme differentiates it without strain: employment relations differ, some exchanging service for salary, security, and career, others exchanging labour for wages by the hour, and the occupational classes built on those differences are the glory of the neo Weberian tradition. The relational scheme differentiates it with effort and with insight the market scheme lacks: by asking who controls whose labour, who appropriates and who decides, it identifies the internal structure of the salariat, the manager as capital's functionary and the expert as a contradictory location, privileged by skill scarcity and subordinated by employment at once. Each handling reveals what the other conceals. The market scheme sees the gradient of advantage with unmatched clarity and is blind to the relations of command that produce it. The relational scheme sees the command and is chronically tempted to underrate the gradient, treating as one class people whose life chances diverge so widely that their politics diverge with them. A reader who has grasped why both temptations arise has understood the comparison more deeply than a reader who has merely picked the handling they prefer.

Public employment deserves a paragraph of its own, because it stresses both schemes in a place neither classical author fully mapped. The teacher, the nurse, the administrator, and the inspector are employed by the state, outside the circuit of capital in the direct sense, yet sorted by every class schema that empirical research uses. The market scheme absorbs them by their employment relations and credentials, which works descriptively and explains their chances well. The relational scheme must work harder, treating public labour through its relation to the surplus produced elsewhere and to the functions of the state, which yields genuine insights about austerity and the fiscal politics of class while never quite giving the public professional a home as natural as the factory worker's. Weber, who analysed bureaucracy as a world of its own, stands closer to the phenomenon than either successor scheme, and the point returns in the verdict: his original triad, with office and honour given their due, sometimes handles the administrative strata better than the purified class concepts of both his heirs and Marx's.

## A worked comparison: one factory, two readings

Abstractions in this subject stay honest only when they are forced through a single concrete case, so consider one manufacturing firm of a few hundred employees, of a kind found in every industrial region: owned by a family holding company, managed by a salaried chief executive, staffed by engineers, line supervisors, machinists, assemblers, cleaners, and a small office of accountants and sales staff, with a canteen run by an outside contractor. Nothing about the firm is exotic, which is the point. The two frameworks, applied to the same building, produce two different maps of it, and the differences between the maps are the comparison made visible.

The Marxian reading sorts the firm by relation to production. The holding company stands at the pole of capital, appropriating the surplus the firm's labour produces and deciding, through the executive it appoints, how that surplus is divided between reinvestment, dividends, and debt service. The chief executive, though salaried, functions as capital's personification inside the plant, rewarded in proportion to the surplus extracted and dismissed when extraction falters. Supervisors occupy the contradictory ground of the foreman's classic position, selling their labour power while exercising capital's authority over others. The machinists and assemblers are the productive core whose working day divides into necessary and surplus portions. The office staff and sales personnel perform the labour of circulation, necessary to the firm, paid from the surplus rather than productive of it in the strict sense. The cleaners, employed by a contractor, stand at one remove, their labour purchased as a service by a second firm that itself extracts surplus from their day. The reading yields a structure of relations, an account of where the firm's income originates, and a prediction: conflict inside the plant will organise along the line between those who appropriate and direct and those who produce and obey, and the wage bargain will be its permanent low level expression.

The Weberian reading sorts the same building by market situation, status, and party, and a different structure emerges. Class situations are dealt by property and by the market value of services: the engineers' credentials command scarcity and security, the machinists' craft skills command a premium that automation is eroding, the assemblers' labour is abundant and cheap, the cleaners' cheaper still and stripped of security by the contracting form. Status lines cut across these: the engineers form a status group with its own honour, dress, and disdain for the commercial office; the office staff claim a respectability the shop floor is denied in local estimation, though their pay may be lower; the family's name carries a local prestige that opens doors no balance sheet explains. Party, in Weber's sense, appears in the organised groupings that contend for power over the firm's common life: the union branch with its staff and internal factions, the management cadre as an organised interest, the family's holding board as the decisive apparatus. The reading yields a distribution of chances and honours and a different prediction: behaviour inside the plant will follow these lines as readily as the line of production, and in a dispute over shift patterns, canteen provision, or the honour of a craft, the status and party map will predict allegiances the production map gets wrong.

Neither map is the firm. Each is an instrument that makes some of the firm's reality legible by leaving the rest in shadow, and the practical researcher, like the practical trade unionist or manager, uses both, whether or not the theories are acknowledged. The union organiser who thinks only in production terms will be ambushed by craft honour and by the cleaners' exclusion from the bargaining unit. The human resources manager who thinks only in market terms will be ambushed by the solidarity that a common relation to the appropriation of surplus generates on the line. The worked case therefore teaches the article's central lesson in miniature. The frameworks are rival answers because they begin in different places, production and exchange, and each beginning illuminates the territory the other leaves dark. Declaring one map the territory is the error; learning to read both, and knowing which question each answers, is the competence the comparison exists to build.

## The modern contest: Wright against Goldthorpe

The classical dispute did not stay classical. In the second half of the twentieth century, two research programmes rebuilt the rival frameworks as instruments of survey sociology and then, in a development both classical authors would have recognised as the compliment of serious opposition, tested them against each other. The neo Weberian programme is associated above all with John Goldthorpe and the class schema developed with his collaborators for the study of social mobility. Its classes are constructed from employment relations: employers, the self employed, and employees differentiated by the kind of contract they hold, a service relationship of salary, security, and delegated authority on one side, a labour contract of wages and close supervision on the other, with intermediate forms between. The schema's Weberian descent is exact. Classes are market and work situations, validated by the life chances they predict, above all the chances of mobility and immobility between generations, and the programme's great empirical monument is the finding of persistent class inequalities in mobility chances beneath rising absolute movement, a pattern the schema was built to detect. The wider assessment of that evidence is taken up in this series in the article on [class structure evidence](/2011/11/15/class-structure-evidence/), which examines how much of the structure survives its own data.

The neo Marxist programme is associated with Erik Olin Wright, whose reconstruction began from the admission that Marx's two poles could not classify modern employment and proceeded to rebuild the relational criterion rather than abandon it. Wright's class locations are defined by three assets within production: ownership of capital, control of organisational resources, and possession of scarce skills and credentials. Managers and supervisors occupy contradictory locations, dominant through organisation while dominated as employees; experts occupy another, privileged by skill exploitation while excluded from ownership; small employers and the self employed fill out a map of twelve locations in its mature form. The programme's testable claims were distinctive: that class location so defined would explain income and class attitudes beyond what occupation, education, and market situation alone explain, because it captures exploitation and domination rather than advantage in general. Wright's own comparative projects, run across several countries with common instruments, are the most direct attempt in the discipline's history to give a Marxian class analysis an empirical examination of Weberian rigour, and his work is examined in depth in this series in the article on [erik olin wright class analysis](/2020/02/15/erik-olin-wright-class-analysis/).

### What happened when the two schemas were tested against each other?

Direct comparisons, run on shared datasets in several countries, found the schemas performing comparably on the standard outcomes: income, mobility chances, and attitudes separated classes in both, with neither schema dominating across all measures. The durable divergence lay elsewhere, in the questions each was built to answer, exploitation and authority on one side, chances and mobility on the other.

The honest report on the contest, which this article is bound to give without embroidery, is that the predicted rout never occurred in either direction. Where the outcomes are the standard ones of stratification research, the two schemas divide the honours, the occupational schema generally cleaner on mobility and the relational schema generally adding something on income within occupations and on the patterning of attitudes toward authority and redistribution. Where the outcomes are the distinctively Marxian ones, the distribution of control over production, the experience of domination at work, the relational schema wins on its home ground almost by construction, since the rival schema does not measure those things at all. And where the outcome is the distinctively Weberian one, the fine structure of chances across a biography and a generation, the occupational schema wins on its ground for the same reason. The contest therefore ends by confirming the article's thesis from the empirical side. The frameworks are not two estimates of one quantity, between which research must choose the more accurate. They are two instruments pointed at different strata of the same social reality, and their comparable performance on shared variables is less significant than their divergent reach on the variables only one of them sees.

Two further findings from the comparative literature deserve record, because students cite the contest as though it were a football result. The first is how much classification agreement the schemas show at the extremes and how much they diverge in the middle, which is the empirical shadow of the classical problem of the intermediate strata: owners and routine workers are sorted alike by both, while managers, experts, and supervisors migrate between categories as the criterion changes. The second is that both schemas outperform the crude alternatives, income bands and prestige scales, by margins that vindicate the classical investment in theory: how a scheme is built matters to what it finds, and schemes built from explicit accounts of production or of markets find more than schemes built from convenience. A discipline that had settled the Marx versus Weber question by discarding one side would, on this evidence, have discarded predictive and explanatory power it demonstrably uses. The contest's real verdict is pluralist in method and particularist in substance: use the instrument built for your question, and do not mistake its silence on other questions for their unimportance.

## Mobility as a testing ground

Social mobility is the arena in which the Weberian framework most plainly sets the terms, because mobility is by definition a matter of chances: the chance that a person born into one class situation arrives, across a lifetime or a generation, in another. The great mobility studies, designed in the Weberian lineage, established the patterns any class theory must accommodate: substantial absolute mobility driven by changes in the occupational structure itself, as agricultural and manual jobs contracted and service and professional jobs expanded; relative chances that remained stubbornly unequal, so that the odds of a professional's child reaching the professions exceeded the odds for a manual worker's child by ratios that narrowed slowly if at all; and a distinction between the mobility a society experiences because its shape changes and the openness of its competition for places, which can stagnate while movement all around it increases. A class framework that could not speak this language would forfeit the subject, and it is a strength of the Marxian tradition's best empirical work that it learned to speak it, translating its claims into the idiom of chances and odds while keeping its own questions about the structure whose shape generates the movement.

Yet mobility also exposes what the chances framework does not ask. A society can exhibit generous movement between generations while the relations that define its classes stand untouched, each cohort of individuals circulating through positions whose relative rewards and powers remain as they were. Circulation of persons is not transformation of structure, and a mobility table, however refined, cannot distinguish a society that is equalising from a society that is merely reshuffling. The Marxian question survives the mobility evidence intact: not who arrives where, but what the places are, how their occupants relate, and whether the process that fills and empties them alters the relations between them. Conversely, the Weberian question survives every Marxian answer: whatever the structure, individuals live the chances dealt to their position, and a theory that cannot state those chances cannot describe their lives. Mobility research at its best is the standing demonstration that the comparison has no global winner, only questions with better and worse instruments, which is the verdict the next section formalises.

## Which framework answers which question

The comparison can now be decided in the only form the evidence supports: not a coronation but an allocation, question by question, under a stated criterion. The criterion used here is explanatory reach relative to the question asked: for each standard question in the study of class, which framework's categories engage the mechanism that produces the phenomenon, rather than merely correlating with it? Judged by that criterion, the allocation falls out with reasonable clarity, and its clarity is itself a finding, because a comparison that was mere tribal costume would allocate everything to one side.

For the question of where economic advantage originates, why the returns to property persist and grow, and what happens inside production between the hiring and the sale, the Marxian framework answers better, because its categories were built for that mechanism and the Weberian categories begin downstream of it. For the question of how advantage is distributed as chances across lives and generations, who faces which odds of security, mobility, health, and schooling, the Weberian framework answers better, because life chances and market situation are that question's native concepts and the relational categories reach it only by translation. For honour, prestige, and the monopolies groups build around them, Weber answers better and by a wide margin; Marx has a theory of status as derivative, but the derivation repeatedly underestimates the autonomy the evidence shows. For organised power, the verdict divides: Marx explains the interests organisations form around, Weber explains the organisations, their staffs, their oligarchies, and their survival beyond their purposes, and since political outcomes depend on both, neither framework may claim the territory. For historical change, the frameworks answer different halves: Marx explains why the structure of positions transforms, Weber explains why the administrative shell persists through transformations and why rationalisation proceeds under every flag. For the experience of domination at work, the advantage returns to the relational account and its heirs, whose categories of authority were designed to register what occupational classes flatten.

The allocation has a practical corollary for reading and for research design. Choose the framework whose categories engage your mechanism, state the choice, and treat the other framework as the audit of your blind spots rather than as a defeated rival. A study of wage inequality that uses occupational classes should be pressed on what it assumes about production; a study of exploitation that uses relational locations should be pressed on chances, honour, and organisation. This is not eclecticism, the lazy hope that two theories averaged make a better one. It is the recognition that the classical authors were answering overlapping but distinct questions, and that the overlap zone, where both claim the same phenomenon, is exactly where critical comparison does its work. Examination answers that reproduce the allocation with its reasons, instead of a verdict without them, demonstrate the skill the question exists to test, a point developed for candidates in this series in the [marxist sociology exam answers](/2021/08/15/marxist-sociology-exam-answers/).

One question remains that the allocation deliberately postpones, because it is the question on which the frameworks are genuinely irreconcilable and on which a choice, if made, should be made knowingly. It is the question of whether class society is a transient form. Marx's framework entails that the relations defining classes generate their own supersession; Weber's entails that stratification, rationalisation, and bureaucracy have no terminus and that every successor order reproduces hierarchy in new materials. No dataset adjudicates a claim about the long run of history, and the comparative studies reviewed here, spanning decades, cannot reach it. The honest teacher marks the question as open, notes which framework a student has implicitly answered it with, and refuses to let a verdict on schemas in the small masquerade as a verdict on history in the large. That refusal is not evasion. It is the boundary of what comparison can do, drawn where the evidence ends.

## Can the two be combined?

The temptation to merge the frameworks is permanent, because each is visibly strong where the other is weak, and the history of theory since both men wrote is littered with syntheses, some fruitful and some merely additive. The fruitful kind respects a condition the additive kind violates: a combination must state, for each phenomenon, which mechanism is doing the work, rather than stacking vocabularies until every concept has a synonym in the other system. Closure theory, developed in the Weberian line, combined honour and exclusion with a materialist account of usurpation and exclusion as twin strategies of subordinate and dominant groups, and earned its place by generating research. Bourdieu's synthesis of economic, cultural, and social capital transformed both inheritances, though at the cost, critics note, of a concept of capital so extended that its Marxian precision about production dissolves. Wright's later work incorporated Weberian closure into the analysis of exploitation itself, treating skill scarcity and organisational control as mechanisms by which advantage is monopolised, a synthesis from the Marxian side that concedes the Weberian point about monopoly while keeping the relational frame. These successes share a discipline: they combine at the level of mechanisms, not of labels.

The failures share the opposite habit. A scheme that lists class, status, and party and then adds exploitation as a fourth dimension has learned nothing from either author, because the terms belong to rival answers about where stratification is generated, and listing them together suppresses the rivalry that makes each informative. The agreement clause is the correct model for honest combination. Begin from what the frameworks share, the property divide, follow each development separately until its mechanism is clear, and only then ask whether a mechanism from one can be housed in the other's architecture without contradiction. Sometimes it can: organisational oligarchy can be housed in a Marxian account of parties as a law of the vessel rather than of the cargo. Sometimes it cannot: a historical terminus cannot be housed in Weber's process without breaking it, nor can the full independence of status be housed in Marx's last instance without qualification becoming surrender. Combination, done properly, is therefore a series of small treaties with stated terms, not a merger. Students who present it as a merger reveal that they have summarised both frameworks and understood the question of neither.

## Reading the two definitions side by side

Close reading is unfashionable in survey courses, and this comparison suffers for it, because the two definitions repay word by word attention. Marx's unfinished chapter on classes in the third volume of Capital poses its question with deceptive simplicity: what constitutes a class? His first answer reaches for identity of revenues and sources of revenue, wage labourers, capitalists, and landowners drawing wages, profit, and rent from distinct sources. He then immediately objects to his own answer, noting that physicians and officials would form classes by the same test, and that the infinite fragmentation of interests and positions would multiply classes without end. The manuscript breaks off there, at the threshold of the deeper criterion the whole work implies: not revenue but the relation to production that the revenue expresses, together with the opposition of interests that relation creates. The lesson for readers is that Marx's considered view has to be assembled from his practice, and his practice, in the historical works, is to define classes by their place in production and then to complicate the definition with fractions, strata, and the weight of inherited forms until the map matches the territory he is explaining.

Weber's definition, set beside it, shows the opposite literary character: complete, compressed, and deliberately unhistorical. Every clause fences off a misunderstanding. A class is a set of people sharing a causal component of their life chances, which makes class an analyst's pattern rather than a group with a will. The component is economic interest in goods and income opportunities, which excludes honour from class and reserves it for status. It operates under the conditions of markets, which fixes the arena. And the definition issues in no expectation about what the people so classified will think or do, which reserves action for separate investigation. Where Marx's fragment strains toward a theory of society in motion, Weber's paragraph strains toward an instrument that will sit still while societies are compared with it. Students who set the two texts side by side, rather than the two slides, discover that the famous contrast between one dimension and three is the least interesting sentence that can be written about either. The interesting sentences concern motion and stillness, exploitation and chances, history and typology, and they are all present in the primary material for any reader willing to slow down.

There is a discipline for keeping the primary material straight while revising a comparison of this size, and it is worth naming for the students this article serves. Keep the two definitions, the agreement clause, and the seven point grid in a single study file, each quotation pinned to its source and each cell of the grid annotated with the paragraph that defends it, so that revision tests memory against text rather than against other summaries. A tool built for exactly that kind of structured note keeping is the [VaultBook study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) companion to this series, and candidates who maintain the file across a course arrive at the examination with the comparison already organised as an argument rather than as two lists.

## Shared blind spots: household, gender, and the derivation problem

A fair comparison must also record where both frameworks fail together, because their shared silences shaped the research that inherited them. The largest is the household. Both classical schemes classify individuals by their own market or production position, and both were written as though the paradigmatic individual were a man whose position a household then shared. The difficulty became acute in the successor schemas: occupational schemes conventionally classified married women by their husbands' occupations, a practice defended on grounds of the family's shared standard of living and attacked, in a celebrated exchange, for dissolving women's own employment into a derived status their experience contradicted. The feminist critique that followed established points neither classical author had faced: that the household is itself a site of production and appropriation, of unpaid labour on which the paid economy rests; that women's class chances are jointly determined by their own positions and by the domestic division of labour; and that a scheme which cannot classify half the population without borrowing a husband's position has a defect in its foundations rather than in its coding rules.

The two traditions absorbed the critique unequally, and the pattern of absorption is instructive. The Weberian lineage revised its coding, classifying individuals by their own employment and treating household class as a separate, empirical question, which its individualist method made relatively natural. The Marxian lineage faced a deeper argument, because the critique identified a second system of appropriation, domestic labour, that the theory of exploitation had not centred, and the resulting debate about the relation between capitalism and patriarchy reproduced, inside feminist theory, the very structure of the Marx versus Weber problem: one system subordinated to another in the last instance, or two systems with logics of their own. Readers alert to the echo will see why this article insists that the comparison is a template for theorising stratification as such. Wherever two orders of inequality intersect, honour and property, gender and class, race and class, the same choices recur: derivation or independence, one ledger or several currencies, a direction of history or an open field. Weber's pluralism and Marx's monism are less two doctrines than the two permanent options, and their classical confrontation remains the cleanest place to learn what each option costs and buys.

## Ethnicity, caste, and the status order at its limit

Weber himself supplied the limiting case that tests his status concept: caste. In his analysis, a caste is a status group closed to the maximum degree, its honour guaranteed by religious sanction, its boundaries policed by rules of commensality and marriage so strict that the status order hardens into a ritual architecture. Ethnic groups he treated in the same family of concepts, communities bound by a belief in common descent, subjective and often fictitious, yet causally powerful because honour attaches to the belief. These analyses give the Weberian framework its well earned advantage in societies and in strata where descent, confession, and race organise life chances as powerfully as property does, and they explain why empirical researchers working on racial inequality so often build with Weberian materials, closure, honour, and monopolised opportunity, whatever their theoretical sympathies. A market position can be identical while honour differs absolutely, and the difference shows in outcomes no class scheme reading only the market can predict.

The Marxian tradition's handling of the same phenomena is the mirror of its handling of status in general: race and caste are real, causally weighty, and ultimately to be explained by the material orders they serve and stabilise, whether the labour regimes of plantation and mine or the segmentation of labour markets that cheapens divided workforces. The strongest versions of this account are genuinely explanatory, showing how racial orders were built by specific modes of production and rebuilt as those modes changed. The weakest commit the derivation error in its pure form, treating every honour order as a mask whose wearer is always capital, and they fail on the evidence Weber's cases assemble: honour orders that precede the capitalism supposed to explain them, that survive its transformations, and that mobilise people against their measurable class interests with a reliability no mask metaphor earns. Here the allocation of the verdict section finds some of its firmest ground. On descent based stratification, Weber's framework engages the mechanism; Marx's engages the uses to which the mechanism is put by an economic order; and research that needs the first without the second, or the second without the first, will misdescribe its object in predictable directions.

## The state in the two schemes

Neither classical author made the state a class, and both made it central, in ways that diverge characteristically. For Marx, the state is bound to the class order as its concentrated political expression: in the polemical formula, the executive of the modern state manages the common affairs of the bourgeoisie, and in the subtler historical analyses, the state acquires a relative autonomy, balancing classes, serving the long term interests of capital against the short term greed of capitalists, and in the extreme case of Bonapartism, rising above a society of deadlocked classes to rule in the name of order while preserving the property relations beneath. The tradition that followed split over how much autonomy to grant, and the split, between instrumental and structural readings, is the state theory version of this article's recurring choice. For Weber, the state is defined without reference to class at all, as the organisation claiming a monopoly of legitimate violence within a territory, staffed by officials whose power rests on office and expertise. Class enters his state analysis as one of the forces contending to capture or staff the apparatus, not as the apparatus's essence. The divergence explains a durable asymmetry in the literatures: Marxian state theory asks whose interests the state serves and is embarrassed by apparatuses that serve their own; Weberian state theory asks how the apparatus rules and is comparatively incurious about the interests sedimented in its design. Modern states, which are simultaneously employers, welfare distributors, and the largest single dealers of life chances in their societies, strain both accounts and reward the reader who holds them together: the apparatus in Weber's sense, distributing the chances that Marx's question, in whose interest, refuses to stop asking about.

## Attitudes, consciousness, and the survey evidence

One further arena of the modern contest deserves its own account, because it concerns the subject on which the classical frameworks made their most psychologically committal claims: what people in class positions think. Marx's framework expects position to educate opinion, so that interests formed in production surface as attitudes toward redistribution, authority, and the legitimacy of the order itself. Weber's framework expects a looser coupling, since status honour, confessional loyalty, and party organisation mediate between position and opinion, and a worker's politics may be dealt by his church, his region, or his union's apparatus as much as by his market situation. Survey research built to test the coupling found, characteristically, that both expectations capture part of the pattern. Class location, on either schema, predicts attitudes on economic questions with a regularity no rival variable displaces, and the relational schema's finer locations, managers against experts against routine workers, add texture the occupational schema sometimes misses. On questions of authority, morality, and national identity, the class signal weakens and the status and confessional signals strengthen, which is Weber's mediation thesis confirmed in his own terms. Class consciousness in the strong Marxian sense, a unified outlook ripening toward a unified politics, appears in the surveys as a gradient rather than a destiny: real, measurable, and permanently cross cut, exactly the finding that the sophisticated convergence described earlier, action as conditional achievement, would lead a reader to expect. The evidence does not crown a winner. It describes a world in which position presses on opinion through the channels both men identified, and in which the press is never the only force in the room.

## The two mechanisms compared directly

Strip both frameworks to their moving parts and the contrast becomes a contrast between two mechanisms. The Marxian mechanism is a transfer. Labour power is bought at its value, set to work for longer than the time its value requires, and the excess is appropriated as surplus value, which then appears, divided and disguised, as profit, interest, and rent. Every class phenomenon in the framework is downstream of that transfer: accumulation is the transfer reinvested, exploitation is the transfer named, class interest is the interest each side has in the transfer's size, and class conflict is the struggle over its terms, fought first over the length and intensity of the working day. The Weberian mechanism is a deal of chances. Property and marketable skills are brought to market, the market prices them, and the prices, repeated across a lifetime of bargains, deal out the probabilities that compose a biography. Every class phenomenon in that framework is downstream of the deal: inequality is the spread of the odds, mobility is movement between deals across generations, insecurity is a bad hand held permanently, and privilege is a hand that cannot lose. A transfer and a deal are different kinds of social machinery. A transfer has two parties bound together, each defined by the other, and it produces a surplus someone must get and someone must lose. A deal has as many parties as the market differentiates, bound only by the prices they face, and it produces a distribution without specifying any moment at which one party takes from another.

The difference in machinery explains the difference in what each framework finds scandalous, obvious, or invisible. To the transfer framework, the market's fairness is the standing puzzle: the bargain is free, equal, and just by the standards of exchange, and the transfer happens anyway, inside production, which is why the critique must descend into the hidden abode of production to find its object. To the deal framework, the transfer framework's puzzle is misplaced ingenuity: the inequality is already present in the endowments the parties bring, visible in the open, and a sociology that needs a hidden mechanism to explain unequal outcomes has overlooked the distribution of property and credentials it could have measured at the door. Each framework regards the other's central fact as either an illusion of perspective or a truth stated at the wrong level. The mature judgement, which the verdict section formalised, is that both mechanisms operate in the same economy at once: endowments are dealt, and then, inside the production the endowments organise, value is transferred. An account with only the deal cannot say where profit comes from. An account with only the transfer cannot say why the dealt hands differ so finely among the propertyless themselves. The mechanisms are rivals as explanations of class because each claims to be the foundation; they are complements as descriptions of an economy because the economy contains both a market and a production process, in that order, every working day.

## Two laboratories: the England of Marx and the Germany of Weber

Each framework was generalised from a national experience, and the experiences differ in ways that left marks on the theories. Marx's laboratory was England, the most developed capitalist economy of his century, where the factory system had concentrated workers visibly, where parliamentary reports documented the working day in prosecutorial detail, and where the polar structure his theory describes came closest to empirical visibility. His model case is an economy simplifying itself: handicraft and manufacture dissolving into large industry, the peasantry shrinking, the classes of the theory emerging from the dissolution like figures from a clearing fog. It is no accident that a theory formed there expects clarification, the intermediate forms proving transitional because in England, within his lifetime, many of them were.

Weber's laboratory was the Germany of the Kaiserreich, a society in which capitalist industry expanded inside a shell of pre capitalist rank: a landed aristocracy, the Junkers east of the Elbe, retaining political command and social honour out of proportion to their economic dynamism; a bureaucracy of university trained officials forming a status order of its own; a bourgeoisie that Weber himself criticised for political immaturity, for aping aristocratic styles instead of asserting its own; and the largest socialist workers' party in Europe, organised, disciplined, and quarantined by an authoritarian state. Nothing in that landscape simplified. Every economic position arrived wrapped in a status order, a confessional allegiance, or an apparatus. A theory formed there would naturally treat stratification as irreducibly plural, honour and office as powers in themselves, and the state as an actor no class analysis could dissolve. Weber's very first reputation rested on empirical studies of East Elbian agricultural labour, on the transformation of Junker estates into capitalist enterprises worked by Polish seasonal labour, a case in which class, nationality, status, and state policy interlocked exactly as his mature scheme would lead a researcher to expect. The comparative moral is worth stating to students plainly: theories of class carry the fingerprints of the class structures their authors watched, and part of judging Marx against Weber is judging England's trajectory against Germany's, the simplification that largely happened against the layering that largely persisted.

Later history supplied each laboratory with the other's phenomena, which is why neither framework could retire. The twentieth century's advanced economies generated the salaried, credentialed, bureaucratised middle strata that Weber's Germany prefigured, and they generated them inside economies whose crises, concentrations, and conflicts over the surplus continued to behave in ways Marx's England prefigured. A theory of class adequate to that century needed both inheritances, and the successor schemas examined earlier can be read as the two laboratory traditions submitting their instruments to a common world. The grid's historical dynamic row, direction against rationalisation, is at bottom the difference between expecting the fog to clear and expecting the layers to thicken. The evidence of a century and a half is that both processes occur, in different sectors at different speeds, which is less a refutation of both men than a measure of how much reality each one's home case taught him to see.

## Ideas, religion, and legitimacy

No account of Weber is complete without the dimension of his work that most decisively separates him from any simple materialism: his demonstration that ideas and beliefs do causal work in economic history. The Protestant ethic thesis argued that a religious doctrine, by way of the psychological pressures it placed on believers, helped to form the disciplined, methodical conduct that rational capitalism required. Whatever the verdict on the thesis in detail, its methodological point entered the discipline permanently: the material order does not simply secrete its legitimating ideas, and beliefs, once institutionalised, select and channel economic conduct in ways a theory of interests alone cannot predict. Weber's own summary image was of interests governing conduct directly while ideas, like switchmen, determine the tracks along which the dynamics of interest push action. Set beside Marx's treatment of religion as ideology, as the inverted consciousness of an inverted world and the opium through which suffering is borne, the contrast is the status argument repeated at the level of culture: derivation against relative independence, ideas as reflections against ideas as switchmen. The truth the comparative literature has converged on resembles the verdict on status. Beliefs plainly serve orders and plainly outlive and redirect them, and the ratio between service and independence is an empirical property of cases rather than a postulate either framework may simply assume.

Legitimacy is the political form of the same problem, and Weber's typology of legitimate domination, traditional, charismatic, and rational legal, supplies stratification research with something Marx's framework reaches only indirectly: an account of why the stratified order is obeyed. For Marx, obedience is secured by a mixture of coercion, habit, and ideology, and its breakdown is expected when the relations of production enter crisis. For Weber, every order of inequality rests on a belief in its legitimacy held in some measure by the dominated themselves, and the form of the belief, custom, devotion to a leader, or faith in legality, shapes the order's stability and its mode of collapse. Bureaucratic societies obey rules; charismatic movements obey persons; traditional orders obey the sanctity of the old. A class analysis that ignores legitimacy cannot explain why objectively similar class structures exhibit wildly different political temperatures, which is the same lesson the collective action section drew from the other side. Position deals the grievances; honour organises their meaning; legitimacy decides whether the meaning indicts the order or consecrates it. Weber's triad of class, status, and party turns out to be shadowed by a second triad of domination's forms, and the complete Weberian instrument is larger than the famous three nouns suggest.

## The countryside: peasants, Junkers, and seasonal labour

The rural world tests both frameworks at their foundations, because agriculture is where the pre capitalist orders survived longest inside capitalist economies. Marx's peasantry is one of his most original constructions: a class defined by a shared condition, small property worked by family labour, and by an incapacity for the collective action his theory expects from classes, since the mode of production isolates its members instead of concentrating them. The sack of potatoes image, however harsh, encodes a genuine analytical claim, that class formation depends on the connective tissue a mode of production supplies, dense in factories, absent in scattered smallholdings. His analysis of ground rent extends the transfer mechanism into agriculture, showing how surplus extracted from rural labour divides between tenant capitalists and landowners, and his late letters on the Russian commune show him refusing, against his own systematists, to make the English path of peasant dissolution a universal fate. The countryside in Marx is thus neither an afterthought nor a simple residue: it is the case that proves his theory of class formation is about relations and connections, not about poverty or numbers.

Weber came to sociology through the countryside rather than arriving at it late. His studies of East Elbian labour documented a transformation both frameworks need: the patriarchal estate, worked by resident labourers bound by payments in kind and by traditional authority, dissolving under market pressure into capitalist farming worked by migrant seasonal labour, freer in contract and harsher in condition, while the Junker landowners retained the political command and social honour of the old order inside the new one. Every Weberian theme is present in embryo: market situation displacing traditional status, honour surviving its economic base, the state shaping the labour supply through border and settlement policy, and national status conflict, German against Polish labour, cutting across the class line the transformation was drawing. Read the two rural literatures together and the comparison achieves a concreteness the abstract sections cannot: Marx explains the dissolution of the peasantry as a moment in capital's formation and finds its politics in the class's structural isolation; Weber explains the same dissolution as a re dealing of chances and honours and finds its politics in status, nation, and state. Agrarian change since, from collectivisation to agribusiness, has supplied evidence in both men's currencies, and rural sociology remains one of the fields where the choice of framework visibly changes the monograph that gets written.

## What each framework expects from a crisis

A final test sharpens the comparison, because crises are the moments when class structures confess their mechanics. In the Marxian expectation, crisis is endogenous and revelatory: the contradictions of accumulation, overproduction against restricted consumption, the falling tendency of the rate of profit, periodically convulse the order, bankrupt the weak capitals, discipline the strong, throw the reserve of unemployed labour into visibility, and strip the customary veils from class relations, so that politics polarises along the lines production has drawn. Depressions in this reading are the theory's home weather, the times when its categories stop being abstractions. In the Weberian expectation, crisis has no such scripted meaning. An economic collapse re deals life chances suddenly and brutally, and status orders defend themselves, parties contend for the apparatus that will manage the emergency, and legitimacy, the belief that the order deserves obedience, is the variable on which outcomes turn: orders fall not when their economics fail but when their legitimacy does, and they survive economic failure that would seem unsurvivable when the belief holds. The historical crises of the century after both men offer cases to each reading, collapses that polarised classes exactly as the transfer mechanism predicts, and collapses metabolised by apparatuses and legitimacy beliefs exactly as the deal mechanism predicts, sometimes in the same country in successive decades. The sober conclusion repeats the pattern of every previous test. Crises reveal the transfer, and they are decided by the deal of chances, honour, and organised power, and an analyst equipped with only one of those sentences will misread the next emergency in a predictable direction.

## Education: the hinge where the frameworks meet

If one institution forces the two frameworks into the same room, it is education, because schooling is simultaneously a market asset, a status ritual, and a mechanism by which class relations reproduce themselves across generations. Weber saw the status face with unmatched clarity. His sociology of education treats the diploma as a patent of status as much as a certificate of skill, the examination as the ritual by which a stratum of the cultivated and the credentialed constitutes itself, and the struggle between the man of specialised training and the man of general cultivation as one of the permanent contests of modern culture. On his account, educational institutions manufacture honour: they certify not only competence but membership, and the certified then monopolise the offices and professions for which the certificate is demanded, a closure whose economic yield is real but whose motive and form are matters of status. The credential inflation that later researchers documented, each generation requiring more schooling for the same position, reads naturally in this framework as an arms race of honour and monopoly rather than as a response to the technical needs of production.

The Marxian reading of the same institution begins from reproduction. Schools, on this account, sort and form the labour power a capitalist economy requires: they distribute the cognitive skills the division of labour consumes, and, as the correspondence principle argued, they rehearse in their authority structures, their grading, and their discipline the relations of the workplace each stratum's children are destined to enter, so that education legitimises the class order by appearing to reward merit while reliably delivering advantage to the already advantaged. The empirical core of the claim survived its strongest formulations: attainment tracks class origin with a stubbornness that reforms dent and do not remove, and the mechanisms, resources, expectations, cultural familiarity with the institution's codes, are exactly the sort of accumulated chances the Weberian calls life chances operating a generation early. Education is therefore the comparison's most instructive tie. The Weberian mechanism, closure by credentialled status groups, and the Marxian mechanism, reproduction of the relations of production through the formation of labour power, describe one process from its two ends, and the strongest modern work on schooling is written, self consciously or not, in both vocabularies at once. A student who can show this, with the diploma as the shared object, demonstrates the comparative skill at its highest level: not ranking the frameworks but catching them in the act of explaining the same corridor of the same school.

## The postwar settlement: Parsons, Dahrendorf, and the textbook

The caricature this article opened with was not born in classrooms by accident. It descends from a postwar settlement in Anglo American sociology that organised the classical inheritance for teaching, and naming its architects explains why the distortion took the shape it did. Talcott Parsons, who translated Weber and built him into a general theory of action, presented stratification largely as an order of prestige and moral evaluation, a ranking by shared values, which thinned Weber's property grounded class concept into a scale of honour and made the contrast with Marx's economics look total. Ralf Dahrendorf, writing from the other side, announced that Marx's property based classes had been superseded by authority based classes in imperatively coordinated associations, a Weberian revision so complete that property dropped out of class altogether, and his influence taught a generation that the modern corporation had dissolved the classical problem. Between prestige from one direction and authority from the other, the agreement clause disappeared from view: both postwar readings made Weber the theorist of everything in stratification that was not property, when his text had made property its foundation.

Recovering the clause is therefore more than pedantry; it corrects the discipline's own transmission of its founders. The scholarly recovery, in the work of historians of theory and in the empirical programmes that rebuilt the schemas from the primary texts, restored the harder and more interesting comparison this article has conducted. Goldthorpe's employment relations and Wright's production locations are both, in part, protests against the postwar thinning: each returns to a classical mechanism, market chances grounded in property and contract, surplus and control grounded in production, and each finds, in the return, the predictive and explanatory power the thinned versions had spent. The lesson generalises beyond this topic and is worth stating to every reader who meets theory first in summaries. A tradition's textbook form is a settlement among its interpreters, reached for reasons of pedagogy and polemic that expire, while the texts remain. The comparison of Marx and Weber is the discipline's standing demonstration that going back to the texts is not antiquarian piety but a research technique, since the sentence that dissolves the caricature was waiting in the most anthologised essay in the field the whole time.

## The rival both rejected: class as a ladder

One agreement between Marx and Weber is so complete that summaries rarely mention it, because it does not fit the contrast format: both men rejected the idea that class is a ladder of income or prestige, a single scale on which every person occupies a rung. Gradational schemes, which divide populations into bands by earnings or score occupations by surveyed prestige, were available to both and are refused by both, for the same underlying reason. A ladder describes a distribution and explains nothing; it has positions but no relations, movement but no mechanism, and it cannot say why the rungs are spaced as they are or what holds the ladder up. Prestige scales in particular drew Weber's methodological scorn in practice, since honour in his scheme is a group phenomenon, claimed and defended collectively, not a fluid in which individuals float at measurable depths. Marx's refusal is the mirror image: income bands mix exploiters and exploited in every bracket, a foreman and a small master sharing a rung while standing in opposite relations to the surplus, so that the ladder conceals the very structure class analysis exists to expose. The functionalist alternative of the postwar years, which treated stratification as a necessary ranking of positions by their importance and their rewards as the price of filling them, was attacked from both traditions for exactly this gradational innocence, and the wider map that includes it is drawn in the functionalism comparison already linked from this article.

The refusal matters for how class statistics should be read by everyone who consumes them, which in an age of inequality reporting is everyone. When a report announces that the middle class is shrinking because the share of households inside an income band has fallen, it is using a ladder, and both classical frameworks protest in unison: nothing has been shown about relations, chances, honour, or power until the band's occupants are sorted by what they own, what they control, what they can sell, and what befalls them across a life. When a survey reports that most respondents call themselves middle class, it has measured an honour claim and an aspiration, data for the status order, not a census of positions. The two men's joint refusal of the ladder is the deepest methodological bequest of the comparison, deeper even than their dispute, because it defines what a class theory is: not a sorting of people by how much they have, but an account of the relations and mechanisms by which having and lacking are produced, distributed, and defended. Every section of this article has been an elaboration of that definition in its two rival forms.

## Reading an election: class voting and its decline

Political sociology gave the comparison its most public empirical theatre in the study of class voting, the tendency of classes to support distinct parties, and in the long argument over whether that tendency has died. The classical Marxian expectation is the strong one: as classes form and organise, their parties confront each other, and electoral politics becomes the surface on which the conflict of production is fought by other means. The classical Weberian expectation is permissive: class is one identity among several that parties may mobilise, alongside confession, region, language, and nation, and no trend guarantees its primacy. The postwar decades in Western democracies appeared, at first, to run a controlled experiment between the expectations. Class voting was substantial everywhere, measured by indices comparing the parties of manual workers and the middle class, and then, across most democracies, it declined, unevenly and with national exceptions, while voting by religion, region, and education rose in relative weight. The Weberian reading claims the pattern as a vindication of contingency: class never had the electoral field to itself, and as other cleavages were mobilised and as parties changed their appeals, the class signal faded without any corresponding disappearance of class itself. The Marxian reading answers that the dealignment tracks real changes in the class structure and its organisation, the shrinkage of the concentrated industrial workforce, the weakening of the unions that converted position into politics, the recomposition of labour into services, so that the electoral change is itself a class phenomenon, explained by the production relations whose political expression is in question.

The exchange is the collective action debate in electoral dress, and its resolution follows the pattern established there. Dealignment is real but relative, and its form vindicates the conditional synthesis: class predicts vote most weakly where the organisational and status channels that carry class into politics have been dismantled or captured by rivals, and most strongly where parties and unions still perform the conversion. The comparison also warns against a journalistic shortcut in both directions. A low class voting index does not show that class has ceased to structure politics, since the policy menu itself may have converged under pressures class analysis explains. A high index does not show that production relations are being voted on directly, since the parties carrying the class signal are organisations with staffs, histories, and honour of their own, exactly as Weber's party concept insists. Elections, like factories, are readable in both maps, and the skilled analyst states which map produced which feature of the reading.

## The comparison leaves home: class across borders

Both frameworks were built inside national societies, and both are strained, differently and instructively, by a world in which production and markets cross borders faster than politics does. The Marxian tradition globalised early and eagerly, since a theory of accumulation expects capital to burst national limits: the world market, imperialism, and the international division of labour are extensions of the founding relation, and the transfer mechanism now operates through supply chains in which the surplus produced by workers in one jurisdiction is appropriated in another, a relocation of the hidden abode of production across an ocean. The strain falls on class formation rather than on the relation itself: the parties to the transfer rarely share a state, a language, or an organisation, and the passage from position to action, hard enough inside one country, faces borders as its standing obstacle, which the tradition's internationalism was invented to overcome and has chronically failed to.

The Weberian framework globalises along its own seams, and one of its extensions has become a commonplace of inequality research: citizenship itself as a dealt chance. Where a person is born determines, more powerfully than any class position within a country, the lifetime income they can expect, an effect researchers have quantified as a citizenship premium, the global analogue of life chances dealt not by the market alone but by the status order of states, with passports functioning as honourable and heritable entitlements, policed by closure at borders exactly as status groups police their memberships. Weber's concepts travel here with eerie completeness: the state as a status group writ large, monopolising the opportunities of a territory, defending its privileges by exclusion, and distributing chances by birth. The Marxian counterreading insists that the citizenship premium is itself underwritten by the global organisation of production, by which states' wealth and poverty were made and are remade, and that treating birth as a lottery of chances describes the deal while declining to ask who built the casino. The global case therefore reproduces the domestic verdict at planetary scale, with the added lesson that the two frameworks now share a deficiency neither classical author could have repaired alone: neither built its categories for a stratification order whose units are at once classes, nations, and states, and the research programmes extending them are, in effect, negotiating the next treaty between the two inheritances on new ground.

## Weber's taxonomy in detail

Because summaries flatten Weber's classes into a single list, his actual taxonomy deserves a section of its own, and it contains a surprise for readers formed by the caricature. He distinguishes three overlapping classifications, each answering a different analytical purpose. Property classes are sorted by the kind and yield of property: at the privileged pole, those who live from rents of land, mines, plant, securities, and human beings in slave economies; below them, gradations of smaller rentiers and proprietors; at the unprivileged pole, the propertyless, together with debtors, and, at the historical extremes, the outcast and the enslaved, whom he includes because his concept is built for comparison across economic orders rather than for capitalism alone. Acquisition classes are sorted by the market chances of the services a person can offer: entrepreneurs in commerce, industry, and agriculture, and the liberal professions at the privileged pole; workers differentiated by the scarcity and monopolibility of their skills at the other, with the middle strata of public and private officials, artisans, and peasants ranged between according to the security of their market position. Social classes, the third classification, are not further boxes but totalities of class situations between which mobility is easy and typical, and Weber names four for the modern order: the working class as a whole, increasingly dependent on mechanised production; the petty bourgeoisie; the propertyless intelligentsia and specialists, technicians, officials, and employees whose capital is their training; and the classes privileged through property and education, whose two assets so often coincide.

Two lessons fall out of the taxonomy for the comparison, and both cut against lazy versions of it. The first is that Weber's scheme contains, plainly labelled, the propertyless intelligentsia: a class whose entire market power rests on certified education, foreseen inside the founding text rather than discovered by later sociology. The second is that his fourth social class yokes property and education together as the twin sources of privilege, which is the agreement clause operating inside his own architecture: the privileged class is privileged, in the first instance, because it owns, and its education is the second asset of a class already founded on the first. Set beside Marx's mature treatment, in which the salariat appears as a problem case requiring theoretical repair, Weber's taxonomy shows the pluralist scheme at its genuine best, classifying the credentialed strata without strain. Set beside the objection and reply sections, it also shows the limit the Marxian side presses: the taxonomy sorts its intelligentsia by their chances with perfect adequacy and says nothing about the relation in which their labour stands to the surplus, the question that decides, for the rival framework, what the sorting is ultimately for. Both judgements are true, and holding them together, rather than trading them, is the competence this whole comparison has been building toward.

## Consumption, display, and the market for honour

One more test remains, smaller in scale and surprisingly diagnostic: consumption. What people buy, display, and refuse is where class and status meet in daily life, and the frameworks read the high street differently. The Weberian reading treats consumption as the visible grammar of status. Styles of life are status claims made material: the choice of neighbourhood, school, accent, table manners, leisure, and dress announces membership and polices boundaries, and the announcement is often deliberately uneconomic, since honour is proved precisely by expenditure that utility cannot justify. Thorstein Veblen, whose account of conspicuous consumption was developed independently in the same decades, supplied the tradition with its classic case, consumption undertaken to certify rank before an audience of rivals and inferiors. Later sociology systematised the insight: taste functions as a class marker and a weapon, cultural preferences sorting their holders as reliably as incomes, and the competition for distinction driving an endless refinement of the markers as each is imitated from below and abandoned from above. On this reading, the consumer economy is a status order with prices attached, and its inequalities of display are autonomous facts about honour, not translucent signs of something else.

The Marxian reading does not deny the displays; it asks what funds them and what they accomplish for the order that funds them. Display, on this account, is the form in which surplus returns to visibility as the lifestyle of the classes that appropriate it, and the machinery of desire, advertising, fashion cycles, and credit, is an industry for realising value and reproducing the relations that produce it, training consumers as reliably as production trains workers. Status competition among the propertyless, the reading adds, is the order's cheapest defence, dividing by taste and aspiration the class that a common relation might unite. Each reading catches the other in a characteristic blindness. The status account can describe the grammar of display for a century without asking why the speakers' budgets differ by orders of magnitude, or why the competition's prizes are priced in a currency produced somewhere by someone's labour. The production account can explain the budgets while treating the grammar as decoration, and then cannot say why display takes the precise, honour laden forms it does, forms whose logic, emulation, exclusion, and contempt for the merely useful, is Weber's home territory. Consumption thus returns the verdict this article has reached in every arena, in miniature and in public view: deal and transfer, honour and surplus, each mechanism visible in the same shop window, to the observer who has learned to look twice.

## Applying the allocation: three short cases

The allocation earns its keep only if it changes what an analyst does with a concrete problem, so consider three standing questions of stratification research and watch the choice of framework alter the inquiry. Take first the growth of very large fortunes at the top of modern economies. A Weberian inquiry describes the phenomenon as a transformation of chances: the returns to certain property forms and superstar market positions have stretched the distribution of life chances at the summit, and it will measure the stretch precisely, across income, wealth, and the intergenerational transmission of both. A Marxian inquiry asks the prior question: from which production relations do the returns flow, through which claims on surplus, financial, proprietary, and organisational, are they appropriated, and what has changed in the balance between the classes that the fortunes register? The two studies can cite each other's findings without remainder, yet they are different studies, and a literature containing only the first would know the size of the summit's advantage without knowing its source, while a literature containing only the second would know the source and misjudge the size.

Take, second, the declining security of routine work under contracting, franchising, and platform organisation. The Weberian instrument registers the phenomenon natively, as a deterioration of class situations: identical tasks now carry worse chances, thinner security, and weaker trajectories, and the schema can grade the deterioration occupation by occupation. The Marxian instrument registers the same facts as a reorganisation of the relation itself, capital externalising its risks and its employment obligations while retaining command through contract, algorithm, and brand, so that workers formally outside the firm remain inside the transfer. Here the allocation cuts in favour of using both in sequence: the chances account for measurement, the relational account for the mechanism that engineered the measured decline.

Take, third, the prestige of the learned professions and their resistance to every reform of entry, pay, and jurisdiction. The Weberian account is immediately at home: a status group defending honour and closure, its examinations, ethics codes, and collegial disciplines functioning as the walls of a monopoly whose yield is partly money and partly rank. The Marxian account must work through mediation, situating the professions within the reproduction of the order, as trainers of its labour power, certifiers of its knowledge, and managers of its populations, which explains their strategic importance without quite explaining their internal honour economy, the thing the actors themselves would say they are defending. In this third case the allocation awards the primary mechanism to Weber and the context to Marx, as in the first case it awarded measurement to Weber and origin to Marx, and in the second, mechanism to Marx and measurement to Weber. The pattern across the cases is the article's conclusion in operational form: neither framework is the other's understudy, the allocation varies by question in principled ways, and the analyst's first professional act is to know which question they are asking.

## For students and exam candidates

This comparison is as close to a guaranteed question as the sociology syllabus offers, and it is marked against a known failure pattern: the answer that pairs two classes with three dimensions and calls the second list longer and therefore better. Examiners reward three moves instead. Open with the agreement clause, Weber's own statement that property and lack of property are the basic categories of all class situations, because it proves you have read Weber rather than a summary of him and it reframes everything after it. Then state the real ground of difference, production against market, and develop it through life chances on one side and exploitation on the other, since those are the mechanisms each location generates. Close with the allocation rather than a coronation: which framework answers which question better, with the concession on standard outcome variables paid honestly and the upstream claim about the origin of market positions stated as the Marxian counterweight. Answers structured that way can absorb any wording of the question, whether it asks for differences, similarities, an evaluation, or a judgement about combination, because the structure is the argument and the argument survives rewording. The commonest mark loser is the supplement theory, Weber as Marx plus nuance; the second commonest is the winner theory, a verdict with no criterion; the third is quoting the triad without defining a single term. Define class situation, status, and party in Weber's senses, name Goldthorpe and Wright as the modern heirs, and the answer is already operating at the level the question was set to find.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The agreement clause is the single most efficient correction available for this topic: one sentence of Weber, read aloud and placed beside the standard slide, dissolves the misconception around which most weak answers are organised. Build the seminar around the seven point grid, assigning each row to a pair of students required to defend first one column and then the other, since the rows differ in kind and the exercise exposes which contrasts are empirical, which are methodological, and which are philosophical. The worked factory repays classroom use: give the same staffing list to two groups and let the Marxian and Weberian maps diverge before the class assembles the lesson itself, that instruments reveal by concealing. Set the objection and the reply as a formal debate, with the concession on outcome variables required from the Marxian side and the upstream argument required from the Weberian side in rebuttal, because students who have had to state both have effectively learned the verdict section. Close by marking the boundary the evidence cannot cross, the question of a terminus to class society, and students leave with the rarest examination asset: a comparison they can operate rather than recite.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

For researchers, the live form of this comparison is the Wright and Goldthorpe contest, and its literature should be cited for what it actually found: comparable performance on standard outcomes, divergence on the questions each schema was built to ask, and a shared superiority over gradational measures that vindicates theoretically constructed classes. Treat the choice of schema as a declaration of mechanism, and expect referees from the other tradition to audit the blind spot your choice creates, exploitation and authority on one side, chances and honour on the other. For historians, the frameworks divide the labour of explanation in a way the archives reward: the relational account for the formation and recomposition of classes under changing production, the Weberian for the honour orders, confessional blocs, and apparatuses through which historical actors actually moved. For journalists, the discipline is negative and simple: never present Weber as the man who replaced property with prestige, never report a class statistic without asking which schema produced it, and never let a mobility figure carry a claim about the transformation of structure. The categories in the headlines are somebody's theory; naming whose is the beginning of accuracy.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the difference between Marx and Weber on class?

Marx grounds class in the relations of production: a class is a position defined by ownership or non ownership of the means of production, carrying an interest generated by exploitation and an expectation of collective action. Weber grounds class in market situation, the chances that property and marketable services command in exchange. He treats collective action as contingent and adds status and party as independent dimensions. The frameworks share a foundation in property and diverge over where class is constituted, in production or in the market, and over what follows from it.

### Q: What did Weber mean by class situation?

A class situation is the typical chance for a supply of goods, external living conditions, and personal life experiences, insofar as that chance follows from the power to dispose of goods and skills for income within an economic order. In plainer terms, it is the set of probabilities a person's property and marketable services deal to them in the labour and commodity markets. It is an analyst's pattern, a shared component of life chances, not a community, an organisation, or a group with a will of its own.

### Q: What are status and party in Weber's scheme?

Status is stratification by social honour: prestige claimed by a group and conceded by others, expressed in a distinctive style of life, restricted social intercourse, endogamous marriage, and monopolised privileges. Party is stratification by organised power: any association, from an electoral machine to a revolutionary organisation or a faction with a staff, whose orientation is toward acquiring power and influencing communal action. Both are independent of class in Weber's scheme. Status can obstruct the market, and organisation generates power that wealth alone does not explain.

### Q: Did Weber agree with Marx about property?

On the foundational point, yes, and in his own words. Weber states that property and lack of property are the basic categories of all class situations, which makes ownership the primary divide inside his scheme as well as Marx's. The agreement is this article's agreement clause, and it destroys the common claim that Weber substituted prestige for property. His disagreement with Marx begins after the shared foundation: he locates the divide's operation in the market rather than in production, and he refuses the expectations about collective action and historical direction that Marx builds on it.

### Q: Is Weber's theory a supplement to Marx or a rival?

It is a rival, and treating it as a supplement is the standard error this article exists to correct. A supplement adds dimensions to a framework whose foundations it accepts; Weber rebuilds the foundations, relocating class from production to exchange, withholding the passage from position to action, and granting status and party logics independent of the economy rather than derivative from it. The schemes share the property divide, which makes them comparable, and differ in architecture, which makes them competitors. Supplements can be merged by addition; rivals must be compared by criterion, which is what the verdict section does.

### Q: Which of the two frameworks do sociologists use more today?

In empirical stratification research, the Weberian lineage holds the larger working share, because its occupational class schemas are the standard instruments of mobility and inequality research and code reliably from survey data. The Marxian lineage persists as a smaller but active programme, strongest where the questions concern exploitation, workplace authority, and wealth. Usage also divides by purpose rather than by allegiance alone: official statistics and mobility research run Weberian, critical political economy runs Marxian, and a great deal of published work quietly uses occupational classes for measurement while borrowing relational language for interpretation.

### Q: What are life chances?

Life chances are the probabilities attached to a class situation across a whole biography: the odds of finding and keeping work, of rising or stagnant income, of decent housing, of schooling that opens doors for one's children, of surviving illness and reaching a secure old age. Weber's point is that these odds are dealt by market position before individual effort or merit can operate, and that they accumulate, each outcome reshaping the next hand. The concept explains why people with equal incomes in a single year can occupy unequal class situations, differing in security, trajectory, and exposure to risk.

### Q: How do Wright and Goldthorpe differ?

Erik Olin Wright rebuilds Marx: classes are locations within production defined by ownership of capital, control of organisational resources, and possession of scarce skills, generating contradictory locations for managers and experts and testable claims about exploitation and authority. John Goldthorpe rebuilds Weber: classes are occupational groupings defined by employment relations, the service relationship of salary and security against the labour contract of wages and supervision, built to predict life chances and mobility. They differ as their ancestors did, in the ground of class, and their head to head tests on shared data perform comparably on standard outcomes.

### Q: Which framework better explains social mobility?

The Weberian framework explains mobility better, because mobility is a phenomenon of chances, and its categories were designed to state them. Its successor schemas produced the field's central findings: high absolute mobility driven by structural change, relative chances persistently unequal across generations. The Marxian framework contributes the indispensable supplement: circulation of individuals between positions is not transformation of the structure of positions, and a society can reshuffle its people while its class relations stand untouched. Use Weber to measure the movement; use Marx to ask what the movement leaves unchanged.

### Q: Can you combine Marx and Weber on class?

Yes, at the level of mechanisms, with stated terms, and no, at the level of labels, by simple addition. Successful combinations specify which mechanism explains which phenomenon: closure theory joining honour and exclusion to material strategies, Wright's later work housing skill monopoly inside an exploitation framework, organisational oligarchy admitted into accounts of class parties. Failed combinations stack the vocabularies, class plus status plus party plus exploitation, suppressing the rivalry that makes each term informative. A workable treaty begins from the agreement clause, follows each framework to its mechanism, and combines only where the mechanisms do not contradict.

### Q: How does status explain what a purely economic reading of class misses?

Status explains why advantage and deference fail to track income: the impoverished family of old standing outranking new wealth, the credentialed profession closing its gates by code and examination, the caste or confessional bloc commanding loyalty across every class line, consumption undertaken for distinction rather than use. A purely economic reading must treat these as noise or as disguised wealth effects, and it chronically underpredicts the politics they produce, voting and allegiance that follow honour rather than interest. Weber's status concept names the mechanism, honour organised, defended, and converted into monopolised opportunity.

### Q: What should a student check first when an exam mixes class, status and party?

Check which thinker the question belongs to and whether the terms are being used in his senses. Class, status, and party as a triad is Weber's architecture, and importing Marx into it without comment collapses the comparison the question is testing. Then check the foundations: state the agreement clause, locate class in production for Marx and in the market for Weber, and define status as honour and party as organised power before evaluating anything. Most lost marks on this question are lost in the first three sentences, where the supplement theory and the undefined triad do their damage.

### Q: Why does Weber's account of bureaucracy limit both frameworks?

Because bureaucracy is a structure of domination that neither framework's class concept was built to contain. It limits Marx by identifying an apparatus whose power rests on expertise, files, and office rather than ownership, and which survives changes of property regime, so that transforming ownership does not by itself transform rule. It limits Weberian class analysis because the office deals life chances outside the market, through hierarchy and credential, forcing his own scheme to recruit the status concept to cover officials. Both traditions since have written theories of bureaucracy on ground Weber surveyed first.

### Q: How does the agreement clause change the usual story of Marx against Weber?

The usual story says Weber replaced Marx's economic class with prestige, adding status and party because property was too crude a basis for stratification. The agreement clause, Weber's statement that property and lack of property are the basic categories of all class situations, shows that story to be false at its first step. The real comparison starts from a shared foundation and diverges over location, production against market, over the sequel, expected action against contingency, and over architecture, derivation against independence. Answers and arguments built on the clause are built on Weber's text rather than on his caricature.

### Q: What should a journalist avoid when summarising Weber for a general audience?

Avoid the substitution story, in which Weber is said to have replaced property with prestige; his text grounds class in property explicitly. Avoid presenting class, status, and party as three kinds of people rather than three orders of stratification that every person inhabits at once. Avoid declaring that Weber disproved Marx, when the two built instruments for different questions and their modern successors perform comparably on shared tests. And avoid quoting a class statistic without identifying the schema behind it, since occupational, relational, and income based measures of class answer to different theories.
