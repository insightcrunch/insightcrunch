Learning how to write about Gatsby's characters in an essay changes the moment you accept one claim: a character is a construction to interpret, not a person to describe. Jay Gatsby, Daisy Buchanan, Nick Carraway, Tom Buchanan, Jordan Baker, Myrtle Wilson, George Wilson, and Meyer Wolfsheim do not exist. They are effects produced by sentences, scenes, images, dialogue, and narrative decisions, and an essay earns its marks by arguing what those decisions build, not by narrating what the figure does. The weak character essay reads like a biography of someone who never lived. The strong one reads like a case for a reading, grounded in how the text constructs the figure, and it wins because every paragraph advances an interpretation rather than retelling a plot.

Most students arrive at the character essay with the wrong reflex. They summarize. They write that Gatsby throws parties to attract Daisy, that Daisy chooses Tom, that Myrtle dies in the road, as though the examiner had not read the book. They treat Nick as a friend whose reliability they are judging, Daisy as a woman whose morals they are grading, and Tom as a bully they are condemning. This is narrating, and it is the complication this article exists to defeat. Narrating describes; arguing interprets. The examiner rewards the student who can say not only what a figure does but what the figure is made of on the page, and why that making matters to the novel's meaning.

This article owns one skill and one skill only: writing about characters in essays. It does not rehearse who the figures are, what their arcs contain, or how the cast maps together. That content belongs to the series' [complete map of the novel's characters](/2022/02/07/great-gatsby-characters-complete-map/), and this article routes you there whenever character content is needed, keeping the two territories clean. Here you learn the method: how to argue an interpretation of a character, how to read a figure as constructed by technique, and how to let textual evidence carry your claim while plot stays in the background where it belongs.

It helps to see the distinction through an examiner's eyes. Imagine two scripts answering "Discuss the presentation of Jay Gatsby." The first opens: "Jay Gatsby, born James Gatz in North Dakota, is a mysterious millionaire who throws lavish parties." The examiner sighs, because the script has announced a biography and the next forty minutes will be plot. The second opens: "The novel presents Gatsby as an identity assembled from rumor, spectacle, and catchphrase, a performance the text stages before it ever shows the man." The examiner sits up, because the script has announced an argument and the next forty minutes will be reading. Both students read the book; only the second is doing the task. The difference was decided in the first two sentences, before a single quotation appeared. That is why this article begins with the claim and returns to it in every section: the opening posture determines everything downstream, and the posture is a choice you make before you write a word.

## The Claim: A Character Is a Construction, Not a Person

The namable claim deserves a full unpacking because everything else in this article is its application. A character is a construction to interpret, not a person to describe. Fitzgerald did not meet Jay Gatsby and take notes. He assembled him out of a schedule written on a flyleaf in Hopalong Cassidy, a pink suit, the phrase "old sport," rumors at parties, a green light, shirts thrown into the air, and Nick Carraway's retrospective sentences. Each of those is a technique: a prop, a repeated phrase, a narrating filter, a symbolic object, a set piece. The essay that understands this writes about the assembly. The essay that forgets it writes about a man.

Consider what changes when you adopt the claim. The question "Why does Gatsby throw parties?" invites biography: he hopes Daisy will wander in. The question "How does the novel construct Gatsby through the parties?" invites interpretation: the parties are staged as theater, Nick's narration emphasizes their theatricality, Owl Eyes calls Gatsby "a regular Belasco," and the construction argues that Gatsby performs wealth the way he performs identity. Same novel, same figure, but the second question cannot be answered by retelling. It demands that you argue what the techniques build. That demand is the whole difference between a mid-band essay and a top-band one.

The claim also reorganizes how you think about evidence. If a character were a person, evidence would be biography: what they did, in order. Because a character is a construction, evidence is technique: the diction of a description, the placement of a scene, the repetition of an image, the choice of who narrates a moment, the dialogue a figure is given and the dialogue withheld. When Nick says of Gatsby, "If personality is an unbroken series of successful gestures, then there was something gorgeous about him," that sentence is not a report on a real man's charm. It is Nick constructing Gatsby in prose, and your essay can argue about the construction: why "gestures," why "gorgeous," why Nick needs the performance to be beautiful. The evidence is the sentence, not the man.

One more consequence of the claim is worth stating plainly: it changes what counts as knowing the book. The student who can recite the plot knows the novel the way a tour guide knows a building's floor plan; the student who can argue construction knows it the way the architect does. Examinations are designed to tell these two kinds of knowledge apart, and they do it by asking questions that plot knowledge cannot answer. "Discuss the presentation of Daisy" cannot be answered by knowing what Daisy does; it can only be answered by arguing how she is built. Every hour spent practicing construction-reading is therefore an hour spent on the exact knowledge the examination measures, while every hour spent re-memorizing plot is an hour spent on knowledge the examination takes for granted.

There is a common fear that this way of writing kills the pleasure of the book, reducing living figures to cold machinery. The opposite is true. Treating figures as constructions makes the reading more precise, not less alive. You can still be moved by Gatsby reaching toward the green light; the interpretation simply asks what Fitzgerald built to move you. "He had come a long way to this blue lawn, and his dream must have seemed so close that he could hardly fail to grasp it." That sentence is doing work: the distance compressed into "a long way," the color work of "blue lawn," the cruel nearness of "so close." An essay that argues how that sentence constructs Gatsby's longing is not being mechanical. It is paying the sentence the compliment of attention.

The rest of this article turns the claim into procedure. You will learn how to convert plot facts into interpretive claims, what counts as technique-level evidence, how to read entrances as construction, how to write paragraphs that argue rather than narrate, why naming a figure's effect beats listing traits, how to compare two figures inside one argument, what a fully worked interpretation looks like, and how to keep the whole essay from sounding like an autopsy. Each section gives you moves you can rehearse, with worked examples drawn from the novel's most written-about figures.

A final word on the fallacy before the procedure begins, because it is stubborn. The "person to describe" reflex survives even in students who know better, and it shows up in small tells: the essay that writes "Daisy feels," "Tom wants," "Nick believes," as though feelings and wants were facts to report rather than effects to argue. The test for the fallacy is simple: whenever your sentence attributes an inner state to a figure, ask what textual choice built that state. "Daisy feels restless" becomes "the novel constructs Daisy's restlessness through the pairing of visual stillness with verbal agitation in her entrance scene." The inner state is not denied; it is relocated from the person to the page, where the essay can actually work with it. Run that relocation on every psychology-sentence in your draft, and the fallacy will have nowhere left to hide.

## Turning Plot Into Argument

### How do you turn a plot fact about a character into an interpretive claim?

A plot fact becomes an interpretive claim when you attach it to a technique and a meaning: take what the figure does, name how the text presents it, and argue what that presentation builds. "Gatsby stares at the green light" is a fact; "the scene constructs his longing as visual and unreachable" is a claim an essay can defend.

The conversion has a reliable shape, and once you see it you can apply it to any figure. Start with the raw fact, the thing that happens on the page. Then ask the construction question: how is this moment presented, and by what means? Then answer with an arguable verb: the scene constructs, stages, frames, undercuts, ironizes, elevates. The formula is not a cage; it is a scaffold you can discard once the habit is internal. Fact plus technique plus arguable meaning equals a claim. Without the technique, you have biography. Without the arguable meaning, you have description.

Take Daisy and the famous shirts scene. The plot fact: Gatsby throws his shirts into the air and Daisy cries, "They're such beautiful shirts." The narrating essay stops there, perhaps adding that Daisy is materialistic. The interpreting essay asks how the scene is built. Nick narrates it with sensory excess: shirts in "many-colored disarray," "coral and apple-green and lavender and faint orange." The piling of color does the arguing for you: the scene constructs Daisy's response as aesthetic overwhelm, and her tears land on objects, not on Gatsby. The claim writes itself: the shirts scene stages Daisy's desire as something that attaches to surfaces, which is why her grief in the scene is for the shirts and not for the man throwing them. Notice that the claim is contestable. A reader could argue the tears are for lost time, for the years the shirts represent. Contestable is good. An essay argues; only a summary agrees with itself.

Now take Tom, whose plot facts seem to invite moral condemnation: he breaks Myrtle's nose, he is a brute. The narrating essay delivers the verdict that Tom is cruel and stops, having told the examiner something the examiner already knows. The interpreting essay asks how the cruelty is constructed. Fitzgerald gives Tom a body before he gives him a psychology: "a sturdy straw-haired man of thirty with a rather hard mouth and a supercilious manner," a body that Nick says seems to lean "aggressively forward," with "a cruel body." The physicality is the technique. The claim: the novel builds Tom's power as bodily before it is moral, so that his cruelty reads as an extension of his physique, which makes the class critique land in the flesh rather than in abstraction. That is an interpretation of construction, and it uses the same plot facts the biography used, but it argues them.

The habit to build is a question you ask of every plot fact before it enters your essay: what does this fact look like as a decision Fitzgerald made? Gatsby's schedule in Hopalong Cassidy is a plot fact; as a decision, it is Fitzgerald choosing to show Gatsby's self-invention as a boy's handwritten program, which constructs the American self-made myth as both earnest and childish. Daisy's "I hope she'll be a fool, that's the best thing a girl can be in this world, a beautiful little fool" is a plot fact; as a decision, it is Fitzgerald giving Daisy one of the novel's most quoted lines at the moment of her daughter's birth, which constructs her cynicism as something she articulates clearly and then ignores completely. Every fact survives the conversion. The essay just stops treating facts as the point and starts treating them as the raw material of the point.

A practical toolkit makes the conversion fast: keep a stock of arguable verbs and reach for them whenever a sentence threatens to narrate. The novel constructs, stages, frames, withholds, ironizes, elevates, undercuts, and performs. Each verb forces a technique into the sentence. "Gatsby looks at the green light" narrates; "the novel stages Gatsby's looking as a silhouette against distance" argues, because "stages" demands you say how the looking is presented. "Daisy cries" narrates; "the scene frames Daisy's tears as falling on objects" argues, because "frames" demands you say what the crying is attached to. Practice by taking any three plot facts from your notes and rewriting each with a different verb from the stock. The first attempts will feel forced; by the tenth, the verbs will be choosing your evidence for you, because a verb like "withholds" sends you hunting for what the text refuses to show, and a verb like "ironizes" sends you hunting for the gap between claim and performance. The verb is a search instruction disguised as vocabulary.

### What counts as technique-level evidence about a character?

Technique-level evidence is any textual choice that builds the figure: diction, a repeated image, scene placement, the narrating voice, a figure's dialogue, and what the narration withholds. If you can point to the choice and argue its effect, it is evidence; if you can only point to what happened, it is plot.

Students often believe evidence means quotations, and then they drop quotations into paragraphs like ornaments. A quotation is only evidence when the essay does something with it. "Her voice is full of money" is not evidence until you argue the diction: "full" makes wealth a substance inside the voice, "money" is blunt where the surrounding prose is lyrical, and Nick's suddenness, "he said suddenly," marks the line as a discovery rather than a description. The technique is the bluntness breaking through the lyricism. The interpretation: the novel constructs Daisy's allure as inseparable from wealth at the level of the sentence, which is why no paraphrase of her charm can survive without the money in it. That is what technique-level evidence looks like: a small choice, closely read, carrying a large claim.

Dialogue is a rich source that students underuse. Gatsby's "old sport" is not a quirk to mention in passing; it is a repeated verbal construction, and the essay can argue that the repetition builds Gatsby's identity as performance, an English affectation laid over a North Dakota boy. Tom's "I suppose the latest thing is to sit back and let Mr. Nobody from Nowhere make love to your wife" is not just rudeness; it is class panic given syntax, the sneering diminutive "Mr. Nobody from Nowhere" doing the work of an entire social order. Myrtle's vitality in the apartment party, her voice "surcharged with vitality," is not just energy; it is Fitzgerald constructing her aliveness so that her death lands harder, a technique of contrast. Each of these is a choice on the page, and each supports an arguable reading.

Narration itself is technique-level evidence, and Nick is the richest vein. When Nick writes, "I was within and without, simultaneously enchanted and repelled by the inexhaustible variety of life," he is constructing his own position as a figure: the participant-observer, the insider who stands apart. An essay about Nick can argue that this single sentence builds the novel's entire narrative posture, and that Nick's judgments of every other figure must be read through the distance he claims here. Similarly, Nick's famous opening about reserving judgments is not character background; it is a narrating decision that constructs Nick as tolerant, which makes his later judgments, including his final judgment that Gatsby was worth "the whole damn bunch," land as earned rather than prejudiced. The evidence is the framing, and the interpretation follows the framing.

Placement and structure are technique too, though students reach for them last. That we meet Gatsby first as rumor, then as a silhouette, then as a host who is barely present at his own party, is a structural decision, and the essay can argue that the delayed entrance constructs Gatsby as a figure assembled by other people's talk before he ever speaks. That Daisy's first appearance pairs her beauty with restlessness, "Do you always watch for the longest day of the year and then miss it?" is a placement decision: Fitzgerald introduces her voice before her substance. That George Wilson appears as a washed-out, spiritless man and dies as one is a structural flatness, and the essay can argue the flatness is the point: Wilson is constructed as the figure the careless world does not bother to round. Technique-level evidence is everywhere once you stop looking for what happened and start looking for how it was made to happen on the page.

The drill that builds this habit is the three-sentence analysis, and it is worth practicing until it is automatic. Sentence one names the technique: "Nick's description builds Tom's power through the body." Sentence two quotes or points briefly: "'a cruel body,' leaning 'aggressively forward.'" Sentence three argues the effect: "the physicality precedes any psychology, so the cruelty reads as an extension of the flesh, which makes the class critique land bodily rather than abstractly." Three sentences, one piece of evidence fully argued. Most weak paragraphs fail because they stop after sentence two, the quotation hanging there like a picture on a wall with no label. The analysis sentence is the label, and it is where the marks live. Practice the drill on ten passages from your evidence bank: technique named, evidence given, effect argued. When you can do it in three sentences without strain, your paragraphs will do it naturally, and the examiner will feel the difference on every page: this student does not decorate with quotations, this student argues with them.

## Reading Construction in Practice

### How do you read a character's first entrance as construction?

You read a first entrance as construction by treating it as a staged introduction: note what the narration shows first, what it delays, whose eyes mediate the meeting, and what the figure is doing when first seen. First entrances are deliberate, and the essay argues the deliberation.

Gatsby's entrance is the masterclass, and it repays slow reading. We hear about him for chapters: the rumors, the telephone calls, the car, the parties. When Nick finally meets him, the scene is built as a failure of recognition: Nick apologizes to a stranger for not having been invited, and the stranger turns out to be the host. Fitzgerald could have introduced Gatsby with grandeur; instead he introduces him with mistaken identity, and the technique matters. The construction argues that Gatsby is a figure other people assemble before he assembles himself, and that his identity is from the start a matter of misrecognition. An essay that argues this is not retelling the meeting; it is reading the meeting as a decision.

Daisy's entrance works by a different deliberate choice. Nick finds her and Jordan laid out on a couch "like silver idols," buoyed up by the wind of the open windows, and Daisy's first extended speech is restless and bored: she has been everywhere and seen everything and done everything. The technique is the pairing of visual stillness with verbal restlessness: the idols do not move, but the voice cannot settle. The interpretation: Daisy is constructed from her first scene as beauty that bores itself, which sets up everything that follows, including her inability to choose anything but the safety of Tom. Notice how this reading uses the entrance only; it does not need the rest of the plot to make its case, and that economy is what makes it essay-ready.

Tom's entrance is bodily, as noted, and Myrtle's is vitality, and George Wilson's is depletion: "a blonde, spiritless man, anaemic, and faintly handsome," standing in the valley of ashes like part of the scenery. The pattern across these entrances is the teaching point: Fitzgerald introduces figures through the channel that will define their construction. Gatsby through rumor and misrecognition, Daisy through voice against stillness, Tom through the body, Myrtle through energy, Wilson through its absence, Jordan through poise and dishonesty. An essay that lines up two or three entrances and argues the pattern has a thesis about characterization itself, not just about one figure, and it never once needs to narrate the plot.

The practical move for the student is to isolate the entrance passage, list what is shown and what is withheld, and ask why this figure enters this way. Who speaks first? What does the narrator notice before anything else? What is the figure doing: performing, waiting, watching? The answers are the evidence, and the interpretation is the argument about why Fitzgerald staged it so. Entrances are the novel's own character essays, compressed; your essay expands them.

Re-entrances repay the same reading, and they give the essay a way to argue change or its refusal. Gatsby re-enters in the Plaza scene stripped of the apparatus: no parties, no rumors, no "old sport" landing softly, just a man saying "Can't repeat the past?" with incredulity while Tom dismantles him in front of Daisy. The essay can argue that the re-entrance constructs the collapse of the performance: the techniques that built the mystery are withdrawn, and what remains is the boy from North Dakota with nowhere to stand. Daisy re-enters after Myrtle's death in retreat, and the text constructs the re-entrance as withdrawal into money, the couple conspiring in the house while Gatsby waits by the pool. The interpretation: the novel builds Daisy's exits as returns to the only construction she ever had, wealth as shelter. Tom never re-enters differently at all, which the essay can argue is itself the construction: the figure built as immovable body cannot be staged in transformation, so the novel does not try. Track a figure's entrances across the book, first, middle, and last, and argue what the staging does differently each time; you will have a paragraph sequence that demonstrates command of the whole novel while never narrating it.

### How do you write a paragraph that argues instead of narrates?

You write an arguing paragraph by opening with a claim about construction, supporting it with technique-level evidence, and closing with the meaning of that evidence for your interpretation. The paragraph's first sentence must be something a reader could disagree with; if it is a plot fact, the paragraph will narrate no matter what follows.

Here is the weak version, the kind that fills examination scripts. "Gatsby is mysterious at the start of the novel. Nobody knows where he comes from. There are rumors about him at his parties. Nick does not meet him until chapter three. Then they become friends." Every sentence is true and every sentence narrates. The paragraph tells the story of the novel's first three chapters in miniature. An examiner reading it learns that the student read the book, which was never in doubt, and learns nothing about how the student reads.

Here is the same material argued. "The novel constructs Gatsby's mystery as a social product rather than a personal trait: the rumors at the parties, the telephone calls Nick overhears, and the delayed entrance all make Gatsby something other people talk about before he exists as a speaker. Fitzgerald withholds the figure and supplies the talk, so that when Gatsby finally says 'old sport,' the phrase lands as a performance learned for an audience. The mystery, the essay argues, is not in Gatsby but in the apparatus around him." The first sentence is contestable. The evidence is technique: rumors, calls, delayed entrance, a catchphrase. The closing sentence states the interpretation's payoff. Nothing is narrated that the argument does not use.

The structure underneath is the familiar point, evidence, analysis pattern, but the point must be an interpretive claim about construction and the evidence must be textual choices rather than events. A useful test: cover the evidence sentences and read the point alone. If it still sounds like an argument, the paragraph is healthy. If it collapses into "this happened, then this happened," rewrite the point. Another test: ask of each evidence sentence, "what choice does this describe?" If the answer is "the choice to have the event occur," you are still narrating. If the answer is "the choice to present it this way," you are arguing.

A revision checklist makes the test systematic, and it is worth running on every draft. First, read each paragraph's opening sentence and ask whether a reasonable reader could disagree with it; if not, rewrite it as a construction claim. Second, count the plot facts per paragraph and the technique observations per paragraph; if the facts outnumber the observations, convert or cut. Third, check that every quotation is followed by at least two sentences of analysis naming a technique; a quotation with one sentence of gloss is decoration. Fourth, read the paragraph's closing sentence and ask what the reader takes away; if the takeaway is an event, rewrite the closing as the interpretation's payoff. Fifth, read the transitions between paragraphs and ask whether they carry the argument forward or the story; "the essay now turns to" should be followed by a construction site, not a plot point. Run the five checks, fix what they catch, and the draft will argue. The checklist is mechanical, but its product is not: it forces every paragraph to earn its place as interpretation.

Transitions between paragraphs should carry the argument forward, not the story. "Having established how the parties construct Gatsby as performance, the essay now turns to the scenes where the performance drops" moves the interpretation. "After the parties, Gatsby meets Daisy again" moves the plot. The reader should always know which paragraph's claim is being advanced and how it connects to the thesis. The thesis is the essay's interpretation in one sentence; every paragraph is that interpretation applied to one more piece of construction. When the paragraphs chain this way, the essay cannot drift into narration, because narration has nowhere to attach.

### Why does naming a character's effect beat listing their traits?

Naming a character's effect beats listing traits because traits are static labels and effects are arguable readings of what the text does. "Daisy is beautiful, rich, and careless" lists; "Daisy's carelessness is constructed as a privilege that the novel lets go unpunished" argues.

The trait list is the most common weak move in character essays, and it fails for a precise reason: traits are conclusions without arguments. When a student writes that Jordan is dishonest, athletic, and aloof, the examiner can only nod or shrug; there is nothing to assess because nothing has been argued. The traits may even be accurate. Accuracy is not the problem. The problem is that a list of accurate traits is still a description, and description is what the essay is not for. The strong essay converts each trait into an effect: not "Jordan is dishonest" but "the novel constructs Jordan's dishonesty as the moral weather of her class, small and unpunished, which is why Nick's attraction to her reads as attraction to the very carelessness he claims to hate." The trait becomes a reading. The reading can be debated. Debate is the essay's business.

Effects also solve the evidence problem that trait lists create. A trait invites a student to prove it with plot: Jordan cheated at golf, therefore dishonest. An effect invites the student to prove it with technique: the golf cheating is reported by Nick in a flat aside, never dramatized, which constructs the dishonesty as background radiation rather than scandal, and that construction is the point. Same fact, different use. The trait version narrates the fact to prove the label. The effect version reads the presentation to argue the meaning. Examiners reward the second because it demonstrates the skill the question is testing: not knowledge of the book but reading of it.

There is a further advantage. Effects connect figures to the novel's larger meanings without leaving the character essay's territory. "Tom's cruelty is constructed as bodily" connects to class and power. "Gatsby's hope is constructed as visual, always at a distance" connects to the novel's treatment of the past and the future. "Nick's tolerance is constructed as a narrative posture that the novel gradually ironizes" connects to narration itself. Each effect is a bridge from the figure to the theme, and the bridge is built out of technique, so the essay stays a character essay while reaching the novel's ideas. The trait list cannot build these bridges because it never leaves the figure's surface.

Practice the conversion directly. Take any trait you are tempted to list and rewrite it as an effect with a technique attached. "Myrtle is lively" becomes "Myrtle's vitality is constructed through sensory excess at the apartment party, which makes her the novel's brief image of aliveness before the road kills her." "Wolfsheim is sinister" becomes "Wolfsheim's menace is constructed through small grotesque details, the cufflinks of human molars, which makes his underworld power felt in objects rather than threats." "George Wilson is weak" becomes "Wilson's depletion is constructed as structural flatness, a figure the narration never rounds, which makes him the casualty the careless world does not notice." Each conversion is a paragraph seed. Collect enough of them and the essay writes itself, because each is already an argument.

Watch a full trait list undergo the conversion, because students often arrive with a paragraph that is nothing but traits and need to see the whole thing rebuilt. Take this weak paragraph: "Gatsby is mysterious, wealthy, generous, and obsessive. He is mysterious because nobody knows his past. He is wealthy because he has a mansion and parties. He is generous because he lets anyone come. He is obsessive because he loves Daisy so much." Four traits, four plot proofs, zero interpretation. Now the conversion, trait by trait. "Mysterious" becomes "the novel constructs Gatsby's mystery as a social product, rumors and telephone calls standing in for a past the text withholds." "Wealthy" becomes "the wealth is constructed as spectacle, parties staged as theater, which makes the money a performance rather than a possession." "Generous" becomes "the open-door hospitality is constructed as bait, generosity as strategy, which is why the crowds vanish the moment the performance ends." "Obsessive" becomes "the devotion is constructed as visual and distant, the green light and the silhouette, which makes the love inseparable from the longing's staging." Four effects, four technique arguments, one paragraph that now argues. The raw material never changed; only the verb changed, from "is" to "is constructed as," and that verb change is the entire skill in miniature.

### How do you compare two characters inside one argument?

You compare two figures inside one argument by making the comparison serve a single interpretive claim, not by describing each in turn. The weak comparison is two mini-essays stapled together; the strong one uses the second figure as the evidence that proves the reading of the first.

The stapled version is easy to recognize. A paragraph on Gatsby's hope, a paragraph on Tom's cynicism, a sentence at the end saying they are different. The examiner has read two descriptions and one platitude. The integrated version starts from a claim that needs both figures: "The novel constructs new money as performance and old money as embodiment, and Gatsby and Tom are the two halves of that construction." Now every observation about Gatsby's parties proves the performance half, every observation about Tom's body proves the embodiment half, and the comparison is the argument rather than its decoration.

The technique for integration is to find the shared construction site: the scene, image, or device where the novel builds the two figures against each other. The Plaza Hotel confrontation is the richest such site, because Fitzgerald stages Gatsby and Tom in direct verbal collision, and the essay can argue how the scene constructs the contrast. Gatsby's "Can't repeat the past? Why of course you can!" is built as incredulity, a man arguing with time itself; Tom's "Mr. Nobody from Nowhere" is built as class dismissal, a man arguing with a social order he already owns. The claim: the scene constructs the two men as fighting different enemies, Gatsby fighting time and Tom fighting status, which is why Gatsby loses a fight Tom does not know he is in. The comparison is doing interpretive work that neither figure could do alone.

Daisy and Myrtle offer another shared site: the two women are constructed as contrasting images of desire, Daisy in white and distance, Myrtle in vitality and proximity, and the novel kills the vital one while sparing the careless one. An essay can argue that this contrast constructs the novel's judgment on class: desire is permitted to the rich as atmosphere and fatal to the poor as appetite. Jordan and Nick offer a quieter pairing: both are observers, but Jordan's observation is cynical participation while Nick's is claimed detachment, and the essay can argue that the pairing constructs two versions of the insider-outsider position. In each case the comparison has a thesis, the thesis needs both figures, and the evidence is technique: color, staging, dialogue, narrative framing.

The practical discipline is a ratio: for every sentence describing figure A, there should be a sentence relating it to figure B through the shared claim. If you find yourself writing three sentences about Gatsby with no mention of Tom, you have drifted back into stapling. Return to the claim and ask what this observation about Gatsby proves about the contrast. The claim is the leash. Keep the figures on it and the comparison argues; let them off it and you have written two essays where one was asked for.

A worked integrated comparison will make the discipline concrete. Take Daisy and Myrtle, and take as the shared claim this: the novel constructs the two women as contrasting images of desire, and the contrast does the novel's class judgment. The paragraph opens with the claim, then moves between the figures inside it: "Daisy is constructed in white and distance, the 'silver idols' entrance, the voice that floats; Myrtle is constructed in vitality and proximity, the apartment party's sensory excess, the body that presses forward. The techniques are opposites, atmosphere against appetite, and the novel's judgment follows the construction: the vital woman dies in the road as public spectacle while the careless woman retreats into money unpunished." Every sentence about Daisy is answered by a sentence about Myrtle, and both serve the claim about class. The comparison never staples because the claim never lets go. Practice this paragraph shape with any pairing, Gatsby and Tom at the Plaza, Nick and Jordan as observers, Wilson and Gatsby as dreamers, and the integrated comparison will become your default, which is exactly what examiners hope to see and rarely do.

### What does a worked interpretation of Daisy look like from claim to evidence?

A worked interpretation moves from a contestable thesis through technique-level evidence to a payoff. Here is the thesis: the novel constructs Daisy as a figure whose power is entirely vocal and atmospheric, and whose tragedy is not that she chooses wrongly but that she is built never to choose at all.

The evidence begins with the voice, because the novel does. "Her voice is full of money" is Nick's sudden discovery, and the essay reads the diction: "full" makes wealth a substance, "money" is blunt against the surrounding lyricism, and the suddenness marks it as revelation. The technique constructs Daisy's allure as inseparable from her class position; the voice does not accompany the wealth, it contains it. A second piece of evidence is the first entrance: Daisy laid out "like silver idols" with Jordan, visually still while verbally restless, "Do you always watch for the longest day of the year and then miss it?" The pairing of stillness and restlessness constructs a figure who cannot settle, and the essay argues that this restlessness is the whole of her agency: she moves, she does not decide.

The third piece is the shirts scene, read as construction rather than plot. The sensory excess of the shirts, the piling of color, Daisy's tears falling on objects: the scene stages her feeling as something that attaches to surfaces. The interpretation argues that Daisy is at her most emotional over things, which is why her return to Tom is not a betrayal of feeling but its fulfillment; Tom is the world of beautiful shirts made permanent. The fourth piece is the Plaza scene, where Daisy collapses under pressure: "Oh, you want too much!" The essay reads the line as the construction's confession: asked to choose, the figure built never to choose breaks rather than decides.

The payoff connects the interpretation to the novel's meaning. If Daisy is constructed as voice without decision, then the novel's famous judgment on the careless, the people who "smashed up things and creatures and then retreated back into their money," lands differently: Daisy's carelessness is not cruelty but the structural consequence of a figure built from atmosphere. The essay has argued an interpretation, used only technique-level evidence, and reached a reading of the novel's moral vision through one figure. That is the method in full. Every character essay you write can follow this shape: a contestable thesis about construction, evidence drawn from technique, and a payoff that shows why the construction matters.

The payoff deserves one more note, because students often stop one step short: they argue the construction beautifully and then close with a shrug, restating the thesis instead of extending it. The payoff should answer what the interpretation changes about the novel. If Daisy is voice without decision, then the famous passage about the careless, who "smashed up things and creatures and then retreated back into their money," must be read as Nick constructing a moral judgment on a figure the text built to be unjudgeable, which complicates Nick's own position as judge. That extension turns a good paragraph into a memorable one, because it shows the interpretation doing work beyond its own figure. Always ask, after the evidence is argued: what does this reading change about the book? The answer is your closing sentence, and it is the sentence the examiner will remember.

### How do you keep a construction-based essay from sounding mechanical?

You keep the essay human by remembering that construction is the means and meaning is the end: every technical observation must cash out in what the figure means. The danger is real; an essay can become a list of devices with a thesis stapled on. The defense is to make every paragraph answer "so what does this construction mean?"

The mechanical essay sounds like this: "Fitzgerald uses imagery to construct Gatsby. The green light is an image. The shirts are imagery. The parties use visual imagery." The devices are named, the figure is untouched, and the examiner learns that the student knows the word "imagery." The living essay sounds like this: "The novel builds Gatsby out of things seen at a distance: the light, the silhouette, the parties watched from the edge of the crowd. The construction argues that Gatsby's desire needs distance the way a mirage needs heat; bring it close and it dies, which is exactly what happens when Daisy finally arrives." The same devices appear, but each is argued into meaning. The difference is not the vocabulary; it is the conviction that the technique is doing something worth saying.

One practical habit prevents mechanism: never end a paragraph on evidence. End on the interpretation the evidence supports. The paragraph's last sentence is the reader's takeaway, and if the takeaway is a quotation, the reader takes away decoration. If the takeaway is a claim, the reader takes away an argument. Another habit: vary the kinds of technique you discuss. An essay that reads only diction sounds like a word list; an essay that moves between diction, staging, narration, dialogue, and structure sounds like a reading. The worked Daisy interpretation above does this naturally, and that variety is part of why it feels alive.

Finally, let the essay take a position with some heat. The verdict section of this article will model this, but the principle applies throughout: an interpretation argued with conviction is never mechanical, because conviction is the opposite of inventory. You can argue that Gatsby's construction is gorgeous and doomed, that Tom's is brutal and honest in its brutality, that Nick's is the novel's most careful self-deception. These are positions. Take them. The examiner is not marking your tenderness toward the figures; the examiner is marking the quality of your arguing. A construction-based essay with a pulse beats a biographical essay with a shrug, every time.

Voice is the final defense against mechanism, and it is worth addressing directly because students hear "technique" and reach for dead prose. The essay about construction does not need to sound like a laboratory report; the best ones sound like a reader thinking hard in complete sentences. Compare "Fitzgerald utilizes visual imagery in order to construct the protagonist's longing" with "The novel builds Gatsby's longing out of things seen at a distance, and the distance is the point." The first is accurate and embalmed; the second argues in a human voice. The difference is not informality but energy: strong verbs, short sentences mixed with long ones, and the willingness to say what you mean. Write the essay you would want to read, the one where the interpretation clearly matters to the writer, and the construction method will sound like what it is: the most precise way to say something true about the book.

## The Thesis Workshop: Lines That Argue

The thesis sentence is where the character essay is won or lost, and it deserves its own workshop, because a strong thesis does three jobs at once: it states a contestable interpretation, it names the construction the interpretation reads, and it points at the techniques the essay will argue. Most weak theses fail at least two of these. They state facts, or they state judgments, and they give the essay nowhere to go. The pairs below show the conversion for six figures. Study the pattern, not just the lines: each strong version takes the weak version's material and rebuilds it as a claim about how the text constructs the figure.

Gatsby. Weak: "Gatsby is a mysterious man who loves Daisy." This is two facts stapled together; no reader could disagree, so no essay can argue. Strong: "The novel constructs Gatsby's identity as a performance assembled from rumor, catchphrase, and spectacle, so that his love for Daisy reads as the one sincere feeling inside a staged self." The strong version is contestable, a reader could argue the love is itself performed, and it names the construction sites the essay will visit: the rumors, "old sport," the parties. The essay now has a map.

Daisy. Weak: "Daisy is a careless rich woman." Accurate, and useless: it judges the person instead of reading the text. Strong: "Fitzgerald constructs Daisy as voice without decision, a figure built from atmosphere and restlessness whom the text never permits to choose." The strong version converts the moral label "careless" into a construction claim, and it predicts the evidence: the voice, the first entrance, the offstage decisions. Notice that the strong thesis does not deny the carelessness; it explains it as an effect of technique, which is the more interesting and more defensible claim.

Nick. Weak: "Nick is a reliable narrator who tells Gatsby's story." This surrenders the novel's central tension in the first sentence. Strong: "The novel constructs Nick's reliability as a posture the narration gradually ironizes, making him the book's most careful self-deception rather than its moral center." The strong version takes a position on the most debated question about Nick and frames it as construction: the opening advice as credential, the "within and without" sentence as staged position, the final verdict as disguised judgment. An examiner reading this thesis knows immediately that the essay will argue.

Tom. Weak: "Tom is a cruel bully who cheats on Daisy." Biography plus verdict; the essay that follows can only illustrate. Strong: "Tom's cruelty is constructed as bodily before it is moral, which makes the novel's class critique land in the flesh rather than in abstraction." The strong version finds the technique inside the familiar fact: the "cruel body," the aggressive leaning, the physicality that precedes psychology. The essay that follows will read descriptions, not just condemn behavior.

Myrtle. Weak: "Myrtle wants a better life and dies tragically." Plot plus sentiment; every reader agrees and no one is persuaded. Strong: "Myrtle's vitality is constructed through sensory excess at the apartment party, making her the novel's brief image of aliveness before the road reclaims her." The strong version locates the construction site, the party scene, names the technique, sensory excess, and states the meaning, aliveness as contrast-structure. The essay will argue the scene, not mourn the plot.

Jordan. Weak: "Jordan is dishonest and aloof." A trait list wearing a thesis's clothes. Strong: "The novel constructs Jordan's dishonesty as the moral weather of her class, small and unpunished, which is why Nick's attraction to her indicts his own claimed detachment." The strong version turns the trait into an effect, connects it to technique, the flat aside in which Nick reports the golf cheating, and opens the interpretation onto the novel's larger judgment of carelessness. One sentence, and the essay has both a figure and a theme.

Under examination time pressure, you will not craft these sentences at leisure, so practice the three-step derivation until it is fast. First, pick the construction site: the entrance, the set piece, the repeated image, the narrating frame. Second, name the technique in a verb: the novel stages, frames, builds, withholds, ironizes. Third, attach the meaning: what the construction argues about the figure. Site plus technique-verb plus meaning equals a thesis in under two minutes. Drill it with every figure until the strong version is your instinct. The examiners who mark thousands of scripts can spot a thesis that argues within seconds; give them one, and the rest of the essay inherits its momentum.

## The Character-Essay Table: Weak Writing and Strong Alternatives

The table below is the article's findable artifact: a side-by-side reference pairing the weak moves that fill character essays with the strong interpreting alternatives that replace them. Use it as a revision checklist. Run each paragraph of a draft against the left column; wherever you recognize your own move, apply the right column's replacement.

| Weak character writing | Strong interpreting alternative |
|---|---|
| Narrating the figure's plot in order, from introduction to fate | Arguing a thesis about how the text constructs the figure, using plot only as raw material |
| Treating the figure as a real person with motives to psychoanalyze | Treating the figure as an effect of technique, asking what each choice builds |
| Listing traits: beautiful, rich, careless, mysterious | Naming effects: how the text builds carelessness as unpunished privilege, mystery as social rumor |
| Opening with biography: birth, background, summary of life | Opening with the interpretation: the claim about construction, stated as something contestable |
| Dropping quotations as decoration after a plot point | Embedding brief quotations as evidence, with the diction and presentation argued |
| Judging the figure morally: villain, victim, hero | Arguing the construction: what the text builds the figure to mean, including its moral vision |
| Retelling a key scene at length | Reading a key scene as staged: what is shown, what is withheld, whose eyes mediate |
| Describing two figures in separate blocks, then comparing | Comparing inside one claim, using the second figure as evidence for the reading of the first |
| Ending with a verdict on the person: she was weak, he was great | Ending with the interpretation's payoff: what the construction reveals about the novel |
| Using only plot facts as evidence | Using technique-level evidence: diction, image, dialogue, narration, placement, structure |
| Writing "is" sentences about the figure's inner life | Writing "is constructed as" sentences that relocate psychology to the page |
| Treating the thesis as a fact everyone accepts | Treating the thesis as a contestable claim the essay must defend |

Read the table as a diagnostic instrument, not as decoration. The left column names the move; the right column names its replacement; the work is recognizing your own draft in the left column, which is easier than it sounds once you know what to look for. Take a finished draft and highlight every sentence that states a plot fact in one color and every sentence that argues a construction claim in another. If the first color dominates, the table tells you exactly which rows to apply: convert the biography opening with row four, convert the trait list with row three, convert the dropped quotations with row five. Students who run this diagnostic on two consecutive drafts typically find the same three rows recurring, which reveals their personal weak pattern, and fixing a recurring pattern improves every future essay, not just the current one. The table's value compounds: it is a revision tool for this draft and a habit-builder for the examination.

## Before and After: The Same Paragraph Rewritten

Nothing teaches the move faster than watching it happen to a real paragraph, so here is a weak character paragraph about Nick, followed by the same material rewritten as interpretation. The weak version is the kind of writing that earns middle marks: accurate, fluent, and entirely descriptive.

Weak version: "Nick Carraway is the narrator of the novel. He comes from the Midwest and moves to West Egg. He tries not to judge people because his father told him to reserve judgments. He watches Gatsby and Daisy and Tom. At the end he decides Gatsby was better than all of them. He goes back to the Midwest." This narrates Nick's arc in six sentences. It is not wrong. It is also not an essay. No sentence in it could be disagreed with, which means no sentence in it argues.

Strong version: "The novel constructs Nick's tolerance as a narrative posture rather than a virtue: his father's advice about reserving judgments opens the book as a credential, and Nick spends nine chapters cashing it, judging everyone while claiming he does not. The famous 'within and without' sentence builds the position explicitly, enchantment and repulsion held together, which lets Nick participate in the very world he condemns. The interpretation this supports is that Nick is the novel's most careful self-deception, a figure whose judgments arrive disguised as observations. His final verdict on Gatsby, that he was worth 'the whole damn bunch,' is therefore not objectivity but the last judgment of a man who never stopped judging." Every sentence is contestable. The evidence is technique: the opening advice as credential, the "within and without" sentence as constructed position, the final verdict as disguised judgment. The plot facts are all still present, but they are working for an argument now.

Do the same exercise with any figure. Take a paragraph you have written, underline every sentence that states a plot fact, and rewrite each underlined sentence as a claim about construction. "Daisy married Tom" becomes "the novel constructs Daisy's marriage as the choice the text never lets her make on the page, reported rather than dramatized." "Wolfsheim fixed the World Series" becomes "the underworld is constructed through Wolfsheim as rumor and grotesque detail, never shown directly, which makes Gatsby's wealth feel both enormous and unexaminable." The rewrite is the skill. Practice it until the strong version is your first draft rather than your second.

Here is a second pair, this time on Daisy, to show that the rewrite works on any figure and any paragraph shape. Weak version: "Daisy Buchanan is beautiful and rich. She married Tom but she loves Gatsby. At the Plaza Hotel she gets upset and goes back to Tom. She lets Gatsby take the blame for Myrtle's death. She is a careless person." Five sentences, five facts, zero arguments. The paragraph could have been written by someone who skimmed a summary; it demonstrates nothing the question is testing.

Strong version: "The novel constructs Daisy as a figure the text never permits to choose: her marriage to Tom is reported rather than dramatized, her reunion with Gatsby happens in scenes staged around her rather than by her, and her collapse at the Plaza, 'Oh, you want too much!', is built as breakdown rather than decision. Even her flight after Myrtle's death is constructed as retreat into the one thing the text has always attached to her, money, rather than as a moral choice. The interpretation this supports is that Daisy's carelessness is structural, the consequence of a figure built from atmosphere, which is why judging her as a person misses what the novel built her to mean." The facts are all present, but each has been converted: reported rather than dramatized, staged around rather than by, breakdown rather than decision, retreat rather than choice. The paragraph argues, and its argument is contestable, which is exactly what makes it an essay.

## Attacking Common Character Prompts: How to Write About Gatsby's Characters Under Pressure

Examination prompts about characters come in predictable shapes, and each shape tempts a specific weak response. This section takes five of the commonest prompts and shows the conversion: what the prompt tempts you to do, how to rebuild it as a construction question, a sample thesis, and the evidence plan that follows. Memorize the conversions rather than the sample theses; the prompts will vary, but the shapes recur.

Prompt one: "Is Gatsby a hero or a villain?" The temptation is the moral verdict, a courtroom essay that weighs his bootlegging against his devotion and delivers a sentence. The conversion: the prompt is really asking how the novel constructs Gatsby's moral ambiguity, so rebuild it as a construction question. Sample thesis: "The novel constructs Gatsby as a figure whose criminal means and sincere longing are built from the same materials, spectacle and performance, which is why the hero-villain verdict the prompt invites cannot hold." Evidence plan: the Wolfsheim connection constructed through rumor and grotesque detail, never shown directly; the parties constructed as theater; the green light scenes constructed as sincere visual longing. Each paragraph argues one construction site, and the essay concludes that the text builds the ambiguity deliberately rather than resolving it.

Prompt two: "Discuss the presentation of Daisy Buchanan." The temptation is the biography: beautiful, rich, married Tom, loved Gatsby, careless. The conversion: "presentation" is the examination board's word for construction, so the prompt is already asking the right question and the essay must answer it directly. Sample thesis: "Fitzgerald presents Daisy as voice without decision, constructing her from atmospheric effects, restless speech, staged entrances, and offstage choices." Evidence plan: the "silver idols" entrance, "her voice is full of money," the shirts scene, the Plaza collapse. Because the prompt says "presentation," every paragraph can open with a technique-verb, "the novel presents," "Fitzgerald stages," "the narration frames," which keeps the essay locked on construction without any extra effort.

Prompt three: "Who is the most sympathetic character in the novel?" The temptation is the popularity contest, a ranking of feelings. The conversion: sympathy is an effect the text constructs, so the essay argues which construction produces it and how. Sample thesis: "The novel constructs its deepest sympathy for George Wilson, not through interiority, which the text withholds, but through structural depletion: a figure the narration never rounds becomes the casualty the careless world does not notice." Evidence plan: the "spiritless" and "anaemic" diction, the valley of ashes staging, the hollow dialogue, the erasure of his ending. The essay answers the prompt's question while demonstrating the method, and it stands out precisely because most scripts will pick Gatsby and narrate his sadness.

Prompt four: "How does Fitzgerald present Tom Buchanan?" The temptation is condemnation: list the cruelty, deliver the verdict. The conversion is the same as prompt two, but the figure demands a different construction reading. Sample thesis: "Tom is constructed as power made bodily: the 'cruel body,' the aggressive physicality, and the bullying syntax build a figure whose class dominance needs no justification because it is written into his flesh." Evidence plan: the entrance description, "Mr. Nobody from Nowhere" as class panic in grammar, the Plaza scene where Tom wins by owning the terms of the fight, the final retreat into money. The essay that argues this never needs to say Tom is a bad man; the construction says it, and the essay says something more interesting about how.

Prompt five: "Explore the significance of Nick Carraway." The temptation is plot summary with "narrator" sprinkled in. The conversion: significance means function in the novel's design, so the essay argues Nick's double construction. Sample thesis: "Nick is constructed as the novel's participant-observer, and the significance of that construction is that every judgment in the book arrives through a figure whose own compromises the narration quietly records." Evidence plan: the opening advice as credential, "within and without" as staged position, the Jordan entanglement as character evidence, the final verdict as disguised judgment. The essay treats "significance" as a question about what the construction does for the novel, which is what the word means at this level.

The pattern across all five: read the prompt's key verb, "discuss," "explore," "how," as an instruction to argue construction, convert any moral or ranking question into a question about effects the text builds, and let the sample thesis shape guide your own. Under time pressure, spend the first five minutes on this conversion and on the thesis sentence; an essay that starts with the right question can survive imperfect paragraphs, while an essay that starts with the wrong question cannot be saved by polished ones.

## Planning Under Time: The 45-Minute Character Essay

Method must survive the examination hall, so this section narrates a complete timed plan as prose, minute by minute, for a forty-five minute character essay. The plan assumes you have read the novel and practiced the conversions above; it is about deployment, not learning.

Minutes zero to five belong to the prompt and the thesis. Read the prompt twice, underline its key verb and any named figure, and convert it on the planning page: write the construction question it is really asking, then draft the thesis using the three-step derivation, site plus technique-verb plus meaning. Do not start writing until the thesis is a contestable sentence; five minutes spent here saves fifteen later, because every paragraph decision refers back to this line.

Minutes five to twelve belong to evidence selection. List the construction sites you know for the figure: entrance, key scenes, repeated images, dialogue moments, narrating frames. Beside each, write the technique and the one-line argument it supports. Select five or six, ordered so the essay builds: start with the entrance or the most visual construction, move through dialogue and image, and end with the narration or structural point that opens onto meaning. Cross out the rest; the discipline of selection is what keeps the essay from becoming a survey.

Minutes twelve to forty belong to writing, roughly seven minutes per paragraph for four body paragraphs plus the opening and closing. Open with the thesis and the essay's map, no biography. Each body paragraph follows the paragraph shape from the earlier section: contestable point about construction, brief evidence, technique analysis, closing payoff. If a paragraph starts narrating, the thesis sentence on your planning page is the corrective: ask what this paragraph proves about the construction claim, and rewrite the point until it answers. Keep quotations short; under time, a four-word quotation fully argued beats a four-line quotation dropped cold.

Minutes forty to forty-five belong to the close and the check. Write the conclusion as consolidation plus payoff: restate the interpretation with the evidence behind it, state what it changes about the novel, and stop. Then run the fastest version of the revision checklist: does every paragraph open with an arguable point, does every quotation have analysis after it, does the conclusion judge the construction rather than the person? Fix the worst offender only; there is no time for more, and one strong fix beats three rushed ones.

The plan works because it front-loads the thinking. Students who run out of time usually spent zero minutes on the thesis and wrote themselves into a narrating corner by minute twenty. The five-minute thesis and the seven-minute evidence selection are the essay's skeleton; the writing is just the flesh. Practice the plan twice on past prompts before the examination, timing yourself strictly, and the hall will feel like rehearsal.

## Reading the Secondary Figures

The method proves itself on the figures students ignore, so this section works three secondary figures as short demonstration readings. If the construction approach can produce an arguable interpretation of Klipspringer, it can handle anyone.

Klipspringer, the "boarder" at Gatsby's mansion, appears as comic furniture: he plays the piano, he misses the funeral, he calls about a pair of shoes. The narrating essay, if it mentions him at all, lists him as an example of the careless crowds. The interpreting essay argues his construction: Klipspringer is built as the novel's purest parasite, a figure defined entirely by what he takes, piano, lodging, shoes, and the essay can argue that his final phone call, asking about the shoes rather than mourning the man, is staged as the construction's punchline. The technique is reduction: Fitzgerald gives Klipspringer no interiority, no backstory, only appetites, and the interpretation is that the minor figure exists to make the major theme visible in miniature. The carelessness of the rich, which needs chapters to establish in Daisy and Tom, is established in Klipspringer in three lines. That is an essay paragraph from a figure most students never notice.

Catherine, Myrtle's sister, does different work. She appears at the apartment party, gossiping, drinking, and later she lies to protect the Buchanans about Myrtle's marriage. The construction reading: Catherine is built as the novel's false witness, a figure whose talk is always wrong, the rumors about Gatsby's past, the lies about the marriage, and the essay can argue that her unreliability constructs a world where talk replaces truth. The technique is the gap between her certainty and her accuracy; she is sure and she is wrong, every time. The interpretation connects to Nick's narration: in a novel told by a man who claims not to judge, Catherine is the cautionary example of what talk without judgment produces. Again, a minor figure yields a major claim, because the method reads construction rather than importance.

Michaelis, the coffee-house owner who witnesses Myrtle's death, does the subtlest work of the three. He is the only neutral observer in the novel's most violent scene, and the text constructs him as such: he tries to calm Wilson, he reports what he saw, he disappears. The essay can argue that Michaelis is constructed as the novel's control sample, the ordinary decency against which the carelessness of the rich is measured. The technique is his very flatness: he has no arc, no desires, no style, and in a novel of performed identities, the absence of performance is itself meaningful. The interpretation: Fitzgerald builds one unconstructed man to show, by contrast, how constructed everyone else is. That is a sophisticated thesis from a figure with barely a page of presence, and it demonstrates the method's real power: importance is not the criterion, construction is.

## Evidence That Proves Construction

A student who understands the method still needs a working inventory of where technique-level evidence lives, so this section maps the richest sites in the novel. Think of it as a field guide: when you need evidence for a character interpretation, these are the places to look first.

Diction in physical description is the most accessible site. Fitzgerald rarely describes a figure neutrally; the adjectives are doing argumentative work. Tom's "supercilious manner" and "cruel body" build power as physique. Wilson is "spiritless" and "anaemic," depletion as vocabulary. Myrtle is "surcharged with vitality," energy as excess. Jordan's "slender" poise and "erect" carriage build control. An essay can argue any of these word choices as construction: why this adjective, what it builds, what it excludes. The method for reading diction is to ask what a neutral description would have said and argue the difference.

Repeated images and objects form the second site. The green light, the shirts, the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg, the yellow car, Wolfsheim's molars, Gatsby's schedule: each is an object the text repeats or dwells on, and each constructs the figure it attaches to. The schedule constructs Gatsby's self-invention as programmatic, a boy's earnest plan; the molars construct Wolfsheim's menace as bodily and grotesque; the yellow car constructs the careless world's lethal glamour. The essay's move is to argue what the repetition builds: an object that appears once is a prop, an object the narration returns to is a construction site.

Dialogue and voice form the third site, and it deserves emphasis because students quote speech without reading it. Gatsby's "old sport" is a performed identity in two syllables, repeated until the repetition is the point. Daisy's restless questions are voice as evasion. Tom's bullying syntax is class as grammar. Wilson's hollow questions in the garage are depletion as speech. The method: quote briefly, argue the diction and rhythm, connect to the interpretation. Speech is the figure constructing itself in real time, which makes it the most direct evidence of construction available.

Narration and framing form the fourth site, and Nick is both the richest source and the subtlest. Everything about every figure arrives through Nick's retrospective sentences, which means every character interpretation is also, at one remove, an interpretation of Nick. The essay can argue this openly: "Gatsby as Nick builds him" is a more precise thesis than "Gatsby as he is." The withheld information is evidence too: that we never see Gatsby's criminal dealings directly, that Daisy's decision happens offstage, that the narration lingers on parties and rushes past grief. What the text refuses to show is a construction decision, and the essay that argues the refusal is reading at the highest level.

Structure and placement form the fifth site. Entrances, as shown earlier, are staged. So are exits: Gatsby's death is constructed as lonely aftermath, the phone that never rings; Myrtle's death is constructed as public spectacle, the crowd and the blood; Wilson's end is constructed as erasure. The order of revelation is technique: that we learn Gatsby's real name late, that the schedule appears after the myth, that Nick's judgment of the whole "bunch" comes last. An essay that argues placement, that this scene comes here to do this work, is arguing construction at the architectural level, and examiners reward it because it shows command of the whole novel rather than of isolated moments.

Two worked mini-readings will show how the sites combine in practice. First, Wolfsheim's cufflinks. The plot fact: Wolfsheim wears cuff buttons made of human molars. The narrating essay mentions this as color, proof that he is a gangster. The interpreting essay reads the technique: the menace is constructed through a small grotesque object rather than through any shown act of violence, and the object is displayed casually, in a lunch scene, which makes the underworld feel domesticated and therefore more chilling. The interpretation: the novel builds its criminal world as rumor plus grotesque detail, never dramatized directly, which keeps Gatsby's wealth enormous and unexaminable. One object, closely read, carries a claim about the novel's whole treatment of crime.

Second, the schedule in Hopalong Cassidy. The plot fact: the young James Gatz wrote a daily schedule, "study electricity," "practice elocution," on the flyleaf of a dime novel. The narrating essay treats it as biography, proof of Gatsby's ambition. The interpreting essay reads the technique: the schedule is placed after Gatsby's death, revealed by his father, which constructs it as an epitaph rather than a plan, and its earnest particulars, the boy's misspelled resolve, construct self-invention as both touching and childish. The interpretation: the novel builds the American self-made myth as a document, a piece of paper, which is why Gatsby's grandeur always has a paper-thin quality the narration never lets you forget. The placement does half the arguing: revealed at the funeral, the schedule reframes everything that came before.

## A Complete Essay Skeleton, Worked

Everything in this article converges on the actual essay, so here is a full skeleton for a forty-five minute answer to the prompt "Discuss the presentation of Jay Gatsby," showing how the method assembles into a complete piece. The skeleton is not a script to memorize; it is a demonstration of the architecture, paragraph by paragraph, so you can see the thesis distributing its work.

The opening paragraph states the thesis and maps the essay: "The novel presents Gatsby as an identity assembled from rumor, spectacle, and catchphrase, a performance the text stages before it ever shows the man, so that the sincerity of his longing reads as the one unperformed thing inside a performed self. The essay argues this through the delayed entrance, the parties as theater, the 'old sport' refrain, and the green light scenes." Four sentences: thesis, then the map. No biography, no throat-clearing.

Body paragraph one takes the delayed entrance: the rumors, the telephone calls, the mistaken-identity meeting. The point: the novel withholds the figure and supplies the talk, constructing Gatsby as something other people assemble. The evidence: the party gossip, Nick's apology to the stranger who is the host. The analysis: the misrecognition is staged, and it argues that Gatsby's identity is from the start a matter of how he is seen. The closing payoff: the mystery is not in the man but in the apparatus around him.

Body paragraph two takes the parties as theater: Owl Eyes' "regular Belasco," the guest list as a catalogue, Nick's observation of the spectacle from its edge. The point: the parties are constructed as performance, and the performance is the point. The evidence: the theatrical vocabulary inside the novel itself, the way the narration frames the crowds as audience. The analysis: wealth is staged as spectacle, which makes Gatsby's power a matter of display rather than possession. The payoff: the crowds vanish when the show ends because they were never guests, only audience.

Body paragraph three takes "old sport": the repetition, the English affectation, the moments when it lands and the moments when it jars. The point: the catchphrase is a verbal construction of identity, repeated until the repetition is the meaning. The evidence: two or three brief deployments, one smooth and one awkward. The analysis: the phrase builds Gatsby's self as learned behavior, a North Dakota boy's idea of an English gentleman. The payoff: the performance has seams, and the seams are where the real Gatsby shows through.

Body paragraph four takes the green light scenes: the silhouette, the blue lawn sentence, the final pages. The point: against all the performance, the longing is constructed as sincere and visual, staged at a distance. The evidence: "He had come a long way to this blue lawn," the reaching Nick describes. The analysis: the distance is the technique, desire needs the unreachable the way the performance needs the audience. The payoff: this is the one unperformed thing, which is why it is the one thing the novel lets stand.

The conclusion consolidates: the four construction sites together build a figure who is performance all the way down except for the longing, and that exception is the novel's judgment on him, gorgeous and doomed. Then the final payoff: the presentation argues that identity in the novel's world is always staged, which connects Gatsby's construction to the book's larger vision. Five paragraphs, one thesis, every paragraph arguing construction. That is the whole method in one essay.

## Verdict

The position this article takes is simple and worth stating bluntly: the character essay that narrates is not a weaker version of the character essay that argues; it is a different task done by mistake. Narrating answers a question nobody asked. The examiner did not set the essay to discover whether you read the book. The examiner set it to discover whether you can read. Every paragraph that retells plot is a paragraph that declines the actual assignment, and no amount of accurate summary compensates for that refusal, because accuracy was never the scarce resource. Interpretation is.

This has a consequence students should hear clearly. You do not need to know more about the figures. You need to argue more about the sentences. The student who knows every plot point and argues nothing will lose to the student who knows the key scenes and argues everything, and this is not an injustice of examining but its exact purpose. The series' complete map of the novel's characters exists so that content knowledge is never the bottleneck; what remains, and what this article has taught, is the skill of turning that knowledge into interpretation. A character is a construction to interpret, not a person to describe. Write the essay that believes it.

There is a further verdict inside the method, and it concerns which interpretations deserve your conviction. The best character theses are the ones that surprise you slightly as you write them: that Nick is the novel's most careful self-deception rather than its moral center, that Daisy's tragedy is structural rather than personal, that Tom's brutality is the novel's most honest construction. If your thesis feels safe, it is probably description wearing an argument's clothes. Push it one step further, to the claim you would need evidence to defend, and then defend it. That push, from the safe observation to the contestable reading, is the entire skill. Everything else is practice.

And there is a last verdict about what this skill is for beyond the examination. Learning to read a character as a construction is learning to see how effects are made: how a voice is built from diction, how sympathy is staged, how a judgment is smuggled inside a description. That is not only an examination skill; it is the beginning of reading everything, novels, films, speeches, with the maker's eyes. The examination rewards it because the examination is trying, however imperfectly, to reward readers. Become the reader who asks what was built and how, and the marks will follow as a side effect of the more important change.

## Study: Gathering Evidence and Practicing the Skill

Method without practice stays theoretical, so this section turns the article into a study plan. The skill has two halves: gathering technique-level evidence for a character interpretation, and rehearsing the essay under conditions that produce feedback. Each half has a dedicated tool, and the plan below uses both.

Begin with evidence. Choose one figure and one interpretation to argue: for example, that the novel constructs Jordan as the moral weather of her class, or that it constructs Wilson as structural depletion. Then gather the technique-level evidence the interpretation needs, working through the five sites mapped above: diction in description, repeated images and objects, dialogue and voice, narration and framing, structure and placement. The place to do this gathering is the annotated text at [VaultBook's Great Gatsby annotated text](https://vaultbook.net/tools/great-gatsby-annotated-text.html), where you can collect the exact sentences, images, and dialogue passages your interpretation will argue, building an evidence bank organized by technique rather than by plot order. Aim for ten to twelve pieces of evidence per figure, each tagged with the technique it exemplifies and the claim it supports. An evidence bank organized this way becomes the skeleton of the essay: each entry is a paragraph waiting to be written.

Then practice the writing, because the conversion from evidence to argument happens on the page, not in the notes. Take one worked interpretation, such as the Daisy reading developed earlier, and rewrite it from memory as a timed essay, forcing yourself to open with the thesis, argue each paragraph from technique, and close on the payoff. The place to do this rehearsal is [ReportMedic's Great Gatsby essay practice](https://reportmedic.org/tools/great-gatsby-essay-practice.html), where you can practice character essays and get feedback on whether your paragraphs argue or narrate, which is the exact distinction this article has trained. Run the before-and-after exercise from the earlier section on your own draft: underline every plot-fact sentence, rewrite each as a construction claim, and compare the two versions. Repeat the cycle for a second figure, ideally one that shares a construction site with the first, such as Gatsby and Tom at the Plaza, so that your second essay practices the integrated comparison as well.

Two companion articles in this series extend the skill sideways. The series' hub for essay method, [how to write a Great Gatsby essay](/2030/04/01/how-to-write-gatsby-essay/), situates the character essay inside the whole essay process, from prompt to polished draft. The series' guide to [writing about the novel's themes](/2030/08/19/gatsby-essay-themes/) teaches the neighboring skill of turning a theme into an arguable claim, which pairs naturally with the character work here: the strongest essays argue a character interpretation that opens onto a theme, and the two guides together show both moves. For the construction side of the method, the series' study of [characterization technique](/2026/05/11/gatsby-characterization-technique/) analyzes how Fitzgerald builds figures at the level of craft, which deepens everything this article teaches about reading construction on the page.

A four-week practice cycle turns the method into instinct. In week one, build evidence banks for three figures, Gatsby, Daisy, and Nick, ten to twelve technique-tagged entries each, using the annotated text to gather exact sentences. In week two, write the thesis workshop drill daily: pick a figure, derive a contestable thesis in under two minutes with the three-step method, and check it against the three jobs a thesis must do. In week three, write two timed forty-five minute essays, one single-figure and one integrated comparison, and run the five-check revision list on each draft. In week four, do the before-and-after rewrite on your weakest paragraph from week three, then write one final timed essay on an unseen prompt, converting the prompt with the five shapes from the prompts section. Students who complete the cycle report the same change: the strong version stops being the second draft and becomes the first, because the conversions have moved from the page into the reflexes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you write about Gatsby's characters in an essay?

You write about them by arguing an interpretation of each figure as a construction of technique, not by narrating their lives. Start with a contestable thesis about how the text builds the character, support every paragraph with technique-level evidence such as diction, imagery, dialogue, narration, and scene placement, and close on what the construction means for the novel. Route character content, who the figures are and how their arcs run, to a reliable character map, and keep this essay focused on the skill: reading construction and arguing it. The examiner rewards interpretation grounded in textual choices, not plot knowledge, so every sentence should advance your reading rather than retell events the examiner already knows.

### Q: How do you argue an interpretation of a character?

You argue an interpretation by making a contestable claim about how the text constructs the figure and then proving it with technique-level evidence. The claim must be something a reader could disagree with: not "Gatsby is hopeful" but "the novel constructs Gatsby's hope as visual and unreachable, staged at a distance." Each paragraph then takes one piece of construction, a repeated image, a diction choice, a narrating decision, a placed scene, and argues what it builds. You handle the obvious objection, the reading that cuts against yours, and show why your construction account explains more of the text. An interpretation is argued the way any case is argued: thesis, evidence, analysis, and a payoff that connects the figure to the novel's meaning.

### Q: How are characters constructed by technique?

Characters are constructed by the deliberate textual choices that produce them on the page: the diction of their descriptions, the images and objects attached to them, the dialogue they are given, the narration that frames them, and the structural placement of their scenes. Fitzgerald does not report Gatsby; he assembles him from rumors, a delayed entrance, a catchphrase, a green light, and Nick's retrospective sentences. Each choice builds an effect, and the essay's job is to name the choice, argue its effect, and connect the effect to an interpretation. Nothing about a figure is accidental at this level of reading: entrances are staged, descriptions are argued in adjectives, and even what the narration withholds is a construction decision the essay can interpret.

### Q: Why is narrating a character weak?

Narrating is weak because it answers a question nobody asked: the examiner already knows the plot and is testing whether you can read, not whether you read. A narrating paragraph tells what the figure did, in order, which demonstrates memory rather than interpretation. It also wastes the essay's scarcest resource, space, on material that cannot earn analysis marks, since marks follow arguable claims supported by textual evidence. Most damagingly, narrating trains the wrong habit: the student who narrates practices recall, while the student who argues practices reading, and only the second habit transfers to unseen prompts. The fix is mechanical and reliable: convert every plot fact into a claim about construction before it enters the essay.

### Q: Why not treat characters as real people?

Treating characters as real people misidentifies the object of study: there is no person, only sentences, scenes, and decisions that produce the effect of a person. Psychoanalyzing Gatsby's motives or morally grading Daisy treats the construction as a patient or a defendant, which leads the essay into speculation the text cannot settle. The text can settle how a figure is built, because the building is on the page: the diction, the images, the dialogue, the framing. Arguing construction keeps the essay verifiable, since every claim can be tested against textual choices, while arguing about a "real" Gatsby drifts into invention. The discipline also protects the essay from the commonest dead end, the moral verdict, which judges the figure instead of interpreting the novel.

### Q: How do you use evidence over plot retelling?

You use evidence by making every quotation and textual reference serve an interpretive claim about construction, never by letting plot do the paragraph's work. The method: state the claim first, then introduce the brief evidence, then argue what the evidence's technique builds. "Her voice is full of money" becomes evidence only when you argue the diction, the bluntness of "money" against surrounding lyricism, and what that construction means for Daisy. Keep quotations short, embed them in your own sentences, and follow each with analysis that names a technique. A paragraph with three plot facts and one undecorated quotation is retelling; a paragraph with one claim, one brief quotation, and three sentences of technique analysis is arguing.

### Q: How many characters can one essay interpret well?

One essay interprets one character deeply far better than it surveys three shallowly, and two characters well only when a single claim needs both. Depth is what earns marks: a thesis about construction, argued through five or six pieces of technique-level evidence, needs the space of a full essay. Adding a second figure halves that space unless the comparison itself is the argument, as with Gatsby and Tom constructed as performance against embodiment. Three figures in one essay almost always collapses into trait-listing, because there is no room left to argue construction. Choose the figure your thesis needs, commit the essay's space to proving the reading, and bring in a second figure only as evidence for the claim, never as a second topic.

### Q: Which character should you write about when the prompt names no one?

Choose the figure whose construction gives you the most technique-level evidence for an arguable thesis, not the figure you find most interesting. Prompts that ask about illusion, carelessness, class, or the American Dream without naming a figure are invitations to select your own best case. Gatsby suits illusion and self-invention, Daisy and Tom suit carelessness and class, Nick suits narration and judgment, Myrtle and Wilson suit the costs paid by the powerless. Test each candidate with the thesis question: can I state a contestable claim about how the text builds this figure, and do I have five strong pieces of technique evidence? The figure that passes that test is your answer, and the essay should name the choice early so the examiner sees the selection as deliberate.

### Q: How do you write about a character with almost no dialogue?

You write about them through the techniques that construct them in speech's absence: diction of description, narrative framing, placement, and what other figures' dialogue reveals. George Wilson is the model case: "spiritless" and "anaemic" in diction, staged in the valley of ashes as part of the scenery, given hollow questions rather than statements. The essay argues that the near-silence is itself the construction, a figure the narration never bothers to round, which makes him the casualty the careless world overlooks. Gather the few lines the figure does speak and read them closely, since scarcity makes each word load-bearing. Then argue the withholding: what does it mean that the text builds this figure out of absence? That question is the thesis.

### Q: How do you write about a minor character such as Owl Eyes in an essay?

You write about a minor figure by arguing the specific construction job the text gives them, not by inflating them into a major figure. Owl Eyes appears briefly, yet his lines do concentrated work: "This fella's a regular Belasco" names Gatsby's theatricality from inside the novel, and his wonder at the real books argues that Gatsby's performance has genuine substance behind it. The essay's thesis can be about function: the minor figure is constructed as the novel's internal audience, saying what the narration will not say directly. Limit the evidence to the figure's actual appearances, argue each closely, and connect the function to your larger reading. A paragraph that proves a minor figure's precise construction job demonstrates more skill than three paragraphs of padded biography.

### Q: How do you write about a static character without a flat essay?

You write about a static figure by arguing that the stasis is the construction, not by apologizing for it. Tom Buchanan barely changes, and the essay's thesis can be that the novel builds his changelessness as the point: old money does not need to develop because it already owns the world it inhabits. The evidence is the bodily construction, the "cruel body" that never softens, the dialogue that never doubts, the final retreat into money that mirrors the opening. A dynamic arc is only one kind of construction; a static figure is constructed too, and the essay argues what the refusal to change means. The flat essay describes the stasis; the strong essay interprets it, asking why this figure is built to be immovable and what that immovability costs everyone around him.

### Q: How do you open a character essay with interpretation instead of biography?

You open with your contestable thesis about construction, stated in the first paragraph, with no biographical throat-clearing before it. The weak opening summarizes who the figure is and where they come from; the strong opening announces the reading: "The novel constructs Daisy as voice without decision, a figure built from atmosphere whom the text never permits to choose." Follow the thesis with a sentence naming the techniques you will argue, diction, image, scene placement, so the examiner sees the essay's map immediately. Biographical facts can enter later, but only as raw material for claims, never as the opening's substance. An opening that argues tells the examiner within seconds that this essay will interpret rather than narrate, which frames everything that follows.

### Q: How do you use a character's spoken dialogue as evidence?

You use dialogue as evidence by quoting briefly and arguing the diction, rhythm, and dramatic situation of the speech, never by letting the quotation speak for itself. Gatsby's "old sport" is evidence when you argue the repetition as performed identity; Tom's "Mr. Nobody from Nowhere" is evidence when you argue the diminutive as class panic in syntax; Daisy's "Oh, you want too much!" is evidence when you argue it as the confession of a figure built never to choose. Introduce each quotation inside your own sentence, keep it short enough to analyze word by word, and follow it with the technique claim: what does this choice of words build? Dialogue is the figure constructing itself aloud, which makes it the most direct construction evidence available, but only the analysis converts it from decoration into proof.

### Q: How do you handle a prompt that asks whether a character is sympathetic?

You handle it by converting the sympathy question into a construction question, because "is she sympathetic" invites moral grading while "how is she constructed" invites interpretation. The method: acknowledge the prompt's terms, then argue how the text builds the figure's effect on the reader. Daisy can be read as constructed for ambivalence: the voice enchants, the choices evade, and the narration withholds the interiority that would settle the verdict. Your thesis takes a position on the construction, not on the person: "the novel constructs Daisy to defeat sympathy and judgment alike, building a figure of pure atmosphere." Answer the actual question, but answer it as a reader of technique: what the text does to produce the sympathy problem is more arguable, and more mark-worthy, than the verdict itself.

### Q: How do you write about Nick as both narrator and character?

You write about Nick by keeping the two roles distinct and arguing how they interact, since the narration constructs every other figure and the character participates in the story. The thesis can be that Nick's claimed detachment is itself a construction: the opening advice about reserving judgments builds a credential of tolerance that the subsequent judgments quietly spend. Evidence for the narrator role includes the retrospective framing, the "within and without" positioning, and the selective withholding of information. Evidence for the character role includes his entanglement with Jordan, his complicity at the Plaza, and his final verdict on Gatsby. The interpretation argues that the two roles contaminate each other: the character's compromises ironize the narrator's claims, which is why Nick is the novel's most careful self-deception rather than its moral center.

### Q: How do you handle counter-evidence that cuts against your character reading?

You handle counter-evidence by conceding it and absorbing it, because an interpretation that survives its strongest objection is more persuasive than one that pretends the objection does not exist. If your thesis is that Daisy is constructed as incapable of choice, the counter-evidence is her moment of apparent decision at the Plaza, or the genuine feeling in the shirts scene. The strong essay names the counter-evidence directly, grants its force, and then argues why the construction account still explains more: the Plaza collapse reads as breakdown rather than decision, the tears fall on objects rather than on Gatsby. This move, concession plus reabsorption, demonstrates the highest-level skill examiners reward: not the avoidance of difficulty but its mastery. A thesis with no counter-evidence is usually a description; invite the objection and defeat it.

### Q: How do you disagree with a common reading of a character?

You disagree by granting the common reading its evidence, then arguing that a construction-based reading explains that evidence better. The common reading of Gatsby as a romantic hero has real textual support: the longing, the parties, the devotion. The disagreeing essay does not deny those passages; it re-reads them as construction: the longing is staged at a distance, the parties are theater, the devotion attaches to an image. The structure is respectful and lethal: "readers see X because the text shows Y, but attending to how Y is built reveals Z." Never dismiss the common reading as stupid; its persistence is data about the text's surface, and your essay explains the surface while arguing beneath it. The disagreement earns marks precisely because it is argued from technique rather than asserted from taste.

### Q: What should a character essay conclusion do beyond judging the character?

A character essay conclusion should consolidate the interpretation and state its payoff for the novel's meaning, not deliver a final moral grade. The weak conclusion summarizes the traits and issues a verdict: she was weak, he was great. The strong conclusion returns to the thesis with the evidence now behind it and answers the "so what": if Daisy is constructed as voice without decision, then the novel's judgment on carelessness lands as structural rather than personal, which changes how we read the ending. The conclusion can also widen the lens briefly, connecting the construction to a theme or to the novel's method, but it must not introduce new evidence. End on the interpretation's significance, stated with conviction, so the examiner's last impression is of an argument completed rather than a person sentenced.

### Q: How do you keep a character interpretation connected to a theme?

You keep the connection by building it out of the same technique-level evidence, so the theme emerges from the character's construction rather than being bolted on. If your thesis is that Gatsby's hope is constructed as visual and unreachable, the theme of illusion follows naturally: the construction is the theme made concrete in a figure. The method is to let the final section of each paragraph gesture outward: after arguing what the technique builds in the figure, add one sentence on what that building means for the novel's larger concern. The danger is the theme paragraph that abandons the character, drifting into general claims about the American Dream with no textual anchor. The discipline: every thematic claim must be traceable back to a construction choice about your figure, or it does not belong in a character essay.

### Q: How do you use the characters map to plan a character essay?

You use the characters map as the content supply and this article's method as the argument engine, keeping the two jobs separate. Before drafting, consult the map for the figure's arc, key scenes, relationships, and recurring images, and extract the raw material: entrances, set pieces, dialogue moments, descriptive passages. Then close the map and do the interpretive work here: convert each piece of material into a construction claim, select the five or six strongest technique-level evidences, and arrange them under your thesis. The map answers "what is there"; the essay answers "what does it mean and how is it built." Planning this way prevents the commonest failure, the essay that reprints the map as prose, because the map's content must pass through the thesis filter before it earns a place in a paragraph.
