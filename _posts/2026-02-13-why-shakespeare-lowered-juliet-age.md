## Why Did Shakespeare Lower Juliet's Age? The Verdict in Brief

Juliet's age is the smallest number in the play and the most argued over. In Arthur Brooke's poem, the source Shakespeare followed most closely, Julietta is sixteen. In the play, her father states the figure in the second scene of the first act and states it as a shortfall: "She hath not seen the change of fourteen years" (1.2.9). Somewhere between the poem of 1562 and the stage of the middle 1590s, the heroine lost more than two years, and she lost them in a text that then insists on the loss three separate times before the first act closes. The popular account of the tragedy, the account carried by posters, trailers, and Valentine merchandise, quietly restores the missing years, because a heroine of sixteen reads as a young lover and a heroine of thirteen reads as a problem. Restoring the change, and arguing about it properly, is the business of this article.

![Juliet's lowered age in the sources and the play - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-41.webp)

The verdict argued here can be stated in one sentence and then defended for the length of the piece. The lowered age is a deliberate structural choice, not a slip, not a printer's accident, and not a concession to the boy who first played the part: it is the premise on which the play's whole machinery of haste, parental power, and interrupted maturation is built, and the legal-historical evidence that is usually summoned to excuse or explain it in fact shows how abnormal the figure was for the play's first audience. Three rival explanations compete for the change. The dramatic reading holds that the playwright wanted intensified innocence and speed, a child bride whose vulnerability sharpens every scene of adult pressure. The legal-historical reading holds that the number reflects the marriage practice of the world the story came from, or of the world that watched it. The source-critical reading holds that the number is not a choice at all but a hardened error, a figure misread, miscopied, or halved somewhere between Brooke's page and the prompt book. Each reading will be given its strongest form, tested against the texts, and weighed. The dramatic reading, deepened by Coppélia Kahn's account of coming of age, survives the testing. The legal-historical reading explains the floor of the number but not its selection. The source-critical reading fails on the evidence of the early texts themselves.

One boundary needs marking before the evidence is opened. This article owns the cross-source question: why the number dropped between the tellers and the play. What the drop does inside the finished play, scene by scene, belongs to the companion study of [Juliet at thirteen and the daring of Shakespeare's heroine](/2022/07/01/juliet-thirteen-years-old-heroine/), and the general question of both lovers' ages, including the conspicuous blank where Romeo's age should stand, is treated in full in the survey of [how old Romeo and Juliet are and why the question matters](/2022/02/18/how-old-are-romeo-and-juliet-age-question/). Here the quarry is narrower and harder: a single numeral, its documentary trail, and the three explanations that fight over it.

A word about method belongs at the threshold, because the three explanations differ less in the evidence they cite than in what they count as evidence. The dramatic reading argues from the play's finished design, trusting that effects sustained across five acts were intended by the mind that sustained them. The legal-historical reading argues from records outside the play, trusting that a dramatist's fiction is best explained by the documented world around it. The source-critical reading argues from the mechanics of transmission, trusting documents over designs. Each trust is reasonable in its own territory, and the adjudication offered here does not pretend otherwise. It proceeds instead by asking which explanation survives contact with all three territories at once: the design, the records, and the documents. An explanation that accounts for the play's internal argument about the number, for the number's position below both English and Italian norms, and for the number's stability across the early texts will be preferred over explanations that account for one territory by ignoring the other two. That standard is set now so that the verdict, when it arrives, can be checked against it.

## The Evidence: Sixteen in Brooke, Not Yet Fourteen in the Play

Every serious discussion of the lowered age begins with Geoffrey Bullough, whose Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare, published in its first volume in 1957, printed the relevant texts side by side and fixed the map that source study still uses. Bullough's settlement of the descent is the foundation on which any age argument must stand. The tale begins in Italy with Luigi da Porto, whose Giulietta e Romeo, written in the 1520s and printed at Venice in 1530, gave the story its Verona setting, its feuding houses, and its double death. Matteo Bandello retold it at greater length in the Novelle of 1554, the second part, ninth tale, adding the nurse's expanded part and the elaborated tomb sequence. Pierre Boaistuau carried it into French in the Histoires Tragiques of 1559, tightening Bandello's frame and darkening its moral weather. Arthur Brooke Englished Boaistuau in verse as The Tragicall Historye of Romeus and Juliet in 1562, a poem of some three thousand lines in poulter's measure, and William Painter Englished the same French prose in The Palace of Pleasure in 1566 and 1567. Shakespeare worked chiefly from Brooke, with Painter available as a prose control, and the age of the heroine is one of the cleanest measurable differences between the poem he read and the play he wrote.

The Italian tellers treat their heroine as a marriageable young woman of the novella world and spend no emphasis on a precise count of her years; the tale's machinery needs her marriageable, not numbered. Brooke is the first teller on the road to the play to fix a figure, and the figure he fixes is sixteen. His Julietta is a maid of sixteen years, of an age at which his own prefatory moral can hold her responsible for her choices, since Brooke's address to the reader condemns the lovers as warning examples of disobedience and ungoverned desire. The sixteen matters to the poem's didactic frame: a heroine of sixteen who defies her parents, deceives her nurse's employers, and dies by her own hand can be exhibited as a moral agent in a way that a child of thirteen cannot so easily be. The poem's whole economy of blame presumes a young woman old enough to be blamed.

Against that sixteen, the play sets a figure that is not even a round number. Capulet does not call his daughter thirteen. He says she "hath not seen the change of fourteen years" (1.2.9), a phrasing that measures her against a birthday still in the future and so places her, at the play's opening, at thirteen years old with her fourteenth birthday a fortnight away. The Nurse's longer reckoning in the following scene closes the arithmetic: "Come Lammas Eve at night shall she be fourteen" (1.3.17-18), Lammas Eve falling on the last day of July, with "a fortnight and odd days" (1.3.15) to run. The action of the play therefore opens in the middle of July, days before the heroine turns fourteen, and closes with her death before that birthday arrives. The delta between source and play is precise: from sixteen completed years to thirteen years and eleven and a half months, a reduction of just over two years, executed not by altering a digit in a narrative aside but by building the new figure into dialogue that three different speakers handle.

### How many years separate Brooke's Julietta from Shakespeare's Juliet?

Just over two years separate them. Brooke's Julietta stands at sixteen completed years, a maid of an age his preface can treat as accountable. The play's heroine stands at thirteen, a fortnight short of fourteen when the action opens, since her birthday falls on Lammas Eve and the Nurse counts "a fortnight and odd days" still to run before it.

The documentary trail behind those two numbers is unusually clean, and its cleanliness is itself evidence. Brooke's poem survives in its 1562 printing and in the text Bullough reprints; the figure of sixteen is the poem's settled reading, not a variant. The play's figure is attested in both early quartos, the First Quarto of 1597 and the Second Quarto of 1599, and the Lammas arithmetic that supports it is woven into the prose and verse of the first act in both. A source student comparing the two texts is not comparing a firm number with a wobble. The comparison is between two firm numbers that disagree, and disagreement of that kind, between a source and a dramatist known to have handled his source freely, is the signature of a decision. The full anatomy of the poem from which the sixteen descends, its publication, its measures, and its afterlife, is given in the study of [Arthur Brooke's 'Romeus and Juliet' of 1562](/2025/09/19/arthur-brooke-romeus-and-juliet-1562/), and the complete catalogue of the dramatist's departures from it, of which the age is a single entry, is set out in the companion account of [what Shakespeare changed from Brooke](/2025/09/26/what-shakespeare-changed-from-brooke/). What those two studies establish at catalogue length, this article takes as its floor: the change happened, it is measurable, and it is one deliberate entry among many deliberate entries.

The trail has one more feature that the rival explanations must all confront. Shakespeare did not merely receive the lower figure passively from some intermediate telling. No surviving version between Bandello and the play supplies a heroine of thirteen. Boaistuau keeps the French Juliette at the age his Italian original implies, a marriageable young woman, and Painter's prose Julietta follows the French. The descent of the number is therefore not a slope but a step: sixteen in the poem of 1562, sixteen implied through the prose of 1567, and then, in the play of the middle 1590s, not yet fourteen. Steps of that kind are made by a person, at a desk, for reasons a critic can hope to recover. The three candidate reasons now require examination in the order of their strength, beginning with the play's own text, which is the only witness that can cross-examine all three.

## The Road From Siena to London: What Each Telling Did to Her Years

The step from sixteen to not yet fourteen looks smaller when the whole road is in view, and the road is long. The tale's earliest surviving form on the line that leads to the play is Masuccio Salernitano's story of Mariotto and Ganozza, the thirty-third tale of Il Novellino, printed at Naples in 1476. Masuccio's heroine is a Sienese wife in everything but ceremony, a grown woman conducting a secret marriage and a feigned death with a resourcefulness the tale admires; no teller in the Italian line pauses over her years, because the novella's interest lies in the machinery of secrecy and return, not in the bride's calendar. Da Porto, who moved the story to Verona, named the families from a line of Dante, and gave the lovers the deaths the world remembers, keeps that silence. His Giulietta belongs to the great house of the Cappelletti and moves through the tale as a marriageable young woman whose beauty at a carnival dance undoes Romeo; the novella counts her family's enmities and her lover's exile with care and never counts her age at all. Bandello, expanding Da Porto at leisure in the Novelle of 1554, frames the tale for a courtly audience of ladies and gentlemen and lingers over the nurse, the go-betweens, and the tomb; his Giulietta, like Da Porto's, is a young woman of the marriage market, her years unnumbered because in her world the number carried no argument.

The number enters the tradition in French and English dress, and it enters as a moral instrument. Boaistuau, in the Histoires Tragiques of 1559, took Bandello's tale and re-aimed it as a warning: his narration judges the lovers, thickens the providential weather around them, and treats the story as an exemplum of desire punished. A heroine exhibited as a warning must be old enough to be warned against imitating, and the French telling accordingly keeps her in the zone of accountability, a young woman answerable for her choices. Brooke, translating Boaistuau into English verse in 1562, sharpened the same frame in his address to the reader, where the lovers are displayed as a spectacle of disobedience, "unfortunate" in their ends and culpable in their conduct, and it is in this moralized atmosphere that the first fixed figure appears: Julietta is sixteen. The number is not decoration. Sixteen is the age at which Brooke's didactic machinery can engage, old enough for the preface's blame to bite, young enough for the blame to sting. Painter, Englished from the same French source in the prose of The Palace of Pleasure, keeps the heroine in the same register, a young woman of sixteen in the line he follows, and offers his readers the tale as a mirror of disordered love rather than as the tragedy of a child.

Set against that century of telling, the play's figure announces itself as an intervention in a settled convention. Every teller before the dramatist either leaves the heroine unnumbered or fixes her at the accountable age the moral frame requires. Shakespeare, who discarded Brooke's moral frame wholesale, converting the poem's warning into the play's sympathy, discarded with it the number the frame had needed. The reduction is of a piece with that larger conversion: a heroine who can no longer be blamed as Brooke blamed her must be young enough for blame to slide off her and onto the adults who trade, threaten, and gamble with her, and thirteen accomplishes that transfer as sixteen never could. The road from Siena thus supplies the debate's context and its first adjudication in embryo. The number fell at the exact point where the story stopped being a warning against the lovers and became an indictment of their world, and a change that coincides so precisely with a change of meaning is unlikely to be the one change in the adaptation that happened by accident. One further possibility along the road can be closed here. Brooke's preface mentions that he had seen the story presented on a stage, and a lost earlier dramatization has sometimes been hypothesized behind the remark. No text of any such piece survives, no age from it can be quoted, and Bullough's settlement treats the surviving documents as the evidence. A hypothesis that requires an invisible intermediary to carry the number is not an explanation of the number; it is a postponement of the question, and the debate proceeds on the texts that exist.

## Brooke's Sixteen Under the Microscope: A Number With Its Own Job

Fairness to the defeated explanations requires fairness to Brooke, whose sixteen is sometimes treated in this debate as the neutral fact from which the play deviated. It was nothing of the kind. The poem's number does work of its own, and seeing the work clarifies, by contrast, what the play's number was hired to do. Brooke's narrative runs on a calendar of months: the lovers meet at a feast, court across a season of window conversations and exchanged tokens, marry in secret, and then enjoy a period of stolen meetings, in Brooke stretched over some three months of nights, before the duel and the exile tear the arrangement apart. A heroine conducting that campaign, sustaining a double life under her parents' roof across a quarter of a year, deceiving her household with a practiced consistency, is a figure the poem needs to be credible as an agent, and sixteen is the youngest age at which the period's imagination grants a girl that competence without strain. The number is the poem's plausibility device: it lets the narrative ask for deliberation from its heroine and lets the preface demand responsibility from her, since a maid of sixteen who chooses, persists, and dissembles is a moral subject in full.

The poem's handling of its heroine's interior life follows the same logic. Brooke's Julietta deliberates, wavers, reproaches herself, and resolves, in long narrated passages that presume a mind old enough to be interesting to a moralist. The narrator's judgments land on her accordingly: she is unfortunate, but she is also warned against, an example of what disobedience and secret marriage harvest, and the harvest rhetoric requires a sower who knew the seed. Even the poem's pacing conspires with the number. Sixteen years of life behind the heroine matches the months of courtship before her; the poem is in no hurry, because its world contains no emergency that a sixteen-year-old cannot be left to mishandle at leisure. When Shakespeare compressed the months to days, he did not merely shorten the same story. He changed the kind of creature at its center, from an agent with time to err into a child with no time at all, and the age reduction is the biographical form of that change, the calendar rewrite applied to a person. The two numbers thus stand in the debate as rival designs, not as original and corruption: Brooke's sixteen, engineered for blame within a slow exemplum; the play's thirteen, engineered for indictment within a fast tragedy. Source study rarely gets so clean a controlled comparison, two tellings of one story differing in a single measurable quantity whose function can be read off each design, and the comparison is the deepest reason the accident theory fails to attract the scholars who know the poem best. Accidents do not usually produce a second, coherent design. This one would have had to.

## The Play States the Lowered Age Three Times in Its First Act

A dramatist who had merely miscopied a number would state it once and move on. The play states its heroine's age three times in two scenes, from three mouths, with three different dramatic purposes, and the pattern of the statements is the strongest internal evidence that the figure is load-bearing. The first statement is Capulet's, made to Paris in 1.2, and it is a refusal dressed as a postponement: "My child is yet a stranger in the world, / She hath not seen the change of fourteen years; / Let two more summers wither in their pride / Ere we may think her ripe to be a bride" (1.2.8-11). The second statement is the Nurse's, made in 1.3, and it is a chronicle, worked out on her fingers through the memory of an earthquake and a weaning: the girl was weaned eleven years before the action, on the day of the earthquake, and "Come Lammas Eve at night shall she be fourteen" (1.3.17-18). The third statement is Lady Capulet's, in the same scene, and it is a comparison that turns the number against the postponement her husband has just pronounced: "By my count / I was your mother much upon these years / That you are now a maid" (1.3.72-74). Father, nurse, and mother each handle the figure, and each handling does different work.

Capulet's statement contains the detail that unlocks the whole debate, and it is a detail the source-critical and legal-historical readings both tend to skate past. The father's preferred waiting period is two summers. His daughter stands a fortnight short of fourteen. Two more summers would carry her to the edge of sixteen, which is, to the season, the age Brooke gave her. The play therefore contains its source's number inside itself, as the father's plan. Shakespeare did not lose sixteen on the way from the poem; he reassigned it, placing it in the mouth of the one character who wants the marriage delayed, and then arranged the plot so that the delay is refused, overridden, and buried. Paris presses, Capulet relents within two acts, the wedding is advanced from Thursday to Wednesday, and the bride dies at thirteen. The source's sixteen survives in the play as the road not taken, the age at which this story, in its father's version of it, might have been allowed to happen in safety. A change that is staged inside the text as a contested decision between the father's timetable and everyone else's urgency is not behaving like an accident of transmission. It is behaving like a theme.

The Nurse's statement does different work, and its texture matters. Where Capulet gives a figure, the Nurse gives a calendar, and a calendar built from the furniture of a servant's memory: the earthquake eleven years past, the dove-house wall, the broken brow, the wormwood on the dug. The effect of all that circumstantial comedy is to make the age feel documented rather than declared, anchored in the recollection of the woman who raised the child and can date her life by its accidents. The arithmetic embedded in the comedy is internally exact. Weaned eleven years ago at the age of about three, the girl is about fourteen now, a fortnight short of the birthday the Nurse is counting toward. Editors from the eighteenth century onward have worried the earthquake line as a dating clue for the play itself, but within the fiction its function is simpler and stronger: it converts the heroine's age from a claim into a record. The audience is not told that Juliet is young. The audience watches her age being proved, by the only witness qualified to prove it, in a scene whose whole energy is the energy of a household that knows this child's years the way households know them, by feasts and falls and weanings.

Lady Capulet's statement then weaponizes the proved figure. Her argument to her daughter is that the count is no obstacle, since she herself was a mother at much the same age, and that Verona holds esteemed ladies made mothers younger still. Paris, in the scene before, has made the same move in a single line: "Younger than she are happy mothers made" (1.2.12). The play is thus conducting, inside its first act, the very debate this article is conducting outside it. Capulet states the legal-historical caution, that early-made mothers are marred early, and answers Paris with the period's own prudential proverb: "And too soon marred are those so early made" (1.2.13). His wife and the suitor state the counter-case from practice. The heroine's age is not background information in this play. It is the subject of the play's first marital negotiation, argued by the parties to the marriage market in competing idioms, and the dramatist who lowered the number also wrote the argument about the number. That is the behavior of a designer, and the dramatic reading of the change begins from exactly this observation.

### Does the play itself debate whether its heroine is old enough to marry?

The play debates the question openly in its first act. Capulet postpones the match on the ground of his daughter's years, answering Paris that those "too soon marred are those so early made," while Paris counters that younger girls are "happy mothers made" and Lady Capulet cites her own early motherhood as precedent for proceeding.

## The Dramatic Reading: Innocence, Haste, and the Marriage Market

The dramatic reading, in its plainest form, holds that the age was lowered to intensify the heroine's vulnerability and the story's speed. Its evidence is the play's whole design. Shakespeare compressed Brooke's months of courtship into a span of days, cut the lovers' married life from a season of meetings to a single night, and halved the potion's sleep. The lowered age belongs to that pattern of compression: every quantity in the source that could be reduced in the direction of pressure was reduced. A bride of sixteen courted over months is a young woman in a slow story. A bride of thirteen married within a day of meeting her husband, widowed in effect within three, and dead within five, is a child inside a machine that gives her no interval in which to grow into her situation. The number and the calendar are the same decision taken twice, once in years and once in days, and both decisions serve the tragedy's signature effect, the sensation that everything happens a beat before anyone in Verona is ready for it.

The vulnerability half of the reading is equally text-bound. A heroine of thirteen stands in a different relation to every power the play arrays against her. Her father's authority over her is at its legal and customary maximum; the threat he hurls in 3.5, to drag her to church on a hurdle or cast her out to beg and starve, lands differently against a girl not yet fourteen than against a young woman of sixteen who might plausibly be imagined weathering exile. Her mother's distance, her nurse's defection, the Friar's willingness to spend her in a desperate stratagem, Paris's serene assumption that her grief can be cured by a wedding date: each pressure is calibrated by the audience's knowledge that the person at the center of the design cannot vote, cannot leave, cannot earn, cannot appeal. Lowering the age lowers every one of her defenses at once, and it does so silently, since the fact once established in Act 1 colors scenes that never mention it again. The tomb is the dividend. When the Prince closes the play over two bodies, the arithmetic the audience carries out of the theatre includes hers: the girl who died in the vault was thirteen years old, and the play has made certain, three times, that the audience knows it.

The marriage-market half of the reading is the sharpest, because the lowered age sharpens the contrast between the two transactions the play sets running in parallel. In one transaction, Juliet is a commodity with a delivery date: Paris applies, Capulet haggles over ripeness, the date is set, advanced, and enforced, and the bride's consent is treated first as a courtesy to be canvassed ("Can you like of Paris's love?", 1.3.97) and later as an irrelevance to be overridden. In the other transaction, the same girl is a choosing agent who proposes marriage herself, sets its terms, and dispatches her nurse to arrange it. The younger the commodity in the first transaction, the more naked the market looks; the younger the agent in the second, the more astonishing the agency. At sixteen the contrast is pointed. At thirteen it is an indictment, of a household prepared to trade a child on a timetable and of a world in which that child's only route to a chosen life is a secret wedding and a feigned death. The lowered age is what turns the Capulet marriage negotiations from period color into the play's central obscenity, and it does so by two years' worth of arithmetic.

Coppélia Kahn's reading, in the essay "Coming of Age in Verona," first published in the late 1970s and collected in The Woman's Part in 1980, takes the dramatic reading one level deeper and supplies the version of it that this article ultimately adopts. Kahn reads the tragedy as a drama of maturation conducted under patriarchal pressure: the play's young men must come of age inside a code of masculine violence that the feud maintains, and Romeo's path to manhood runs through the duel, the marriage, and the tomb. Juliet's coming of age is the compressed counterpart. Where Romeo is educated by stages, she is educated all at once, passing in days from obedience through desire, secrecy, defiance, and a solitary confrontation with death that she faces, in the potion speech of 4.3, entirely alone. On Kahn's account the heroine's extreme youth is not decorative pathos laid over that structure. It is the structure's premise. A coming-of-age story requires a protagonist who has not come of age, and the lower the starting point, the more violently the maturation registers. The thirteen-year-old who out-reasons her nurse, out-faces her father, and drinks the vial on her own judgment is the play's proof that its heroine grows up faster than its hero, and the two stolen years are what make the proof legible. Kahn's Juliet is not principally a victim of the number. She is the character whose growth the number measures.

## The Father's Timetable and Its Collapse

The lowered age does one more piece of dramatic work that the three rival explanations all understate, and it is visible only when Capulet's conduct is tracked across the whole play rather than sampled in the first act. In 1.2 the father is the age's defender. He names the shortfall, prescribes two summers of waiting, coins the prudential proverb about early-marred mothers, and adds the condition that his daughter's consent must crown the arrangement: his will, he tells Paris, is that she choose within the scope of his approval. He also tells Paris why the matter is delicate: "The earth hath swallowed all my hopes but she; / She is the hopeful lady of my earth" (1.2.14-15). Juliet is a sole surviving child, the single vessel of the line, and the caution of the first act is the caution of a man protecting his only heir. Every element of that scene presumes leisure: time to wait, a choice to be canvassed, a ripeness to be awaited.

The play then destroys the leisure and watches the caution die with it. Tybalt's death in 3.1 transforms the household's position; by 3.4, on the Monday night of the same crowded week, Capulet is promising Paris the wedding he had deferred for two summers, fixing it for Thursday with the air of a man settling a debt: the bride, he assures the Count, will be ruled by her father in all respects. No argument about age is offered, because none is needed; the proverb of 1.2 is not refuted, merely outrun. In 3.5 the enforcement arrives, and the father who had stipulated his daughter's consent now threatens to drag her to church on a hurdle, to disown her, to leave her to hang, beg, starve, and die in the streets, because she receives the Thursday match with thanks and refusal. In 4.2, delighted by her staged submission, he advances the wedding a full day, from Thursday to Wednesday, compressing even the Friar's timetable and cutting the potion's margin to the bone: "I'll have this knot knit up tomorrow morning." The arc is exact. The play opens with a father citing his daughter's age as the reason for delay, and closes the movement with the same father treating her age as no consideration at all, first deferring the marriage by two years and then advancing it by a day, each swing dictated by the market and the feud rather than by the girl.

This arc is the dramatic reading's most concrete exhibit, because it cannot be produced with Brooke's number. A father who waits for a daughter of sixteen to reach eighteen and then loses patience is a figure of ordinary inconsistency. A father who pronounces thirteen too young, in a set speech, on stated principle, and within four days is hurrying that same thirteen-year-old to the altar under threat of destitution, is the play's anatomy of how Verona's protections actually function: they hold precisely as long as nothing presses on them. The lowered age is what gives the anatomy its measurements. It establishes the safeguard's stated height, two summers above the heroine's head, so that the audience can watch the safeguard collapse from a known elevation. Kahn's maturation reading absorbs this arc naturally, since the father's withdrawal of protection is the external form of the process she describes, a daughter forced to come of age in the space her father's failed guardianship leaves vacant. The legal-historical reading, by contrast, has nothing to say about the arc at all: law and custom can report what Verona permitted, but the collapse of Capulet's own standard is an event inside a designed fiction, and only an explanation in terms of design can reach it.

## The Legal-Historical Reading: Marriage Ages in Italy and England

The legal-historical reading proposes that the number needs no dramatic explanation because it reflects practice: girls could marry at such ages, in the story's Italy or in the audience's England, and the playwright simply wrote the world as it was. Given its strongest form, the reading rests on three genuine bodies of evidence, and each deserves to be stated fairly before its limits are drawn. The first is canon law. The church's law of marriage, settled in the medieval decretals and still governing in both Catholic Italy and post-Reformation England, set the minimum age of valid consent at twelve for a girl and fourteen for a boy. Betrothals could be arranged far earlier, from the age of seven, but a marriage contracted by a girl of twelve was, in law, a marriage. A heroine of thirteen, a fortnight from fourteen, stood comfortably inside the legal bound. Nothing in the play's plot requires an impossibility: the Friar marries a couple one of whom is below the age at which the church would have refused her consent. The second body of evidence is Italian practice. In the city-states of the novella tradition, and above all in the Florence whose tax records historians have mined, elite girls married in their middle to late teens as a matter of course, and brides of fourteen and fifteen appear in the records of great houses without remark. Christiane Klapisch-Zuber's work on the Florentine catasto of 1427 established the pattern: women marrying at an average age in the late teens, men near thirty, the gap itself a structure of that society. Brooke's sixteen is unremarkable in that world, and even the play's thirteen is only an early instance of a recognized range rather than a freak. The third body of evidence is the aristocratic exception in England itself, where dynastic marriages could ignore every norm: Lady Margaret Beaufort was married to Edmund Tudor at twelve and delivered the future Henry VII at thirteen, a precedent at the very summit of the society that watched the play.

Against that case stands the demographic record of ordinary England, and it is devastating to the reading in its simple form. The parish-register studies associated with Peter Laslett and the Cambridge Group, and the model John Hajnal set out in his account of the European marriage pattern, converge on a finding that has survived decades of testing: in the England of the play's first audiences, women married for the first time at an average age of about twenty-five or twenty-six, and men at about twenty-seven or twenty-eight. Early teenage marriage existed at the level of the peerage and the very rich, where property settlements overrode custom, and it existed in law. In the parishes it was a rarity so pronounced that a bride of thirteen would have been a spectacle. The play's first audience, in other words, did not watch Capulet's negotiation as a mirror of its own practice. It watched a number it knew to be exceptional, set in a foreign city whose marriage customs English travelers already regarded as precocious, and the exceptionality is part of what the number was doing in the theatre. A dramatist reproducing the ordinary practice of his audience would have written a heroine of twenty. A dramatist reproducing the ordinary practice of the novella's Florence would have written Brooke's sixteen, or a year on either side of it. The play's figure is below both norms, and a reading that explains the change by practice therefore explains the wrong thing: it accounts for why thirteen was possible, and is silent on why thirteen was chosen over the two available defaults.

The reading recovers some ground when it is stated more precisely, as an account of the number's floor rather than of its selection. Canon law supplies the floor: below twelve the marriage would have been voidable and the plot's machinery of consent, consummation, and bigamy would have collapsed into legal nonsense, since Juliet's second marriage to Paris is threatened as a crime precisely because the first marriage binds. The playwright who wanted the youngest defensible heroine could go to thirteen, a year clear of the canonical minimum, and no lower. Italian practice supplies the plausibility: a Verona audience inside the fiction, and a London audience thinking about Italy, could accept the figure as foreign custom rather than fantasy. But a floor and a plausibility together still describe the edges of the choice, not the choice. What selected thirteen from the range that law and custom left open, the range running from twelve to Brooke's sixteen and beyond, was the dramatic design examined in the previous section. The legal-historical reading is best understood as the change's enabling condition. It tells the critic why the number could be written. It cannot tell the critic why it was.

The Italian evidence has a financial instrument behind it that sharpens the contrast with England. Florence established a state dowry fund, the Monte delle doti, in 1425, into which fathers deposited sums at a daughter's birth or childhood against her marriage in her middle teens; the fund's maturities, commonly at seven and a half or fifteen years, built the timing of elite marriages into the city's fiscal machinery. A Florentine girl's wedding date could be, in a real sense, an accounting event, scheduled by deposits made when she was small. Nothing in England corresponded to that machinery. English portions were negotiated family by family, and the demographic consequence was the late-marrying pattern the parish registers record. The two systems frame the play's number from either side. Against the Florentine machinery, Brooke's sixteen is the natural output of a world that financed daughters toward teenage weddings as policy. Against the English pattern, even sixteen is early, and thirteen is off the scale of ordinary experience. Shakespeare, who took his story from the Italian world and his audience from the English one, chose a figure that was unremarkable in neither, and the doubleness of that choice, foreign enough to be plausible in Verona, extreme enough to register in London, is itself a theatrical calculation of a high order.

Betrothal practice completes the legal picture, and it cuts in an unexpected direction. Canon law distinguished the contract of future marriage, the sponsalia de futuro that families could arrange for children as young as seven, from the consent of the present tense that made a marriage binding, which required the canonical ages of twelve and fourteen. Between those two instruments lay a whole economy of negotiation: children could be promised, portions settled, and alliances announced years before any wedding, with the understanding that either party, reaching the age of consent, could in strict law refuse and dissolve the arrangement. English church courts heard exactly such dissolutions, and Italian houses practiced the same two-stage rhythm at a higher tempo, the promise in childhood and the wedding in the middle teens. The play's first act is written in the grammar of that economy. Paris's suit is a negotiation for a promise; Capulet's conditions, the waiting period and the canvassing of his daughter's liking, are the moves of a father handling a de futuro arrangement whose binding stage he wishes to defer. What makes the play's Verona brutal is that the second stage keeps being collapsed into the first under pressure, the promise demanded as an immediate wedding, the daughter's theoretical right of refusal at consent treated as mutiny when she attempts its equivalent. The lowered age is what exposes the collapse, because at thirteen the distance between the two stages of the period's own system is at its most visible: the girl stands precisely in the gap between a promise that may be made over her and a marriage that may be enforced on her, and the plot is the story of that gap being closed over her protests. Legal history, fully stated, therefore does more than set the floor of the number. It supplies the very structure, promise against wedding, deferral against enforcement, that the dramatist's reduction renders legible, and in doing so it hands the dramatic reading its best external corroboration while remaining, as an explanation of the reduction itself, an account of conditions rather than of choice.

### How old was the typical English bride of the period?

Far older than the heroine of this play. Parish-register research associated with Peter Laslett and the Cambridge Group, and John Hajnal's model of the European marriage pattern, put the average age of English women at first marriage at about twenty-five or twenty-six, with men two or three years older still, in the period of the play's first performances.

## The Play's Internal Demography: A Mother Not Yet Thirty

The legal-historical evidence has an internal counterpart that critics quote less often than it deserves, because the play itself supplies a small demography of the Capulet household, and every figure in it is consistent with the lowered age and inconsistent with the source's. Lady Capulet's self-comparison in 1.3 is the key entry. She was, by her own count, a mother at much the age her daughter has now reached, which places her, at the play's opening, at roughly twenty-six to twenty-eight years old: a woman not yet thirty pressing a marriage on a daughter not yet fourteen, in a scene mediated by a nurse who has buried a husband and a child of her own. The generational clock of this household runs at roughly thirteen-year intervals. A London audience whose own families spaced generations by twenty-five years or more, mothers bearing first children in their middle twenties and grandmothers presiding in their fifties, was being shown a compressed female life-cycle in which a woman could be a grandmother before thirty and a widow, like the Nurse, while her charge was still a child. The play's Verona is not merely a place where girls marry early. It is a place where womanhood itself is accelerated, and the heroine's number is the gear that sets the whole clock's speed.

The Nurse's biography fills in the demography's darker side. Her daughter Susan, born in the same year as Juliet, "God rest all Christian souls," died in infancy; the Nurse's milk, diverted by that death, nourished the Capulet heir, which is why the woman's memory is the household's archive of the child's years. Capulet's confession to Paris, that the earth has swallowed all his hopes but this one daughter, completes the picture: the nursery of a great house reduced by mortality to a single survivor. Inside such a demography, the marriage negotiation of 1.2 acquires a pressure that pure discussions of marriageable age miss. The sole heir of a line, in a city of feuds, with a mother who was herself a mother at thirteen and a suitor of the Prince's kin pressing his suit, is not a child whose majority anyone can afford to await. The lowered age thus interlocks with the play's mortality data to produce the negotiation's desperation, and the interlocking is design again: the dramatist did not inherit the dead siblings, the dead Susan, or the mother's arithmetic from any single passage of Brooke, but arranged them around the number he had chosen, so that the fiction's whole population seems to conspire toward the early marriage its heroine's years should have forbidden. A source critic hunting for a miscopied digit must explain not a digit but this entire demographic consistency, and the hunting ends where it began: consistency on that scale is written, not miscopied.

## The Source-Critical Reading: A Misread or Halved Number?

The third candidate explanation denies that any choice occurred. On this reading, the figure in the play descends from an error: the dramatist misread his source, or a scribe or compositor corrupted the figure in transmission, or a larger number somewhere in the tradition was halved or garbled on its way into the prompt book, and the accident then hardened into the text that editors print. The reading has an initial plausibility that honest criticism should concede. Numbers are the most fragile cargo a text can carry. They corrupt in copying more readily than sense does, because a corrupted numeral often leaves grammar intact; the textual history of every heavily transmitted work, from chronicles to ledgers, is littered with figures that slipped. Brooke's poem, moreover, reached Shakespeare through a chain of tellings, Da Porto to Bandello to Boaistuau to Brooke, with Painter branching alongside, and chains of that length are exactly where figures get lost. If any feature of the story was going to arrive damaged, an age stated once in a long poem was a prime candidate.

The reading then meets the evidence, and the evidence refuses it at four points. The first is the state of the early texts. Both the First Quarto of 1597 and the Second Quarto of 1599, texts whose transmission histories differ and whose agreements therefore carry weight, present the same figure, worked into the same Lammas arithmetic. A compositor's slip explains a wrong digit in one printing. It does not explain a wrong figure sustained across two printings, in prose and verse, in the mouths of three speakers, with its supporting calendar intact. The second point is the figure's form. The play never states a bare numeral that a copyist could have garbled. It states a relationship, not yet fourteen, a fortnight and odd days to Lammas Eve, eleven years since the weaning at the earthquake, two more summers to ripeness. To corrupt that figure, transmission would have had to rewrite four interlocking statements in two scenes and keep them consistent with one another, which is not corruption but authorship. The third point is the direction of the supposed error. Brooke's sixteen stands in a poem written out in words within metrical lines, not in a cipher awaiting misreading, and no plausible misreading of Brooke's text yields the play's figure: sixteen misread gives nothing that a fortnight-to-Lammas computation can be built on, and the halving sometimes proposed would give eight, a number no one has ever suggested for the heroine and one the play's marriage plot could not use. The fourth point is the reassignment already noticed: the play preserves the source's sixteen internally, as the age Capulet's two summers would produce. An author who had lost the source's number could not quote it back as his character's unheeded plan.

The First Quarto deserves a sentence of its own in this cross-examination, because it is the witness the accident theory would most want and least gets. The 1597 text is a reported text, shorter than the Second Quarto, widely held to descend from performance memory rather than from the author's papers, and it is exactly the kind of document in which a fragile number should wobble if wobbling were in its nature. It does not wobble. The reported text carries the same age, the same Lammas reckoning, the same architecture of the first act's debate, which means the figure survived not only the printing house but the memories of the performers who carried the play out of the theatre and into the stationer's shop. A number embedded that firmly in what a company remembered, because the number was what the scenes were about, is a number the scenes cannot lose without losing themselves.

What remains of the source-critical reading, after those four tests, is a caution rather than an explanation. Source study cannot watch the dramatist's eye move across Brooke's page, and the precise mental event by which sixteen became not-yet-fourteen is unrecoverable in principle. A critic committed to austerity may decline to call any undocumented change a design. But austerity of that kind proves too much, since it would dissolve every other entry in the catalogue of Shakespeare's alterations, the compressed months, the deepened Mercutio, the Friar's altered fate, into a fog of possible accidents, and the catalogue as a whole shows a pattern of purposeful reworking too consistent to be fog. The honest conclusion is narrower. The misreading hypothesis is not merely unproven; it is contradicted by the only witnesses competent to testify, the early texts, whose agreement and internal architecture leave no room for the accident it requires. Of the three rival explanations, it is the one the evidence actively excludes.

## The Boy-Actor Explanation: Strongest Form, Weakest Evidence

A fourth candidate circulates in classroom discussion and deserves a section of its own because the brief for this article names it and because its failure is instructive. The boy-actor explanation holds that the heroine's age was set by the body available to play her: female roles on the Elizabethan stage were taken by boy apprentices, a Juliet of thirteen matched the apprentice who would speak her, and the dramatist wrote the number to fit his company. Stated carefully, the explanation has a surface logic. The role is enormous, among the largest female parts in the period's drama, demanding a performer who could carry verse, comedy, terror, and a death scene; the companies did train boys to exactly that pitch; and a playwright writing for a known performer wrote to that performer's measure in matters of range and stamina.

The explanation fails, however, on a distinction it cannot cross, the distinction between casting and text. A company casts the performer it has for the part the text defines; the text of this play defines its heroine's age in dialogue, three times, as a fact other characters argue about. Nothing about an apprentice's actual years could require Capulet to postpone a wedding for two summers, or the Nurse to count eleven years from an earthquake, or Lady Capulet to plead her own early motherhood. Had the available boy been seventeen, the dialogue would still say not yet fourteen, and the fiction would proceed exactly as written, because the fiction's number is a property of the invented Verona, not of the London stage. The explanation also lacks any documentary footing: no record identifies the first Juliet, his age, or any transaction between the company and the text on this point, and the same companies fielded boys as Cleopatra, as Lady Macbeth, and as heroines of every age the drama required, without the texts bending their stated years toward the performers. Where the stage genuinely shaped this play's heroine, it shaped the demands of the part, its length, its registers, its solo scenes. The one thing it demonstrably did not shape is the numeral, because the numeral is doing argumentative work in scenes where the performer's body is irrelevant. The boy-actor explanation is therefore set aside, neither offensive nor absurd, but a confusion of categories: it answers a question about performance practice when the question on the table is about composition.

## Romeo's Blank: The Asymmetry of the Two Numbers

The debate over the heroine's age has a silent partner, and its silence is evidence. Romeo's age is never stated anywhere in the play. No parent counts his years, no nurse reckons his birthdays, no suitor's arithmetic applies to him; he is a youth in the vocabulary of everyone who addresses him, "gentle youth" to the Friar's "young waverer," and the fiction lets the matter rest there. Brooke's poem is equally unforthcoming about Romeus. The asymmetry is systematic across both texts, and it sharpens the question of the play's change in a way the rival explanations must each digest. In the world the story inhabits, a daughter's age is a fact of law and market: it determines when she may consent, when she may be traded, when her father's authority over her marriage is at its height. A son's age determines none of those things with the same precision, because no one is negotiating for his hand against a calendar. The texts count the years of the party whose years are priced.

Shakespeare's intervention respects that logic while intensifying it. He inherited a heroine whose age his source had fixed for moral purposes and a hero whose age no teller had fixed at all, and his change moved only the figure that the fiction's economy actually uses. Had the reduction been an accident of transmission, it would be a strange accident that struck the one number the play argues about and spared the blank beside it; accidents do not usually confine themselves to the load-bearing wall. Had the reduction been a reflection of stage practice, it would presumably have shown some answering adjustment in the hero, whose performer was drawn from the same company of apprentices. The dramatic reading alone predicts the observed pattern, because on that reading the number is a working part: it is lowered where lowering changes the machine's behavior, and left blank where a figure would change nothing. The point can be pressed one step further. The play needs Romeo's age unstated, because his freedom of movement, his access to the feud's violence, his banishment rather than wardship, all presume a male past the threshold of accountability, while it needs Juliet's age stated to the fortnight, because her unfreedom is the tragedy's subject. The asymmetry is not a gap in the evidence. It is the evidence, arranged by gender exactly as the marriage market arranged it, and it votes, silently, for design.

## The Friar's Calculation: A Child Spent as Policy

One further exhibit belongs to the dramatic reading, and it concerns the adult who might have stopped the machine and instead oiled it. Friar Laurence consents to the secret marriage within minutes of hearing of it, and he states his reason without disguise: "For this alliance may so happy prove / To turn your households' rancour to pure love" (2.3.91-92). The marriage is, for him, an instrument of civic policy, a move in Verona's politics, and the lovers are its material. He is not ignorant of the risk; his own idiom is saturated with warnings about speed, from "Wisely and slow. They stumble that run fast" (2.3.94) to the great caution of 2.6 against violent delights. Yet the man who preaches slowness solemnizes a marriage between a youth of unstated years and a girl of thirteen on the afternoon of the day after their meeting, and later stakes that same girl's life on a potion, a letter, and a waking timed to the hour. The lowered age is the multiplier on every one of those decisions. With Brooke's sixteen, the Friar's conduct is rashness in a good cause, the standard material of blame debates. With thirteen, the conduct changes moral species: the play's wisest adult is shown spending a child as an instrument, serenely, for reasons of state, and the horror of the tomb sequence is prepared in that serenity. A dramatist seeking merely to reproduce his source's story had no need to sharpen the Friar's culpability in this way, and no way to sharpen it more cheaply than by the two years he removed. The number reaches, in other words, into a character who never mentions it, which is the reach of a premise rather than of a detail.

## Bullough, Kahn, and the Editors: Where the Critics Divide

The three explanations map, with unusual neatness, onto three kinds of critic, and the debate among them is the real subject of the scholarship on this change. Geoffrey Bullough represents the source-critical temperament at its best. His collection did not merely assert the descent of the story; it printed the texts, Brooke's poem and Painter's prose chief among them, so that any reader could measure the dramatist's departures directly. Measured that way, the age change appears in Bullough's framework as one entry in a register of alterations whose cumulative character is unmistakable: compression of time, redistribution of sympathy, removal of Brooke's moralizing frame. The source scholar's instinct, formed by that register, is to treat the lowered age as the dramatist's deliberate workmanship and to ask what work it does in the design. On the question of accident versus design, Bullough's whole method is an argument for design, since a playwright demonstrably reworking his source at every joint is not plausibly nodding at this one.

The editorial tradition speaks next, and it speaks with a caution the source scholar sometimes lacks. Jill L. Levenson's Oxford edition of the play, published in 2000 and now the standard scholarly text for many readers, registers the source figure against the play's figure in its commentary and treats the reduction as part of the play's intensification of its heroine's situation. Brian Gibbons, in the second Arden edition of 1980, similarly folds the lowered age into his account of the play's heightened pathos, the tenderness and exposure that distinguish the heroine from her more knowing counterpart in the poem. The editors' shared position is worth naming precisely, because it is the consensus the debate starts from: the change is real, it is the dramatist's, and its primary registered effect is pathos, the increased vulnerability of a younger girl inside the same plot. Neither editor entertains the misreading hypothesis, and the apparatus of neither gives it house room, a silence that itself constitutes evidence about how the hypothesis fares among those who have collated the texts most closely.

Kahn's disagreement with that consensus is the genuine critical dispute in this territory, and it is a disagreement about what kind of fact the number is. For the editorial tradition as represented by Gibbons and Levenson, the lowered age is chiefly an intensifier: it raises the emotional pressure of scenes whose structure it leaves untouched. For Kahn, the number is structural. Her coming-of-age reading makes the heroine's immaturity at the outset the datum from which the play's meaning is generated, since the tragedy, on her account, is precisely the spectacle of maturation forced at unnatural speed by a patriarchal order that first infantilizes its daughters and then demands they perform as wives. If Kahn is right, the age cannot be treated as a garnish of pathos over an unchanged design, because removing it, imagining the heroine at Brooke's sixteen throughout, changes what the play is about: a sixteen-year-old's forced growth is a social problem, while a thirteen-year-old's forced growth is the play's whole indictment of the world doing the forcing. The disagreement is therefore not about emphasis. It is about whether two years of arithmetic carry thematic weight or only emotional color.

Adjudication requires a test, and the text supplies one. If the lowered age were merely an intensifier, its work would be done once the audience registered the heroine's youth, and the play could state the number once, in passing, and rely on the performer's presence to sustain the effect. The play does the opposite. It states the number three times, argues about it between the parents and the suitor, builds the father's alternative timetable out of the source's own figure, and gives the heroine a mother whose biography repeats the number as precedent. That is the treatment a dramatist gives a premise, not a garnish. The adjudication therefore goes to Kahn on the central point: the lowered age is structural, a load-bearing element of a maturation tragedy, and the editorial language of pathos, while accurate about the effect on an audience, understates the fact. Bullough's framework supplies the necessary correction to Kahn in return, since it is the source comparison that proves the structure was chosen against an available alternative rather than inherited with the story. The three critics thus divide the truth among them, and the order matters: Bullough establishes that the change was made, the editors register what it feels like, and Kahn explains what it is for.

The disagreement can be restated as a wager, which is often the quickest way to see what an adjudication must decide. If the editorial account is sufficient, then a production or a reading that quietly treated the heroine as sixteen, Brooke's age, should lose nothing but a frisson: the scenes would land a little softer and otherwise stand. If Kahn's account is right, the same experiment should damage the play's logic at identifiable joints: the father's refusal in 1.2, the mother's precedent in 1.3, the Friar's willingness to gamble, the enforced Thursday wedding, the potion soliloquy's solitude. The counterfactual test is run in full later in this article, and its results are not ambiguous. Joint after joint depends on the heroine being below the age her own father calls ripe. A reading that predicts where a text will break when altered is stronger than a reading that merely describes how the text feels when intact, and on that methodological ground as well, the structural account prevails over the account from pathos, while absorbing it: the pathos is real, and it is what the structure feels like from inside the audience.

### Where do Bullough and Kahn part company over the lowered age?

Bullough's source study establishes the change as one entry in a register of deliberate alterations, the poem's sixteen set against the play's not yet fourteen. Kahn accepts the fact and contests its description: where the editorial tradition reads increased pathos, she reads the premise of a coming-of-age tragedy, a heroine whose forced maturation is the play's subject.

Read as a whole, the ledger exposes the pattern that the cliché about the play conceals. The reasons that survive scrutiny are all reasons about design: effects the dramatist could calculate, contrasts he could stage, a maturation arc he could measure out in days. The reasons that fail are all reasons about circumstance: laws that merely permitted the figure, customs that pointed at a different figure, a stage practice that could not reach the dialogue, a copying accident the witnesses exclude. The change sits on the design side of the ledger in every row that carries weight, and that distribution is the empirical form of this article's verdict. Shakespeare lowered Juliet's age the way he compressed Brooke's months and halved the potion's sleep: because the tragedy he was building ran on pressure, and a heroine of thirteen is pressure that no scene containing her can escape.

## The Counterfactual Test: What Restoring Sixteen Would Cost

An explanation earns its keep by surviving a counterfactual, and the lowered age invites the cleanest counterfactual in source study: restore Brooke's sixteen and watch the play. The experiment costs nothing to run and pays out at every joint. Begin in 1.2. A Capulet whose daughter is sixteen, in a Verona modeled on the Italian marriage world, has no prudential speech to make, because sixteen sits inside the customary range his own culture observes; the "two more summers" proposal would defer the match to eighteen, an age at which even Paris's impatience would look reasonable and Capulet's caution eccentric. The scene's actual tension, a father resisting his world's own market on grounds his world half recognizes, evaporates into a negotiation about dates. Move to 1.3 and the mother's precedent loses its edge in the same proportion: Lady Capulet urging a sixteen-year-old toward a match her own history parallels is a conventional mother, where the play's mother, citing her motherhood at thirteen to a daughter of thirteen, is a whole social order reproducing itself before the audience's eyes. The Nurse's calendar, in the counterfactual, becomes antiquarian comedy without stakes, since nothing in the plot turns on whether the bride is sixteen or seventeen.

The losses compound as the machine accelerates. The Friar's gamble, examined earlier, subsides from the spending of a child to the mismanagement of a young woman's elopement, blameworthy in the ordinary way and no longer diagnostic of Verona's adult order. The enforced wedding of 3.5 loses its obscenity in exact measure: a father compelling a daughter of sixteen is exercising a period-normal authority harshly, while a father compelling a daughter of thirteen, against his own stated principle of four days earlier, is exhibiting the collapse of every safeguard the play has named. The potion scene's terror depends on solitude and incapacity in the same way; a heroine of sixteen drinking the vial is brave, and a heroine of thirteen drinking it is brave in a register that implicates every adult who left her no other instrument. Even the tomb is repriced. Audiences do not leave the theatre computing, but they leave with a sum in their feelings, and the sum at the play's close includes the ages of the two bodies on the stage floor: a dead girl of sixteen is a tragedy of young love, and a dead girl of thirteen, whose birthday the audience has been taught to count toward and who dies days short of it, is a tragedy of the world that arranged her. The counterfactual thus performs the adjudication Kahn's reading predicts. The number is not the play's decoration. It is one of the play's premises, and the debate among explanations ends where the experiment ends: with the dramatic and structural account standing, because it is the only account under which the play, altered back, breaks.

## Modern Discomfort: Dramatic Intent Is Not Staging Ethics

No honest treatment of this subject can close without the discomfort, and the discomfort deserves more respect than it usually receives from both of its camps. One camp treats the heroine's age as a scandal that invalidates the play's reputation, as though a fact about the fiction, faced squarely, dissolved four centuries of response. The other camp treats any unease as philistinism, a failure to grant the past its difference. Both camps make the same mistake: they fuse two questions that the evidence keeps apart. The first question is what the dramatist did and why, a question about a text of the middle 1590s, answerable from sources, laws, customs, and the play's internal architecture. The second question is what a theatre, a film studio, or a classroom should do with that text, a question about living performers and present audiences, answerable from ethics and law rather than from bibliography. The lowered age belongs entirely to the first question. The discomfort belongs to both, but it changes nothing in the first, and confusing the two produces bad criticism in one direction and bad productions in the other.

The history of performance shows the second question being answered in practice, with varying honesty. The stage convention inherited from the boy actors, the casting of adult women in the part through the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, meant that for most of the play's life the heroine's stated years were a fact the audience held in mind while watching a performer plainly older, and the discrepancy was accepted as one of theatre's ordinary contracts. Film broke that contract by pursuing literalism. Franco Zeffirelli's 1968 production cast performers in their later teens, Olivia Hussey at sixteen and Leonard Whiting at seventeen, bringing the screen heroine closer to the text's figure than the stage usually dared, and the film's famous bedroom scene made the casting itself a subject of argument that has never fully closed: in 2023 the two performers sued the studio over that scene, and a California court dismissed the claim, an episode that belongs to the ethics of production rather than to the interpretation of the play but that no account of the age question can now omit. Baz Luhrmann's 1996 film cast Claire Danes at seventeen. The pattern across screen versions is consistent: directors reach toward the text's youth for authenticity and stop at the edge of what the law and the audience will bear, and the stopping point, not the text, is where the ethics live.

For the classroom and the study, the discipline this article recommends is the distinction itself. The text says what it says: a heroine of thirteen, married in secret, dead before fourteen, in a Verona whose adults argue about her years in the first act and proceed anyway. Teaching that fact plainly, with the source comparison open beside it, does more for students than either shielding them from the number or brandishing it. The number is where the play's critique of its own world is sharpest, since the adults of Verona are shown debating the girl's readiness in the language of law and custom and then overriding their own debate under pressure of alliance and pride. A production or a syllabus that restores Brooke's sixteen in effect, by casting, cutting, or silence, is not protecting its audience from Shakespeare. It is protecting Shakespeare's Verona from its audience, reversing the direction of the indictment the lowered age was chosen to deliver.

The stage's longer record reinforces the point from the other side. Through the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the part was a prize for leading actresses, women in their thirties and beyond whose art consisted partly in making a mature instrument suggest a girl, and no manager of the period proposed that fidelity required a child in the role. The stated age did its work as a datum of the fiction while the performer supplied the craft, and playgoers schooled in that contract found nothing fraudulent in it. The pressure toward literal casting is a creature of the camera and of a later culture of authenticity, and it generates the modern ethical problem in its acute form: the closer a production brings the performer's body to the text's number, the more the production's duty of care displaces interpretation as the governing question. School and youth productions invert the problem once more, placing actual young teenagers inside a fiction about a teenager, under obligations of safeguarding that the fiction itself never imagined. In every era the same distribution holds. The number is constant, given, and not negotiable, because it is the text's. Everything else, the body on the stage, the camera's closeness, the classroom's framing, is a decision made by the living about the dead author's arithmetic, and it is as a decision, open to argument and answerable to standards the text cannot set, that it should be debated.

### What changes when the heroine is played by an adult?

The stated age survives intact as a fact of the dialogue, since Capulet, the Nurse, and Lady Capulet all speak it, but its force changes from spectacle to irony: the audience holds the number in mind against a performer older, and the discrepancy becomes part of the production's contract with the text rather than a property of the fiction alone.

## The Verdict: A Deliberate Number, Chosen for Structure

The evidence admits of a ranking, and this article's business was to produce one. The source-critical reading, that the lowered age is a misread or halved number hardened into design, is excluded by the witnesses: two early quartos in agreement, an age stated as interlocking calendar arithmetic rather than as a portable digit, a source whose sixteen is written in words inside metrical lines and survives inside the play itself as the father's rejected timetable. The boy-actor reading is excluded by category: casting follows texts, and no performer's body can explain why three characters argue about a number in the fiction. The legal-historical reading survives in reduced and honorable form, as the account of the change's conditions: canon law set the floor at twelve, Italian custom made a young teenage bride legible, and English practice, with its parish averages in the middle twenties and its dynastic exceptions at the summit, guaranteed that the figure would strike its first audience as foreign, early, and meant. What the legal-historical reading cannot do is select the number, and selection is the act to be explained.

Selection belongs to the dramatic reading, in the deepened form Kahn gives it. The dramatist who cut Brooke's months to days, who halved the potion's sleep, who turned a season of married meetings into one dawn parting, cut the heroine's years by the same logic and for a connected purpose: to build a tragedy whose engine is maturation under intolerable pressure. A girl of thirteen, argued over by her parents in the play's first marital negotiation, traded on a timetable, married in secret, and dead before the birthday three characters have taught the audience to count toward, embodies that engine in a single figure. The two years taken from Brooke's Julietta are the smallest change in the catalogue of Shakespeare's alterations and the one that most completely expresses the catalogue's principle, that everything inherited was to be made faster, younger, and less survivable. The cliché of the play as a romance of timeless young love survives by ignoring the change. Restoring it restores the play: not a valentine, but a designed and debated act of arithmetic, whose victim is counted out, fortnight by fortnight, in the hearing of the audience.

A residue of honest uncertainty should be acknowledged at the end, because a verdict that claims more than the evidence gives is worth no more than the accident theory it displaces. The precise mixture of motives inside the dramatist's decision is not recoverable: no memorandum survives in which the reduction is proposed and defended, and the weighting offered here among vulnerability, market contrast, compression, and maturation structure is an inference from effects, not a transcript of intentions. It is also possible, as a limiting case, that the dramatist reached the figure by instinct rather than by calculation, the way a practiced writer adjusts a quantity until the design feels true. Neither concession assists the rival explanations. Instinct operating on a design is still design, and the question the debate asks is not whether the choice was deliberated in words but whether it belongs to the play's making or to its transmission and circumstances. On that question the evidence speaks in one direction, from the quartos' agreement, from the interlocking calendar, from the father's confiscated timetable, from the mother's repeating arithmetic, and from the counterfactual wreckage that restoring the source's number leaves behind. What the cliché of the play remembers is two young lovers. What the texts record is a girl of thirteen, a poem that made her sixteen, and a dramatist who took the two years away on purpose. The debate about why he did it is settled in its outline and open in its shading, which is to say it is settled the way literary questions are ever settled: by evidence, weighed in public, against the strongest forms of the alternatives.

### Q: What does Geoffrey Bullough's source collection establish about the change in Juliet's age?

Bullough's Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare, first volume published in 1957, establishes the documentary floor of the whole debate by printing the play's narrative sources, Brooke's poem and Painter's prose chief among them, in forms any reader can measure against the play. Measured that way, the age change appears as one entry in a register of deliberate alterations that includes the compression of months into days and the removal of Brooke's moralizing frame. Bullough's method is itself an argument about the change: a dramatist demonstrably reworking his source at every joint is not plausibly nodding at this one. His collection also fixes the descent of the story through Da Porto, Bandello, and Boaistuau, which is what allows the age to be tracked as a quantity that stays at sixteen through the English sources and falls only in the play. Without Bullough's parallel texts, the debate would run on assertion. With them, it runs on measurement.

### Q: What is the misreading theory of the lowered age, and why has it not persuaded editors?

The misreading theory holds that the play's figure descends from an error somewhere between Brooke's page and the printed play: a number misread by the dramatist, miscopied by a scribe, or garbled by a compositor, then hardened into the text. It has not persuaded the editors who have collated the texts most closely because the evidence leaves it no room to operate. Both early quartos, the First of 1597 and the Second of 1599, present the same figure sustained by the same calendar arithmetic, and agreement across two different transmission histories is precisely what a copying accident cannot produce. The figure is also stated as a relationship rather than a portable digit, not yet fourteen, a fortnight and odd days to Lammas Eve, eleven years since a weaning, so corrupting it would require rewriting four interlocking statements consistently. Editors register the theory, where they register it at all, only to set it aside, and their apparatus gives it no house room.

### Q: Does Boaistuau's French version keep the heroine at sixteen?

Boaistuau's French telling keeps the heroine in the same accountable register that Brooke's sixteen later makes exact. The Histoires Tragiques of 1559 retells Bandello with a tightened moral frame, judging the lovers as an exemplum of desire punished, and a heroine exhibited as a warning must be old enough for blame to attach to her choices. The French Juliette accordingly belongs to the zone of the marriageable and answerable young woman, not to childhood; no statement in Boaistuau anticipates the play's reduction. This matters to the debate because Boaistuau is the link Brooke actually translated, the immediate source of the English poem. The age that reaches Shakespeare through his principal line of descent is therefore the age of accountability all the way down: sixteen in the English poem, in the same register in the French prose behind it, and a marriageable young woman in the Italian tellings behind that. The fall to not yet fourteen occurs at one point only, in the play.

### Q: What age does William Painter's prose version give its Julietta?

Painter's prose version gives its Julietta the age of the French line it follows, the sixteen of the accountable young woman rather than the play's not yet fourteen. The tale of Rhomeo and Julietta in The Palace of Pleasure, published in 1566 and 1567, translates Boaistuau closely, and where the French keeps the heroine in the register of a marriageable and blameworthy young woman, the English prose keeps her there too. Painter's importance to the age debate is that of a control text. Because his version descends from the same French source as Brooke's poem by an independent route, agreement between Painter and Brooke on the heroine's register shows that the sixteen belongs to the tradition Shakespeare received, not to some idiosyncrasy of the poem. When the play departs from both English witnesses at once, the departure cannot be laid on the tradition. It belongs to the dramatist, which is exactly what the design explanations claim and the accident explanations struggle to answer.

### Q: Does Luigi da Porto's novella state Giulietta's age anywhere?

Da Porto's novella states no age for Giulietta anywhere. The tale that first set the story in Verona, named its houses from Dante, and fixed its double death treats its heroine throughout as a marriageable young woman of a great family, beautiful at the carnival dance, conducted through courtship, secret marriage, exile, and the tomb without any count of her years. The silence is itself informative for the debate. A precise figure for the heroine enters the tradition late, with the moralizing French and English tellings that needed her old enough to blame, and Brooke's sixteen is the first hard number on the road to the play. Shakespeare's reduction is therefore not a departure from an ancient datum. It is a departure from the moral frame's datum, taken at the moment he discarded the moral frame itself, which is why the age debate and the debate about the play's sympathy for its lovers are, at root, the same debate conducted in different vocabularies.

### Q: What was the youngest age at which canon law allowed a girl to consent to marriage?

Canon law allowed a girl to consent to a valid marriage at twelve. The medieval decretals that governed marriage across Latin Christendom, and that post-Reformation England retained on this point, set the minimum age of consent at twelve for girls and fourteen for boys; betrothals, which promised a future marriage, could be arranged from the age of seven, but the binding consent itself required the canonical years. The figure matters to the age debate because it fixes the floor beneath Shakespeare's choice. A heroine of eleven would have made the Friar's marriage voidable and the plot's machinery of consent and bigamy legally incoherent. A heroine of thirteen stands one clear year above the floor, the youngest age at which the play's marriage plot remains fully operative in law. The legal evidence thus explains the change's limit with precision. It cannot explain the change itself, since everything from twelve to Brooke's sixteen was equally available to a dramatist whom law alone was guiding.

### Q: What age would Capulet's two more summers have made Juliet on her wedding day?

Capulet's two more summers would have made Juliet very nearly sixteen on her wedding day, which is the age Brooke's poem gives her. The arithmetic is exact. She stands in 1.2 at a fortnight short of fourteen, her birthday falling on Lammas Eve at the end of July. Two summers of waiting carry her past her fifteenth birthday to the approach of her sixteenth, the father's ripeness arriving on the source's schedule. The play therefore preserves Brooke's number inside itself, reassigned from a fact about the heroine to a plan of her father's, and then arranges for the plan to be refused, overridden, and finally inverted when Capulet advances the wedding by a day. This is among the strongest single exhibits in the whole debate. A dramatist who had lost or garbled his source's figure could not quote it back as his character's unheeded timetable. The sixteen is in the play, in the one place a designer would put it: in the mouth of the man whose caution the plot is built to destroy.

### Q: What does Paris's answer about younger happy mothers concede in the play's own age debate?

Paris's answer concedes that the heroine's age is an obstacle requiring an answer, which is why he bothers to answer it. His single line in 1.2, "Younger than she are happy mothers made," meets Capulet's postponement on its own ground, conceding the fact of the shortfall and disputing only its force, by appealing to practice: younger girls than this one have become happy mothers. Capulet's proverb in reply, that those too soon made are too soon marred, shows the same concession from the other side, since a proverb about the dangers of early marriage grants that early marriage is the thing under discussion. The exchange matters to the source debate because it proves the lowered age is not inert information the play happens to carry. Within two scenes of the opening, the heroine's years are the explicit subject of negotiation between the two men arranging her marriage, argued in competing idioms of precedent and prudence. A number the fiction itself debates is a number its author placed there to be debated.

### Q: Could the boy actor who first played Juliet explain the number written into the text?

The boy actor cannot explain the number, because casting follows texts and not the reverse. Female roles were played by apprentices, and the part of Juliet is exceptionally long and demanding, but nothing about a performer's actual years could require the dialogue to fix the heroine at not yet fourteen, to argue about the figure between her parents and her suitor, or to build the father's rejected timetable out of the source's sixteen. No record identifies the first performer of the part or his age, and the same companies fielded boys as heroines of every age the drama required, from Cleopatra to Lady Macbeth, without the texts bending their stated years toward the performers. What the stage plausibly shaped is the part's demands, its length, registers, and solo scenes. The numeral sits inside the fiction's own argument, in scenes where the performer's body is irrelevant, and explanations of composition must answer to the fiction. The boy-actor account answers a question about performance practice that the debate is not asking.

### Q: What is the strongest single piece of evidence that the lowered age was deliberate?

The strongest single piece of evidence is Capulet's two more summers, which would have brought his daughter to the edge of sixteen, the exact age Brooke's poem assigns her. A change produced by misreading, miscopying, or drift would have erased the source's figure from the play. Instead the figure survives in the play, intact and functional, as the father's preferred timetable, while a lower figure governs the action. The dramatist demonstrably had both numbers in hand and assigned each its place: sixteen to the delay that Verona refuses, not yet fourteen to the haste that Verona enacts. Supporting evidence surrounds that exhibit on every side, in the agreement of both early quartos, in the interlocking Lammas arithmetic that no single corruption could produce, and in the pattern of the dramatist's other alterations, every one of which compresses the source in the direction of pressure. But the two summers alone settle the question of accident. Accidents do not preserve what they destroy in a character's mouth.

### Q: How do editors annotate the line about not having seen the change of fourteen years?

Editors annotate Capulet's line, "She hath not seen the change of fourteen years" at 1.2.9, on three fronts at once. The gloss explains the idiom, that to see the change of a year is to complete it, so the heroine has not yet finished her fourteenth year and stands at thirteen. The source note registers the departure from Brooke, whose Julietta is sixteen, making the line one of the standard places where a commentary records the dramatist's alteration of his material. The calendar note connects the line forward to the Nurse's reckoning in 1.3, where the fortnight and odd days to Lammas Eve fix the birthday the line implies. In the Oxford edition prepared by Jill L. Levenson and in Brian Gibbons's Arden 2 text alike, the annotation treats the reading as secure in both quartos and the reduction as the dramatist's own. No edition of standing prints a variant age or entertains a corruption at the line, a silence that measures how completely the accident theory has failed among collators.

### Q: Why does the play return to the age three times within its first act instead of stating it once?

The play returns to the age three times because each statement does different work, and a premise needs establishing from more than one direction. Capulet's statement in 1.2 frames the number as policy: a reason for postponing a marriage, attached to a timetable and a condition about consent. The Nurse's statement in 1.3 frames it as biography: a calendar worked out from an earthquake, a weaning, and a birthday feast, which converts the figure from a claim into a household record. Lady Capulet's statement later in the same scene frames it as precedent: her own motherhood at much the same age, produced to dissolve the obstacle the number seemed to pose. Policy, record, and precedent are the three idioms in which Verona conducts its business, and the heroine's age is made to pass through all three before the act closes. A dramatist conveying mere information states it once. A dramatist installing the premise on which his tragedy's pressures will all be calculated states it in every register his world speaks.

### Q: Why does the text say not yet fourteen rather than calling Juliet thirteen outright?

The phrasing measures the heroine against a birthday rather than assigning her a settled age, and the difference carries dramatic information. To call her thirteen would state a static fact, a number she simply has. To say she hath not seen the change of fourteen years sets her in motion toward a threshold the play then makes her miss, since she dies days before the Lammas Eve birthday the Nurse is counting toward. The shortfall phrasing also fits the speakers who use the number. Capulet is negotiating, and a girl described as short of fourteen by a fortnight is being priced at the top of her range, presented as nearly ripe rather than plainly a child. The Nurse is chronicling, and her arithmetic of fortnights and feasts is the idiom of a household that experiences a child's age as an approach toward dates. The wording, in short, is chosen the way the number itself was chosen: for the work it does in the mouths and the scenes that carry it, which is the recurring signature of this change at every level of the text.

### Q: What separates the question of dramatic intent from the ethics of staging the play?

The two questions have different objects, different evidence, and different standards, and keeping them separate protects both. Dramatic intent is a question about a text of the middle 1590s: what its author changed from his sources and why, answerable from quartos, poems, laws, customs, and the play's internal architecture. Staging ethics is a question about living people: what a company, studio, or school may properly ask of performers, some of them minors, and what it owes its audience, answerable from law, safeguarding practice, and present moral standards. Confusing the questions corrupts each. A production that casts adults has not altered the text's fact, and a scholar who establishes the text's fact has not licensed any casting whatsoever. The discomfort the heroine's age produces is real and belongs in both conversations, but it operates differently in each: in the study it is evidence about the play's design, since the design plainly wants the number felt; in the theatre it is a responsibility, since real bodies stand where the fiction's arithmetic falls.

### Q: Is there any record of the play's earliest audiences objecting to Juliet's age?

No record survives of the play's earliest audiences objecting to the heroine's age. The documentary trace of the play's first decades concerns its popularity rather than any scandal at the number: the First Quarto's title page advertises a play often acted with great applause, the Second Quarto presents a corrected and enlarged text for a market that wanted more of it, and Francis Meres, cataloguing the dramatist's excellences in 1598, names the tragedy among his best for love without remarking on the heroine's years. Silence of that kind is evidence with limits, since applause records rarely inventory objections, but the contrast with the tale's earlier reception is still worth noting. Brooke's preface shows a sixteenth-century reader objecting strenuously to the lovers' conduct, their disobedience and secret marriage, while taking the heroine's greater age entirely for granted. The documented early discomfort attaches to what the lovers do. Discomfort attaching to how old the heroine is belongs to the play's later history, and grows as the societies receiving the play raise their own ages of expectation.

### Q: What does the Nurse's dead daughter Susan have to do with the fixing of Juliet's age?

Susan is the Nurse's own daughter, born in the same year as Juliet and dead in infancy, and her brief appearance in 1.3 is part of how the play certifies its heroine's age. The Nurse's memory is the household's archive: she can date the heroine's life by the earthquake, by the weaning on the day her own child would have been nursing age, and by the Lammas Eve birthday she counts toward on her fingers. Susan's death is what placed Juliet at the Nurse's breast and made the servant, rather than the mother, the keeper of the child's chronology. When the Nurse works out the age in the play's third scene, she is therefore not reciting information the author needed delivered. She is the one person in Verona whose biography is entangled with the number, testifying to it out of her own losses. The scene roots the figure in grief and memory at the moment of its most elaborate statement, which is a further reason the number resists every explanation in terms of accident: accidents do not acquire witnesses with motives to remember them.

### Q: How does the lowered age alter the Friar's share of responsibility for the marriage?

The lowered age increases the Friar's share of responsibility in kind and not merely in degree. Marrying a couple in secret was, for a friar, an irregular act with any bride; the play's own presentation of his consent stresses policy rather than pastoral care, since he agrees within minutes, for the stated reason that the alliance may turn the households' rancour to love. With a bride of Brooke's sixteen, that conduct reads as rashness in a good cause, the standard material of the long debate over the Friar's culpability. With a bride of thirteen, a fortnight short of the birthday her father has just cited as a reason for delay, the same conduct becomes the spending of a child as an instrument of civic strategy, by the one adult who hears her confession, knows her years, and might have refused. The lowered age also sharpens his second gamble, the potion, staked on the same girl's solitary nerve. The number never passes the Friar's lips, yet it reprices every decision he makes, which is how a premise behaves inside a design.
