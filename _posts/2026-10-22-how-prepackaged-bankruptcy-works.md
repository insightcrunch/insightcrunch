## A Bankruptcy Negotiated Before It Is Filed

A prepackaged Chapter 11 case begins when a company negotiates its restructuring plan and solicits creditor votes before filing a petition, then files under Chapter 11 of the Bankruptcy Code so a bankruptcy court can confirm the voted plan and bind creditors who refused to agree. The case does not shorten the restructuring bargain. The bargain moves to the weeks and months before the filing, the court case becomes an audit of a deal already struck, and every gain in speed is purchased by narrowing who gets heard before the terms harden into a confirmed plan.

![Bankruptcy courtroom and creditor documents for a prepackaged Chapter 11 restructuring filing - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The sequence reverses the order most observers expect from a court supervised process. In a conventional case, the filing comes first and the negotiation follows under judicial supervision, with disclosure, objection deadlines, and hearings shaping the exchange in public. In the pre-filing bargain, the debtor and its largest creditors settle the central economic questions in private conference rooms, through term sheets and drafts exchanged under confidentiality agreements, and the petition arrives after the votes that matter have been counted. Section 1126(b) of the Bankruptcy Code permits that reversal when the solicitation complied with applicable nonbankruptcy law or, where no such law governs, when creditors received adequate information before voting.

The audit character of the filed case follows from that reversal. The judge still tests feasibility, classification, good faith, and compliance with the confirmation standards in section 1129, and dissenting creditors still hold objection rights. The practical center of gravity, however, sits earlier. By the time a disclosure statement reaches the courtroom, the capital structure has been divided on paper, new money has been promised by identified backstoppers, and exit financing has been priced. A creditor encountering the plan for the first time at filing confronts not an opening proposal but a coalition with signed commitments, deadlines, and termination rights calibrated to keep the coalition intact.

That structure explains both the attraction and the cost. A company with a sound operating business and an unsound balance sheet can pass through court in weeks rather than seasons, preserving contracts, licenses, and customer relationships that a long case would place at risk. The price is participation. Trade creditors, small bondholders, landlords, and litigants rarely sit in the room where recoveries are allocated, and their protection narrows to the disclosure they receive, the vote they cast, and the objections they can raise after the architecture is set. Speed and voice trade against each other in every such case, and the trade is structural rather than accidental.

The device is neither a loophole nor a shortcut around Chapter 11. Confirmation still requires acceptance by impaired classes or satisfaction of the cramdown standards, including the absolute priority rule when a dissenting class is forced to accept the plan. What changes is timing and audience. Negotiation happens before the automatic stay, before the appointment of an official committee, and before the public docket becomes the record of the dispute. Understanding a prearranged vote therefore requires attention to two proceedings at once, the private negotiation that produces the plan and the public case that tests whether the private negotiation may bind everyone else.

## The Problem a Prepackaged Case Solves

Time under court supervision burns value through channels that compound each other. A supplier that once shipped on thirty day terms demands cash on delivery after a filing becomes likely, and the shift drains liquidity precisely when liquidity is scarcest. A customer with a multi year contract begins qualifying a second source, not from disloyalty but from prudence, and volume lost during the case rarely returns in full. Professional fees for debtor counsel, lender counsel, financial advisers, and committee professionals accumulate by the month, paid from the same estate that creditors expect to divide. Management attention, the scarcest resource in a distressed firm, is consumed by cash collateral hearings, first day motions, and claim disputes rather than by operations.

The arithmetic turns a balance sheet problem into an operating problem if the case lasts long enough. A manufacturer entering Chapter 11 with adequate plants, trained workers, and a defensible market position can exit smaller simply because the process lasted eighteen months. Aircraft lessors, licensors, and franchisors hold statutory or contractual leverage that grows with delay. Section 365 of the Bankruptcy Code gives the debtor time to assume or reject executory contracts, yet counterparties price the uncertainty into every renewal discussion. Employees with portable skills leave early, and replacement hiring under a bankruptcy cloud costs more for weaker candidates. Each loss looks modest alone. Together they explain why creditors who agree on the destination still fight to shorten the road.

Public rescues offer a useful contrast because legislatures faced the same value destruction at national scale. During the financial crisis of 2007 to 2009, lawmakers chose direct intervention partly because ordinary insolvency timelines threatened wider collapse, and the history traced in [financial crisis legislation](/2014/02/15/financial-crisis-legislation-2008/) shows how rescue laws substituted public capital and guarantees for slow creditor by creditor negotiation. A private company holds no such option. It cannot appropriate funds, guarantee its own bonds, or compel dissenters outside a court process. The pre-filing bargain is the private analogue, an attempt to capture the speed of a rescue while keeping the binding force that only confirmation supplies.

Delay also widens the range of outcomes in ways that punish agreement. Valuation in a bankruptcy case is not fixed at filing. Commodity prices move, interest rates reset, a patent dispute resolves, a major contract renews or lapses, and each movement reopens allocations that creditors thought settled. A negotiation conducted before filing locks a valuation premise into the plan support agreement, and the short case that follows reduces the window in which events can unravel the premise. Creditors accept a possibly imperfect division in exchange for a division that holds. That exchange sits at the heart of the device, and it clarifies why sophisticated lenders often prefer a fast imperfect recovery to a slow theoretical maximum that fees and defections consume.

The problem, then, is not simply expense. The problem is that an open ended negotiation inside a public case changes the asset being divided while the division is debated. A prearranged case attacks that problem by moving debate to a setting without a docket, without weekly fee accrual at case rates, and without the signaling effect of a petition. The filing becomes a ratification step measured in weeks, and the estate preserves value that a longer proceeding would have spent on its own administration.

## What Chapter 11 Does When No Deal Exists

Chapter 11 supplies the legal machinery that a private workout lacks, beginning with the automatic stay in section 362 of the Bankruptcy Code. The stay halts collection actions, foreclosures, setoffs, and most litigation against the debtor the moment the petition is filed, without a separate injunction motion. A landlord cannot evict for prepetition arrears, a lender cannot sweep pledged accounts outside the Code, and a judgment creditor cannot levy on operating assets. The stay converts a race among individual creditors into a collective proceeding, and that conversion is the foundation on which every later bargain rests. Without it, the fastest creditor dismantles the business before the others organize.

The filing also creates the bankruptcy estate under section 541, gathering legal and equitable interests of the debtor into a single pool administered for creditors as a group. Claims are sorted against that pool. Secured claims attach to specific collateral, priority claims receive the order Congress set in section 507, and general unsecured claims share what remains after higher ranks are satisfied. The claims allowance process under section 502 tests validity and amount, turning informal demands into numbers that a plan can classify and pay. Until claims are known, no allocation can be final, which is why even a heavily negotiated case still requires schedules, a bar date, and reconciliation of filed proofs of claim.

Management usually remains in control as debtor in possession under section 1107. The debtor in possession holds most powers of a trustee, operates the business in the ordinary course, and proposes the plan, while the court and the United States Trustee supervise major transactions outside ordinary business. That continuity matters. Outside directors and replacement executives rarely know the customer base, the plant constraints, or the regulatory history as well as incumbent managers, yet incumbents also negotiated the debts that failed. Chapter 11 balances continuity against suspicion through disclosure duties, examiner and trustee motions under section 1104, and the constant possibility that exclusivity will end and a competing plan will appear.

Exclusivity is the debtor bargaining advantage Congress built into section 1121. For the first 120 days after filing, only the debtor may file a plan, and the debtor holds 180 days to solicit acceptance of that plan before rivals may compete. Courts may extend or reduce those periods for cause, and large cases routinely see extensions measured in months. Exclusivity gives the debtor agenda control, the power to set the first complete allocation and force creditors to react to it. In a case without a prior deal, exclusivity frames the entire negotiation, because creditors bargain in the shadow of a debtor proposal that enjoys a head start no rival plan can match until the period lapses or the court ends it.

When no deal exists at filing, those tools produce a negotiation conducted inside the case, hearing by hearing. The debtor seeks cash collateral authority, negotiates debtor in possession financing, litigates valuation, and assembles classes claim by claim. Each step is public, contestable, and slow. The machinery works, and thousands of reorganizations have used it to preserve going concern value. The machinery also defines the baseline against which a pre-filing bargain is measured, because every protection a creditor receives in an open case, time to organize, court supervised disclosure, committee investigation, competing plan threats, is a protection the faster structure must reproduce in compressed form or consciously trade away.

## The Layers of a Distressed Capital Structure

A distressed capital structure is a stack of promises made in different markets at different prices, and the order of the stack decides who must be satisfied, who may be impaired, and who negotiates from strength. Secured debt sits at the top because the lender took a lien on identified collateral, a plant, receivables, inventory, or all assets, and section 506 of the Bankruptcy Code recognizes the secured claim to the extent of the collateral value. A lender owed 400 million dollars against collateral worth 400 million dollars expects payment in full or return of the collateral. A lender owed the same amount against collateral worth 250 million dollars holds a secured claim for that value and an unsecured claim for the deficiency, and the split changes its vote and its strategy.

Senior unsecured bonds occupy the next layer. Bondholders under an indenture administered by a trustee, governed in public issues by the Trust Indenture Act of 1939, lent without specific collateral and relied on covenants, ratings, and the general credit of the issuer. In distress those bondholders often hold the fulcrum security, the layer where value runs out. If enterprise value covers secured debt and part of the bonds, bondholders receive the equity of the reorganized company in exchange for forgiving the unpaid balance. If value covers the bonds in full, equity retains a stake and bondholders act as creditors expecting cash or new debt. Identifying the fulcrum layer is the central valuation contest in most large cases, because the layer that absorbs the loss also captures the ownership.

Subordinated debt stands below senior debt by contract. Subordination provisions require the junior holder to turn over distributions until senior obligations are paid as the agreement specifies, and section 510(a) of the Bankruptcy Code enforces that bargain in bankruptcy. Trade claims sit in a different posture. Suppliers, service providers, and landlords usually hold general unsecured claims without indenture organization or counsel at the outset, yet a critical vendor may hold practical power exceeding its legal rank because the debtor needs continued supply. Equity ranks last. Shareholders receive nothing under the absolute priority rule unless senior classes accept the plan or are paid in full, a principle that explains why old equity fights valuation hardest of all. The loss sequence described in [how AT1 bonds work](/2026/10/16/how-at1-bonds-work/) for bank capital instruments mirrors the same logic, instruments designed to absorb loss before depositors and senior creditors are touched.

The order decides who negotiates because each layer prices its consent differently. Secured lenders negotiate over collateral protection, interest, and fees, and they can often block a plan that undervalues their lien. Fulcrum bondholders negotiate over ownership percentages, board seats, and new money rights. Junior layers negotiate over releases and warrants. A plan designer reads the stack as a map of vetoes. Classes expected to receive nothing get deemed to reject under section 1126(g), classes unimpaired are deemed to accept, and the contested ground lies in the impaired classes whose votes confirmation requires.

No layer negotiates in isolation because value moves between layers as assumptions change. A higher enterprise value lifts every junior layer and can turn a wipeout into a recovery, while a lower value pushes loss upward into layers that expected safety. The stack supplies the order of payment. Valuation supplies the depth of the pool. Negotiation happens where order and depth intersect.

## The Parties at the Table and the Power Each One Holds

Debtor management opens the negotiation because management controls the information on which every valuation rests. Managers hold the projections, the customer level detail, the maintenance capital needs, and the knowledge of which contracts the business cannot lose. That control carries fiduciary complexity once insolvency approaches, since directors elected by shareholders must weigh creditor interests as the estate value becomes the contested pool. Management also holds the practical power of preparation. The side that drafts the first term sheet frames the choices, sets the default allocation, and forces others to propose amendments rather than architecture. In a pre-filing negotiation, where no judge supervises the early rounds, drafting power and information power combine into the strongest opening position at the table.

Secured lenders hold the power of collateral and cash. A revolving lender with a lien on receivables and inventory can refuse consent to cash collateral use outside an agreed budget, and without cash the debtor cannot operate long enough to confirm any plan. Term lenders holding a first lien on plant and equipment can demand adequate protection under section 361, replacement liens, cash payments, or relief from the automatic stay when protection fails. Their consent is rarely purchased with ownership alone. Secured lenders bargain for fees, tighter covenants in exit facilities, backstop roles that pay premiums, and plan treatment that respects lien priority even when the business plan asks them to extend maturities or accept new paper in place of cash.

Bondholder groups counter with the power of the class vote. An ad hoc group holding a majority in amount of a bond issue can deliver acceptance of its class under section 1126(c), which requires acceptance by creditors holding at least two thirds in amount and more than one half in number of the claims that vote. A blocking position, more than one third in amount voting against, denies class acceptance and forces the debtor toward cramdown or a revised allocation. Groups organize through common counsel and financial advisers paid, in many large cases, by the debtor under the support agreement, and that payment aligns the group with a negotiated outcome while raising questions about whose interests the early table represented.

The official committee of unsecured creditors enters under section 1102, appointed by the United States Trustee after filing to represent unsecured creditors as a body. The committee investigates the debtor, consults on the plan, and may object to confirmation, and its professionals are paid from the estate under sections 330 and 503. In a pre-filing negotiation the committee arrives after the central bargain exists, which limits its leverage to objection, litigation threat, and negotiation over the residue left for general unsecured claims. That late arrival is a deliberate feature of the faster structure and its sharpest point of criticism, because the statutory representative of the broadest creditor class begins work facing signed lockups held by the largest holders.

The United States Trustee and the bankruptcy judge hold different forms of public power. The Trustee, a component of the Department of Justice operating under 28 United States Code section 586, appoints committees, monitors fee applications, and polices compliance with procedural duties, without holding a claim to trade. The judge holds confirmation power under section 1129, rules on disclosure adequacy, valuation disputes, and stay relief, and may refuse a plan that fails any statutory requirement regardless of the votes behind it. Neither negotiates recoveries. Both define the boundary inside which negotiation counts.

## The Restructuring Support Agreement That Binds the Signatories

A restructuring support agreement converts a negotiation into a commitment. The document records the plan terms the signatories agree to support, the treatment each class will receive, the source of new money, the governance of the reorganized company, and the conditions under which support may end. Milestones give the commitment a calendar, dates for filing the petition, obtaining approval of the disclosure statement, completing solicitation, and reaching confirmation, each tied to termination rights if the date passes without performance. Lockup provisions bind signatories to vote for the plan and against alternatives, and transfer restrictions require a buyer of a signatory claim to assume the support duties, so the coalition cannot dissolve through trading while the case proceeds.

### What is a restructuring support agreement in plain terms?

A restructuring support agreement is a contract in which the debtor and key creditors promise to back one specified reorganization plan, vote for it, oppose rival plans, and meet fixed deadlines, in exchange for the treatment, fees, and new money rights the agreement records, until a listed termination event releases them.

Exit financing backstops give the agreement its financial spine. A reorganized company needs cash to pay administrative claims, refinance maturing obligations, and fund operations after emergence, and capital markets rarely commit to a distressed borrower without a backstop fee. Signatory creditors often agree to purchase unsubscribed shares or notes in a rights offering, guaranteeing the raise succeeds, and receive a premium in cash or discounted securities for assuming that risk. Section 1123 of the Bankruptcy Code permits a plan to provide for such sales and distributions, yet the backstop bargain is struck in the support agreement, where allocation of the opportunity among holders can become as contested as the recovery percentages themselves.

Signing thresholds determine whether the agreement takes effect and survives. A debtor rarely proceeds on the signature of a small minority, so the agreement may require holders of a stated percentage, often two thirds in amount of a bond class or a majority of a loan facility, to sign before the filing obligation binds anyone. Additional holders may join later under joinder provisions, and each joinder strengthens the claim that the filed case begins with confirmation within reach. Thresholds also protect signatories. A lender that supports a plan expecting broad participation can exit if participation stalls below the level that makes the plan confirmable, rather than remain locked to a failing coalition while rival creditors organize freely.

Termination events mark the limits of consent. Material breach by the debtor, failure to meet a milestone, a court order inconsistent with the plan terms, or discovery of a materially different financial picture may permit signatories to withdraw. Fiduciary outs, where present, allow a board to consider a superior proposal, and their scope is negotiated intensely because a broad out weakens the lockup while a narrow out exposes directors to challenge. Support must be firm enough that the debtor can solicit votes and file with confidence, and conditional enough that no signatory has promised to support a plan the facts no longer justify.

Courts do not confirm support agreements, and the distinction matters. The agreement binds only its signatories as a contract, enforceable under its governing law, while the plan binds all creditors only after confirmation under section 1141. A nonsignatory creditor owes the coalition nothing and may campaign against the plan, object to disclosure, and litigate valuation. The support agreement succeeds when its signatories hold enough of each impaired class to deliver acceptance, and when its terms stay inside confirmation law.

## Soliciting Votes Before the Petition Is Filed

Solicitation is the act of asking creditors to accept or reject a plan, and Chapter 11 regulates the asking because an uninformed vote cannot bind fairly. Section 1125 requires that a solicitation after filing be preceded or accompanied by a disclosure statement containing adequate information, approved by the court as adequate before the votes count. Adequate information is a fact specific standard shaped by the nature of the debtor, the structure of the claims, and the sophistication of the holders, as the legislative history of the 1978 Code and decades of case law describe it. The disclosure statement typically carries the business history, financial projections, valuation analysis, risk factors, tax consequences, and a comparison showing what creditors would receive in a liquidation under Chapter 7.

Section 1126(b) governs the harder question of votes gathered before any petition exists. Such votes count at confirmation if the solicitation complied with applicable nonbankruptcy law regulating disclosure, or, if no such law applies, if the solicitation gave holders adequate information as section 1125 defines the term. The provision exists because Congress recognized that requiring a filing before any vote would destroy the speed that makes a negotiated case valuable. The provision also creates the central legal risk of the structure. A court that finds the prepetition material incomplete, misleading, or unevenly distributed may designate the votes, disregard them under section 1126(e), and force a resolicitation inside the case, erasing the time the structure was built to save.

Securities law considerations run alongside the bankruptcy standard whenever bonds or shares are solicited. A plan that issues new securities implicates registration duties under the Securities Act of 1933, subject to the exemption in section 1145 of the Bankruptcy Code for securities issued under a confirmed plan, and antifraud duties apply to solicitation material regardless of exemption. Counsel therefore drafts prepetition material to satisfy securities disclosure practice as well as bankruptcy adequacy, with audited financial statements where available, risk disclosure in specific rather than general terms, and equal access to the information package for every holder in a solicited class. General statements about those duties understate the drafting burden. Each projection, each valuation range, and each description of litigation exposure must be supportable when a disappointed holder later reads the document in court.

Care in solicitation also means care in mechanics. Ballots must identify the class, state the treatment, permit acceptance or rejection clearly, and set a deadline that gives holders a genuine interval to decide. Solicitation agents tabulate votes, track transfers that move claims between holders during the voting window, and preserve records connecting each ballot to the claim amount held on the record date. Errors in that chain invite designation motions and objections that consume the early hearings, so experienced solicitation agents reconcile holdings against indenture trustee records and loan registers before the petition date.

The deeper reason is legitimacy. A pre-filing solicitation asks a court to treat private assent as the functional equivalent of a vote supervised from the start, and judges grant that treatment when the record shows informed, uncoerced choice. Holders must receive the plan and disclosure together, material changes after voting may require fresh solicitation, and side arrangements that pay selected holders for support without disclosure threaten both vote designation and confirmation. When the record is clean, section 1126(b) lets the filed case proceed toward confirmation on votes already cast. When the record is flawed, the case reverts to the ordinary timeline, with a bargain that must be renegotiated in public.

## The Majorities That Make a Class Accept a Plan
### How many creditors have to agree before a plan can be confirmed?
Section 1126(c) of the Bankruptcy Code sets the threshold for a class of claims. Acceptance requires creditors holding at least two-thirds in amount and more than one-half in number of the allowed claims in the class that actually vote on the plan.

The counting rule in section 1126(c) measures only claims that vote. A creditor that holds an allowed claim, receives a ballot, and returns no vote adds nothing to either the numerator or the denominator. That design gives organized creditors influence beyond raw holdings, because a small group that votes in full can outweigh a larger group that stays passive. Solicitation therefore matters as much as economics. The plan proponent, often the debtor working with an ad hoc group, must identify holders, deliver disclosure material approved under section 1125, and secure returned ballots before the voting deadline set by the court. In a prepackaged case that work shifts before the petition date, so the tally that section 1126(c) demands is largely known on the day the case opens, and the court is asked to confirm a result that negotiation already produced.

Amount and number operate as separate gates, and failure at either gate defeats acceptance. The amount test protects economic weight, so a holder with a large claim counts heavily toward the two-thirds figure. The number test protects breadth, so a proponent cannot rely on one or two large holders while ignoring dozens of smaller creditors in the same class. Trade classes illustrate the tension. A class may contain a few suppliers with claims above 1,000,000 dollars and hundreds with claims below 10,000 dollars. Winning the amount test through the large suppliers while losing the number test through the smaller suppliers leaves the class rejecting, and the proponent must then consider cramdown under section 1129(b) or renegotiate treatment.

Unimpaired classes change the calculation in a different way. Under section 1126(f), a class that is not impaired under the plan is conclusively presumed to have accepted, and solicitation of votes from that class is unnecessary. Section 1124 defines impairment, and a plan that leaves legal, equitable, and contractual rights unaltered, or that cures defaults and reinstates maturity, produces an unimpaired class. The presumption reflects a judgment that a creditor receiving the full benefit of the original bargain has no reason to object. By contrast, section 1126(g) provides that a class receiving no distribution is deemed to reject, because a class cut out entirely cannot be treated as consenting. Between those poles lie impaired classes whose votes decide contested confirmations.

Designation of votes adds a further control. Section 1126(e) permits the court, on request of a party in interest, to designate a vote that was not cast or procured in good faith or in accordance with the Code. Designation does not punish a creditor for voting economic self-interest, and courts distinguish hard bargaining from conduct that seeks an advantage outside the plan process. The distinction preserves the integrity of the count without converting every aggressive negotiation into bad faith. For a prepackaged plan, a clean record on solicitation and designation often matters more than the headline percentages, because a confirmed tally that survives challenge allows the hearing to focus on valuation and fairness rather than procedure.

## Filing Day and the First Orders a Court Is Asked to Sign

A voluntary case begins when the debtor files a petition under section 301 of the Bankruptcy Code, and that filing creates the estate under section 541 and triggers the automatic stay under section 362. The petition is a short form, but the filing package around the petition carries the real work of the first days. Alongside schedules, a statement of financial affairs, and a list of the largest unsecured creditors, the debtor files first day motions that ask the court to keep the business running while the prearranged bargain moves toward confirmation. In a prepackaged case the plan and disclosure statement may be filed on the same day as the petition, and a motion may ask the court to set a combined hearing and a short schedule, because solicitation already occurred before filing and the Code permits acceptance gathered before commencement to count under section 1126(b) if the solicitation complied with applicable law.

Wage and benefit motions rank among the most urgent first day requests. Section 507(a)(4) gives certain wage claims priority up to a statutory cap per employee, and debtors routinely ask for authority to pay prepetition wages, salaries, and contributions to benefit plans within that framework so that the workforce does not disperse during the opening week. A parallel motion addresses customer programs, warranties, and deposits where honoring ordinary commitments preserves revenue. Another motion seeks authority to maintain cash management systems, bank accounts, and intercompany transfers, because a sudden freeze of accounts can halt payroll and collections even when the underlying business remains solvent on an operating basis. Courts grant interim relief on many such motions at a first day hearing held within days of filing, often on limited notice, and then revisit the requests at a second day hearing after the United States Trustee and creditors have had time to review the papers.

Cash collateral and utility protection present sharper conflicts. Cash collateral, defined in section 363(a), is cash and cash equivalents in which the estate and another entity hold an interest, commonly cash subject to a prepetition lender lien. Section 363(c)(2) bars the debtor from using cash collateral without consent of the secured creditor or court authorization, and section 363(e) entitles the secured party to adequate protection against diminution in the value of that interest. A first day cash collateral motion therefore proposes a budget, reporting duties, and replacement liens, and the order that results sets the financial perimeter for the case. A separate motion under section 366 addresses utility service. Section 366 bars a utility from altering or discontinuing service solely because of a prepetition debt if the debtor furnishes adequate assurance of payment for future service within the period the statute prescribes, and the first day order prevents a cutoff that would close a plant or a data center.

Supplier stability receives similar attention. Debtors ask for authority to pay certain prepetition claims of critical vendors, shippers, and lien claimants whose refusal to perform would interrupt supply, and those requests draw objection because payment ahead of a plan departs from ordinary distribution order. Courts examine the evidentiary showing that the vendor is indispensable, that refusal is credible, and that the payment produces a benefit greater than the cost to other creditors. The first day hearing thus performs two functions at once. The hearing grants the operational orders that keep the enterprise alive, and the hearing signals to every party whether the prearranged support behind the plan remains intact under judicial scrutiny.

## Financing a Company Inside a Chapter 11 Case
### Why would a lender lend money to a company that just filed for bankruptcy?
A lender lends because section 364 of the Bankruptcy Code can give new money priority over existing claims, liens on estate property, and court approved protections that ordinary lending cannot offer. A financing order, a defined budget, and milestones reduce risk and make repayment more predictable.

Debtor in possession financing, called DIP financing in practice, fills a gap that filing itself creates. Trade credit tightens, revolving lenders freeze availability under prepetition agreements, and cash on hand may be cash collateral that requires consent or court authority to use. Section 364 supplies a ladder of inducements that a court can approve after notice and a hearing. Under section 364(a) and section 364(b), the debtor may obtain unsecured credit allowable as an administrative expense under section 503(b)(1). Under section 364(c), if credit is unavailable on lesser terms, the court may authorize borrowing with priority over other administrative expenses, with a lien on unencumbered property, or with a junior lien on encumbered property.

Priming sits at the top of that ladder. Section 364(d) permits a lien senior or equal to an existing lien on the same property only if the debtor cannot obtain credit otherwise and the holder of the existing lien receives adequate protection for the resulting diminution in its interest. A priming fight is therefore both an availability inquiry and a valuation inquiry, because adequate protection depends on the cushion between collateral value and the claims secured by the collateral. A prepetition lender facing priming by a third party may choose to provide the DIP loan itself, and a debtor may accept that offer to avoid litigation delay even when the pricing is demanding. The resulting order often includes a stipulation about the validity and extent of prepetition liens, subject to a challenge period for a creditors committee or another party in interest.

Roll-up loans complicate the priority story. In a roll-up, part of a new DIP facility refinances or converts prepetition debt into postpetition debt with administrative priority and lien protections, so old exposure gains the status of new money without an equivalent amount of fresh cash entering the estate. Supporters argue that a roll-up is the price of a facility that a lender would not provide on a clean basis, and that the total package keeps the business funded. Objectors argue that a roll-up elevates prepetition claims ahead of other creditors before a plan allocates value, and that cross-collateralization, where estate property secures prepetition debt, crosses a further line. Courts scrutinize the proportion between new money and rolled debt, the benefit to the estate, and whether the structure effectively decides plan issues at the financing stage.

Budgets and milestones translate lender protection into case governance. A DIP credit agreement typically attaches a rolling budget, often covering 13 weeks, with permitted variances for receipts and disbursements, minimum liquidity covenants, and reporting duties. Case milestones set dates for filing a plan, obtaining disclosure statement approval, completing a sale process, or reaching an effective date, and a missed milestone can trigger default, termination of the right to use cash, or relief from the stay. Those terms give new money discipline and an exit, and those terms also narrow the space for competing plans or extended negotiation. In a prepackaged case the DIP budget and the restructuring support agreement usually align, so financing does not merely fund operations. Financing enforces the timetable on which the prearranged bargain must close or fail.

## The Stay and the Claims Register

Section 362(a) of the Bankruptcy Code imposes the automatic stay the moment a petition is filed, and the stay reaches a wide field of conduct. The stay halts the commencement or continuation of judicial and administrative actions against the debtor, enforcement of judgments, acts to obtain possession of estate property, acts to create, perfect, or enforce liens, and acts to collect a prepetition claim. Section 362(c) governs duration, and section 362(k) provides damages for a willful violation that injures an individual, while corporate debtors pursue sanctions through contempt and section 105. The breadth is deliberate. A single collective proceeding replaces a race among creditors, preserves the assets that support a plan, and gives the debtor room to test whether the prearranged bargain holds when every claimant is brought into one forum.

Relief from the stay returns individual disputes to a separate track when the statutory grounds are met. Section 362(d)(1) permits relief for cause, including lack of adequate protection of an interest in property. Section 362(d)(2) permits relief when the debtor has no equity in property and the property is not necessary to an effective reorganization. Section 362(d)(3) and section 362(d)(4) address single asset real estate and schemes to delay or hinder creditors through multiple filings or transfers. The moving party bears the burden on equity under section 362(g), while the party opposing relief bears the burden on other issues. In a short prepackaged case, stay relief motions often test the sincerity of the timetable. A secured creditor that believes valuation leaves no equity and no realistic prospect of confirmation presses for relief, and the debtor answers with the support agreement, the DIP budget, and a confirmation schedule measured in weeks rather than years.

Bar dates convert the universe of possible liability into a defined claims pool. Under Federal Rule of Bankruptcy Procedure 3003(c), the court in a Chapter 11 case sets a date by which proofs of claim must be filed, and notice of that date goes to creditors listed on the schedules and to known claimants. A claim filed after the bar date faces disallowance unless an exception or an extension for excusable neglect applies, and the standard from Pioneer Investment Services Company v. Brunswick Associates Limited Partnership (1993) guides that neglect inquiry.

The claims register, maintained under Rule 5005 and Rule 3003, is the record against which a plan is built. Each claim receives a number, a claimant name, and an asserted amount and priority, and the register separates filed claims from scheduled claims that section 1111(a) treats as filed when the schedules list a debt as not contingent, not unliquidated, and not disputed. Reconciliation compares the register with the schedules, contracts, and books, and objections under section 502(b) address grounds such as unenforceability, unmatured interest, and claims exceeding statutory caps for certain lease and employment damages. Disputed, contingent, and unliquidated claims pose a distinct problem for voting and distribution, because a plan cannot wait for every contingency to resolve. Section 502(c) authorizes estimation of a contingent or unliquidated claim when liquidation outside the case would unduly delay administration, and section 1126(a) and Rule 3018 permit temporary allowance for voting. Estimation does not decide ultimate liability in every instance, and a reserve may hold distributions until the underlying claim is liquidated, but estimation allows confirmation to proceed on a claims pool that is workable rather than perfect.

## Valuation, the Number the Whole Fight Is About

Enterprise value decides who receives value under a plan and who receives nothing, and the class at the boundary between value and no value is called the fulcrum security. Senior classes claim that value stops below their claims, so junior classes are out of the money and the senior classes should receive the reorganized equity. Junior classes claim that value extends through their position, so a plan that cancels their interests while senior creditors take ownership transfers wealth upward. Section 1129(b)(2)(B) gives that dispute legal force through the absolute priority rule, and section 1125 and section 1129(a)(7) add disclosure and liquidation comparisons that also depend on value. In a prepackaged case the parties often agree on a valuation range inside a restructuring support agreement, and confirmation tests whether that agreed range can survive cross-examination, a market check, and objection from a holder outside the agreement.

Discounted cash flow analysis is the most contested method in large Chapter 11 cases. An expert projects future cash flows for the reorganized business, selects a discount rate that reflects the cost of capital and the risk of the projections, and adds a terminal value for the period beyond the forecast. Small changes in revenue growth, margins, capital expenditure, or the discount rate move the result by hundreds of millions of dollars, so the hearing becomes a contest over assumptions rather than arithmetic. The method suits a business with a continuing operation and a forecast record, and the method weakens when projections were built for negotiation rather than operations.

Comparable companies and comparable transactions supply a market based cross-check. An expert selects public companies or completed acquisitions with similar products, growth, margins, and risk, applies multiples of revenue or earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization to the debtor, and adjusts for size, liquidity, and control. Selection drives the outcome. A set built from high growth firms lifts value, and a set built from distressed or slow growth firms depresses value, so opposing experts attack inclusion and exclusion choices line by line. A market test offers a different kind of proof. A sale process, a rights offering backstopped by creditors, a third party bid, or trading prices for claims and bonds can reveal what buyers will pay rather than what a model predicts. No single indication controls every case, and courts commonly weigh several methods, discount the weakest evidence, and adopt a value within the range that the record supports.

Edward Altman and the measurement of distress belong to the longer evidence tradition behind that courtroom work. In a 1968 paper in the Journal of Finance, Altman built the Z-score, a discriminant model that combined five financial ratios covering working capital, retained earnings, operating earnings, equity value relative to liabilities, and sales relative to assets into a single predictor of corporate bankruptcy. Altman showed that distress could be measured from observable financial data, tested against outcomes, and debated on method rather than impression. Valuation experts in a confirmation hearing work in that tradition even when the tools differ. Each side must state inputs, show the source of each input, expose sensitivity to change, and accept that a number presented without a testable method carries little weight against a number that a court can reproduce and stress.

## How a Plan Sorts Claims Into Classes

Section 1122(a) of the Bankruptcy Code provides that a plan may place a claim or interest in a particular class only if that claim or interest is substantially similar to the other claims or interests in the class. The statute sets a condition for grouping, and the statute does not state in plain terms that every substantially similar claim must share a class with every other such claim. That gap produces the central classification fight in contested cases. A plan proponent seeks separate classes that produce an accepting impaired class, because section 1129(a)(10) requires at least one impaired class to accept when confirmation is contested, disregarding insider acceptances.

Substantial similarity turns on the nature of the claim against the estate rather than on the identity or motive of the holder. Secured claims differ by collateral and by priority in that collateral, so two secured creditors with liens on different assets, or with first and second liens on the same asset, ordinarily sit in separate classes without controversy. Unsecured deficiency claims arising after collateral is valued under section 506(a) raise a harder question, because a deficiency claim resembles general unsecured debt in economic character while arising from a secured relationship. Pension claims, bond debt, trade payables, and litigation claims may all be unsecured, and a plan may still propose separate classes based on differing legal rights, differing treatment that reflects a real distinction, or differing relationships to the reorganized business.

Gerrymandering is the accusation that follows aggressive classification. The label comes from election law, and in bankruptcy the charge is that class lines were drawn to isolate likely opponents or to concentrate likely supporters. The United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit in In re Greystone III Joint Venture (1991) rejected a plan structure that placed a large deficiency claim in a class apart from trade claims in a setting that suggested vote manipulation, and that decision remains a reference point for the limits of separate classification. Other courts take a more permissive approach and allow separate classification when a rational business justification exists even if a voting benefit also exists. The tension is structural. Section 1122 grants flexibility, section 1129(a)(3) imposes good faith on the plan as a whole, and section 1129(a)(10) creates a strong incentive to engineer one accepting impaired class. Classification doctrine mediates among those provisions without eliminating the incentive.

Impairment under section 1124 decides whether a class votes at all in the ordinary course and whether acceptance must be earned. A class is impaired unless the plan leaves rights unaltered, cures defaults other than certain ipso facto defaults, reinstates maturity, and compensates the holder for damages from reasonable reliance, or unless the plan provides cash equal to the allowed amount on the effective date for a claim, or the allowed amount for an interest under the formulation the statute sets. A plan may deliberately impair a class that could have been left unimpaired, for example by altering payment timing by a small amount, and that choice, sometimes called artificial impairment, can satisfy section 1129(a)(10) if the impairment is real and the class accepts. Opponents argue that a trivial alteration staged for a vote should not unlock cramdown against another class. The classification decision and the impairment decision therefore operate together. Together the two choices determine whose consent counts, whose consent is presumed, and whose rejection the court may override.

## Cramdown, Where a Rejecting Class Is Bound Anyway
### Can a bankruptcy court confirm a plan that a class of creditors rejected?
Yes. Section 1129(b) of the Bankruptcy Code permits confirmation over the rejection of an impaired class if the plan satisfies every other requirement in section 1129(a), does not discriminate unfairly, and is fair and equitable toward the rejecting class in a contested case.

Cramdown supplies the coercive power that makes the pre-filing bargain credible. A restructuring support agreement binds signatories by contract, and section 1129(b) binds nonsignatories and rejecting classes by law when the statutory tests are met. With that power, the negotiation changes character. Each side bargains in the shadow of a predicted cramdown outcome, and a settlement inside that predicted range saves the cost and risk of a contested hearing. The doctrine therefore shapes plans that never require a cramdown ruling, because the ruling that a court would likely enter defines the fallback against which concessions are measured.

Fair and equitable has a defined meaning that differs by the kind of class rejecting. For a secured class, section 1129(b)(2)(A) offers three routes, including retention of liens with deferred cash payments whose present value equals the allowed secured claim, a sale of collateral free and clear with the lien attaching to proceeds and with credit bidding protection under section 363(k), or provision of the indubitable equivalent of the claim. For an unsecured class, section 1129(b)(2)(B) requires payment in full in present value terms or a bar on any junior class receiving or retaining property under the plan. For a class of interests, section 1129(b)(2)(C) applies a parallel structure. Unfair discrimination is a separate test, and a plan may satisfy fair and equitable treatment for each class while still discriminating unfairly between classes of equal rank through materially different treatment without a reasonable basis.

The absolute priority rule gives the unsecured and interest tests historical depth. The rule traces to Northern Pacific Railway Company v. Boyd (1913), a receivership case in which the Supreme Court of the United States protected a general creditor against a reorganization that preserved value for shareholders while excluding that creditor from participation. The lineage matters because the rule began as a response to insider arrangements that froze out creditors, and cramdown litigation still carries that suspicion toward plans in which old equity or junior holders keep value while a senior class takes a loss.

New value is the contested exception argued against that bar. In Case v. Los Angeles Lumber Products (1940), the Supreme Court described, without squarely adopting as a holding on the facts before the Court, the possibility that former equity holders might retain an interest under a plan by contributing new capital that was necessary, substantial, and reasonably equivalent to the interest retained. Under the Code, courts divide over whether that possibility survives and over the conditions that apply when old equity seeks to buy back into the reorganized firm. A related fight concerns exclusivity and market exposure, sharpened in Bank of America National Trust and Savings Association v. 203 North LaSalle Street Partnership (1999), where the Supreme Court held that old equity could not retain ownership through an exclusive opportunity to contribute new capital without market testing. The lesson for a prepackaged plan is practical. A new value contribution written into a support agreement without a market check invites objection from the rejecting class, and a contribution exposed to competing bids stands on firmer ground when the court reaches the fair and equitable inquiry.

## The Confirmation Hearing as an Audit Rather Than a Negotiation

The confirmation hearing in a prepackaged case resembles an audit because the bargaining that shapes the plan has finished before the petition, and the judge tests the finished product against a statutory checklist rather than mediating a fresh compromise. Section 1129(a) of the Bankruptcy Code supplies that checklist, and the plan proponent bears the burden of proof on each element by a preponderance of the evidence. Failure on a single mandatory element blocks confirmation even when every voting class has accepted, a limit that preserves judicial control over a process otherwise driven by consent. Counsel therefore presents the disclosure statement record, the tabulation report from the solicitation agent, and valuation testimony as exhibits in sequence, and the hearing proceeds element by element instead of drifting into open ended negotiation.

The best interests of creditors test in section 1129(a)(7) asks whether each holder of an impaired claim or interest in a nonaccepting position receives or retains property worth at least as much as that holder would receive in a Chapter 7 liquidation on the effective date. The comparison depends on a liquidation analysis, often prepared by a financial adviser such as Houlihan Lokey or Evercore, that values assets under a forced sale assumption and subtracts Chapter 7 trustee costs and priority claims. A creditor who voted yes can still benefit from the protection because the test operates holder by holder, not class by class, and a plan that leaves a dissenting bondholder worse off than liquidation fails regardless of the overall vote margin. Courts test discount rates, wind down costs, and the treatment of causes of action behind the analysis, because a generous liquidation value can make a thin plan appear generous by comparison.

Feasibility under section 1129(a)(11) requires a finding that confirmation does not make liquidation or further financial restructuring likely, and the inquiry turns the business plan into evidence. Projections for revenue, margins, capital spending, and debt service on exit financing face cross examination, and judges in the Southern District of New York and the District of Delaware regularly ask how sensitive the forecast remains to a downturn in the debtor core markets. Good faith under section 1129(a)(3) requires that the plan was proposed in good faith and not by means forbidden by law, a standard courts apply to the totality of the circumstances, including whether the solicitation misled voters or favored insiders without disclosure. Compliance provisions complete the audit: section 1129(a)(1) and (a)(2) require that the plan and the proponent comply with the Code, including the disclosure and solicitation rules in sections 1125 and 1126, so a defect in the prepetition vote infects confirmation itself.

A judge retains real power to refuse. Confirmation can be denied for an improper classification scheme that gerrymanders a friendly impaired class under section 1122, for a cramdown that violates the absolute priority rule under section 1129(b), or for projections that rest on hope rather than documented contracts and order books. The court can also refuse to approve a third party release that exceeds the authority recognized in Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P., decided by the Supreme Court in 2024, which barred nonconsensual releases of nondebtors in the circumstances presented there. Denial rarely ends in immediate dismissal; more often the proponent seeks an adjournment, cures a disclosure defect, or adjusts treatment at the margin, but consent narrows judicial discretion without removing it.

## Emergence Day and the New Balance Sheet

Emergence begins on the effective date, the date the plan declares that its conditions have closed and its exchanges become binding on every holder, including holders who never voted and holders who voted no. Conditions in a standard plan include entry of the confirmation order, expiration or waiver of any stay of that order, execution of exit financing documents, and receipt of regulatory approvals where an industry regulator must consent to a change of control. Once the conditions close, the revesting provision returns property of the estate to the reorganized debtor free of claims discharged under section 1141, old contracts that were assumed remain in force, and rejected contracts convert into prepetition damage claims paid at the plan rate. The calendar effect concentrates months of legal consequence into a single closing checklist administered by counsel and the claims agent, and public companies then file a Form 8-K with the Securities and Exchange Commission reporting the capital change and the material terms of the new instruments.

### What happens to shares in a company after a prepackaged bankruptcy?

Old shares are cancelled on the effective date and cease trading, holders receive only the treatment the plan assigns to existing interests, often nothing or a small share of new equity or warrants, and new shares are issued to creditors exchanging debt, creating a different owner base under a reset charter and bylaws.

The new balance sheet records the exchange in accounting terms under Accounting Standards Codification 852, which governs reorganizations and fresh start reporting when its conditions are met. Debt that was impaired is reduced to the reorganized principal, equity held by former creditors replaces the cancelled stock, and assets may be restated to reorganization value based on the valuation accepted at confirmation. Exit financing, often a revolving facility from a bank group led by JPMorgan Chase or Bank of America and a term loan placed with institutional lenders, funds on the effective date and supplies working capital for the first operating cycle outside court supervision. Interest on the old instruments stops accruing as a bankruptcy claim at the petition date for unsecured debt, and the new instruments set fresh maturities, covenants, and pricing that the feasibility finding already tested against projected cash flow.

Governance resets with the capital. A new board takes office under the plan supplement, frequently mixing directors designated by the largest new equity holders, such as an Elliott Investment Management or Apollo Global Management position converted from bonds, with independent directors recruited for industry experience. The chief executive may remain, particularly when management supported the prepetition deal, or a chief restructuring officer from a firm such as Alvarez and Marsal may hand control to a successor selected during the case. Charter provisions on staggered terms, takeover defenses, and shareholder voting thresholds change at the same moment, so control after emergence reflects the creditor bargain rather than the shareholder register that existed before filing. For trade creditors and employees, the practical signal is simpler: the automatic stay dissolves, ordinary payment terms resume under the reorganized company name, and the docket closes except for claims objections and fee applications that trail behind the effective date for weeks.

## What Employees, Customers and Suppliers Experience

Operations continue through a prepackaged case because the automatic stay in section 362 halts collection and termination efforts while wages, rent, and supply deliveries proceed on a timetable the short case is designed to protect. Employees generally report to the same supervisors, work the same shifts, and sell or make the same products, and the brevity of the court phase reduces the period in which a competitor can recruit staff by pointing to bankruptcy headlines. First day motions filed with the petition ask the court to authorize payment of prepetition wages, salaries, and benefits within the priority cap in section 507(a)(4), and to honor vacation, sick leave, and health coverage so a paycheck gap never opens. Judges grant wage motions on an interim basis at the first hearing, often within forty eight hours, because the Code treats earned wages as priority claims and because a workforce that stops being paid stops operating the business whose value the plan depends on preserving.

Customers experience the filing through notice letters, call center scripts, and the practical question whether orders will ship and warranties will be honored. A consumer brand or an industrial supplier that depends on long production runs must explain that assumed contracts remain enforceable and that deposits and service obligations continue under the reorganized company, and sales teams carry that explanation while the docket remains open. Suppliers face a sharper distinction. A vendor deemed critical may receive payment of a prepetition balance under a critical vendor motion, a practice courts approve on a showing that the vendor would refuse shipment without payment and that the refusal would harm the estate more than the payment harms other creditors. Vendors outside that order wait for plan treatment, deliver on cash terms in the interim, and weigh the risk that a short case still leaves an unsecured balance impaired.

Contract assumption and rejection under section 365 determines much of that experience. An executory contract or unexpired lease that the debtor assumes must generally cure defaults, compensate for pecuniary loss, and provide adequate assurance of future performance, and the counterparty can object if the reorganized company lacks the capacity to perform. Rejection constitutes a breach deemed to occur before the petition, leaving a damage claim rather than a right to force performance, and landlords receive a capped claim for rejected real property leases under section 502(b)(6). In retail and restaurant cases the choice among store leases drives the footprint after emergence, while in manufacturing cases supply and licensing agreements receive closer protection because rejection would break the production chain the plan assumes.

Pension obligations add a separate layer handled in general statutory terms. A single employer defined benefit plan insured by the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation does not vanish at filing; the sponsor remains responsible for funding obligations unless the plan terminates under the standards in Title IV of the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974, and the agency becomes trustee in a distress or involuntary termination and pays guaranteed benefits subject to statutory limits. Multiemployer plan withdrawal liability, retiree health obligations under section 1114, and collective bargaining agreements under section 1113 each follow distinct procedures with court findings and negotiation steps, so a prepackaged timetable must accommodate any such issue before solicitation rather than discover it after votes are cast. The employee experience therefore depends less on the label prepackaged than on whether the prepetition bargain priced labor and benefit commitments honestly.

## The Costs That Speed Is Supposed to Cut

Speed is supposed to cut costs that grow with calendar time inside Chapter 11, and the largest category is professional fees paid by the estate under sections 327 through 331. Debtors counsel, creditors committee counsel, financial advisers, investment bankers, and claims agents bill monthly, file interim fee applications, and face review by the United States Trustee, and a case that lasts eighteen months multiplies each retainer across eighteen billing cycles. A prepackaged case compresses that exposure into document preparation before filing and a short confirmation sprint after filing, so the fee total reflects negotiation intensity rather than docket duration. The saving is relative rather than absolute: restructuring practices at firms such as Kirkland and Ellis or Weil, Gotshal and Manges command premium rates, and parallel teams for the company, an ad hoc bondholder group, and a sponsor can each bill seven figures before the petition is filed, shifting expense earlier instead of eliminating the expense.

United States Trustee quarterly fees form a second time based charge. Under 28 U.S.C. section 1930(a)(6), as amended by the Bankruptcy Administration Improvement Act of 2020, fees scale with disbursements during each calendar quarter the case remains open, so every additional quarter adds a payment that produces no value for creditors beyond administration of the system itself. Management attention carries a cost no invoice captures. Executives in a long case divide days among operations, deposition preparation, contested hearings, and lender reporting, and boards meet on litigation strategy instead of pricing, hiring, or capital allocation. A forty day case returns that attention sooner, and the return matters most in seasonal or contract driven businesses where a missed selling cycle or a lost bid cannot be recovered after emergence at any fee saving.

Customer and supplier attrition compounds quietly during a long case. A customer that needs a five year service commitment may choose a rival with no bankruptcy filing, a supplier may shorten terms from net sixty to cash on delivery, and a skilled employee may accept a stable offer elsewhere, each decision shrinking the revenue base that feasibility projections assumed. Interest and postpetition fee accrual on secured debt adds a contractual meter: an oversecured lender entitled to interest and reasonable fees under section 506(b) watches its claim grow while the case proceeds, reducing the value available for junior classes and tightening the margin for error in the plan. Adequate protection payments, cash collateral orders, and debtor in possession financing charges operate in the same direction, rewarding a timetable that stops the meter earlier.

The pre-filing negotiation itself is not free, and honest comparison counts both sides of the ledger. Solicitation requires a disclosure statement drafted to securities law standards even when the section 1125(a)(1) court approval step is replaced by a showing of adequate information under section 1125(g), proxy solicitors must locate holders through clearing systems such as the Depository Trust Company, and lockup negotiations among funds holding bonds in different tranches consume weeks of partner time. Consent fees, early bird premiums for holders who sign a restructuring support agreement promptly, and backstop fees for commitments to fund a rights offering all price the speed that the short court phase later displays. The economic claim for the prepackaged route is therefore narrow and testable: weeks in court cost less than months in court for the same bargain, provided the bargain holds, the solicitation was valid, and attrition during negotiation did not already spend the saving before the petition reached the clerk.

## Worked Example One, CIT Group and the Forty Day Case

CIT Group supplies the clearest large company illustration because the scale was substantial, the timetable was measured in weeks, and the loss speed could not prevent remained visible after emergence. CIT, a commercial finance company that lent to middle market businesses and factored receivables for retailers and manufacturers, entered the financial crisis holding debt that capital markets would no longer refinance on workable terms. Management first pursued a voluntary exchange offer outside court, seeking maturity extensions and principal reductions by consent, and the offer failed to reach the thresholds needed to stabilize the balance sheet without a filing. The company then solicited votes on a prepackaged plan from bondholders before filing, and bondholders accepted the plan in the prepetition solicitation, converting a contested recapitalization into a confirmation case with the central exchange already approved by the impaired classes whose debt would change.

CIT Group Inc. filed on November 1, 2009 in the Southern District of New York, and the case moved through disclosure, confirmation, and the effective date on a schedule built around the vote already cast. The court confirmed the plan after testing the solicitation, the classification of bondholder claims, and the feasibility of a finance company whose funding model still depended on market access, and CIT emerged on December 10, 2009 with about $10.5 billion of debt reduced. The interval from November 1, 2009 to December 10, 2009 covered first day relief, claims procedures, and confirmation without a prolonged fight over the exchange ratio, because that ratio had been the subject of the exchange offer and the solicitation that preceded the petition. Common stock held before filing was cancelled, bondholders received new notes and new common equity under the plan, and the reorganized company continued lending operations under a smaller and longer dated liability structure.

What was settled before filing explains what the court could finish quickly. The principal amount of reduction, the mix of new debt and equity, the treatment of operating subsidiaries that did not file, and the governance of the reorganized parent were negotiated with bondholder representatives and locked into plan documents before the petition, leaving the judge to audit disclosure, vote validity, and feasibility rather than construct a capital structure from competing proposals. What the court did in about forty days still mattered: the automatic stay stopped a disorderly collection race, section 1141 discharged claims within the plan terms and bound holdouts in an accepting class structure, and the confirmation order gave counterparties a single enforceable document instead of thousands of individual exchange consents a private transaction could not compel from nonparticipating holders.

What speed could not protect was equally concrete. The United States government investment through the Troubled Asset Relief Program, under which the Treasury Department had purchased $2,330,000,000 in preferred stock and warrants in December 2008, was lost, a result the Treasury acknowledged when the investment was written off. Bondholder acceptance and a fast confirmation could restructure funded debt, but neither device could restore a junior government capital infusion, nor guarantee that a finance company entering weak credit markets would prosper. CIT later faced further strain and was acquired by First Citizens BancShares in a merger that closed on January 3, 2022, a later chapter that does not erase the mechanism lesson of 2009: prepackaging moved the fight before the filing and shortened the stay, while losses already embedded in the capital structure stayed where priority placed them.

## Worked Example Two, Belk, J.Crew and the Sale Alternative

Belk, J.Crew, and Chrysler mark three distinct routes, and the distinctions determine who kept control, who bore losses, and which statute did the binding work. Belk was a prepackaged plan case in the strict sense. The regional department store chain based in Charlotte, North Carolina, owned by the private equity firm Sycamore Partners, filed on February 23, 2021 in the Southern District of Texas after arranging creditor support, and the court confirmed the plan within about a day, in a single compressed sequence. The plan eliminated about $450,000,000 of debt, supplied new financing for operations, and left Sycamore Partners retaining control of the reorganized retailer, an outcome that illustrates the sponsor advantage when lenders accept new terms rather than forcing a change of ownership.

J.Crew was also a plan case built on a restructuring support agreement, though on a less compressed timetable than Belk and with a different ownership result. J.Crew Group, the apparel retailer known for its J.Crew and Madewell brands, filed on May 4, 2020 in the Eastern District of Virginia after lenders and noteholders signed a restructuring support agreement that set the exchange terms before the petition. At emergence in September 2020 the company converted about $1,650,000,000 of debt into equity, lenders took the dominant equity position, and Anchorage Capital Group became the leading shareholder, replacing prior sponsor ownership through TPG Capital and Leonard Green and Partners. The route remained a prearranged plan rather than a one day sprint in the Belk mold, yet the bargain still preceded the case and the court phase ratified an allocation the support agreement had already priced.

Chrysler took the contrasting route and was not a prepackaged plan at all. Chrysler LLC filed on April 30, 2009 in the Southern District of New York, and the central transaction was a sale under section 363 of the Bankruptcy Code to a new company, often called New Chrysler and later Chrysler Group LLC, sponsored by Fiat, rather than confirmation of a plan that restructured the old company in place. The sale closed and the new company emerged from the transaction in 42 days, on June 10, 2009, acquiring the operating assets, brands, plants, and assumed liabilities specified in the sale order while unwanted assets and claims remained in the old estate, later called Old Carco LLC, for liquidation. Secured lenders who objected to the allocation of sale value litigated through the sale approval and on appeal, and the structure traded the disclosure, voting, and absolute priority architecture of a plan for the speed of a sale order under section 363(f), which transferred assets free and clear of interests within the terms the court approved.

The comparison states the trade plainly. A prepackaged plan such as Belk preserves the corporate shell, discharges claims through confirmation, and can preserve sponsor control when creditors consent. A support agreement plan such as J.Crew trades more time for a broader creditor coalition and a transfer of ownership to converting lenders. A section 363 sale such as Chrysler discards the old shell, moves selected assets to a buyer, and leaves objectors to challenge value and good faith in a sale hearing rather than vote on a plan, gaining asset separation while surrendering plan protections that voting and cramdown standards supply. Speed appears in each route, but the source of speed differs: consent collected early, consent organized by contract, or a buyer ready to close.

## When the Prearranged Bargain Comes Apart

A prearranged bargain comes apart when an assumption built into the solicitation or the support agreement fails before confirmation, and the failure modes follow the points where consent, value, or time was stretched thinnest. A defective solicitation sits first on the list. If the disclosure sent to voters lacked material information, targeted the wrong record holders through the Depository Trust Company chain, or counted votes from holders who acquired claims after the record date without proper transfer documentation, opposing parties can ask the court to designate and disregard votes under section 1126(e) for procurement not in good faith, or to deny confirmation for noncompliance with section 1125. Courts have discarded votes and delayed cases where solicitation materials presented projections the debtor own records contradicted, and a solicitation begun before filing must still satisfy the adequate information standard that section 1125(g) preserves for prepetition voting. A plan that loses its accepting impaired class after votes are thrown out faces cramdown litigation the timetable never budgeted for, turning a sprint into the contested case the structure was built to avoid.

Holdout classes create a second fracture. A restructuring support agreement binds signatories contractually, through lockup and transfer restrictions, but non signatories retain statutory rights, and a class that rejects by the two thirds in amount and majority in number thresholds in section 1126(c) forces the proponent toward cramdown under section 1129(b), with valuation and absolute priority disputes that require expert testimony and briefing. Deterioration between signing and filing creates a third fracture that no vote can cure. A retailer that signs in January on holiday sales and files in March after a supplier failure or a demand collapse may present projections that no witness can defend under the feasibility standard, and counterparties argue the plan no longer matches the company in the disclosure statement. Material adverse change clauses and milestones then matter as drafting, not decoration, because each side measures whether the exit right has triggered before sunk costs compel a filing on stale terms.

Fiduciary out clauses supply a deliberate exit rather than a defect. A board that signs a support agreement retains duties to the estate and to creditors as a whole, and a properly drafted agreement permits termination when a superior alternative appears, when disclosure proves inaccurate, or when continued support would breach fiduciary duties. Exercise of the out converts the prearranged case into a free fall case, a filing without a locked plan in which the automatic stay still protects the debtor but every major term returns to negotiation under court deadlines such as the exclusivity periods in section 1121. Conversion can run further, to Chapter 7 liquidation under section 1112 when no plan can be confirmed and the business cannot support administrative costs, or to a section 363 sale assembled in haste from the remains of the failed plan coalition.

The choice between a private workout and court protection received systematic treatment from Stuart Gilson, with Kose John and Larry H. P. Lang, in a 1990 study in the Journal of Financial Economics of troubled debt restructurings. Gilson, John, and Lang documented that the two paths attracted measurably different situations: private workouts succeeded more often when debt was held by fewer creditors and concentrated in bank loans a small group could renegotiate, while firms with widely dispersed public bonds, many creditor classes, and severe disputes over value or priority turned to court because holdouts could block voluntary exchanges requiring near unanimous consent for core terms. The presence of a large secured lender able to coordinate and the depth of financial distress also predicted the route and the outcome. A prepackaged filing attempts to capture the coordination benefit of court compulsion while keeping negotiation privacy, and failure returns the company to whichever force, dispersion, disagreement, or deterioration, made privacy insufficient in the first place.

## Five Routes Through Distress, Set Side by Side

Five routes answer the same underlying problem, a capital structure that the business underneath it can no longer carry, and they differ mainly in where the hard bargaining happens and how much of the enterprise survives the process. The table that follows settles the comparison at the level that matters most for planning, which route moves the bargain where, and what each route risks in exchange for the position it chooses. A workout moves the bargain entirely into private negotiation and risks nothing in court because no court is involved. A prepackaged case moves the bargain to before the filing and risks the validity of the votes that carry it. A prearranged case splits the difference, fixing a framework early and leaving the vote for later. A free fall case leaves the bargain inside the courtroom, where every party can watch it and every month of watching costs money. A liquidation abandons the bargain altogether and converts the question from who owns the future business into who gets paid from its pieces.

| Route | Where the bargain is struck | Who votes | Time under court supervision | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Out of court workout | Private negotiation | Every creditor whose consent is needed | None | One holdout can block the whole exchange |
| Prepackaged Chapter 11 | Private negotiation before filing | Creditor classes before filing | Weeks | A defective solicitation can void the votes |
| Prearranged Chapter 11 | Framework agreed before filing | Creditor classes after filing | Months | Terms drift as the case exposes new facts |
| Free fall Chapter 11 | Inside the court case | Creditor classes after filing | Many months | Value burns while the parties negotiate |
| Chapter 7 liquidation | No bargain, a trustee sells assets | No plan vote | Until assets are sold | The business ends and jobs go with it |

The prearranged row rewards a closer look because it occupies the ground that boards most often choose when a full prepackaged solicitation is not practical. Under a prearranged structure, the debtor and its principal creditors sign a restructuring support agreement that records the framework, the debt to be exchanged, the new money to be supplied, and the governance of the company after exit, while leaving the formal vote to a solicitation run inside the case under court supervision. That sequencing buys flexibility. Facts that surface after filing, a contract lost, a valuation revised, a tax position clarified, can be folded into the plan without unwinding votes already cast under a disclosure statement written before those facts were known. The price of that flexibility is time and exposure. Months under supervision mean months of professional fees, months of supplier anxiety, and months in which a creditor who dislikes the framework can organize against it with the standing that only a pending case confers. The prepackaged route trades that flexibility away for speed, and the prearranged route trades speed away for the ability to revise, and neither trade is free.

## Alternatives a Board Weighs Before Any Filing

A board that sees distress coming has more choices than the choice between filing and not filing, and the quality of the eventual case often depends on how seriously those choices were tested while time remained. The oldest alternative is the out of court exchange offer, in which the company asks bondholders to swap existing notes for new notes with a longer maturity, a lower coupon, or a partial equity component. For privately held bank debt, consent thresholds in the credit agreement may allow such an exchange with a majority or a supermajority. For widely held bonds, section 316(b) of the Trust Indenture Act of 1939 creates the holdout problem that shapes everything else, because the statute protects each holder's right to receive payment of principal and interest on the stated dates, and that right cannot be impaired without that holder's own consent. A minority can therefore refuse the exchange, keep the old claim intact, and wait to be paid on terms the majority accepted changing, which is why exchange offers so often pair the new notes with exit consents that strip protective covenants from the old notes left behind.

A second alternative is a sale of the company as a going concern before any filing, either to a strategic buyer that values the operations inside its own business or to a financial buyer willing to fund the purchase with new debt sized to the earnings the business can support. A sale solves the capital structure by replacing it, and it can preserve employment and customer relationships that a public filing would strain. Its weakness is price discovery under pressure, since buyers discount for the seller's visible urgency, and a sale that transfers value away from creditors while insolvency looms invites later challenge. A merger works on similar logic, combining the distressed company with a stronger partner so that the combined balance sheet carries what neither the distressed company nor its creditors could fix alone, a pattern examined in accounts of [why media companies merge](/2026/10/05/why-media-companies-merge/), where scale and library value supplied the rationale that standalone finances could not.

A third route is an amend and extend negotiation with existing lenders, in which maturities are pushed outward, covenants are reset, and pricing rises in exchange for time. That route preserves the existing ownership and avoids any transfer of control, but it only helps when the problem is timing rather than solvency. A company whose debt exceeds the value of the business at any maturity gains little from a longer runway. The final alternative is doing nothing, allowing maturities to arrive and defaults to occur while the board hopes for an operating recovery or a market turn. Directors of a solvent company owe their duties to the company and its shareholders, and as insolvency approaches the interests of creditors move closer to the center of the analysis under the law of the state of incorporation, with the precise duties depending on that law and on the company's actual financial condition. Boards that document their deliberations, take advice from independent counsel and bankers, and test each alternative against a realistic valuation give any later reviewer, court or creditor, a record that the choice made was a considered one rather than a default reached by drift.

## The Case Against Speed, Given Its Best Form

The strongest case against the prepackaged form is not that it fails on its own terms, because by its own terms it usually succeeds, with confirmation arriving quickly and the business exiting with the agreed structure in place. The strongest case is that success arrives by deciding the contested questions at the moment when the fewest affected parties can contest them. Lynn LoPucki of the University of California, Los Angeles, writing in Courting Failure (2005), argued that competition among courts and among professionals for large cases produced a system in which the parties who arrange a case, management, the senior lenders, and their advisers, shape the process to entrench the arrangers, selecting venues, timetables, and structures that reward the insiders who designed them and leave little room for anyone outside the design to change the outcome. Read at full strength, the argument is not that any single deal was corrupt, but that a market for cases disciplines courts toward the preferences of repeat arrangers, and that discipline operates quietly, through what gets proposed, funded, and approved without a fight.

The committee system supplies a concrete version of the same objection. An official committee of unsecured creditors is appointed after filing, staffed and funded inside the case, and charged with representing holders who negotiated nothing before the petition date. In a prepackaged case, that committee inherits a valuation that was locked before its appointment, embedded in a plan that supporting creditors have already voted to accept. The committee can object, hire valuation experts, and litigate, but it litigates uphill against a record built by parties who had months of access to the company's projections and advisers. Junior claimants face the finished deal in an even weaker position. Their recovery, if any, was sized in a negotiation where their class may have had no representative at all, and the disclosure that explains the bargain reached them compressed into a solicitation package designed to obtain a yes vote from impaired classes before filing, a document they had no seat in designing and limited time to test once the case began.

Getty Images filed a prepackaged case in the District of Delaware on October 21, 2026, with up to $533 million of new equity committed by affiliates of the Getty family and Koch Equity Development, as Reuters reported; the filing stands here only as an instance of the form being used at scale, with no comment on its merits.

Where does the thesis of this account survive that critique, and where must it be narrowed? It survives on the question of binding power. Only a confirmed plan can bind a dissenting creditor to an exchange, discharge old claims, and vest assets free of successor disputes, so moving the bargain earlier does not remove the court from the system; it changes the court's work from hosting the negotiation to auditing its fairness, disclosure, and vote. The thesis must be narrowed on the question of whose audit it is. An audit conducted at speed, on a record the arrangers assembled, with objectors appointed after the terms hardened, is a thinner check than the phrase suggests. The honest formulation is therefore conditional. A prepackaged case earns its speed when disclosure was full, solicitation was clean, valuation was tested by someone with no stake in the answer, and the court treats confirmation as an examination rather than a ceremony. Absent those conditions, speed is not efficiency purchased by preparation; it is scrutiny surrendered by design.

## How Other Systems Run the Same Bargain

Other legal systems face the same arithmetic, an enterprise worth more alive than in pieces and a creditor body too divided to agree unaided, and they distribute the bargaining power differently. The United Kingdom offers the restructuring plan under Part 26A of the Companies Act 2006, introduced by the Corporate Insolvency and Governance Act 2020. A company proposes a plan to creditors or members, class meetings vote, and the court may sanction the plan where approval reaches 75 percent by value in a class. The distinctive feature is cross class cramdown, under which the court can sanction a plan despite a dissenting class where that class would be no worse off than in the relevant alternative, usually administration or liquidation, and where at least one class with a genuine economic interest in that alternative approved the plan. The process runs through court hearings on class composition and fairness, with valuation evidence contested in open proceedings. Notably, the English model includes no pre-filing vote solicitation of the American kind, no mechanism by which votes gathered privately before any filing arrive at the first hearing already counted; the negotiation may precede the application, but the vote belongs to the court supervised stage.

India chose a different allocation again. Under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016, once the National Company Law Tribunal admits an application, control passes away from the board to a resolution professional, and a committee of creditors directs the search for a resolution plan within a statutory timetable, a design described in the [Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016 guide](/2012/04/01/insolvency-bankruptcy-code-2016-guide/). The model is often summarized as creditor in control, in contrast to the American debtor in possession tradition in which management stays in place and proposes the plan. Promoters face restrictions on bidding for their own company in defined circumstances, and the tribunal's role centers on admitting the case, policing the timetable, and approving or rejecting the plan the committee selects.

What each system refuses to copy from the American model is as instructive as what each borrows. The English system refuses the private pre-filing vote and the long exclusivity that can leave a debtor's proposal as the only plan on the table for many months; it keeps the decisive vote and the valuation fight inside hearings that dissenting classes can attend from the start. The Indian system refuses the premise that incumbent management should keep control while creditors wait; it treats loss of control as the price of admission to a collective process and imposes deadlines the American system would regard as rigid. Both systems also work without the breadth of the American discharge and channeling practice in mass claim cases, and both leave secured creditors with leverage shaped by their own property and enforcement law rather than by a single federal code. The comparison clarifies the American bargain by contrast. Prepackaged practice in the United States pushes negotiation as far before the filing as private ordering allows, and then asks the court to supply the one thing private ordering cannot, a binding answer for the creditor who never agreed. England keeps the vote public and supervised. India reallocates control itself. Each system pays for its choice, in speed, in control, or in the range of outcomes a plan can impose.

## Reading a Restructuring Announcement Like an Analyst

A restructuring announcement is written to reassure, and an analyst reads it against the documents that carry legal weight rather than against its adjectives. Three documents matter most. The restructuring support agreement summary shows who has committed to vote for the deal, which creditors signed, what percentage of each class they hold, what conditions let them walk away, and what termination events would release them. A deal supported by holders of two thirds of the funded debt reads differently from a deal supported by a single large holder whose consent others are merely invited to join. The plan term sheet gives the operative economics in compressed form, the classes, the treatment of each class, the new instruments to be issued, and the releases contemplated. The disclosure statement, longer and court tested, supplies the projections, the valuation range, the liquidation analysis, and the risk factors against which the term sheet's numbers can be checked. Reading the three in that order, commitment first, economics second, justification third, prevents the projections from framing a bargain whose support has not yet been verified.

The numbers to find first are few and decisive. Debt reduced states the size of the problem the deal claims to solve, expressed as the difference between obligations entering the process and obligations carried afterward, including any debt reinstated rather than discharged. New money committed states who is paying for the solution, whether the commitment comes as exit financing, a rights offering backstopped by existing holders, or fresh equity from a sponsor, and whether that money is fully committed or subject to conditions and syndication. Ownership of the company after exit states who won. If senior lenders convert debt into most of the new equity, control has moved to the fulcrum class regardless of how the announcement phrases continuity. If existing shareholders retain a large stake while creditors took deep reductions, the valuation supporting that split deserves the closest reading in the file. For readers working through a full set of case papers for the first time, a companion [reading reference](https://vaultbook.net) is available.

Dates predict the timetable more reliably than any statement about momentum. The petition date starts the clocks that matter. A solicitation completed before filing points toward a confirmation hearing measured in weeks, and the announced date of that hearing, once set, is the date around which objections, committee formation, and any competing analysis must organize. A bar date for filing claims, a deadline for objecting to confirmation, and a target effective date each narrow the window in which an uncommitted creditor can still affect the outcome. You can use those dates as a discipline: read the support agreement before the objection deadline rather than after it, compare the valuation in the disclosure statement with the consideration in the term sheet, and treat any gap between the announced timetable and the dates actually entered on the docket as the first measurable sign that the bargain struck in private is meeting resistance in public. Announcements describe intention. Dockets record what survived contact with the process.

## What Emergence Cannot Repair

A confirmed plan repairs a balance sheet. It reduces principal, extends maturities, converts debt into equity, and lowers the interest burden to a level the projected cash flow can carry. None of that repairs the business that sits underneath the balance sheet, and the distance between those two repairs explains why some companies exit a case in strong legal shape and weak commercial shape at the same time. Unit economics decide the second question. A company whose product has lost its market, whose customers moved to a substitute that costs less or performs better, emerges with less debt and the same problem it carried into the filing. Lower interest expense improves the arithmetic of each period without changing what each sale contributes after the cost of making and delivering the product. Where contribution per unit is negative, volume makes the position worse rather than better, and no allocation of claims among creditors changes the sign of that figure.

The comparison that matters after exit is therefore between the interest burden and the operating result before interest. A restructuring that cuts annual interest from a level the business could never reach to a level it can reach in a good year has bought time rather than safety, because a single weak year, a lost contract, a rise in input costs, returns the company to the same negotiation with a creditor body that now holds the equity and remembers the last exchange. Durable repair pairs the financial restructuring with an operating one, closing or selling divisions whose economics cannot be fixed, repricing products, renegotiating supply and lease obligations where the code permits, and sizing the remaining debt to the earnings of the smaller, sounder business rather than to the earnings the larger business once reported. Plans that skip that work tend to protect going concern value on paper while the underlying enterprise continues to shrink toward the point where the paper valuation can no longer be defended.

Practitioners have a shorthand for the outcome, a second filing by a company that restructured the balance sheet without restructuring the business, described generally as a Chapter 22 filing, meaning Chapter 11 used twice by the same enterprise. Some retailers and energy companies have filed twice in that pattern, in general terms, after an initial case reduced debt while store economics or commodity exposure continued to erode the cash flow the plan depended on. The label is informal and carries no legal consequence, but it names a real diagnostic point. A second filing signals that the first proceeding answered the question of who should bear past losses while leaving unanswered the question of how the business earns money after the losses are allocated. Emergence, properly understood, is a legal event with a date and an order. Recovery is an operating condition with neither. Confusing the first for the second is the most common error in reading any restructuring, and the costliest, because capital committed on the strength of a confirmed plan alone is capital committed to a structure whose foundations the plan never inspected.

## The Bargain Moves, the Audit Remains

Strip the procedure to its working parts and a prepackaged case is an answer to a single practical difficulty: hundreds of creditors cannot be bound by a negotiation they did not join, yet waiting to negotiate until all of them can be heard inside a case burns the value the negotiation exists to preserve. The response developed over decades of practice is to move the conversation to the only place it can move, earlier, into private rooms where the holders who matter most can trade recovery against certainty, new money against control, and speed against the concessions that speed demands. What arrives at the courthouse afterward looks less like the start of a dispute than like the record of one already settled, term sheet, votes, valuation, and financing assembled into a single request for approval. That appearance is accurate and incomplete at once. The deal is real, and so is the fact that parties who were absent from the rooms, trade creditors, junior holders, employees with claims, committees appointed after the petition, meet the deal at the point where changing it costs the most and time to study it runs shortest.

The audit that remains is therefore not a formality layered over private ordering; it is the source of the ordering's power. Confirmation testing, disclosure review, classification and vote scrutiny, feasibility, and the protection of a dissenting class against a plan that pays a senior class more than its entitlement while leaving the junior class with nothing, together form the mechanism by which a private agreement acquires public force. Each element exists because the order that follows does something no contract can do. It binds the creditor who never agreed, discharges claims the holder refused to release, and vests the reorganized company with its assets under a judgment that later courts and counterparties will respect. Private negotiation cannot manufacture that effect at any price, which is why sophisticated parties who could settle entirely among themselves still file, still solicit under court tested disclosure when required, and still submit their bargain to objection and hearing.

That dependence gives the structure its final shape. The bargain moves because moving it preserves value, shortens supervision, and lets a business return to ordinary commercial life before suppliers, customers, and employees conclude that the filing is the story of the company rather than an episode in it. The audit remains because the power being exercised, the power to rewrite the rights of a nonconsenting holder by majority vote and judicial order, is a public power that no private agreement can borrow without submitting to public conditions. Speed is the benefit the system advertises. Binding force is the benefit the system actually sells, and speed is worth purchasing only because the binding force waits at the end of a short case as surely as at the end of a long one. Courts remain necessary for the plainest of reasons: only a court can bind the creditor who never agreed, and that binding power is exactly what makes the pre-filing bargain worth striking in the first place.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is a prepackaged bankruptcy in simple terms?

A prepackaged bankruptcy is a Chapter 11 case in which the company and its main creditors agree on a reorganization plan and gather the votes needed to approve it before the case is filed in court. The filing then asks the court to confirm a bargain that already has documented support, rather than opening a negotiation among parties who have not yet agreed on anything. Because the solicitation and voting happened in advance, the period spent under court supervision can be far shorter than in a conventional case, sometimes measured in weeks. The court still reviews disclosure, vote counting, and fairness to classes that did not consent, and confirmation remains a judicial decision rather than a registration of a private deal. Creditors who never signed anything are bound by a confirmed plan in the same way as creditors who negotiated it, which is the central reason the court stage cannot be skipped.

### Q: What is the difference between a prepackaged and a prearranged bankruptcy?

The difference lies in when the vote happens and how complete the agreement is on the day of filing. In a prepackaged case, the plan is fully drafted, disclosed to creditors, and voted on before the petition, so the court receives a finished proposal with ballots already cast. In a prearranged case, the company and key creditors agree on a framework, usually recorded in a restructuring support agreement, but the formal solicitation and vote take place inside the case under court supervision. The prearranged route leaves room to adjust terms as new facts appear during the case, at the cost of a longer stay under supervision and more opportunity for opposition to organize. The prepackaged route maximizes speed and certainty of outcome when it works, while carrying greater risk that a defect in the earlier solicitation will require work to be redone.

### Q: Why do companies negotiate a bankruptcy plan before filing?

Companies negotiate in advance because time inside a court case is expensive in ways that go beyond professional fees. Suppliers tighten terms, customers question whether orders will be honored, employees consider leaving, and lenders charge for financing provided to a borrower under supervision. A plan negotiated before filing shortens that exposed period and reduces the chance that the business loses value while creditors argue over how to divide it. Advance negotiation also lets the parties test whether a deal exists at all; if the holders of the main debt classes will not support any workable structure, the board learns that fact privately and can consider a sale or another route. Finally, a supported plan gives the court a concrete proposal to evaluate from the first hearing, which shapes scheduling, financing, and the expectations of every party watching the docket.

### Q: Does a company keep operating during a prepackaged bankruptcy?

Yes, in the ordinary course the company continues operating throughout a prepackaged case, and continuity of operations is one of the main purposes of arranging the case in advance. Management typically remains in place as debtor in possession, employees are paid under court orders covering wages owed for work before filing, and vendors supply goods and services on terms the court helps stabilize through orders governing critical suppliers and ordinary business payments. Customers usually experience little interruption because the case is short and the plan was designed to preserve the business rather than sell or close it. Operations can still be affected by the attention management must give to court deadlines, by counterparties seeking assurance about contracts, and by publicity surrounding the filing. The legal status of the company changes on the petition date even when daily work does not, since major decisions outside the ordinary course require court approval.

### Q: What is a disclosure statement in a Chapter 11 case?

A disclosure statement is the document a plan proponent provides to creditors and interest holders so they can make an informed judgment about whether to accept or reject a proposed plan. The bankruptcy code requires it to contain adequate information, a standard courts apply in light of the size and complexity of the case and the sophistication of the holders being solicited. In practice the document describes the company's history and business, explains the events that led to the filing, sets out the plan's treatment of each class, presents financial projections, offers a valuation analysis, compares recoveries with what a liquidation would produce, and lists risks that could prevent the plan from performing as projected. In a prepackaged case the solicitation materials used before filing must satisfy the same informational purpose, and the court examines them at confirmation. Holders vote on the plan, but the disclosure statement is the record on which the quality of that vote is judged.

### Q: What does it mean for a claim to be impaired under a plan?

A claim is impaired under a plan when the plan alters the holder's legal, equitable, or contractual rights compared with the terms that governed before the case. Paying a creditor less than the full amount owed, extending the payment date beyond the original maturity, reducing the interest rate, or converting a debt claim into equity all count as impairment, even where the holder expects to recover good value. A class that is not impaired is treated as having accepted the plan without voting, because its rights pass through unchanged. A class that receives nothing and retains no rights is treated as having rejected it. Impairment status therefore determines who votes, whose acceptance counts toward confirmation, and which protective tests the court applies. The concept matters in prepackaged cases because the classes solicited before filing are the impaired classes whose votes the proponent needs.

### Q: What is the role of the United States Trustee in a corporate case?

The United States Trustee is an official within the Department of Justice charged with supervising the administration of bankruptcy cases, distinct from the judge who decides disputes and from any trustee appointed to run a particular estate. In a large corporate case the office appoints the official committee of unsecured creditors, reviews applications to employ and pay professionals, monitors fee requests, watches for fraud and abuse, and may object to plans, disclosure statements, or transactions that conflict with the code. The office also appoints an examiner or a Chapter 11 trustee in the limited circumstances where the court orders such an appointment. This supervisory role continues in a prepackaged case even though the timetable is compressed, and the office's views on solicitation procedures, committee needs, and fees can influence how quickly a short case can responsibly move. The office represents the integrity of the system rather than any single creditor's recovery.

### Q: Why do so many large companies file for bankruptcy in Delaware?

Large companies file in Delaware in significant numbers because many of them are incorporated there, and incorporation in a state gives a company a basis for filing in that state under the venue rules. Over decades, that flow of cases built a specialized bench, a deep body of precedent on the issues large cases present, and a local bar experienced in moving complex matters on tight schedules. Predictability is the practical attraction; counsel can advise a board with reasonable confidence about how a judge will approach first day relief, financing, sale procedures, and confirmation standards. Critics have long argued that concentration of filings in a small number of districts gives debtors and their lenders too much influence over where and how cases proceed, and reform proposals surface periodically. Venue remains proper in other districts connected to a company's operations or affiliates, and some large cases are filed elsewhere.

### Q: What is exit financing and where does it come from?

Exit financing is the borrowing a reorganized company arranges to take effect when it leaves Chapter 11, replacing or refinancing obligations from the case and funding the payments the plan promises at emergence. Sources include revolving credit facilities and term loans from banks and institutional lenders, new notes sold to investors, and in some structures commitments from existing creditors or sponsors who backstop a rights offering. The financing is negotiated alongside the plan because confirmation requires a showing that the company can perform after exit, and lenders will not commit without seeing the final capital structure, projected cash flow, and governance. Terms reflect the risk of lending to a borrower with a recent filing, so pricing and covenants are usually stricter than the company would accept in calmer conditions. A well sized facility gives suppliers and customers evidence that the reorganized business can fund ordinary operations.

### Q: What happens to pensions when an employer goes through Chapter 11?

Treatment of pensions depends on the kind of plan involved and on obligations that arise under pension law separate from the bankruptcy code. A defined contribution arrangement holds assets in trust for participants, and those assets are generally not property of the employer's estate. A defined benefit plan creates a funding obligation for the employer and potential claims involving the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation, the federal insurer that takes over certain terminated plans and pays guaranteed benefits within statutory limits. In a Chapter 11 case the debtor may seek to continue a plan, negotiate changes with unions and the insurer, or pursue termination through the processes pension law provides, each route carrying its own standards and litigation risk. Retiree health benefits follow a separate statutory procedure with a court appointed representative. Outcomes vary widely by case, and participants' rights turn on plan documents, funding history, and guarantees rather than on the plan of reorganization alone.

### Q: Can a confirmed bankruptcy plan be challenged afterwards?

Challenges after confirmation are narrow by design, because the system's value depends on parties being able to rely on a confirmed plan. An order confirming a plan can be appealed, and appellate courts sometimes apply a doctrine that limits review where the plan has already been substantially carried out and unwinding it would harm parties who relied on it. The bankruptcy code also permits revocation of a confirmation order procured by fraud, on request made within a limited period after entry, following notice and a hearing. Separately, a plan may be modified after confirmation only within the limits the code sets and generally before substantial consummation. Errors in vote counting or disclosure are normally litigated before or at confirmation rather than afterward. The practical lesson embedded in those rules is that objections carry their greatest weight during the case, when the court can still condition or deny confirmation.

### Q: What is a section 363 sale and how is it different from a plan?

A section 363 sale is a sale of estate assets outside the ordinary course of business, approved by the court under the section of the code that gives the sale its name. The buyer typically acquires the assets free and clear of many claims and interests, which attach instead to the sale proceeds, and the process runs on bidding procedures with an auction where competing interest exists. The difference from a plan is one of function. A sale transfers assets and produces a fund; it does not by itself restructure the seller's liabilities, discharge claims, or reorganize ownership, which is why sale proceeds often still require a plan or a liquidation process for distribution. Sales can move faster than plans and can preserve a business under new ownership while the old estate winds down. Because a sale of substantially everything can decide the case's outcome early, courts examine whether it is a genuine sale or a plan in disguise.

### Q: Who pays the professional fees in a large Chapter 11 case?

The estate pays the professional fees of the debtor and of official committees, subject to court approval, which means the cost is ultimately borne by the creditors and owners whose recoveries fund the case. Lawyers, bankers, and advisers for the debtor and for committees file fee applications describing their work, and the court reviews them for reasonableness with the United States Trustee also examining the requests. Creditors acting on their own account generally pay their own advisers, although support agreements sometimes include payment of fees for the creditor groups whose signatures made a prearranged or prepackaged deal possible, as part of the negotiated economics. Fee levels in large cases draw recurring criticism, and courts have responded with guidelines, fee examiners in some matters, and closer review of staffing and rates. The total cost is one reason parties value a short case; every month under supervision carries a professional meter that reduces the fund available for distribution.

### Q: What is a fulcrum security in a restructuring?

A fulcrum security is the class of debt that sits at the point where the company's value runs out, so that holders above it are covered in full or nearly so and holders below it recover little or nothing. Because that class bears the real economic risk of the reorganization, it usually receives the new equity issued under the plan and with it control of the reorganized company. Identifying the fulcrum security is a central exercise in any restructuring analysis, since it predicts who will negotiate hardest, who will fund new money, and whose consent the plan most needs. Its identity depends entirely on valuation; a higher enterprise value pushes the fulcrum point down the capital structure, while a lower value pushes it up. Arguments over valuation in large cases are therefore often arguments over which class is the fulcrum, conducted in the language of projections, discount rates, and comparable companies rather than in the language of control.

### Q: How are bondholders treated differently from bank lenders in a plan?

Bondholders and bank lenders differ in ways that shape both negotiation and plan treatment. Bank debt is usually held by a manageable group of institutions under a credit agreement with an agent, covenants, and often security interests in specific assets, which makes amendment and negotiation practical. Bonds are held by many investors through indentures, trade in public markets, and carry the consent protection for payment terms that the Trust Indenture Act supplies, which makes out of court changes harder and class voting inside a case more important. Secured status, where it exists, drives treatment more than the label on the instrument; a secured bond and a secured loan may sit in the same class if their rights are substantially similar. Differences in treatment within a plan must rest on real differences in legal rights, since classes group claims of similar character. Information flow also differs, since bank groups often receive confidential projections under nondisclosure agreements while public bondholders rely on public disclosure.

### Q: What is the feasibility requirement for confirming a plan?

Feasibility is the confirmation requirement that a plan not be likely to be followed by liquidation or by the need for further financial reorganization, unless the plan itself proposes such an outcome. The court tests the reorganized company's projections against its obligations, examining revenue assumptions, margins, capital spending needs, working capital, debt service, and the financing committed for exit. The standard does not demand a guarantee of success, and courts confirm plans for businesses in difficult industries where the evidence shows a reasonable prospect of performance. It does demand more than optimism; projections built on unproven contracts, unexplained margin recovery, or refinancing that no lender has committed to invite denial or a demand for more equity and less debt. In a prepackaged case the feasibility inquiry is part of the audit the court performs on a bargain struck earlier, and it is one of the points where a judge can slow a fast case by requiring evidence the parties treated as settled among themselves.

### Q: Can shareholders ever keep an interest when creditors are not paid in full?

Shareholders can retain an interest in limited circumstances even where creditors are not paid in full, and each circumstance has its own legal basis. A senior class may consent to give part of its own recovery to holders below it, a practice often described as gifting, where the governing law permits it. Creditors may also vote to accept a plan that leaves existing owners with a stake, since a consenting class changes the analysis that applies to a dissenting one. A contested basis is a contribution of new value by existing owners in exchange for their retained interest, a doctrine with a long and disputed history in the case law. Where a dissenting impaired class is involved and no exception applies, the absolute priority rule bars junior holders from receiving or keeping property on account of their old interests before the dissenting class is paid in full. Outcomes turn on consent, valuation, and the source of whatever owners keep.

### Q: What is a claims bar date and what happens after it passes?

A claims bar date is the deadline the court sets for filing proofs of claim in a bankruptcy case. Creditors receive notice of the date, and the claims register records what was filed, by whom, and in what amount. A claim filed after the deadline without an excuse the court accepts may be disallowed or treated as late under the rules governing the case, which can reduce or eliminate the holder's distribution under a plan. Scheduled creditors whose claims are listed accurately and not marked as disputed, contingent, or unliquidated may not need to file at all, a detail that makes reading the notice carefully important for any holder. Bar dates serve the plan process by fixing the universe of claims the plan must classify and fund. In a fast prepackaged case the bar date may fall after confirmation for some categories, and the plan and notice documents state which holders must file and by when.

### Q: Why did prepackaged bankruptcies become common in large corporate cases?

Prepackaged cases became common because they align the incentives of the parties who bear the cost of a long case. Debtors gain a shorter period of public distress and lower professional expense. Senior creditors gain a negotiated outcome they helped design, with new money and governance terms settled before a court timetable creates leverage for other parties. Courts and the system gain dockets that resolve without months of contested hearings where a genuine consensus exists. The growth of restructuring support agreements, standardized solicitation practice, and a professional community experienced in running votes before filing turned an occasional technique into a standard option for companies with concentrated funded debt. The form fits bond and loan structures held by institutions able to organize and sign commitments. It fits less well where claims are diffuse, disputed, or dominated by litigation, which is why conventional cases remain common alongside it.

### Q: What records from a bankruptcy case can the public read?

Most of the record in a corporate bankruptcy case is public. The docket lists every filing in order, and the documents themselves, petitions, schedules of assets and liabilities, statements of financial affairs, plans, disclosure statements, motions, objections, and court orders, can be read through the federal courts' electronic access system, subject to the fees and rules that system applies. Claims registers show who filed claims and in what amounts. Transcripts of hearings and monthly operating reports add a running account of the company's condition during the case. Some material is filed under seal or redacted where courts find cause, for example to protect commercially sensitive terms, personal information, or the integrity of a sale process, and certain personal data is excluded from public filing by rule. Committees and debtors also maintain case websites in large matters that collect key documents without charge, which often provide the fastest route to the plan, the disclosure statement, and hearing notices.
