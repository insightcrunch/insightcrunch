## Music in Performance History: The Layer the Cliche Forgets

Music is the interpretation nobody quotes. A production of the tragedy can be described by its sets, its costumes, its cuts and its stars, and the description will still have missed the element that told the audience, minute by minute, how to feel about what it saw: the sound that framed the feud, sweetened the ball, thickened the street scenes and fell silent, pointedly, at the tomb. The performance history of the play is usually written as a history of actors and directors. Written as a history of music, it becomes a different and in some ways a more honest record, because scoring decisions are interpretation decisions, taken afresh by every era and disguised as decoration. The Elizabethan company played the ball with a hired consort and then staged a scene in which musicians refuse to play at all. The eighteenth century sang a dirge over Juliet that the text never requested. The nineteenth century wrapped the whole evening in an orchestral score and played music between the acts while the scenery changed. The twentieth century first stripped the score away in the name of the verse, then handed the play to resident composers, and ended with amplified songs and electronic weather. Each of those choices argues something about what the tragedy is. The consort argues that Verona is a working city whose pleasures and griefs are serviced by tradesmen. The dirge argues that Juliet's funeral deserves a liturgy the play withheld. The Victorian score argues that the audience's feelings need conducting. The modern soundscape argues that atmosphere is a design material like light. None of these arguments is neutral, and the history of the play's stage music is the history of productions confessing their readings in sound before a word was spoken.

The verdict of this survey can be stated at the outset, because everything that follows is its evidence. Music in the theatre of this play does its greatest work when it stays inside the fiction: played by visible musicians, hired by characters, refused by tradesmen, danced to by masked guests, requested by a clown and withheld. The text calls for very little music, and what it calls for is almost all of it diegetic, music the characters themselves hear. The eras that honoured that economy, the Elizabethan original and the platform reformers who recovered it, let the play's own sound design operate, and the play's sound design is subtle: festivity that curdles, a wedding band that will not play for a corpse, a tomb with no music in it at all. The eras that flooded the play with added scoring, above all the nineteenth century's orchestral productions, did not merely decorate the tragedy; they rebalanced it, softening its abruptness, upholstering its silences, and teaching audiences to receive as sentiment what the text delivers as shock. Modern sound design has recovered the economy by other means, and the best contemporary productions treat sound the way the first company treated its consort: as a scarce resource whose withholding is an event. The survey that follows tests that verdict era by era, keeping two categories separate throughout, because the whole subject dissolves into confusion when they merge. Text-called music is music the play itself demands, at named moments, for named dramatic reasons. Added scoring is everything a production lays over the text from outside. The first is evidence about the play. The second is evidence about the production. Both are worth studying, and they are not the same study.

One discipline governs the citations. Quotations from the play follow the Arden Third Series text edited by René Weis, and act, scene and line numbers are his; where the Second Quarto's directions differ from a modern editor's composite, the difference is flagged rather than smoothed. The scholarship of stage music is younger than the scholarship of staging, and its founding works still set the terms: F. W. Sternfeld's Music in Shakespearean Tragedy, first published in 1963 and revised in 1967, which treated the tragedies' music as a structural element rather than an ornament; John H. Long's studies of the music in the original productions, which insisted that an audience formed by a musical culture would have been puzzled and disturbed if the stage world had ignored music; and, against both, Andrew Gurr's account of the early playhouses, which restricted the amphitheatre's provision to drums, trumpets, fiddles and flutes and denied the adult companies a resident consort before the move indoors. That quarrel, between the historians who hear the early stage as saturated with skilled music and the historian who hears it as making do with noise and a few hired fiddles, runs underneath every later section of this survey, because every modern production that reconstructs an Elizabethan ball is silently taking a side in it.

## What Music Does the Text Itself Call For?

The inventory is shorter than the play's reputation suggests, and its shortness is the first fact of the subject. The tragedy contains exactly one unambiguous direction for music to be played as part of the action: at the Capulet ball, where the Second Quarto's direction, in Weis's modernized form, reads "Music plays, and they dance" (1.5). Around that single explicit cue cluster a handful of demands that are nearly as firm. The wedding morning of the fourth act is built on hired music: Capulet sends for the bridegroom and promises that the County will be there "with music straight" (4.4), and the musicians who then appear, first as the wedding band and then as the refused mourners of 4.5, are characters with names, instruments and a wage dispute. Their scene is the play's one sustained piece of writing about music itself, and it ends with the band dismissed unplayed: "Come, we'll to dinner; I mean we'll go carry her to church, and leave these musicians to their silver sound" is the burden of its close in paraphrase; the text's own summary is the First Musician's resignation, "Faith, we may put up our pipes and be gone" (4.5). Beyond these, the calls thin into implication. Paris comes to the house in the wedding procession's expectation of music. The watch, the friar and the families at the tomb are given no music at all, and the absence is structural: the play's loudest scenes are its festive ones, and its catastrophe happens in a silence the text enforces by simply never asking for sound. A production that underscores the tomb is therefore not filling a gap the text left neutral. It is contradicting a decision the text made.

Set against that sparse inventory, the play's talk about music is lavish, and the contrast is itself a finding. Romeo hears Juliet's voice as music and begs the night to teach it; the aubade of 3.5 is an argument about birdsong conducted in musical terms, the lark whose song is "full of harsh discords and unpleasing sharps" against the nightingale whose notes would keep the lovers in bed and in danger. Mercutio turns the language of the consort into an insult, punning on Tybalt's "consort" with minstrels until the word for harmonious ensemble playing becomes a provocation to fight. Juliet, waiting, apostrophizes the cords her nurse carries as instruments of a music that will not sound. The play thinks in music constantly and plays music rarely, and the rarity is what gives the played moments their weight. Sternfeld's structural reading of the tragedies began from precisely this economy: in his account, the music a tragedy actually performs is rationed so that each sounding carries the charge of the scenes around it, and the ball's consort music in this play is charged twice over, once by the feast's splendour and once by the knowledge, which the audience holds and the dancers do not, that the measures are counting down to a recognition and a feud reignited. Long's complementary point was about competence: the first audiences lived inside a culture of household and civic music-making, heard consorts at weddings and waits in the streets, and would have registered the difference between the ball's skilled ensemble and the wedding band's haggling as a social fact about Verona, not as background. The text's economy only works on ears that know what the music costs and who is paying for it. Both historians, in other words, treat the sparse inventory as design. Gurr's restriction of the amphitheatre's means does not overturn the design; it sharpens it, since a company with limited musical resources that spends them at the ball and the wedding is making its priorities audible.

### What does the direction "Music plays" actually instruct?

The direction instructs the company to sound the ball into being: hired musicians play, the guests dance, and the scene's recognitions happen inside live music the characters can hear. It names no tune, no dance and no instruments, so every production since has had to compose or choose what the quarto leaves open, and that choice is interpretation.

The editorial history of the cues shows editors quietly enlarging the text's demands, and the enlargement is a small history of the subject in itself. Weis prints the quarto directions with their spareness intact and marks supplements as supplements; the Oxford tradition represented by Jill Levenson's edition admits performance-derived directions with a freer hand, regularizing entrances of musicians and clarifying who plays when. Neither practice is dishonest, but they produce different plays for a music director: the conservative text offers a ball, a wedding band and a refusal, while the freer text offers a continuous musical scaffolding that later centuries then mistook for the author's provision. The scholarly point matters because the Victorian era's defenders of heavy scoring appealed to the play's supposed musical richness, when the richness lay in its talk and its editors, not in its cues. A survey of stage music has to begin by refusing that inheritance: the text calls for a dance band twice, once festive and once funereal, and for silence at the grave. Everything else is the history of what theatres could not resist adding.

The absences deserve the same precision as the cues, because Sternfeld built his reading of the tragedies partly on what each play withholds. The history plays and the Roman tragedies spend trumpets freely: flourishes, sennets and tuckets herald authority whenever it enters. This play, whose Verona is governed by a Prince who enters to scold a street brawl, spends none. The Prince's appearances are unheralded in the early texts; the city's authority arrives to the sound of a fray, not a flourish, and the one flourish-like expectation the action raises, the wedding procession, collapses into the musicians' refusal before it can sound. Drums, the wars' instrument, are absent with the wars; the feud is a civilian disorder fought with rapiers in a square, and the text keeps it acoustic, voices and steel, where a history play would have scored it. Even the night watch and the final assembly gather without ceremony. The inventory's meaning is distributional: this tragedy's public world makes its music at feasts and weddings, in the service of pleasure and alliance, and the state that should command the trumpets commands nothing but silence and speeches. A production that heralds the Prince or drums the brawls is importing the sound of a different genre, and the importation is legible to any audience that has heard the histories played straight. The text's sound design, in short, is generic as well as local: a tragedy that begins in a dance and ends in a hush, with the machinery of martial and civic music conspicuously uninstalled.

## The Ball Consort: What Played While the Measures Turned

The ball is the one place where the text, the fiction and the company's resources all demand real music in real time, and it is therefore the test case for every claim about early staging. Inside the fiction, the Capulets have hired musicians for a great feast; Capulet's welcome invokes the dancing by name, the servingmen clear the floor, and the direction sounds. What the first audiences heard was, on the documentary evidence, a small professional ensemble of the kind that played at weddings and civic feasts across London: string players whose instruments the play itself names in its jokes, since the wedding band of 4.5 is led by Simon Catling, whose surname is the catgut of fiddle strings, Hugh Rebeck, named for the small bowed rebec, and James Soundpost, named for the wooden post inside a fiddle's body that carries its tone. The joke names are a casting of instruments. The band the audience saw refusing to play in the fourth act was, in the fiction's economy and very likely in the company's personnel, the band it had heard playing at the ball in the first act, and the identity matters: the same tradesmen who serviced Verona's pleasure are found, three days later, haggling over their dinner beside its corpse. Whether the ball's ensemble rose to a full broken consort, the mixed ensemble of strings, woodwind and plucked instruments that the period prized for indoor dance music, is the point on which the scholarship divides, and the division deserves its day in this survey because productions still spend money on it.

Long and the tradition he represents held that the Chamberlain's Men disposed of skilled musicians and consort music as a normal part of their provision, and that the ball would have sounded rich: viols and violins, flute or recorder, lute and cittern or bandora in the mixed texture the broken consort made fashionable, playing the measures and galliards of the contemporary dance repertoire. Gurr's counter-account, built on playhouse documents rather than on the plays' internal evidence, held that the outdoor amphitheatres kept music to a functional minimum, that consort music of the refined kind belonged to the indoor hall playhouses and their boy companies, and that the adult companies hired casual musicians by the occasion, exactly as the Capulets hire theirs. The quarrel is instructive in its evidence. The internal evidence, the plays' own assumption that bands appear, play dances, tune and haggle, is abundant; the external evidence, contracts, inventories and payments, is thin for the amphitheatres and richer for the halls. The adjudication this survey adopts is a split verdict with a practical edge. On the documentary question, Gurr's caution governs: no surviving paper proves a resident consort at the Theatre or the Globe in the mid-1590s, and any production claiming archaeological certainty for its ball band is claiming more than the record holds. On the theatrical question, Long and Sternfeld govern: the fiction requires competent dance music from visible professionals, the company's clown was written into the band's refusal scene, and the play's effects depend on the audience hearing skill, not scraping. The original ball almost certainly sounded better than the minimum and less certainly like a court consort; the honest reconstruction is a small, expert, hired band, which happens to be what the text describes.

### Which instruments made up a broken consort at a stage ball?

A broken consort mixed the instrument families that a whole consort kept apart: bowed strings such as viols or violins, a flute or recorder, and plucked strings such as lute, cittern and bandora, over a bass viol. The mixture gave dance music both carrying power and glitter, and its presence at the first balls remains inference, not record.

What the ball's music had to do, whatever its instrumentation, is recoverable from the scene's construction, and here the survey can be firm. It had to establish festivity at a level that makes the masquers' intrusion plausible and Tybalt's rage proportionate. It had to cover, in the fiction's own acoustics, private speech in a public room, so that a shared sonnet can happen inside a crowd. It had to pace the recognitions, since Romeo's "The measure done, I'll watch her place of stand" (1.4) makes the dance's ending the trigger of his approach, and the production's choice of when the music stops is a choice about when the tragedy starts. And it had to be stoppable: the scene's deepest musical event is not any tune but the hush in which Capulet restrains Tybalt and the dancing resumes over a feud deferred, so that the audience hears pleasure reassert itself on top of violence postponed. The staging of that scene as a whole, its masks, its measures and its choreography, belongs to the companion study of [the ball's music, masks and dance as a single scene's staging](/2027/12/24/staging-the-ball-music-dance/), which owns the dance floor; this survey's claim is narrower and prior: before the ball is a dance problem it is a hiring problem, and the play solves it by putting working musicians on the payroll and on the stage.

The repertoire question, what tunes filled the measures, has its own documentary landscape, and the survey should map it without pretending it maps the first night. The dance music of the period circulated in manuscript and print collections compiled for exactly the labour the scene depicts: John Playford's The English Dancing Master of 1651, the great surviving anthology of the trade, preserves hundreds of country dance tunes with their figures, among them the "Heart's ease" Peter will later demand by name, and the virginal books and consort partbooks preserve the pavanes, galliards and almains of the indoor repertoire. None of this proves what the Chamberlain's Men's band played in the mid-1590s, and the survey claims none of it as the ball's playlist. What the collections prove is the trade's standardization: a hired band in London worked from a shared, portable repertoire whose items any dancing master could call and any competent fiddler could supply, so that the ball's sound, whatever its tunes, was the sound of a known social technology rather than of a composition. That is why the text can leave the choice to the band without loss, and why the later centuries' ball music, composed, published and copyrighted, represents a genuine change of kind: the dance floor's music became an author's property, credited and reviewed, where the quarto's world had treated it as a tradesman's stock. The modern production that reconstructs the ball from Playford is performing an archaeology of the trade; the production that commissions a score is continuing the Victorian settlement by other means; and the difference between them is a position on where the ball's music lives, in a repertoire or in a work.

## Heart's Ease Refused: The Musicians' Scene and the Sound of a Wage

If the ball is the play's music at work, the scene of Peter and the musicians in 4.5 is the play's music at law: a negotiation about what playing is for, conducted over a body. The scene's position is audacious. Juliet has been found apparently dead; the house has passed through the great lament, in which grief performs itself in formal, almost musical periods of wailing; and then the hired band, come to play the bride to church, is told by Peter to play "Heart's ease" instead. The request is a small anthology of the period's tune culture, and editors from Gibbons to Weis have glossed its terms without exhausting its cruelty. "Heart's ease" was a lively country dance tune, and it survives; Peter wants it because, he says, his heart plays "My heart is full", the name of a grave tune that does not survive; and he asks besides for "some merry dump to comfort me", a dump being by definition a mournful air, so that the request contradicts itself in its own vocabulary. The clown wants music as medicine, music to alter a state of the heart, and he wants it from men who have come to sell music as labour. The First Musician refuses on professional grounds that are also moral ones: "Not a dump we, 'tis no time to play now" (4.5). The band will not play the funeral house a dance, and will not play at all without the occasion they were hired for. Peter's answer is the scene's famous reduction of art to economics: musicians have "silver sound" because "musicians have no gold for sounding" (4.5). Praise is the only currency their playing earns, and in this house, on this morning, it is not legal tender.

The Second Quarto's casting of the scene sharpens it: the direction names Will Kempe, the company's clown, in Peter's place, so the first audiences watched the most famous comic in London demanding a tune from three fiddlers and being refused. Sternfeld treated the scene as the tragedy's built-in commentary on its own music, the point where the play states the terms on which sound may enter it: music in this world is hired, occasional and answerable to the event, and when the event turns to death the music stops, not because the playwright lacked a score but because the fiction's tradesmen have professional honour. That reading has worn well, and this survey adopts it, with one addition from the performance record: the scene is also the play's insurance policy against its own later sentimentalization. A text that contains a band refusing to play at a funeral has anticipated, and mocked in advance, every production that would lay a dirge under Juliet's bier as though grief required orchestral permission. The eighteenth century played the dirge anyway, and the quarrel between the scene's refusal and the dirge's compliance is the central quarrel of the play's musical afterlife. Directors who cut the musicians' scene, as companies have done in every century including the first revival generations, are not merely trimming comedy after a death; they are removing the play's own argument about its music, and then, very often, replacing the argument with the thing it refused, an underscored lament. The cutting history belongs to the scene's own studies; the survey registers the pattern and its irony: the scene that explains why the music stops is the scene productions stop.

### Why did Peter's requested tune never sound?

The tune never sounds because the musicians refuse it on professional and moral grounds: a dance tune in a house of mourning is no playing matter for the time, and their labour is hired for an occasion death has cancelled. The refusal is the play's doctrine of stage music, spoken by its tradesmen.

The scene's names carry the survey's last point about the early stage. Catling, Rebeck and Soundpost are not whimsical labels; they are the instrumentarium speaking. A company that jokes in string, rebec and soundpost is a company whose audience handled and hired such things, which returns the argument to Long's competence thesis: the comedy of the scene is professional comedy, legible to hearers who knew what a fiddler's soundpost was and what a wedding band cost. Productions that play the scene before audiences innocent of the terms lose the jokes but keep the structure, the refusal, which is why the scene survives translation into every later idiom of the play: whatever the century's instruments, a band that will not play is an image no audience needs glossed.

One more feature of the scene belongs to the survey because it predicts the play's whole musical afterlife: its prose. The musicians speak the play's last sustained prose, and the descent from the lament's verse periods into their haggling is a change of key as surely as any modulation, grief's high style giving way to the sound of men pricing their labour. Editors have sometimes treated the scene's position as an accident of the clown's contract, Kempe requiring his scene; the survey follows the structural reading instead. The play has spent three acts teaching its audience that music in Verona is hired, and the fourth act collects the debt: the hirelings present themselves at the moment of maximum feeling and decline, in prose, to feel on hire. Every later era that underscored the lament was answering this scene without knowing it, supplying from the pit the performed feeling the band onstage withholds, and the contest between the scene and the score, between withheld and supplied emotion, is the form the play's musical history takes for the next three hundred years.


## The Wedding Morning: Music Promised at Daybreak

One cue in the inventory earns a section of its own because it is the hinge on which the text's whole sound design turns, and it sounds before it is refused. The wedding morning of the fourth act is composed, in the fiction, as a musical event: the household rises before dawn, the bridegroom is expected in procession, and Capulet's preparations carry the promise that Paris will arrive with the hired band in attendance, the same labour that sounded the ball now summoned to sound a marriage. Morning music at a great house's wedding was a social instruments' set piece, the waits' and household bands' stock occasion, and the text counts on its audience's knowledge of the form: a festivity louder and more public than the ball's, since a wedding procession advertises an alliance to the whole street. The act therefore opens inside an expectation of music so strong that the music's non-arrival becomes the act's plot. What arrives instead is the discovery of the body, and the band, when it enters, enters a house where its function has been annulled, to be bargained with by a clown and dismissed by a proverb. The survey stresses the expectation because productions so often begin the act at the discovery, trimming the preparations, and thereby lose the design's first term: the refusal only wounds because the promise was believed. The fourth act is the ball played backwards, the same hiring machinery, the same tradesmen, the same household, with the sound withdrawn, and its place in the tragedy's diminuendo is exact, the middle term between the ball's full ensemble and the tomb's empty quiet. A survey that kept only three moments of the play's music would keep those three, the ball, the refusal and the silence, and would lose less of the truth than a survey that documented every added note of the nineteenth century, because the three moments are the design and the rest is commentary.

## Imagined Music: The Lark, the Nightingale and the Consort Pun

Between the music the text plays and the music it refuses stands a third category that productions cannot stage and cannot afford to ignore: the music the play imagines, in metaphors its characters reach for whenever feeling outruns plain speech. The lovers conduct their deepest argument of the play as a dispute about a song neither of them can stop hearing. In the aubade, Juliet insists the singer outside the window is the nightingale, whose song licenses the night to continue, and Romeo corrects her with the lark, "the herald of the morn", whose notes are the warrant for his departure and his death if he stays. Juliet's description of the lark's song as "full of harsh discords and unpleasing sharps" (3.5) is the tragedy's most technical piece of music criticism, and its terms are exact: discords and sharps are what the same song becomes when its meaning changes, the melody unaltered and the harmony intolerable. The passage is the play's own theory of stage music in miniature. Sound means by context; the identical notes that blessed the wedding night accuse the wedding morning; and a production's score, which is also the same notes at different moments, lives under the same law. No composer is named in the aubade, because the composer is the situation.

Mercutio works the same vein from the comic side, and his music is all aggression. His taunt that Tybalt "consorts" with Romeo carries the period's double sense in a single word: to consort is to keep company, and a consort is an ensemble of musicians, so the accusation that Tybalt consorts with minstrels demotes the duellist to a hired fiddler's companion in the act of challenging him. The joke that follows, of the fiddlestick that will make Tybalt dance, completes the figure: the sword as bow, the duel as a tune the victim dances to whether he will or not. When the fight comes, the metaphor is cashed with terrible literalness, and Mercutio dies of the dance he named. A survey of stage music files these passages under language rather than sound, but they govern sound all the same, because they teach the audience to hear the play's actual music through its metaphors: the ball's consort is the harmonious ensemble the feud will turn into a consort of swords, and the wedding band's refusal is the moment the metaphors stop, the point where the play will no longer pretend that its violence is a kind of music. Productions that score the street scenes with the ball's own themes, darkened, are not decorating the text; they are obeying its imagery.

Juliet's waiting speech of 3.2 extends the imagined register to the props of music. The cords the Nurse carries enter her rhetoric as instruments of an ascent she figures in terms of strain and pitch, and her address to them, "Poor ropes, you are beguiled", treats stage properties as fellow performers who have been promised a performance and denied it. The speech is the solo counterpart of the musicians' scene: a character alone with the equipment of an event that will not happen as planned, apostrophizing the equipment. Between them, the two scenes frame the tragedy's central musical fact, that its happiest sounds are all prospective, tunes named before they play, dances promised, bands hired, and that the play's actual sounded music shrinks as the action darkens, from the ball's full ensemble to a band that packs up its pipes to a tomb with no music in it at all. That diminuendo is written into the text's cue structure, and the productions that have understood the play best, in whatever century, are the ones that let the audience hear the music leaving.

The table's most instructive row is the one that never changes shape: the third column. Across four centuries of radical disagreement about everything else, the ball is played, in some idiom, by nearly every production that plays the text at length, because the scene cannot proceed without it; and the wedding band's refusal, when it is allowed to stand, stops the music in every era with the same small shock. What changes is everything around those fixed points: whether the funeral sings, whether the acts are glued with orchestral entr'actes, whether the tomb has a pulse under it. The survey's rows are also a warning about sources: the added column is documented unevenly, richly for the eras that published their scores, thinly for the eras that hired their bands by the night, so that the table records an asymmetry of survival as faithfully as an asymmetry of practice.

## Song, Dirge and Masquerade: What the Eighteenth Century Added

The long eighteenth century inherited a play it performed in adaptation and a stage that sold music as an attraction in its own right, and its additions to the score are best understood as commerce before they are understood as interpretation. Between the acts of spoken plays, audiences expected entr'acte entertainment, songs and dances unconnected with the drama; inside the drama, the century's acting versions, descending through Otway's Roman rewrite and Cibber's version to Garrick's, adjusted the play's own music to the same appetite. The ball acquired advertised dance novelties: the bills for Garrick's production promoted a "New Masquerade Dance proper to the Play", a composed set piece wearing the fiction as its excuse, whose relation to the quarto's bare "Music plays, and they dance" is the relation of a trade fair to a wedding. The dance was a selling point, named in the advertisements because novelty sold seats, and its existence proves the era's premise: the ball's music was no longer the Capulets' hired band but the theatre's hired attraction, answerable to the box office rather than to Verona.

Garrick's greater addition reversed the musicians' refusal itself. For the funeral procession of Juliet, his production introduced a sung dirge, words of his own, set by William Boyce, in which a chorus processed and sang the heroine to her tomb. The dirge's long stage life, examined as a version's property in the study of Garrick's adaptation, is registered here for its survey significance: it is the first great counter-argument to the text's sound design. Where the quarto's band refuses to play and the mourning is spoken by the family in formal lament, Garrick's theatre concluded that the moment required composed music, professionally sung, and audiences agreed for generations; the funeral became a musical set piece, excerpted and remembered, while the scene that explains why music should stop was thinned to make room. Sternfeld's framework names what happened with precision: the dirge transfers music from the fiction to the auditorium, from hired tradesmen inside Verona to a chorus addressing the audience's feelings from outside it, and in that transfer the tragedy's economy of sound is inverted. Music stops being something Verona does and becomes something the theatre does to its hearers. Every later era's scoring debate is a negotiation with that transfer: the Victorians completed it, the reformers reversed it, and the modern soundscape disguises it, but the dirge is where the play's stage music changed sides.

The dirge's afterlife in print tells the same story from the archive's side. Boyce's setting circulated beyond the playhouse as a piece of music in its own right, excerpted, republished and sung in contexts that had forgotten the production, so that the funeral's sound became detachable from the tragedy it was composed to serve, an early instance of the fate that awaits all successful stage music, from the Victorian library score to the modern soundtrack album: it escapes the fiction, acquires an independent career, and returns to later productions as a classic the new staging must cite or conspicuously refuse. The survey notes the pattern because it explains the dirge's otherwise puzzling longevity, its persistence in the acting tradition long after Garrick's text had been revised away. A spoken reading can be cut in a rehearsal; a famous tune must be argued with, and the argument, repeated across generations of managers, is itself part of the record this survey reads: the funeral's music had become a property of the audience's memory, and properties of that kind are the hardest scenery to strike.

### What did the Drury Lane dirge change about the funeral?

The dirge changed the funeral from a spoken lament guarded by a band's refusal into a sung ceremony performed for the audience, and it held the stage for generations because the change flattered a real hunger: to have Juliet's death ritualized in music the text withholds. Its cost was the musicians' scene, whose refusal the dirge's compliance made expendable.

The century's third lesson is quieter. The theatre bands of the period were working orchestras of a dozen or more players, and the play's evening was saturated with music that nobody counted as part of the play: overtures, act tunes, the songs between the acts. A survey confined to the drama's cues understates the eighteenth-century experience, in which the tragedy arrived wrapped in an hour of miscellaneous music and the boundary between the play's sound and the evening's sound was porous by design. That porosity is the condition Garrick's additions exploited, and it is why the era's scoring cannot be judged by modern categories: added music was not an intervention in a silent text but a reallocation within a musical evening. The reformers of a later century, who treated every added note as an intrusion, were reacting against an ecology their grandfathers had found natural.

## The Musical Evening: Act Tunes, Songs and the Audience Between the Acts

The eighteenth century's additions make full sense only inside the musical economy of its playhouse, where a tragedy was one item in an evening saturated with sound. The mainpiece arrived with an overture, was punctuated by act tunes, shared the bill with songs and dances between the acts, and was followed by an afterpiece, often a musical one. A spectator of Garrick's Verona therefore heard the play's cues as peaks in a continuous musical landscape rather than as events in a silence, and the era's scoring decisions, the advertised masquerade dance, the sung dirge, were bids for prominence within that landscape, moments when the play itself, rather than the evening's miscellaneous entertainers, supplied the music the house had paid to hear. The dirge's genius as showmanship lies exactly there: it reclaimed the funeral from the afterpiece, giving the mainpiece a musical climax to rival the entr'acte songs, and its long tenancy of the stage measures how completely it won that contest. Judged by the standards of its own playhouse, the addition was not an intrusion into a spoken purity that nobody present had experienced; it was the tragedy's promotion within a musical bill.

That framing also disciplines the survey's judgement of the era's losses. The musicians' scene suffered in the eighteenth century not from any doctrine about music but from the acting versions' general compression, and its haggling prose sat awkwardly in an evening whose comedy was increasingly supplied by the afterpiece rather than by the mainpiece's clownage. When the scene was cut, the play lost its internal critic of the dirge, and the two events are connected in the survey's accounting even where no manager intended the connection: the version that sings the funeral is a version that has muted the tradesmen who would have refused to. The nineteenth century inherited both the dirge's premise and the scene's absence as settled practice, and built its orchestral system on ground the eighteenth had already cleared, which is why the survey treats the two centuries as one long transfer of music from the stage to the pit, in two instalments, the first artisanal and the second industrial.

The theatre bands themselves deserve a sentence of record, because the survey's eras are staffed by working musicians whose conditions shaped what could be scored. The Georgian playhouse orchestra was a standing body of professionals, engaged by the season, reading at sight, supplying overtures, act tunes, accompaniments and dance music for the mainpiece and its satellites alike; the masquerade dance and the dirge were written for such a band and chorus, forces a management already employed for the evening's other purposes. The Victorian music director inherited that band enlarged and disciplined; the mid-twentieth-century resident composer inherited its shrunken ghost and rebuilt it as a department. Behind every row of the survey's table stands a payroll, and the history of the play's music is in part the history of how many musicians a theatre could afford to keep in the building, a fact the fourth act's wage dispute, in which three fiddlers haggle over their dinner beside a corpse, states more honestly than any archive.

## The Victorian Orchestra: Incidental Scores and the Entr'acte System

The nineteenth century industrialized the transfer Garrick began. The pictorial stage, with its deep sets, its scene changes that took minutes of carpentry, and its audiences trained by melodrama to expect music under emotion, required a score the way it required gaslight: continuously, competently and invisibly. The great London productions of the play, of which the Lyceum staging of 1882 is this series' documented exemplar, disposed of a house orchestra and a music director as standard plant, and the music's functions multiplied beyond anything the text imagines. An overture, often assembled from the themes the evening would use, tuned the audience before the curtain. Entr'acte music covered the scene changes, converting dead time into mood and teaching the house to carry the previous scene's feeling across the interval like a held breath. Under the dialogue, melodramatic underscoring marked villains, swelled at recognitions and melted at love scenes, in a code audiences read fluently. Published incidental music, issued in parts for provincial and touring use, standardized the system: a manager in a garrison town could hire the same emotional machinery as the Lyceum, and the play's sound became, for the first time, a reproducible commodity with a catalogue.

Judged as engineering, the system was superb, and the survey should say so before it judges at all. The entr'acte solved a real problem, the scene change, with a real art; the underscoring solved the pictorial stage's real problem, its slowness, by keeping feeling in motion while the eye waited; and the standard of playing in the great houses was high, the orchestras staffed by professionals whose descendants are the theatre bands of the musical stage. Judged as interpretation, the system's cost is written in what it did to the play's own sound design. The ball, in the pictorial productions, became a spectacle of costume and choreography whose music served the picture. The musicians' scene, an embarrassment of haggling prose after a pictorial lament, was cut in production after production, and with it went the refusal that polices the funeral. The tomb, which the text leaves silent, received its swell of strings, so that the audience's tears were conducted at the very moment the play intends desolation. And the dirge tradition, Garrick's inheritance, persisted inside the orchestral funeral as a matter of course. The Victorian score did not fail to understand the play; it understood a different play, one in which feeling is the product and music its delivery system, and it delivered that play with complete professional sincerity. Sternfeld's history of the tragedies' music is severe on the period for exactly this reason: in his account the nineteenth century's scores are the point where music's structural role, its rationing and refusal, is most thoroughly lost, replaced by a continuous emotional commentary that tells the audience what the verse was already telling it, in a louder voice.

The counter-case deserves its paragraph, because the era's musicians were not vandals. The pictorial stage's tempo was its own necessity: a production that pauses for a full scene change in silence loses its house, and the entr'acte was the price of the scenery the audience had paid to see. Nor was underscoring alien to the text's own century, which used music under dumb shows and at moments of wonder; the Victorians extended a device rather than inventing one. The adjudication this survey reaches is narrower than Sternfeld's severity and harder than the era's self-satisfaction: the Victorian system is acquitted of incompetence and convicted of proportion. A score that can do everything was allowed to do everything, all evening, at every cue the text offered and hundreds it did not, until the play's deliberate silences, the refused tune, the unaccompanied tomb, had been paved over. The proof of the conviction is the reform that followed, which did not argue that the Victorians played badly; it argued that they played constantly, and that constancy, not quality, was the offence.

The system's traces survive in the material record with unusual completeness, and the survey's Victorian row rests on objects as much as on memoirs. Printed incidental music, hired from theatrical libraries and returned with the prompt copies, preserves the era's cue-by-cue decisions: which entrances took a flourish, which love scenes took the string tremolo, where the funeral music entered and how long it played under the procession. Read against the acting texts of the same productions, the parts show the musicians' scene's absence as a physical fact, its pages simply missing from the band's folders, and they show something subtler as well: the gradual migration of the ball's music from the stage to the pit, the dancers' band becoming an orchestral number as the spectacle outgrew the fiction's payroll. A production's sound world, in the Victorian system, was the most completely documented part of its interpretation and the least discussed, filed in the music library while the reviews debated the scenery. That inversion of survival and attention is the survey's standing caution about its own evidence: the eras that published their scores loom larger in any history of stage music than their influence warrants, and the eras that hired their bands by the night, beginning with the first, recede behind the poverty of their paper.

## The Reform: Platform Stages Take the Score Away

The reaction, when it came, came from practitioners who wanted the verse back, and its musical consequence was a subtraction so complete that it reads, in the survey, as a second invention of the text's economy. William Poel's platform experiments of the 1890s and Harley Granville-Barker's Savoy productions a decade later rebuilt Shakespearean staging around continuous action on an open stage, and continuous action abolished the entr'acte's reason for existing: no scene changes, no carpentry, no need for orchestral glue. The underscoring went with the glue. What remained was the music the text itself calls for, restored to audibility by the same movement that restored the musicians' scene as part of the unexpurgated text, and Granville-Barker's practical criticism treats the play's pace as a musical property of the verse itself, a tempo to be conducted by the actor-manager rather than illustrated by an orchestra. The reform's doctrine can be put as a slogan the century turned into practice: the verse is the music. A production honouring it does not need to be told how the lovers feel by a string tremolo, because the lines, spoken at the reform's brisk tempo on an open stage, generate their own pressure. The ball and the band return to their textual scale, small bands of visible players, and the tomb recovers its silence, which audiences formed by melodrama at first found bare and then found frightening, the intended effect arriving a century late.

The reform also changed the evidence base for this whole subject, in a way the survey depends on and should acknowledge. Editions followed productions: as the platform text displaced the acting version, editors printed the musicians' scene entire, marked the ball's direction plainly, and stopped regularizing the cues into a continuous musical scaffolding. Levenson's Oxford edition, the performance-centred text this series cites alongside Weis, is the reform's scholarly heir, treating staging evidence, including musical staging, as part of what an editor must report; Weis's conservatism and Levenson's performance brief disagree about how far an editor may go in supplying what the quartos omit, and on music the disagreement has a clean practical form. Weis's text gives a music director three firm cues and a set of implications; Levenson's gives a richer performance record in which later centuries' scoring is documented as evidence about the play's possibilities. For the survey, the adjudication is functional: cite Weis for what the text demands, cite the performance record for what theatres have supplied, and never let the second masquerade as the first. That rule, trivially obvious once stated, is violated by a surprising share of programme notes, which attribute Victorian emotional machinery to Shakespeare's intentions with the quarto's "Music plays" produced as the warrant.

### What did the platform reform change about the play's music?

The reform abolished the score's infrastructure: continuous action on an open stage removed the scene changes the entr'actes existed to cover, and with them went the underscoring, the overture and the conducted emotion. What survived was the text's own ration, a ball band, a refused tune and a silent tomb, heard again as design rather than as poverty.

## The Reform's Long Shadow: What Subtraction Taught the Century

The reform's settlement lasted, as doctrine, for most of the twentieth century, and its longevity deserves explanation, because it shaped the conditions under which every later system in this survey negotiated. Its power was institutional as much as aesthetic: the platform text became the scholarly text, the scholarly text became the school text, and generations of practitioners met the play first in editions that printed the musicians' scene entire and the ball's direction bare, so that the reformed disposition of sound felt like the play itself rather than like a party's victory. Directors who added music after the reform therefore added it knowingly, as a decision requiring defence, where their Victorian grandfathers had added it as a department requires a budget. That inversion of the burden of proof is the reform's deepest legacy, and the survey's standard inherits it: added scoring must justify itself scene by scene against a text whose silences are presumed intentional, while the text's own cues need no defence at all. The mid-century resident composer worked under that burden and mostly honoured it; the amplified era challenged it at the ball and honoured it at the tomb; the soundscape has internalized it as the doctrine of rationing. Even the productions that defy the standard define themselves against it, advertising their scores as concepts and their silences as choices, which is the reform's victory wearing the dress of its opponents. Poel's platform and Granville-Barker's prefaces thus govern the subject's present tense more completely than any system that replaced them, because they settled not what the play's music is but who must argue, and about what, whenever it changes.

## Resident Composers and Functional Scores: The Middle of the Twentieth Century

Between the reform's subtraction and the present day's sound design stands the mid-twentieth-century settlement, in which the large companies professionalized music as a department and the incidental score returned, chastened, as a craft answerable to the production rather than to the audience's tear ducts. The type of the settlement is the resident composer: the Royal Shakespeare Company's long association with Guy Woolfenden, who joined the company in the early 1960s and scored its Shakespeare for decades, made the composer a member of the production team, writing to rehearsal, to the director's concept and to the verse's tempo, in forces the studio and main house could actually field. At Stratford a generation earlier, Peter Brook's 1947 production had carried incidental music by Roberto Gerhard, a composer of concert-hall standing, and the pairing is prophetic: the score as a serious musician's response to a serious staging, functional in length, modern in idiom, and content to fall silent. The Old Vic's 1960 production, whose casting and design this series has examined, belongs to the same settlement, its music credited within a company economy that treated the composer as it treated the fight arranger, as a named professional solving a defined problem. The mid-century score's signature is restraint under commission: overtures shrink to preludes or vanish, underscoring thins to transitions and dances, and the cues the text demands, the ball above all, are composed with a care that advertises the department's standards.

The settlement's weakness was the obverse of its strength. A department must be used; a resident composer must compose; and the mid-century productions drifted, at their least vigilant, toward scoring as house style, a pleasant modern wash laid over scenes the reform would have left bare, neither Garrick's imposition nor the platform's austerity but a habit. The critics of the period are instructive precisely because they so rarely mention the music at all, a silence this survey reads in two directions. Charitably, functional scoring had achieved invisibility, doing its work below the threshold of notice. Severely, music had become the least examined element of the production, praised as atmosphere when it was praised at all, and the vocabulary for judging it had decayed since the Victorians, who at least knew a score was an argument. The disagreement between those readings is adjudicated in the next section's evidence, the review record; here the survey registers the settlement's achievement in its own terms. The mid-century companies solved the proportion problem that convicted the Victorians: their scores do less, at fewer moments, with named authorship and rehearsal-room accountability. What they had not yet solved was the source problem: whether the sound world of Verona should be composed new for each production at all, or found, in the musics the audience already carries in its ears.

## What the Resident Composer Actually Wrote: Craft, Cue Sheets and Restraint

The mid-century settlement repays a closer look at its working methods, because its restraint was a technique before it was a taste. A resident composer wrote from the rehearsal room, timing cues against the production's actual tempo rather than against a printed text's imagined one, and the cue sheet, the document that governed the band's night, is the era's characteristic artefact: a list of numbered cues, each fixed to a line or an action, each scored for the forces available, each capable of being cut, extended or vamped in performance as the staging required. The flexibility was the point. Against the Victorian library score, a fixed commodity rented to strangers, the resident score was a bespoke instrument that belonged to one production and died with it, which is why so much mid-century stage music survives only as a credit line and a reviewer's adjective. The system traded posterity for fit, and the trade was deliberate: music conceived as part of a staging's total design has no more reason to outlive the staging than its lighting plot does.

Within that craft, the ball posed the era's defining problem in miniature. A resident composer inherited three irreconcilable instructions: the fiction wants a hired dance band, the audience wants the scene's festivity to register at full theatrical voltage, and the production's concept wants a sound world consistent with its Verona, whether that Verona is Renaissance, Risorgimento or postwar. The solutions on record span the range the problem allows: period-inflected composition for visible strings, in the antiquarian manner the reform had legitimised; full-blooded romantic scoring from the pit, the Victorian solution in modern dress; and the characterful compromise, a small onstage band amplified and arranged beyond its acoustic means, the fiction's payroll wearing the pit's power. Gerhard's presence at Stratford in 1947 stands at the head of this history as its most distinguished name, a modernist of European standing writing inside a verse production's discipline, and his example licensed two generations of composers to treat the play's music as composition rather than arrangement. The survey's judgement on the settlement can now be completed: its scores served their productions better than any earlier system served theirs, and its failure, where it failed, was the failure of all bespoke craft in a repertory economy, that the habit of commissioning outlived the occasions that justified it, until the soundscape arrived to ask whether the commission should go to a composer at all.

## Songs the Audience Already Knows: Amplification and the Modern-Dress Settlement

The late twentieth century answered the source problem by raiding the audience's own memory. Modern-dress production, which clothes Verona in the audience's century, discovered that its sound could be clothed the same way: amplified bands at the ball playing the dance music of the moment, recorded tracks under the street scenes, love themes quarried from the charts. Michael Bogdanov's 1986 staging for the Royal Shakespeare Company, whose modern-dress Verona this series has examined as a production, is the era's emblem in this survey as in others: a Verona of scooters and sunglasses whose sound world was the audience's Italy of the imagination, contemporary, amplified and knowing. The device has a logic the purists underrate. The Elizabethan ball worked because its tunes were the hearers' own dance music, socially exact and bodily familiar; a period consort before a modern audience produces admiration, not recognition, and the modern-dress ball that plays the audience's music restores the original relation between the scene and its hearers at the price of the original notes. Sternfeld's structuralism supports the trade more readily than his reputation suggests: if the ball's music functions as festivity the audience must feel in its own pulse, then the function is served by any idiom the house can dance to in its seats, and betrayed by an antiquarian prettiness it can only observe.

The costs are real and the survey should itemize them. Amplification flattens the acoustic geography the text exploits, the difference between music inside the fiction, played by hired hands in the room, and music laid over it from the booth, since a amplified track has no employer and cannot be refused by anybody's Peter. Recorded pop carries its own words and associations into scenes that must compete with them, and a famous song at the ball imports a lyric argument the production did not write. The tomb, in the amplified era, acquired a pulse: low electronic pressure under the final scene, defended as the sound of the vault and the blood, and attacked, by the reform's heirs, as the Victorian swell returned in a synthesizer's clothing. The parallel is exact enough to be the survey's judgement on the period: the technology changed, the temptation did not, and the productions that resisted it at the tomb while exploiting it at the ball were applying, consciously or not, the text's own distribution of sound, festivity amplified, catastrophe bare.

The pop ball, examined closely, shows the era solving the text's own equations with new variables. The measures' function was to delay Romeo's approach until recognition could ripen, and a contemporary dance track performs the delay with the audience's complicity, since the house knows the song's length as the Elizabethans knew a measure's, and feels the approach timed against a ending everyone can predict. The sonnet inside the noise, which the consort covered by convention, the amplified ball covers by volume and by the social licence of a party at full tilt, and the recognition's danger is sharpened by the same means, since a room enjoying itself at that level has the furthest to fall when the name is spoken. Where the device fails, it fails by surplus: a set piece so pleasurable that the production cannot get its tragedy back, the ball remembered as the evening's peak and the deaths as its epilogue. The Elizabethan structure guarded against exactly that surfeit by rationing, one dance sequence in five acts, and the modern productions that hold their ball to the text's proportion, however contemporary the idiom, are obeying the oldest instruction in the subject, that festivity in this play is a scarce resource whose scarcity is its meaning.

The boundary of this survey is crossed, instructively, by the adaptations that left the spoken play altogether, and two of them mark the fence. Prokofiev's ballet, whose score rebuilt the tragedy as dance from its famous Dance of the Knights onward, is the neighbouring art at its greatest: a complete musical Verona with no words at all, treated in its own study of [Prokofiev's ballet and the Dance of the Knights](/2028/11/24/prokofiev-ballet-dance-of-knights/). Leonard Bernstein's score for the Broadway transposition did the same work inside musical theatre, where the ball becomes a dance at a gym and the feud acquires a mambo, in the adaptation examined as [West Side Story on Broadway](/2028/08/25/west-side-story-broadway-1957/). Both works prove, by their success on the far side of the fence, the survey's premise from inside it: the story's musical charge is so strong that whole masterpieces have been built by releasing it from the text. The operatic afterlife, Gounod's above all, belongs to the Italian reception and its studies, and is registered here at the fence's third post: opera took the love music and the tomb and left the musicians' scene, inevitably, behind, since an art in which everyone sings cannot stage a refusal to play. What remains inside the fence, the spoken production and its scoring problem, is the harder and less glamorous subject, and the one this survey owns.

## The Electronic Soundscape: Sound as a Design Material

The twenty-first-century stage completes the survey's arc by dissolving the category of the score into the wider category of sound. The contemporary production's sound designer, a credited head of department alongside the set and lighting designers, composes the whole acoustic environment: the city's bells and traffic under the street scenes, the pressure change as the tomb's door opens, the subsonic pulse that tells a body before a mind that the feud has turned mortal, and, at the ball, a sound system that can place the band inside the room or inside the audience's ribs at the designer's choice. Two gains over every earlier settlement are structural. The soundscape can be diegetic at scale, building the fiction's own noises, the market, the watch, the distant church, into a continuous Verona that the consort could only hint at and the Victorian orchestra could only pave. And it can be withdrawn with a precision no orchestra matches, dropping the tomb into a true, engineered silence that makes a footstep an event. The best contemporary sound design is therefore the reform's heir by technological means: an economy of sound, rationed and placed, whose withholding is composed as carefully as its utterance.

Its characteristic failure is the Victorian failure in modern dress, and the survey's verdict on the era is the verdict on the century before it, adjusted for the new means. A soundscape that never stops, a low wash of city and pulse laid under every scene to glue the evening together, is the entr'acte system rebuilt in software, and it exacts the entr'acte's price: the play's contrasts flatten, the festive scenes lose their privilege of being the loud ones, and the catastrophe is deprived of the silence the text assigns it. The difference is that the modern failure is harder to hear happening, since the wash sits below the threshold of attention where a string swell announced itself. Reviewers, whose predecessors at least noticed orchestras, write about sound design in a vocabulary of immersion and atmosphere that records everything except judgement. Against that drift, the survey's standard stays where the text put it: music and sound earn their place scene by scene, inside the fiction where possible, refused where the fiction refuses, and silent at the tomb. A production's soundscape can be audited against that standard as exactly as its casting, and the audit is this article's practical bequest to its readers.

The designer's Verona is also, for the first time in the survey, a Verona that can be heard thinking. Spatial sound lets a production place the feud in the building: a wedding bell answered across the stage by a rival bell, the watch's approach arriving through the audience before it arrives onstage, the ball's music leaking through a wall into the street where Tybalt waits, each effect an argument about the city's divisions conducted in acoustics. The ball scene gains a dimension no earlier settlement could give it, since the design can now hold the dance at full festive power in one part of the space while thinning it, door by door, toward the orchard's quiet, staging the lovers' withdrawal from the city's sound as a literal journey across the sound field. Such writing is composition by any honest definition, whatever the department is called, and the survey files the soundscape's best practitioners where they belong, in the line of the play's composers, with the caveat their own craft insists on: their instrument includes the noises the fiction makes for itself, and their scores are judged, as the consort's were, by how exactly the sound answers to the event.

### How does an electronic soundscape differ from an incidental score?

A score is composed music, authored and notated, laid over or inside the action at chosen moments; a soundscape is the whole designed acoustic environment, music, noise, pressure and silence together, built by a sound department rather than an orchestra. The shift moves authorship from the composer to the designer and makes silence itself one of the materials under composition.

## One Cue, Every Answer: The Funeral as the Survey's Test Case

The whole argument of this survey can be cross-examined at a single point of the play, and the point is the funeral. The text equips the moment with an anti-score: a formal spoken lament by the family, and then the band's refusal, so that the audience watches grief performed in words and watches music decline to perform it. Ask each era what it did at that point and the era confesses its whole system. The early stage, on the evidence of the text it played, let the refusal stand as the moment's music, the sound of pipes being put away. The eighteenth century, in Garrick's hands, overruled the refusal with a composed dirge, and the overruling required the scene that contained the refusal to shrink, so that the funeral's sound and the scene's survival traded against each other in the acting version like two tenants of one room. The nineteenth century industrialized Garrick's answer: the procession acquired orchestral ceremony, the lament acquired underscoring, and the refusal disappeared entirely, so that the era's funeral is the text's funeral with its guard removed and its volume raised, a rite the audience is conducted through by professionals. The reform restored the guard and removed the rite, recovering in the spoken lament and the packed-up pipes a bleakness the pictorial stage had forgotten the play contained. The mid-century companies, characteristically, negotiated: a brief composed lament for strings at the procession, the refusal retained in shortened form, the two answers spliced into an evening that hoped to keep the rite's beauty and the refusal's point, and sometimes did. The amplified era gave the funeral the audience's own mourning music, songs of the charts' bereavement, an answer whose emotional directness is undeniable and whose fiction is nobody's: Verona has no such song, the mourners cannot hear it, and the moment belongs wholly to the auditorium. The soundscape, at its most disciplined, has restored the refusal's logic in its own medium, letting the city's sound thin and fail around the procession until the act ends in the acoustic the text prescribes.

Two conclusions follow from the test case, and they are the survey's conclusions in concentrated form. The first is that additions at this cue are never local. Each era's funeral music reorganized the act around it: the dirge needed the scene cut, the orchestral procession needed the lament conducted, the restored refusal needed the evening's tempo protected from the entr'acte habit, so that a decision about forty bars of music turns out to be a decision about the production's whole relation to the text. Scoring is interpretation not because it colours scenes but because it load-bears: remove an era's funeral music and its staging collapses into incoherence, which is the definition of a structural choice. The second conclusion is that the refusal has proved indestructible. Cut for two centuries, it returned with the text in the reform and has survived every subsequent system, because it solves a problem every production feels, how to keep a famous grief from becoming a performed routine. The band that will not play is the play's own review of its funeral, filed in advance, and the eras read, in the end, by whether they dared to let it be heard. A student who masters this one cue's history possesses the survey in embryo, and the table's rows can be reconstructed from it: each era's ball, band and tomb are its funeral answer, generalized.

## The People in the Pit: Music Directors, Bands and Departments

Every row of the survey's table was executed by musicians whose working arrangements left marks on the sound, and a performance history that ignores the labour force mistakes its subject. The Georgian band was a house institution, its leader a figure of the theatre's public life, its members engaged across the season's whole miscellaneous bill; the music it made for the tragedy was made by men who had played the afterpiece the night before and would play a benefit concert the night after, and the era's additions, the dance novelty and the dirge, were shaped to such an institution's strengths, spectacle and song. The Victorian music director rose with the score's importance into a named artistic officer, billed in programmes, credited in reviews when the score was a selling point, and answerable for the evening's continuous emotional management; the published parts that survive are his paperwork, and their completeness is the trace of his authority. The reform dismissed that officer with his system, and for a generation the play's music reverted to the ad hoc: a small band engaged for the ball, a musical amateur among the actors, silence elsewhere, an arrangement whose poverty the reformers counted as honesty.

The mid-century department rebuilt the office on new terms. The resident composer and the head of music in the large companies professionalized the supply without restoring the catalogue score, and the sound designer's arrival completed the office's migration from the pit to the production team: the contemporary head of sound sits in design meetings, not in the band room, and the musicians, where living players remain, are engaged as one element of the design among recordings, effects and silence. The trajectory explains a paradox of the modern record, that productions have never been more thoroughly sounded or less often served by players the audience can see. The text's own arrangement, a band on the payroll and on the stage, haggling in the fourth act over the price of feeling, stands at the head of this institutional history as its first and sharpest commentator: the play knew, before any of its theatres did, that the question of who pays the musicians is a question about what the music means. A Verona that employs its band gets one kind of ball; a production that hides its sound department gets another kind of evening, and the survey's tables of eras are, at bottom, tables of employments.

## Silence, Engineered: The Soundscape's Debt to the Original Stage

One practice of the contemporary stage deserves the survey's particular notice because it closes the historical circle with an elegance the participants do not always claim for it. The reconstructed playhouses and the original-practices movement restored the visible band as a matter of doctrine, placing costumed musicians in the gallery and the fiction's employ, and in doing so they demonstrated, to audiences who had never read the scholarship, the truth of the survey's central claim: a ball played by hands in the room behaves differently in the drama from the same notes arriving from a booth, because the dancers can tire, the players can be watched, and the music's cessation registers as an act performed by people rather than a fader's decision. The Globe's original-practices stagings of the play are the companion survey's ground and are cited here for their musical finding alone, that a small expert band in shared light recovers the ball's social exactitude, the sound of a household's hired pleasure, with an immediacy no pit orchestra has matched since the fiction's payroll was abolished. The electronic era learned the lesson in its own idiom: the most admired sound designs of the recent stage are those that restore locality and agency to sound, placing sources in the space, letting actors switch the music on and off inside the fiction, and treating the booth's invisibility as a temptation rather than a tool. Four centuries of added machinery thus arrive back at the disposition of the 1590s, a Verona whose music is made by Veronese, with the whole history of the pit standing between as the record of a long, expensive and frequently beautiful detour.

## What the Critics Heard: Music in the Review Record

A survey of stage music runs on evidence of an unusually perishable kind, and its last analytical task is to grade that evidence before resting a verdict on it. Scores survive when they were published, as the Victorian industry published them, or preserved in company archives with their parts and cue sheets; promptbooks note music cues in a shorthand that assumes the reader knows the band; reviews, the richest source for the earlier centuries, describe music last and least, in adjectives that date faster than any other part of the notice. The performance criticism of the play has always subordinated sound to sight, and the student of this subject learns to read the record against its grain: a complaint about an evening's length may be a complaint about its entr'actes, a eulogy of atmosphere may be the only surviving trace of a score, and the silence of the notices about the 1960 Old Vic's music, in a production whose design and casting were minutely reported, is itself a datum about the mid-century settlement's achieved invisibility. The methods for handling such evidence, the grading of proof, inference and silence that the study of the play's staging has had to develop, are set out in the companion account of [performance criticism and the living text](/2027/05/07/performance-criticism-living-text/), and this survey has followed them throughout: documented where the parts and bills survive, inferential where the fiction and the company's known practice converge, and silent, pointedly, where the record is silent, rather than filling the gaps with the sound of certainty.

That discipline requires one final adjudication, the disagreement on which the whole survey turns, stated now in its sharpest form. Sternfeld's structural history holds that the tragedy's music is a designed system whose meaning lies in its placement and rationing, and that later centuries are to be judged by their fidelity to that system. Gurr's documentary history holds that the system's original material basis was thinner than the structuralists assume, that the early stage's music was casual, hired and limited, and that reconstructions of a rich Elizabethan sound world are romances. Levenson's editorial practice, standing between them, treats the performance record itself as the text's continuing life, in which Garrick's dirge and the Victorian score are not corruptions of a pure original but evidence of what the play has been able to mean. The survey's verdict divides the honours by question. On the original means, Gurr's caution stands: the resident consort is unproved, and honesty requires the hedge. On the original design, Sternfeld stands: whatever the means, the text's distribution of music, festive, refused and withheld, is too patterned to be accident, and it remains the standard against which additions are measured. On the later centuries, Levenson's frame stands, with Sternfeld's standard held inside it: the dirge and the scores are the play's life in other ages, neither to be worshipped as the text nor dismissed as noise, but to be judged, as this survey has judged them, by whether their additions understood the design they were adding to. The dirge understood the funeral's hunger and betrayed its guard; the Victorian score understood the audience and paved the design over; the reform recovered the design and starved the evening; the soundscape, at its best, is the first settlement since the original that can hold design and richness together, because it can build Verona's whole sound world and still leave the tomb in silence.

The promptbook evidence has a grammar of its own that the survey's reader must learn, since it is the closest thing the subject has to a recording. A prompter's music cues are typically a word and a number in a margin, "music", "soft music", "dirge", timed to a line the copyist abbreviates, with the band's book, where it survives, supplying the notes those words summoned. The system presumes a shared practice between the desk and the pit that the documents do not spell out, so that the historian reconstructs an era's sound from the pattern of its summonses: how many cues, at which joints of the action, swelling where the modern reader expects silence and absent where the modern reader expects support. The Victorian promptbooks are loquacious by this standard, their cues dense enough to map the underscoring system scene by scene; the reform's are terse to the point of polemic, whole acts passing with the ball's cue and nothing else, the terseness itself a manifesto the survey can read. Even the failures of the record are evidence of a kind: when a promptbook's music cues stop at the interval and the reviews complain of a long evening, the inference that the second half's scoring went unrehearsed, or unplayed, is the kind of graded conjecture this discipline lives on, proof and inference and silence weighed in the open rather than blended into narrative certainty.

### Why do reviews so rarely describe a production's music?

Reviews describe what their vocabulary reaches first: acting, design and direction carry established critical languages, while music in a spoken production falls between the theatre critic's brief and the music critic's, and is praised as atmosphere or blamed as intrusion in terms that record an effect without analysing its cause. The survey's evidence problem begins in that division of labour.

## Verdict: Keep the Music Inside the Fiction

The performance history of the play's music, surveyed whole, describes a long negotiation with a short text. The text calls for a band at a ball, a band at a wedding, a refusal, and silence. Everything else, four centuries of it, is the theatre explaining itself: an age of attractions adding dances, an age of sentiment adding a dirge and then an orchestra, an age of reform subtracting everything, an age of departments composing responsibly, an age of amplification borrowing the audience's own songs, and an age of design composing the air itself. The verdict this survey has defended is that the text's distribution is wiser than any of its supplements taken alone, and that the supplements succeed exactly in proportion as they understand it. Music inside the fiction, played by hands the audience can see, hired by the world it serves, carries meaning no pit can supply, because it can be refused, interrupted, danced to and stopped, and each of those events is dramatic. Music outside the fiction, however beautifully composed, addresses the audience over the characters' heads, and the play, whose catastrophe is a failure of messages to arrive, has a built-in resistance to being told what to feel by a channel its people cannot hear. The finest modern practice has learned to have both by subordinating the second to the first: a soundscape that builds Verona's world and falls silent at its tomb, a ball band the fiction employs, a funeral where the only music is the one the tradesmen will not play. That settlement, reached by four centuries of experiment, is the Elizabethan disposition of the play's sound, recovered at last with better tools. The cliche forgets the stage music because the stage music, at its best, disappears into the fiction. Restoring it to view shows the interpretation that was sounding all along.

The bequest to the next production is a checklist short enough to pin inside a promptbook. Play the ball with musicians the fiction could have hired, at a festivity the audience can feel in its own pulse, and let the music's stopping be an event. Keep the wedding band visible, named and priced, and let its refusal land at full weight, in prose, without a note of help from anywhere. Guard the tomb's silence as the production's most expensive effect, the one all the evening's sound has been spent to purchase. Audit every added note against the question the tradesmen ask by refusing: does this sound answer to the event, or does it merely answer to the audience's wish to be accompanied? And when the record of the production is written, write the music down, its forces, its cues and its silences, with the same care as the casting, because the survey of the next four centuries will be built from such records, and the eras that failed to leave them are the eras whose sound has had to be guessed. The play's first band packed up its pipes and left no playlist. Its historians have been reconstructing the silence ever since, and the reconstruction, done honestly, is the subject this article has tried to serve.

A last word on proportion, since the survey's standard can be misread as puritanism. The argument has never been that the play wants little music in the theatre; it wants its music placed, owned and answerable, and within those terms it welcomes abundance. The ball should blaze. The wedding should promise a street full of sound. The refusal should hurt precisely because the evening has taught the audience to love the band's playing. What the standard forbids is only the transfer, music slipping its employment in the fiction to become the management's commentary on the fiction, swelling where the tradesmen would have stopped. Judged by that standard, the four centuries divide not into the musical and the unmusical but into the eras that trusted the play's distribution of sound and the eras that improved on it, and the improvement, however gorgeous, is where the meaning leaks.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch stage-music survey, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch stage-music survey is this article's table of the play's scoring across six eras, setting each period's musical forces beside its treatment of the text-called cues and its added scoring. The pattern it exposes is an asymmetry: the text-called column barely changes across four centuries, a ball, a wedding band, a refusal and a silent tomb, while the added column swells from nothing to a full orchestra and contracts again to an electronic soundscape. The table therefore reads as a history of theatrical belief, each era's additions confessing what that era thought an audience's feelings required and who should be trusted to produce them.

### Q: At which moments does the text call for music to be played?

The text's demands are few and firm. The Capulet ball requires dance music under the direction that music plays and the guests dance (1.5). The wedding morning requires the hired band whose arrival Capulet promises and whose members then appear as named characters (4.4 and 4.5). Around those certainties stand implications: processional expectation at the wedding, festivity inside the feast scenes. Beyond them the text calls for nothing, and the nothing is patterned: no music is requested at the tomb, in the duels' aftermath or over either death, so that the tragedy's catastrophe unfolds in an enforced quiet that productions disturb at their interpretive peril.

### Q: What is the difference between text-called music and an incidental score?

Text-called music is demanded by the play itself at named moments and is usually audible to the characters: the ball's dancing, the wedding band's attendance. An incidental score is music a production adds from outside the fiction, overtures, entr'actes and underscoring that the characters cannot hear and that addresses the audience directly. The distinction governs the whole history of the subject, because the two kinds of music do different work and fail in different ways: text-called music can be refused, interrupted or danced to inside the story, while incidental music can only swell or stop, and its history is the history of theatres deciding how much of the audience's feeling to conduct from the pit.

### Q: Which instruments would a Capulet ball consort have used?

The play names its band by joke: Simon Catling for catgut strings, Hugh Rebeck for the rebec, James Soundpost for the fiddle's inner post, so bowed strings stand at the centre of the evidence. Around them, the period's dance bands added flute or recorder and plucked strings such as lute, cittern and bandora, the mixture called a broken consort. Whether the first performances fielded the full mixture is disputed between historians who credit the company with a skilled consort and those who restrict the outdoor stage to hired fiddles, flutes, drums and trumpets; a small expert string band is the reading both bodies of evidence can support.

### Q: Why is no tune named for the ball in any early text?

No tune is named because the early texts treat the ball's music as a function, not a composition: the direction requires that music play and the guests dance, and the choice of measures belonged to the musicians the company hired, exactly as the Capulets hire theirs inside the fiction. Dance music was the band's stock in trade, drawn from the shared repertoire of measures and galliards that every professional player carried. The absence has governed the scene's afterlife, since every later production must compose, borrow or reconstruct what the quarto leaves open, making the ball's sound the purest case of scoring as interpretation: the text guarantees that music sounds, and guarantees nothing about which music it is.

### Q: What tunes does Peter name in the musicians' scene, and are any of them played?

Peter names three: "Heart's ease", the lively country dance he asks for; "My heart is full", the grave tune he says his own heart is playing; and a "merry dump", his self-contradictory request, a dump being a mournful air by definition. None of them is played. The First Musician refuses on the ground that it is no time to play, and the band packs up its pipes and leaves, so the scene's entire musical content is a negotiation about music that never sounds. The refusal is the point: the play stages the ethics of its own sound, a band with professional honour declining to decorate a death, and the named tunes survive as titles only, "Heart's ease" in the dance collections, "My heart is full" nowhere at all.

### Q: How did the Drury Lane dirge change the sound of the play's ending?

Garrick's production gave Juliet a sung funeral: a dirge with words by Garrick and music by William Boyce, performed by a processing chorus as the heroine was carried to the tomb. The change moved music from inside the fiction to outside it, replacing the quarto's spoken lament and its band of musicians who refuse to play with composed song addressed to the audience's feelings. It held the stage for generations because it satisfied a hunger the text deliberately leaves unsatisfied, the hunger for ritual. Its survey significance is that every later scoring debate descends from it: the Victorians completed the transfer it began, the reformers reversed it, and modern sound design disguises it.

### Q: What work did entr'acte music do in a Victorian production?

Entr'acte music covered the pictorial stage's scene changes, which could take minutes of carpentry behind the curtain, and it worked on the audience while it worked for the stagehands: it carried the previous scene's mood across the interval, prepared the next scene's atmosphere, and kept the house inside the fiction's emotional weather instead of releasing it to the lobby. Together with the overture and the underscoring under dialogue, the entr'actes formed a continuous system in which feeling was conducted without pause. The system was superb engineering and questionable interpretation, since it abolished the contrasts, above all the silences, on which the text's own sound design depends.

### Q: Why did platform-stage reformers strip the incidental score away?

The reformers, Poel and Granville-Barker chief among them, rebuilt staging around continuous action on an open platform, and continuous action abolished the score's infrastructure: with no scene changes to cover, the entr'acte had no function, and underscoring stood exposed as an intrusion between the verse and the hearer. Their doctrine held that the verse is itself the production's music, its tempo and pressure generated by speech at speed, and that an orchestra telling the audience what the lines were already saying merely duplicated and coarsened the effect. Stripping the score away restored the text's own economy, a ball band, a refused tune and a silent tomb, to audibility after a century of orchestral upholstery.

### Q: What changed when the large companies kept a resident composer?

The resident composer made scoring a rehearsal-room craft instead of a library purchase. In the Royal Shakespeare Company's long association with Guy Woolfenden from the early 1960s, and in Stratford's earlier commissioning of Roberto Gerhard for the 1947 production, the composer joined the production team, wrote to the director's concept and the verse's tempo, and worked in forces the company could field nightly. The change professionalized restraint: scores became shorter, more functional and more accountable than the Victorian catalogue system they replaced. Its risk was habit, music supplied because a department existed to supply it, a wash of competent sound whose presence no single decision had quite authorized.

### Q: How do amplified songs function in a modern-dress production?

Amplified songs restore the ball's original relation between the scene and its audience. The first hearers knew the dance tunes in their bodies; a period consort before a modern house produces admiration without recognition, while the audience's own music, played loud by a visible band in modern dress, makes the festivity bodily again and catches the recognitions inside a sound the room is enjoying too much to want stopped. The device extends beyond the ball when productions lay recorded tracks under street scenes, borrowing a song's stored feeling for the feud. Its dangers are competition and importation: a famous lyric argues its own case inside a scene that did not write it.

### Q: What does an electronic soundscape replace in a modern staging?

A soundscape replaces the orchestra as the production's acoustic author. Where the Victorian system supplied composed music at set points, the sound designer composes the whole environment: the city's bells and markets, weather, crowd pressure, the acoustic of the tomb, and electronic pulses beneath the action, all placed in space and mixed nightly. It also replaces the entr'acte's glue with continuous atmosphere, which is its temptation as well as its power. At its best the soundscape restores the text's economy by other means, building the fiction's own noises and withdrawing them into engineered silence; at its worst it is the Victorian wash rebuilt in software, sound that never stops and therefore never means.

### Q: How does music underline menace in the street scenes?

Productions underline the streets' menace by making the feud audible before it is visible: a recurring figure or timbre attached to the houses' enmity, drums or low strings under the brawls' build-up, the city's festive noise curdling at the edges as a scene turns. The text offers the cue in its structure, since the brawls erupt out of the same public world that dances at the ball, and the strongest stagings keep the two sounds related, the street's pulse a coarsening of the ball's. Weak stagings import a generic thriller pressure that belongs to no Verona in particular. The test is specificity: menace that could underscore any tragedy is decoration, while menace grown from the production's own festive sound is interpretation.

### Q: Why does the tomb scene resist underscoring in most stagings?

The tomb resists underscoring because the text has already scored it, with silence. No music is called for anywhere in the final scene, and the catastrophe's horror depends on small sounds in a quiet place: a spade, a torch, a body's fall, a voice in the dark. Underscoring fills the acoustic space in which those sounds must register, conducts the audience's grief at the moment the play intends desolation, and converts a discovered horror into an accompanied one. Directors who add a low pulse defend it as the vault's atmosphere; the reform tradition answers that the atmosphere is the silence. The scene's resistance is thus a reading of the whole play: its festivity was loud, and its price is quiet.

### Q: How does the wedding music of the fourth act turn into the funeral's silence?

The fourth act is built as a musical promise broken. Capulet's preparations promise the wedding band's attendance; the bridegroom is expected with music; the musicians arrive as characters with names and instruments. Juliet's apparent death cancels the occasion, Peter begs the band for a dance tune as medicine, and the band refuses and departs, so the act's promised sound drains away through negotiation into nothing. The structure is the play in miniature: festivity's machinery, hired and ready, meets death and stops. Productions that cut the musicians' scene lose the draining and keep only the lament, which is why the act so often plays as unrelieved solemnity in shortened versions and as something stranger and colder in full ones.

### Q: What survives of the play's historical stage music, and in what form?

Survival follows publication. The Victorian era's incidental music survives best, because the industry issued scores in printed parts for hire, so complete production music can be reconstructed from catalogues and libraries. The eighteenth century survives in set pieces: Garrick's dirge in its printed setting, the advertised masquerade dance as a bill and a title. The Elizabethan ball survives hardly at all as this play's music, only as the period's dance repertoire from which any band would have drawn. Promptbooks and reviews fill the gaps unevenly, in shorthand and adjectives. The archive therefore flatters the eras that commodified their scores, and a survey has to correct for the flattery when it weighs the evidence.

### Q: How do editors mark music cues that the early texts leave implicit?

Editors divide by temperament. A conservative edition such as Weis's Arden text prints the quarto directions in their spareness, confines supplements to brackets or notes, and leaves a music director three firm cues and a field of implications plainly labelled as such. A performance-centred edition such as Levenson's Oxford text admits a freer hand, regularizing entrances and clarifying who plays when, on the ground that the performance record is part of the text's life. The practical consequence is real: the two kinds of edition hand a production different plays to score. The scholarly virtue is not in either temperament but in the marking, the reader's ability to tell the quarto's demand from the editor's assistance.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch stage-music survey to argue that scoring is interpretation?

Begin from the table's asymmetry: the text-called column constant, the added column swelling and shrinking with each era's beliefs. Choose the era whose addition is most exposed, the dirge that overruled a refusal or the soundscape that paved a silence, and show from the cue structure what the addition changed in the scene's meaning, not merely in its mood. Then test the counter-case, the production that honoured the refusal and the silence, and let the comparison carry the claim: since the same words supported both sound worlds, the difference between them is interpretation, located in the scoring as surely as in any cut or casting. Cite the survey's rows as the evidence base and name the era's forces precisely.

### Q: How does a production decide between live musicians and recorded music?

The decision is economic before it is aesthetic, and honest productions admit the order. Live musicians cost salaries across a run and buy what recording cannot: presence inside the fiction, tempo that breathes with the actors, and the possibility of being refused, interrupted or watched, all of which the text's own music scenes exploit. Recorded music buys precision, scale and effects no pit can field, at the price of fixity and of that visible employment. The text tilts the argument: a play that puts its band onstage, names its players and stages their wage dispute has declared for the living band, and productions that record the ball while staging the refusal are answering the play's question with the cheaper half of it.

### Q: What single change of scoring most alters the play's meaning?

The funeral decides it. A production that sings Juliet to her tomb, whether with Garrick's dirge, a Victorian chorus or a modern lament laid under the procession, declares that the catastrophe deserves ritual consolation and that the audience's grief should be accompanied. A production that holds to the text, spoken lament, a band that will not play, and silence thereafter, declares that the play's world offers no such consolation and that grief in Verona is unaccompanied labour. No other cue carries the same weight, because the choice is binary, public and final: it states, in sound, whether the tragedy ends in a rite or in a refusal, and every audience hears the statement whether or not it can name it.
