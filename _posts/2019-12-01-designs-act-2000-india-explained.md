The Designs Act 2000 protects the one feature of a product that a competitor can copy at a glance and that the copier's customers will never examine twice: how the product looks. A chair, a bottle, a sari border, the pattern pressed into a sheet of glass, the shape of a ceiling fan blade, all of these compete in the market partly on appearance, and appearance is uniquely exposed to imitation because it is displayed to every buyer, including every rival manufacturer, the moment the product is sold. The manufacturer who has spent money developing a look that sells has, without a design registration, almost no legal answer when a competitor adopts the same look the following season. The Act exists to give that manufacturer an answer, and it gives it on strict terms: protection only for appearance and never for function, protection only through registration obtained before the design is disclosed, and protection for a short period that cannot be extended beyond a fixed ceiling. Everything else in the statute is machinery for those three choices.

![The Designs Act 2000 and industrial design in India, covering the definition of a design, registration, the short term and the copyright interface - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-117.webp)

The Act is also the least understood member of the Indian intellectual property cluster, and the misunderstanding is consequential. Designers routinely assume that copyright protects the look of the products they sell, because copyright protected the drawing from which the product was made. Manufacturers routinely assume that a registration, once obtained, can be renewed like a trade mark, or that it covers the clever way a product works. Each assumption is wrong, and the errors are not gentle ones. The designer who relies on copyright discovers, after the fiftieth reproduction of the article, that the copyright has ceased by operation of a provision in the Copyright Act and that no design right was ever acquired because no application was filed. The manufacturer who treats a registration as permanent discovers at the end of fifteen years that the right has expired beyond recall. This article works through the Designs Act 2000 as it is written: its definition and the exclusions inside that definition, the conditions of registration, the procedure and the register, the term, the piracy provision and its remedies, the cancellation route by which weak registrations are destroyed, and the copyright interface that decides more real disputes than any other single rule in this corner of the law.

## What the Designs Act 2000 replaced

Design protection in India is older than most of the legal system that surrounds it. The Patents and Designs Act of 1872 first put design privileges on the statute book, the Inventions and Designs Act of 1888 followed, and the Patents and Designs Act of 1911 then governed inventions and designs in a single statute for most of the twentieth century. When the Patents Act 1970 separated patents into a statute of their own, the design provisions of the 1911 Act were left standing on their own, and they continued to govern Indian design law for a further three decades. The Designs Act 2000, Act 16 of 2000, repealed that remnant. It received the assent of the President on 25 May 2000 and was brought into force on 11 May 2001, and its savings provisions carried forward registrations and pending proceedings made under the older law so that the transition destroyed no existing right.

The 1911 framework had aged in ways that mattered. Its cancellation ground based on prior publication reached only publication inside India, so a design published abroad and copied into an Indian registration was difficult to attack, a defect the Bombay High Court litigation in the Faber-Castell pencil design dispute exposed and the new Act repaired by making disclosure anywhere in the world fatal. The older law also sat uneasily beside India's obligations under the TRIPS Agreement, which required members to protect independently created industrial designs that are new or original, and the 2000 Act was enacted in part to put Indian design law into the shape that obligation assumed. The new statute is a consolidating and amending Act rather than a revolution: much of the 1911 architecture survives, including the central idea that the right is called copyright in the design and is acquired only by registration. What the 2000 Act changed was the reach of the novelty requirement, the classification and administration, and the clarity of the remedies, and it stated the grounds on which a registration may be cancelled in a single provision.

What the new Act did not do is equally important for understanding the disputes that followed. It created no unregistered design right, so the designer who discloses first and files later, or never files, is outside the Act altogether. It left the term where the older pattern had put it, short by the standards of the neighbouring rights. And it left untouched the copyright provision, which lives in the Copyright Act 1957 and not in this Act, that cuts off copyright in an unregistered design after fifty industrial reproductions. The result is a statute whose most important practical rule is found in a different Act, a feature of the design system that this article returns to when it reaches the copyright interface.

One legacy of the long 1911 reign deserves recording because it still shapes instinct in the trade. Under the older law the design right grew up as a manufacturer's right, obtained by mills, foundries and consumer goods companies as a routine incident of product development, and the register's culture followed: portfolios held by companies, filings made by agents as part of product launch paperwork, and very little sense of the individual designer as the system's subject. The 2000 Act's later fee concessions for natural persons, small entities and startups are best read as a deliberate correction of that inheritance, an attempt to widen the register's constituency beyond the firms that had always used it. Whether the correction has changed who files is a question the published material does not answer to the standard this article requires, and it is left open; what can be said is that the statutory design of the right, cheap, quick and visual, suits the individual designer better than any other registered right Indian law offers, and the barrier that remains is knowledge of the system rather than its price.

## Why the 2000 Act took this shape

The policy stated for the 2000 Act, in the materials that accompanied its passage, was that the 1911 regime no longer served an industrial economy of India's scale: the object was a more effective and contemporary protection for designs, machinery to encourage design activity in Indian industry, and alignment with the international obligations and practice that TRIPS had made the common frame. Each element of the statute maps to one of those stated purposes. The worldwide novelty standard and the Locarno-based classification are the international alignment. The short term and the registration machinery are the effectiveness choice: a right designed to be obtained quickly by producers rather than litigated into existence by lawyers. The retention of the artistic work and trade mark exclusions, and the refusal of protection to function, are the older lineage showing through, the judgement of the 1911 draftsmen that appearance, function and reputation are three different assets that three different registers should govern. The comparison with the British model from which the lineage descends is instructive on the term: the United Kingdom's Registered Designs Act 1949, the statute the 1911 Indian Act's successors most resembled, protected a design for an initial five years extendable by two further five year periods, a fifteen year ceiling reached in three steps. India's ten plus five reaches the same ceiling in two steps and front-loads the certainty: the Indian proprietor holds a decade without any renewal act at all, which suits a register used heavily by small producers for whom every renewal deadline is a forfeiture risk.

## How the Act is put together

The Act is a compact statute of forty-eight sections arranged in eleven chapters, and its centre of gravity sits in four places. The definitions in Section 2 fix the boundaries of the subject matter. The registration provisions, running from Section 4 through the sections on the register and the certificate, fix who may obtain the right and on what conditions. Section 11 confers the right itself, which the Act calls copyright in the design. And Sections 19 and 22 supply the two ways the right is tested and enforced: cancellation of the registration before the Controller, and the piracy action before the courts.

The Act should also be read with its rules open beside it, because the Designs Rules carry the operating detail the statute deliberately omits: the forms of application, the manner of preparing and filing representations, the classification of articles, the fees and their concessions, and the procedure on examination, hearing, restoration and cancellation. Much of what practitioners call design law, the number of representations, the endorsement practice, the classification of a novel product, is rule-level law, and it changes by notification without Parliament's involvement, as the classification amendment of 2021 showed. The statute fixes the rights; the rules fix the route to them, and a reading of either without the other mistakes the system in opposite directions.

Administration sits with the Controller-General of Patents, Designs and Trade Marks, whom the Act calls the Controller, and the Designs Wing of the Patent Office is located at Kolkata, the head office of the organisation. Applications may be lodged through the patent office network, but design administration is centred on Kolkata, and the register of designs, in which every registration is entered and which the public may inspect, is maintained under the Controller's authority. The register records the design number, the class in which it is registered, the date of registration, the name and address of the proprietor, and the particulars of assignments and licences that are later brought onto it. The Controller's decisions on registration and cancellation are appealable to the High Court, a feature that matters when the cancellation provisions are considered below.

Three definitions do most of the work in Section 2, and the article takes them in the order in which a dispute meets them. An article, under Section 2(a), means any article of manufacture and any substance, artificial or partly artificial and partly natural, and includes any part of an article capable of being made and sold separately, which is why a component sold on its own, such as a bottle cap or a handle, can carry a design even though it is also part of a larger product. Copyright, under Section 2(c), means the exclusive right to apply a design to any article in the class of articles in which the design is registered, and the use of the word copyright in this Act confuses many readers: it is a statutory label for the design right, not the copyright of the Copyright Act, and the two rights are different in source, term and behaviour. Original, under Section 2(g), in relation to a design, means originating from the author of the design, and the definition expressly includes designs which, though old in themselves, are new in their application to a particular article, a sentence that the Supreme Court's novelty jurisprudence has built on. The remaining definition, the definition of design itself in Section 2(d), is important enough to deserve its own section.

## The definition: what a design is, and what it is not

Section 2(d) is the provision on which the entire Act turns, and it repays slow reading, because each limb of the definition and each exclusion in it decides cases. A design means only the features of shape, configuration, pattern, ornament or composition of lines or colours applied to any article, whether in two dimensional or in three dimensional form or in both, by any industrial process or means, whether manual, mechanical or chemical, separate or combined, which in the finished article appeal to and are judged solely by the eye. The definition then excludes three things. It does not include any mode or principle of construction, or anything which is in substance a mere mechanical device. It does not include any trade mark as defined in the Trade and Merchandise Marks Act 1958, or any property mark as defined in Section 479 of the Indian Penal Code. And it does not include any artistic work as defined in Section 2(c) of the Copyright Act 1957.

Begin with what the definition includes, because the phrases carry weight. The protectable subject matter is features, not the article itself: the registration covers the shape or pattern as applied, and the same article bearing a different appearance is a different legal object. The features must be applied to an article, so a pattern floating free of any product is not a design, and the application must be by an industrial process or means, which is the phrase that separates designs from one-off handicraft in the statute's imagination, although the words manual, mechanical or chemical, separate or combined, make the industrial requirement wide. Above all, the features must, in the finished article, appeal to and be judged solely by the eye. The test is visual and it is applied to the article as finished and sold, not to drawings, not to prototypes, and not to the tooling used in manufacture. A feature that no buyer can see, or whose appeal is to the hand or the ear rather than the eye, sits outside the definition, which is why the internal ribbing of a container, invisible in the finished product, cannot be a design however ingenious it is.

### Why does design law judge a product by the eye alone?

The eye test confines the right to the market in appearances. A design competes for a buyer who sees the product before buying it, and the eye test makes the legal question match the commercial one: whether the look of the finished article, as a purchaser would take it in, carries the features the registration claims. Features that work rather than please are routed to patent law, and features no one can see cannot influence a purchase and so earn nothing.

The exclusions then do the boundary work, and each exclusion hands the subject matter to a different legal regime or to no regime at all. The exclusion of any mode or principle of construction, and of anything which is in substance a mere mechanical device, is the function exclusion, and it is the single most litigated boundary in the definition. A shape that exists because it must exist for the article to work, a configuration dictated by the mechanical task it performs, is not a design, however new it is. The Delhi High Court's reasoning in the construction equipment litigation in Escorts Construction Equipment v Action Construction Equipment applied exactly this logic to deny protection where the features claimed were the working features of the machine. The point is structural rather than hostile: functional innovation has a statute of its own, and the route to protecting a better way of making a product work runs through [the Patents Act 1970 and its twenty year term](/2019/08/15/patents-act-1970-explained/), with its disclosure bargain and its examination for inventive step. Design law refuses the functional claim not because function is unworthy but because granting a registration, obtained without substantive examination of inventiveness, over the way a machine works would hand out patent-strength monopolies at design-office prices.

The trade mark and property mark exclusions perform a quieter separation. A badge, emblem or device that functions as a mark of origin is not a design, and the definition refuses to let the design register become a side door into trade mark subject matter. The artistic work exclusion is the most consequential of the three, and it is deliberately one-sided in its drafting: it excludes artistic works from the definition of design, but it does not say that a drawing can never generate a design. A painting is an artistic work and stays one. A pattern derived from a painting and applied to bed linen by an industrial process is a design, and the courts have had to work out, case by case, where the artistic work ends and the derived design begins. That working out is the subject of the copyright interface section below, because Section 15 of the Copyright Act is where Parliament placed the operative rule.

Two consequences of the definition deserve emphasis before the registration conditions are taken up. First, the design is judged in the article to which it is applied and in the class in which it is registered, so novelty and imitation are always assessed article by article: a pattern famous on wallpaper may be new on a dinner plate, and a shape registered for bottles says nothing about the same shape on lamps. Second, the definition protects appearance features in combination as a whole. Courts comparing two articles compare the overall visual impression the registered features make, not a checklist of elements, and a copyist who changes details while keeping the overall impression has usually changed nothing that matters.

### Lines, colours, and surface: the two dimensional half of the definition

Half of the definition's work is done by its quieter words. A design may consist of a composition of lines or colours, and it may be two dimensional, three dimensional, or both, so the pattern printed on a fabric, the weave visible in a sari border, the label on a packet and the surface ornament of a moulded article are all within the definition although none of them is a shape. This is the limb of Section 2(d) that serves the textile and graphics trades, and it has an important discipline built into it: where the design is a pattern, the registration protects the pattern as applied to the article, judged on the finished article, and not the motif in the abstract. A floral sprig registered for fabric is protected on fabric; its appearance on the designer's sketchbook page is an artistic work question, and its use as a logo is a trade mark question. Colour occupies a deliberately modest place. A composition of colours can be part of a design, but applicants are routinely required to accept that colour alone, divorced from the pattern or configuration, is not the monopoly being granted, and the representations fix the claim: a design registered in representations that show a particular colourway does not straightforwardly cover every colourway, which is why portfolios in the surface trades register the pattern in neutral form and treat colour as the variable the market, not the right, controls. The two dimensional limb also explains the location of one of the Act's hardest borders. A drawing is two dimensional, an article is what the drawing is applied to, and the repeated judicial task in the copyright cases has been to decide whether the claimant is protecting the drawing, a work, or the pattern the drawing became on the article, a design. Section 2(d)'s insistence that the design exists in the finished article, appealing to the eye there, is the statutory root of that distinction, and the cases from Microfibres onwards are the working out of this paragraph of the definition.

## The conditions of registration: Section 4

Section 4 states the four prohibitions that define registrability negatively, and between them they contain the whole of the substantive law an applicant must satisfy. A design shall not be registered if it is not new or original. It shall not be registered if it has been disclosed to the public anywhere in India or in any other country by publication in tangible form or by use or in any other way prior to the filing date or, where applicable, the priority date of the application. It shall not be registered if it is not significantly distinguishable from known designs or from combinations of known designs. And it shall not be registered if it comprises or contains scandalous or obscene matter. Each limb is also, by the combined operation of Sections 19 and 22, a ground on which a granted registration can later be cancelled and a ground of defence in a piracy suit, so Section 4 is not merely an examiner's checklist: it is the validity code of the entire register.

The first two limbs work together and are best understood through the relationship between new and original. A design is new when the appearance it claims has not been seen before on the relevant article; it is original, in the statutory sense of originating from its author, when it is the author's own creation applied to the article, and the definition in Section 2(g) adds that a design old in the abstract may still be original in its application. An old pattern newly applied to an article to which it has never been applied can therefore be registered, because the originality lies in the marriage of the pattern to that article. The disclosure limb then sets the deadline against which novelty is measured. Disclosure to the public anywhere in the world, in tangible publication, by use in trade, or in any other way, before the filing date destroys registrability. The phrase "in any other way" is as wide as language allows: an exhibition abroad, a catalogue, a trade fair display, an offer for sale, all disclose. This worldwide reach was the 2000 Act's deliberate correction of the 1911 position, under which publication outside India did not defeat an Indian registration, and it is the reason the filing decision must be taken before any launch, launch publicity or trade display anywhere, not merely in India.

The third limb, significant distinguishability, operates as a floor below novelty. A design that differs from what is known only in trivial respects, in trade variants or workshop touches that any competent producer might add, is not significantly distinguishable from the known design and fails. The limb matters most for combinations: an applicant who combines features from several known designs must show that the combination as a whole produces an appearance significantly distinguishable from its ingredients, not merely that the precise collocation is unrecorded. The fourth limb, scandalous or obscene matter, is rarely invoked and needs no elaboration beyond noting that it imports a public morality screen into a commercial register.

### How does the Act treat a design the public has already seen?

It refuses it, and it refuses it without a grace period. Prior disclosure of the design to the public anywhere in the world, by publication, by use or in any other way, before the filing date or the priority date bars registration under Section 4, subject only to narrow exceptions such as the protected exhibition route in Section 21. A designer who launches first and files afterwards will ordinarily find that the launch itself is the disclosure that defeats the application.

The fourth bar, scandalous or obscene matter, is the register's morals clause, and it is the bar least often litigated. Its function in the scheme is worth a sentence because it shows what kind of statute this is: the design register is a public record on which the state confers a monopoly, and the state reserves the right to refuse its machinery to matter it will not be associated with, just as the trade mark register does through its own scandalous marks bar. The clause is applied at examination, rarely, and its presence in Section 4 is a reminder that registrability is a public law question as well as a private entitlement: passing the novelty bars earns the applicant a hearing, not an entitlement, and the Controller's grant remains the state's act.

## Novelty in the courts: the eye, the article, and the burden

The courts have given the statutory words a disciplined content, and three propositions from the case law structure most validity disputes. The first is that novelty and originality are judged on the finished article as the eye sees it. In Bharat Glass Tube Ltd v Gopal Glass Works Ltd, decided on 1 May 2008 and reported as AIR 2008 SC 2520, the Supreme Court considered a registered design for a pattern on glass sheets, attacked on the ground that the pattern already existed on engraved rollers used in glass manufacture abroad. The Court refused the attack. The design had been applied to glass sheets, and it was on glass sheets that its novelty had to be tested: the pattern as a feature of rollers used as tooling was not a publication of the design on the article for which it was registered, and the design, judged by the eye on the finished sheet, was new and original. The case also allocated the burden: the party attacking a registration must prove the prior publication relied on, and must prove it in relation to the same design on the same article, not by assembling similarities from the manufacturing process.

The second proposition is that a foreign registration, by itself, is not a disclosure. In Dabur India Ltd v Amit Jain, a Division Bench of the Delhi High Court held that the mere fact that a similar design stood registered in another country did not defeat the Indian registration; what Section 4 and Section 19 punish is disclosure to the public, and a registration certificate in a foreign office, without proof that the design was thereby published to the public in the relevant sense, does not establish that the world had seen the design on the article. The holding is careful and confined: foreign publication proved will still defeat a registration, and the worldwide disclosure bar of the 2000 Act is real. What Dabur rejects is the shortcut from a foreign register entry to invalidity without evidence of actual publication.

The third proposition concerns imitation rather than validity, but it is decided by the same instrument, the eye. In Whirlpool of India Ltd v Videocon Industries Ltd, the Bombay High Court held that copying in design law is judged by the eye alone and that an imitation need not be an exact replica to infringe: the court compares the overall appearance of the two articles as a customer would encounter them, with imperfect recollection, and asks whether the defendant's article reproduces the registered design or a fraudulent or obvious imitation of it. Small departures introduced to disguise the borrowing do not save the copy if the eye, taking the article as a whole, receives the same impression.

These propositions reveal the texture of design adjudication. The questions are visual, the evidence is largely the articles and representations themselves, and the expert apparatus that dominates patent litigation is mostly absent. That makes design cases quicker and cheaper than patent cases, and it also makes the quality of the representations filed at the Design Office decisive, because the registration, the validity inquiry and the infringement inquiry all resolve into a comparison with what those representations show.

### Two comparisons, two different eyes

The case law runs two comparisons that are easily confused and must be kept apart. The novelty comparison sets the design against the prior field: has this appearance, on this article, been disclosed before, and is it significantly distinguishable from what was known? The piracy comparison sets the defendant's article against the registered design: has the defendant taken the registered appearance or an obvious imitation of it? The comparisons differ in material, in question and in the eye that performs them. Novelty is judged on documents, catalogues, earlier products and registrations, and the challenger bears the burden of proving the earlier disclosure, as Bharat Glass Tube settled. Piracy is judged on the two articles, the claimant's registered representations on one side and the defendant's product on the other, and the court's eye is asked to perform the comparison as the market performs it. Customers do not buy with the two products side by side and a checklist of differences; they buy on a general impression retained from an earlier encounter, and Indian courts have accordingly treated the overall visual impression, rather than a forensic inventory of points of difference, as the piracy question, which is why the defendant who changes five details and keeps the impression loses, and the defendant who keeps five details and changes the impression may win. A proprietor drafting representations and a statement of novelty is, whether it knows it or not, drafting the terms of both comparisons: the features claimed are the features the novelty search will test against the prior field and the features the piracy court will look for in the defendant's article.

## Filing: the application, the representations, and the register

Section 5 governs the application. Any person claiming to be the proprietor of a new or original design may apply to the Controller for registration, and the application must be made in the prescribed form for the class of articles to which the design is to be applied, accompanied by the prescribed number of representations of the design. Proprietor is defined in Section 2(j) and covers the author of the design, the person for whom the author executed the design for good consideration, and the person on whom the right has devolved by assignment or operation of law, which is why an employer or a commissioning client, rather than the individual designer on its staff or its panel, is usually the applicant. The application is filed for one class of articles; a design wanted for articles in several classes must be pursued class by class, because the right the Act grants is class-bound.

Two features of the application carry legal consequences beyond the administrative. The first is the statement of novelty. The applicant states, in the prescribed manner, the features of the design in respect of which novelty is claimed, and may disclaim features that are not novel or that form part of trade dress belonging to others. That statement frames the registration: in later proceedings the court reads the claim to novelty against the representations, and a proprietor who has claimed novelty in the whole appearance cannot easily retreat to a single feature, while a proprietor who has disclaimed a feature cannot complain when a rival adopts it. The second is the representation itself, the drawings or photographs that define the right. The registered design is, in law, what the representations disclose, and applicants who file representations showing one view, or showing the article in a context that includes unclaimed matter, narrow or muddy their own right.

Classification is governed by the Designs Rules, and it has been internationalised. India acceded to the Locarno Agreement, the treaty establishing the International Classification for Industrial Designs, in June 2019, and the Designs (Amendment) Rules 2021, notified on 25 January 2021, moved Indian classification onto the current edition of the Locarno Classification, replacing a scheme that had been based on an older edition, and added the new Class 32 for graphic symbols and logos, surface patterns and ornamentation. The same line of rule amendments addressed who pays what: the rules recognise categories of applicants with reduced fees, including natural persons, small entities, whose separate fee category arrived through the 2014 amendment of the rules, and startups, recognised through the later amendments, so that the fee structure deliberately lowers the entry cost for individual designers and small firms. The classification change is administrative rather than substantive, since a class number never made an unregistrable design registrable, but it matters for searching, because novelty searches are run class by class and a common international scheme makes the Indian register legible to foreign applicants and the world register legible to Indian ones.

Examination follows filing. The Controller examines the application, may require amendments, and registers the design if the Section 4 prohibitions are not made out, issuing a certificate of registration. The register of designs then records the registration, and the date of registration is the date of the application, a rule whose importance appears when priority is claimed from an earlier foreign filing: for reciprocity applications made under Section 44 within the prescribed period, the date of registration is the date of the foreign application. There is no opposition stage in design registration of the kind patent law knows: the register is populated on the Controller's examination, and the testing of a registration happens afterwards, through cancellation. Design registration is therefore fast and comparatively cheap, and the register contains a significant number of entries that would not survive a determined cancellation petition, a structural fact about the system that any honest account must state. The proprietor's remedy against copying is real, but a registration is a claim to validity, not a guarantee of it, and the certificate is the beginning of the argument rather than its end.

## The right itself: Section 11 and the short term

Registration confers what Section 11 calls copyright in the design: when a design is registered, the registered proprietor has copyright in the design for ten years from the date of registration. Section 2(c) defines that copyright as the exclusive right to apply the design to any article in the class of articles in which the design is registered. The right is therefore a right to control application of the design to articles of the registered class: to make, sell, import and otherwise deal in articles bearing the design, and to stop others from doing so without licence or written consent. It is not a right in the pattern in the abstract, and it does not extend to articles outside the registered class.

The term is the feature of the Act that surprises every new reader, and it must be stated with precision because other pages so often misstate it. The initial period is ten years from the date of registration. Before that period expires, the proprietor may apply in the prescribed manner, with the prescribed fee, for a single extension of five years, and if the application and fee are in order the Controller extends the copyright for the further five year period. The maximum life of an Indian design registration is accordingly fifteen years. There is no second extension, no renewal cycle, and no mechanism by which the right can be prolonged beyond the fifteen years. When the term ends, the design enters the public domain as a design: anyone may apply it to articles in the class, and the former proprietor's only remaining protections are those that other bodies of law supply on their own conditions, principally trade mark law where the shape has become distinctive of source, and passing off where its demanding conditions can be proved.

If the extension is not sought in time, the design lapses at the end of the tenth year, and Section 12 supplies a limited rescue: the proprietor, or a legal representative or assignee, may apply within one year from the date on which the design ceased to have effect for restoration of the lapsed design, on the ground that the failure to pay the extension fee was unintentional, and the Controller, if satisfied, restores the registration on payment of the unpaid fee and an additional prescribed fee. Restoration is not free of consequences for third parties: Section 13 protects a person who, between the lapse and the restoration, began in good faith to avail himself of the lapsed design, by capping the rights the restored proprietor can enforce against that person. Once the one year restoration window closes, the lapse is final, and finality of this kind is irreversible in a way copyright owners, accustomed to a right that needs no maintenance, rarely anticipate.

### What happens when the fifteen years run out?

The design right ends, wholly and finally, and the appearance becomes free for anyone to use on articles of the class. Fifteen years from the date of registration is the absolute ceiling Section 11 permits: an initial ten year term and one five year extension, with no renewal beyond it. What may survive is a different right on different terms, most plausibly a shape trade mark if the appearance has come to identify one maker, which Section 9 of the Trade Marks Act polices through its own functionality exclusions.

Two maintenance obligations complete the picture of the right while it lives. The first is marking. Section 15 requires the proprietor, before delivery on sale of articles to which the design has been applied, to furnish exact representations to the Controller where that was not done at filing, and to cause each article to be marked with the prescribed mark, word or figures denoting that the design is registered, in practice the word Registered or its abbreviation with the registration number. Marking is not a condition of the right's existence, but it is a condition of its full value: a proprietor who has not marked, and cannot show that all proper steps were taken to mark, recovers no penalty or damages for an infringement unless the infringer knew or had notice of the registration. The second is the record of dealings. Assignments and transmissions of a registered design are governed by Section 30, under which the assignee may apply to be entered in the register as proprietor, and entries of licences and dealings keep the public record aligned with the commercial reality. A design portfolio that changes hands without the register being corrected creates exactly the proof problems in litigation that the register exists to prevent.

## Fifty and out: the decision table

The single most consequential interaction in Indian design law is not inside the Designs Act at all. It is Section 15 of the Copyright Act 1957, and the table below works it as the decision sequence a designer or manufacturer actually faces, because the same facts placed in a different order produce opposite legal outcomes.

| Position of the product appearance | Is the design registered under the Designs Act? | Reproductions of the article by an industrial process | What right survives, and for how long |
|---|---|---|---|
| An appearance capable of registration as a design, not yet applied industrially | No | None so far | Copyright in the underlying artistic work, if any, subsists in full; the design right is available on filing before disclosure |
| A registrable design applied to articles in production | No | Fifty or fewer | Copyright in the design subsists under Section 15(2) of the Copyright Act, and can still be converted into a design registration if no disqualifying disclosure has occurred |
| A registrable design applied to articles in production | No | More than fifty | Copyright in the design has ceased under Section 15(2); no design right exists because none was registered; the appearance is unprotected against copying |
| A registrable design applied to articles in production | Yes | Any number | No copyright subsists in the registered design under Section 15(1); the design right under Section 11 of the Designs Act runs for ten years, extendable once by five |
| A registered design whose term has expired | Registration lapsed or expired | Any number | No design right and no copyright in the design; only trade mark or passing off protection, if their separate conditions are met |
| An appearance that is not capable of design registration at all, such as a purely functional configuration | No, and none possible | Any number | Section 15(2) never engages, because the design is not capable of registration; copyright analysis proceeds on the work's own terms, and function is left to patent law |

Read the table from the third row, which is the disaster the whole system is built around. The designer whose article has been reproduced more than fifty times without a registration has lost the copyright in the design and never acquired the design right, and the loss cannot be undone by a later filing, because by then the design will ordinarily have been disclosed to the public by the very sales that crossed the threshold, and Section 4 bars registration of a disclosed design. The sequence is the point: registration is a decision that has to be taken before manufacture, and manufacture is the step that quietly destroys the alternative.

## The copyright interface: Section 15 of the Copyright Act

The operative text is short. Section 15(1) of the Copyright Act provides that copyright shall not subsist in any design which is registered under the Designs Act 2000. Section 15(2) provides that copyright in any design which is capable of being registered under the Designs Act, but which has not been so registered, shall cease as soon as any article to which the design has been applied has been reproduced more than fifty times by an industrial process by the owner of the copyright or, with his licence, by any other person. Three features of the drafting decide most of the litigation. The first is that Section 15(2) is triggered by capability of registration, not by actual registrability after inquiry: a design that could have been registered is caught even if the owner never considered registering it. The second is that the count is of reproductions of any article to which the design has been applied, by the owner or a licensee: a design used on two products accumulates reproductions across both. The third is that what ceases is copyright in the design, and the case law has had to determine what, if anything, survives in the artwork from which the design was derived.

That determination is the work of the Delhi High Court's Division Bench in Microfibres Inc v Girdhar and Co, decided in 2009 and reported at 2009 (40) PTC 519 (Del) (DB). Microfibres, a maker of upholstery fabrics, sued over fabric patterns, claiming copyright in the drawings from which the patterns were developed. The court's analysis separated two things the argument had run together. An original artistic work, such as a drawing or painting, is protected by copyright as an artistic work, and that protection is not destroyed by the work later being used industrially: the copyright in the original work endures for its ordinary term, the life of the author and sixty years beyond. But a design derived from the artistic work for industrial application to articles is a different legal object. If that design is capable of registration and is not registered, its copyright is governed by Section 15(2), and once the articles bearing it have been reproduced more than fifty times, the copyright in the design ceases, and the owner cannot recover through the drawing what the design route has forfeited. The legislative purpose the court identified was to prevent a design proponent from claiming the long copyright term for what is in substance an industrial monopoly: Parliament gave industrial appearance a short, registered right, and Section 15 stops that settlement being evaded by recharacterising the design as art.

Later decisions have applied the rule at its edges, and two are worth the designer's attention. In Ritika Private Limited v Biba Apparels Private Limited, decided in 2016, the Delhi High Court applied Section 15(2) to fashion: drawings and sketches used to create dresses lost their copyright protection as designs once the dresses made from them crossed fifty in number, and the claim framed in copyright failed for want of a registered design. In Dart Industries Inc v Techno Plast, the Delhi High Court examined drawings for moulded plastic articles of everyday utility and treated the copyright claim in such working drawings with caution, holding the line that copyright in the underlying drawing protects the drawing as a work, and does not convert functional product shapes into copyright subject matter through the back door of the draughtsman's skill. The through-line of the case law is consistent with the statute's design: copyright protects works, the Designs Act protects industrial appearance, and Section 15 is the sluice gate that moves subject matter from the first regime to the second once industry takes it up, whether the owner likes the destination or not.

The wider copyright scheme within which Section 15 sits, the works it protects and the terms it grants, is the subject of [the Copyright Act 1957 explained as a statute in its own right](/2019/10/01/copyright-act-1957-explained/); this article takes from that scheme only the one section that design law cannot be understood without.

For the textile, furniture and consumer goods sectors, where most appearance disputes arise, the rule that decides the case is therefore usually Section 15(2), not the piracy provision of the Designs Act. The claimant arrives with a copyright claim because copyright needs no registration and lasts generations; the defendant answers that the work sued on is a design capable of registration, unregistered, and reproduced beyond fifty; and the action ends at that answer unless the claimant can show that what was copied was the artistic work as a work, rather than the design as applied to articles. Designers who intend industrial production should treat the drawing's copyright as a bridge, not a destination: it holds the position while the design application is prepared and filed, and it is spent, for design purposes, the moment production passes fifty.

## Piracy of a registered design: Section 22

The Designs Act uses the word piracy where patent and copyright law use infringement, and Section 22 defines the wrong. During the existence of copyright in a design, it is not lawful for any person, for the purpose of sale, to apply or cause to be applied to any article in any class of articles in which the design is registered, the design or any fraudulent or obvious imitation of it, except with the licence or written consent of the registered proprietor, or to do anything with a view to enabling the design to be so applied. It is likewise unlawful to import for the purposes of sale, without the proprietor's consent, an article of the registered class bearing the design or a fraudulent or obvious imitation, and unlawful, knowing that the design has been applied without consent, to publish or expose or cause to be published or exposed for sale such an article. The three limbs track the commercial life of a pirated product: the making up, the importing, and the offering for sale by a dealer who knows the position, and each requires the purpose or context of sale, which is why purely private or experimental use of a registered design is outside the section.

The imitation standard is built into the definition of the wrong. Piracy is not confined to exact copying: a fraudulent or obvious imitation suffices, and fraudulence here is about colourable imitation dressed as difference, while obviousness is judged, as the Bombay High Court put it in Whirlpool v Videocon, by the eye comparing the articles as wholes. Section 22(3) then hands the defendant the validity code as a shield: in any suit or proceeding under the section, every ground on which the registration may be cancelled under Section 19 is available as a ground of defence, so a piracy action is always potentially two cases, one about copying and one about whether the registration should ever have been granted. Section 22(4) supplies the forum consequence: where a Section 19 ground is taken as a defence, the suit is transferred to the High Court for decision, taking the validity question to the court that hears appeals from the Controller. The suit itself must be instituted in a court not below the court of District Judge, and under Section 22(5) the court that decrees a piracy suit sends a copy of the decree to the Controller for entry in the register.

The remedies are stated as an election, and the election is the most criticised feature of the Act. Under Section 22(2)(a), the person who contravenes the section is liable for every contravention to pay the registered proprietor a sum not exceeding twenty-five thousand rupees, recoverable as a contract debt, provided that the total sum recoverable in respect of any one design under that clause shall not exceed fifty thousand rupees. Under Section 22(2)(b), if the proprietor elects instead to sue for damages, the infringer is liable to pay such damages as may be awarded and to be restrained by injunction accordingly, with the usual incidents of civil litigation, including accounts of profits and delivery up of infringing articles, available through the general law. The two routes cannot be combined for the same contravention: the proprietor chooses the liquidated statutory sum or the forensic suit for damages and an injunction, and the choice is ordinarily obvious.

### Why is the statutory piracy sum so rarely the route proprietors use?

Because the figures have stood still since the Act was passed. Twenty-five thousand rupees per contravention, capped at fifty thousand rupees for any one design under Section 22(2)(a), was a modest deterrent in the year 2000 and is a trivial one against the turnover of a successful mass market product. A proprietor facing serious piracy therefore elects the Section 22(2)(b) suit for unliquidated damages and an injunction, where the court measures the actual loss, and treats the contract debt route as a small claims device rather than the Act's real remedy.

The criticism is fair and it has never been answered by amendment. The statutory route was designed, one infers, for the small proprietor: a fixed sum recoverable as a debt, sparing the proprietor the expense of proving loss. But a cap fixed in nominal rupees decays with inflation, and nearly two decades of price rise have reduced the route to a formality for any piracy worth suing over. The drafting problem is the absence of an indexation or revision mechanism: Parliament set the figure in the Act itself, so only an amending Act can move it, and design law has not attracted the parliamentary time. Practitioners work around the cap by pleading damages, by joining passing off where the facts support it, and, in the case of composite claims, by relying on the fuller remedial apparatus of the civil courts; but a reader of the Act should understand that its headline remedy figure is the least realistic number in the statute. Two qualifications keep the assessment honest. The injunction, available on the damages route, is the remedy that actually stops piracy, and it is unaffected by the cap. And the cap binds only the statutory route; it is a ceiling on a shortcut, not on the proprietor's true recovery.

## Cancellation: how registrations are destroyed

Section 19 is the register's quality control, applied after the event. Any person interested may at any time after the registration of a design present a petition to the Controller for cancellation of the registration on any of five grounds: that the design has been previously registered in India; that it has been published in India or in any other country prior to the date of registration; that the design is not new or original; that the design is not registrable under the Act; and that it is not a design as defined in Section 2(d). The Controller, after hearing the parties, may make such order on the petition as he thinks fit, and an appeal from the Controller's order lies to the High Court. There is no time limit on a cancellation petition, which means a registration is contestable for the whole of its fifteen year life, and the register's apparent solidity is always provisional.

Prior publication is the ground that does the most work, for the reason Section 4 suggests: because the bar is worldwide and covers disclosure by use, by publication in tangible form or in any other way, the attacker who finds the design on an earlier product, in an earlier catalogue or in the proprietor's own pre-filing sales has usually found the whole case. The burden, as Bharat Glass Tube settled, is on the attacker to prove that publication, and to prove it of the same design on the same article, judged by the eye. The not-a-design ground, Section 19(1)(e), is the definition's second life: features that are a mere mechanical device, or a mode of construction, or an artistic work, expose the registration to cancellation at any time, which is why the function exclusion matters at the filing stage even though the examiner may not press it. A proprietor holds a registration subject to two different revocations of fortune: a defendant in a piracy suit can raise every one of these grounds without filing anything, under Section 22(3), and a competitor can attack the registration directly before the Controller. Prudent proprietors therefore audit their own registrations against Section 19 before they sue, because the piracy action is the occasion on which a weak registration is most likely to be destroyed.

## The shape mark border: after and beside the design right

The definition excludes trade marks, but it does not prevent the same product shape from being the subject matter of both regimes in sequence, and the interaction is one of the most commercially significant in the cluster. A shape registered as a design is protected for a maximum of fifteen years. If, during that life, the shape has been used and promoted so that buyers take the shape itself as an indication of origin, the shape may be registrable as a trade mark, and a trade mark registration is renewable indefinitely in ten year periods. The prospect this opens, of perpetual protection for a product shape by migration from the design register to the trade mark register, is real but fenced. Section 9(3) of the Trade Marks Act 1999 refuses registration to a mark which consists exclusively of the shape of goods which results from the nature of the goods themselves, or the shape of goods which is necessary to obtain a technical result, or the shape which gives substantial value to the goods. The second and third limbs are aimed precisely at the migration strategy: a shape that works, and on one reading a shape whose value lies in its appeal, cannot be locked up forever as a mark. How the border is policed in practice is the subject of [the Trade Marks Act 1999 and its treatment of shape marks](/2019/11/01/trade-marks-act-1999-explained/), and the wise manufacturer treats the two registrations as complements with different clocks rather than as substitutes: the design registration for the launch years, when copying is cheapest and the registration is quickest to obtain, and the trade mark only if the shape earns distinctiveness before the design clock runs out.

The overlap is sometimes run in the other direction. A get-up, label or surface pattern may be both an artistic work and a design, and a logo may be pressed into service as a surface design. Each regime asks its own question of the same visual material: the design register asks whether the features, applied to the article, are new and judged by the eye; the trade mark register asks whether the sign distinguishes the goods of one trader from another's. Neither answer implies the other, and a portfolio that confuses them tends to discover, in litigation, that it holds the wrong right for the claim it wants to make.

## The modern settlement: classification, small applicants, and the screen

The Act that operates in practice has been adjusted at its edges rather than rebuilt. The classification reform described earlier aligned the register with the Locarno system after India's accession in 2019, and the fee amendments lowered the cost of entry for natural persons, small entities and startups, a deliberate policy of pulling individual designers and small firms into a register historically dominated by companies. One contested frontier remains the screen. The Locarno scheme contains a class for screen displays and icons, and the current edition adds Class 32 for graphic symbols and logos, but the Designs Office has consistently declined to register graphical user interfaces and icons as designs, reasoning that they do not answer the statutory definition: a screen display is not an article in the finished, tangible sense the Act contemplates, and the appearance in question exists only when the device is switched on, rather than as a feature of the finished article judged by the eye. Applicants and commentators have pressed the claim as software interfaces became the most commercially valuable product appearances of all, and the Computer-related practice in several foreign design systems accepts such registrations, but the Indian position has held on the statutory text, and a reader should treat the GUI as outside the Indian design right unless the office practice or the Act changes.

The largest structural debate about the Indian settlement is the missing unregistered right, and it deserves both sides stated plainly. Jurisdictions in the European tradition protect an unregistered design for a short period, three years for the unregistered Community design, against copying, on the ground that many designs have a commercial life shorter than the time registration takes and that fashion and seasonal goods are copied within a season, before a registration could issue. The case for the Indian position is the case for registration itself: a public register gives competitors a searchable record of what is claimed, fixes the right's boundaries in representations anyone may inspect, and spares the courts the inquiry into what an unregistered right covers and when it began. The case against is the case of the small designer and the fast sector: the designer who must file before any disclosure, in every class and at the start of the product's life, bears a cost and a procedural burden at the moment of least certainty, and the sector whose products live for a season finds a fifteen year registered right, obtained after examination, a poor fit for a six month market. The Indian system has chosen certainty over spontaneity, and the choice is at least coherent with the rest of the Act: a right defined by representations on a register, judged by the eye, policed through cancellation, is a right built to be looked up. Whether it serves the design economy India has, rather than the one the register imagines, is the open question the next amendment will have to answer.

For the reader placing this statute in its cluster, the field as a whole, the offices that administer the several rights and the forums their disputes travel through are mapped in [the intellectual property law guide for India](/2019/08/01/intellectual-property-law-india-guide/), and this article has deliberately left the comparative questions of term, cost and enforcement across the rights to that map and to the comparison article that follows in the cluster.

## The register as a public instrument

A registration system earns its keep by what third parties can learn from it, and the Act builds three public-facing mechanisms around the register. The first is inspection: after a design is registered, the representations and particulars on the register are open to public inspection, so a manufacturer planning a product can search the register by class, see what appearances are claimed, and shape its own product around them or challenge them. The second is the information provision, under which a person may ask the Controller whether copyright in a design still subsists and obtain information from the register, a mechanism used constantly by traders who receive a warning letter and need to know whether the right asserted against them is alive. The third is the Controller's certificate, which Section 38 makes evidence of the entries it certifies, so that the state of the register can be proved in court without producing the register itself. The system has one deliberate opacity: Section 14 permits directions prohibiting or restricting publication of information about designs that appear to the Controller to fall within classes notified as relevant for defence purposes, so that a design with military sensitivity can be registered without being displayed to the world. The exception proves the design of the whole: publicity is the default because the register is the public's protection against stumbling into piracy, and secrecy is admitted only where a competing public interest displaces it.

The register's publicity also disciplines proprietors. A registration that misdescribes its proprietor, that claims a design for articles in a class the proprietor does not trade in, or whose representations do not match the product actually sold, is a registration whose weaknesses are visible to every competitor who inspects it, and Section 19 gives those competitors the standing to act on what they see. Indian design practice treats the pre-suit register search as a standard step for exactly this reason: the claimant's own filing is the first document the defendant's advisers read, and it is read against Sections 4 and 19 before it is read for anything else.

## The textile and fashion problem

No honest account of Indian design law can pass over the sector that generates a disproportionate share of its disputes. Textile designs, surface patterns for fabric, are designs within Section 2(d) when applied to fabric as an article, and the Indian textile trade works on cycles that the registration system fits awkwardly. A successful print is copied within weeks; a season's range may contain hundreds of patterns; and the cost, delay and administrative effort of registering each pattern before the range is shown to buyers sits badly with a trade in which showing the range is how orders are taken. The TRIPS textile sentence, noted earlier, records that this friction was visible at the international level: cost, examination and publication requirements were not to be allowed to impair the opportunity to obtain textile design protection unreasonably. India's answer has been partial. The fee reductions for small entities help the small mill and the independent designer at the margin. The deeper mismatch, between a right that must be claimed before disclosure and a trade that sells by disclosure, remains, and it is why textile claimants so often arrived in court pleading copyright in the underlying artwork instead, walking into Section 15(2) of the Copyright Act and the line of cases from Microfibres to Ritika that this article has described. Fashion presents the same problem in a sharper form, because a garment's appearance is its design, its season is short, and the fifty reproduction threshold is crossed in the first production run. The garment trade's demand in several jurisdictions has been for a short unregistered right against slavish copying, and the Indian refusal to create one is felt most concretely here: the dress copied in the same season, by a rival who saw it at the same exhibition, is protected only if the maker registered the design before the exhibition, a sequence the trade's own calendar makes unnatural. The Act's defenders answer, correctly, that the registration route is open and quick for those who organise for it, and that the trade's largest houses do exactly that, filing patterns as a routine cost of the season. The dispute is not about whether registration works; it is about who bears the cost of the system's certainty, and on that question the statute has taken the manufacturer's side over the designer's in a way the sectors have not stopped contesting.

## What the Act withholds

The negative space of a statute is as instructive as its grants, and the Designs Act withholds several things a reader might expect it to contain. It contains no criminal remedy: piracy of a registered design is a civil wrong remedied by money and injunction, and the Act provides no offence, no seizure machinery of the kind copyright law gives for infringing copies, and no border enforcement of its own. It provides no protection against independent creation: a design reached honestly by a second designer, however similar, is not piracy, because the wrong consists in applying the registered design or an imitation derived from it, and the eye test, applied to two independently created appearances, points the court to derivation as much as to similarity. It gives the author no moral right: the designer's connection with the design after assignment is a matter of contract, and the Act knows only proprietors. It contains no compulsory licence and no Crown use provision of the patent kind; the state's powers are the narrower directions for secrecy and the security provisions in Chapter X, including the protection of the security of India in Section 46. And it offers no protection at all for the three categories its definition and structure exclude: function, which it sends to patent law; the unregistered appearance, which it abandons entirely; and the screen display, which its administrators read out of the definition. A reader who maps the withholdings sees the Act's true shape: a narrow, registration-bound, civil right over appearance, deliberately fenced on every side, whose strength is the clarity of its fences.

## The TRIPS frame behind the 2000 settlement

The 2000 Act was drafted against an international template, and its main choices become clearer when the template is stated. Articles 25 and 26 of the TRIPS Agreement require members to provide for the protection of independently created industrial designs that are new or original, permit members to exclude designs dictated essentially by technical or functional considerations, and require that the protection available amount to at least ten years. Article 25 adds a sentence aimed squarely at the sector that litigates design law hardest in India: members must ensure that requirements for securing protection for textile designs, in particular cost, examination and publication, do not unreasonably impair the opportunity to seek and obtain such protection. Section 4's worldwide novelty bar, the exclusion of the mere mechanical device in Section 2(d), and Section 11's ten year initial term all track the template closely: the Indian term of ten years extendable to fifteen comfortably exceeds the TRIPS floor, and the textile sentence explains the political sensitivity, visible throughout the Indian case law, around fabric patterns and the speed with which they are copied.

The same frame explains what India declined. TRIPS does not require an unregistered right, and the European system of an unregistered Community design is a regional addition rather than a treaty standard. Nor does TRIPS compel any particular remedy figure, which is why the decay of the Section 22(2)(a) sum is a domestic failure rather than a treaty breach. India's design settlement is, in the end, the treaty minimum executed with care in the definition and without attention in the remedy, and the article's criticisms should be located there: in the choices Parliament and the rule makers made inside the space TRIPS left open, not in the space itself.

## Ownership: the author, the employer, and the client

The right begins with authorship but is usually owned by someone else, and Section 2(j) distributes it. The proprietor of a new or original design means the author of the design; where the author executes the design for another person for good consideration, that other person; a person who has acquired the design or the right to apply it from the author; and any person on whom the right has devolved from the original proprietor. In practice the second limb governs most commercial design: the staff designer in a furniture company, the freelance surface designer retained by a textile mill, and the product design studio commissioned by a manufacturer all create designs whose proprietor, on execution for consideration, is the employer or the client. The individual creator is the author, and authorship matters for originality, since Section 2(g) tests a design by whether it originated from its author, but the exclusive right and the standing to sue for piracy belong to the proprietor, and a dispute about who that is will be fought on the contract under which the work was done.

Two practical consequences follow. A business that commissions design work without a written term assigning or confirming proprietorship invites a threshold dispute in its first piracy suit, because the defendant will test the claimant's title before the court reaches copying at all. And an applicant must apply as proprietor: the Controller registers designs in the name of the person claiming to be the proprietor, and a registration taken in the wrong name is an irregularity that Sections 30 and 31, governing the entry of assignments and transmissions on the register, exist to cure only prospectively. Assignments of a registered design, and of the copyright in it, are effective through the register: the assignee applies to be entered as proprietor, and until entry, the document of assignment is liable to be refused admission as evidence of title in proceedings except by direction of the Controller or the court. The register, once again, is not a formality laid over the right; it is part of the right's machinery.

## Priority and foreign filings: the six month window

Design filings are territorial, and the Act's reciprocity provisions in Section 44 are the bridge between territories. India maintains reciprocal arrangements with convention countries, and an applicant who has filed for the same design in a convention country may file in India claiming the foreign filing date as the priority date, provided the Indian application is made within six months of the foreign application. Where priority is validly claimed, the date of registration in India is the priority date, and disclosures after that date, including the applicant's own foreign launch, do not defeat the Indian application. The window is short and unforgiving, and it imposes a discipline on any designer with export ambitions: the first filing, wherever made, starts a six month clock for every other market, and a product launched at a foreign trade fair before the first filing anywhere has usually spent its novelty before any clock could start. The worldwide reach of Section 4 makes the sequence unforgiving in both directions, since the foreign disclosure counts in India and an Indian disclosure counts abroad. The designer's filing calendar is therefore a single document, not a set of national decisions, and the firms that lose design rights across borders almost always lose them on sequence rather than on substance.

## A design through the system: the worked example

Abstract rules settle into place when followed through a single product, and the system this article has described can be tested on a composite example drawn from the sectors that use it most. Consider a Jaipur furniture maker who develops a dining chair whose backrest carries a distinctive pierced lattice, designed by an in-house designer, and who plans to launch the chair at a trade fair in March and through dealers from April. The legal sequence begins before any of that. The lattice is a feature of shape and pattern applied to an article, judged by the eye: squarely within Section 2(d), provided the piercing is ornamental rather than the structural means by which the backrest carries weight, in which case the function exclusion begins to bite and the statement of novelty should be confined to the ornamental pattern. Because the designer is an employee executing the design for consideration, the furniture maker is the proprietor under Section 2(j) and files in its own name, in the class covering furniture, with representations showing the chair from the angles that display the lattice and a statement of novelty claiming the lattice pattern in the backrest.

The timing is the first test. If the maker files in January, the March trade fair disclosure is harmless, because novelty is judged at the filing date. If the maker exhibits first, meaning to file in June, the March display is a disclosure to the public by use and exhibition that Section 4 makes fatal, unless the narrow exhibition route of Section 21 was used: prior notice to the Controller in the prescribed form before exhibiting at an exhibition to which that section has been extended, and an application within six months of first exhibiting. The route exists for exactly this case, and it is missed by most exhibitors because it must be set up before the fair opens, not after a rival's catalogue appears. On registration, which dates back to the January filing, the maker marks each chair with the prescribed mark and the registration number, as Section 15 requires if the full remedies are to be available, and the design right runs for ten years from the filing date.

Suppose a rival launches a near-identical chair in the third year. The maker's piracy suit is filed in the district court, pleading application of the registered design or an obvious imitation of it for the purpose of sale. The court compares the two chairs by the eye, as wholes, much as a customer would, and the rival's changes to the legs and finish do not answer the claim if the lattice, the feature in which novelty was claimed, produces the same overall impression. The rival's real defence is Section 22(3): an attack on validity, alleging that the lattice appeared on screens and panels sold years earlier, and if the attack is mounted on Section 19 grounds the suit travels to the High Court under Section 22(4) for the validity question to be decided. The maker's election of remedy is made at the outset with the figures of Section 22(2) in view: the capped statutory sum is not worth taking against a rival selling in volume, so the maker sues for damages and an injunction, and it is the injunction, stopping the rival line mid-season, that does the commercial work. Fifteen years from the filing date, the surviving questions are different ones: the registration has expired, the lattice is free to copy as a design, and the maker's only route against an imitator is trade mark law if the lattice has, improbably, become a badge of origin, or passing off on proof of misrepresentation. The example is ordinary in every particular, and it shows the Act's logic in sequence: the whole of the right is won or lost on filing discipline in the first ninety days, and everything afterwards is the working out of that beginning.

## Defences and pressure points in a piracy suit

Because Section 22(3) imports the cancellation grounds into every piracy action, the defence in a design suit has a wider front than in most intellectual property litigation, and a claimant who has not rehearsed the defence has not understood the claim. The validity defences have been described. Beside them stand the denials that go to the act of piracy itself: that the defendant's article does not reproduce the registered design or an imitation of it, judged by the eye as a whole and against the representations and the statement of novelty rather than against the claimant's product, a distinction that matters when the claimant's product has drifted from its own registration; that the application was to an article outside the class of registration, since the right is class-bound; that the acts complained of were not for the purpose of sale, where the defendant's use is genuinely internal or experimental; and that the claimant is not the registered proprietor, or that the registration has expired or lapsed, or that the defendant acts under licence or written consent. On the other side, the claimant's pressure points are the interim injunction, obtained on a prima facie comparison of the articles and the balance of convenience in a market where a season's delay is a lost season, and the marking record, since a defendant who was on notice of the registration cannot shelter behind the Section 15 consequences of the claimant's imperfect marking.

Two evidentiary habits decide more of these suits than legal argument does. The first is the representations: courts compare the defendant's article with the registered representations, and claimants who registered clear, multi-view representations of the features in which novelty lies begin the comparison ahead. The second is the disclosure trail: because prior publication destroys validity and the defendant's best hope is an earlier disclosure, the party that can document the design's history, the designer's dated drawings, the filing receipts, the launch dates, usually controls the factual ground on which Section 4 and Section 19 operate. Design litigation rewards the party that kept records before it needed them, which is the litigation form of the same lesson the registration system teaches at filing.

## Sets, parts, and the spare parts problem

Three boundary questions recur in practice and the Act answers them unevenly. A set of articles, a dinner service, a cutlery set, a range of coordinated products sold together, may be the subject of a single application where the same design is applied to each article of the set, which the rules accommodate because the design, not the individual article, is the unit of the right. A part of an article capable of being made and sold separately is itself an article within Section 2(a), so the maker of a distinctive tap, handle or wheel can register the design of the part, and purchasers of the part as a spare are within the right's reach while it lasts. It is the third question that generates the policy argument: the visible spare part, the car panel, the headlamp cluster, the replacement bumper, whose appearance must match the whole product if it is to serve as a repair part at all. A registration over such a part gives the original manufacturer a fifteen year control over the repair market in that part, because an independent panel maker cannot supply a matching panel without applying the registered design. Other design systems have answered this with repair clauses or must-match exclusions that carve repair parts out of the right; the Indian Act contains no such clause, and the competition concern is met, if at all, only by the brevity of the term and by competition law outside the Act. The reader should register the point as a genuine gap in the statute's design: the same section that protects a chair lattice for fifteen years protects, for fifteen years, a part whose only possible shape is dictated by the product it repairs, and the function exclusion in Section 2(d) does not reach it, because the part's appearance, though compelled by the whole, is not a mere mechanical device.

## Trade variants and the margin of novelty

Between the wholly new design and the copied design lies the territory most applications actually occupy: the variation. A manufacturer takes a known article, a kettle, a chair, a bottle, and alters its proportions, adds a fluting, changes a handle, and asks whether the result is registrable. The Act's two texts govern the answer from opposite sides. Section 2(g) is generous: a design old in itself may be original because it is new in its application, so the known motif on the new article is not condemned by its age. Section 4 is severe: a design that is not significantly distinguishable from known designs, or from a combination of known designs, is barred, so the variation must amount to more than the sum of the stock elements it rearranges. The courts' working expression for failure on this ground is the trade variant: a change that the trade itself would regard as a routine variation of an existing article, the sort of alteration a manufacturer makes to refresh a line, is not new or original merely because no identical article can be pointed to. The test stays visual and stays on the article: the question is whether the finished article's appearance strikes the eye as significantly different from the known field, not whether its maker expended effort, and effort spent on invisible construction earns nothing under this Act. Applicants meet the doctrine first in examination, where the examiner's search turns up the near neighbour, and proprietors meet it again in cancellation, where the near neighbour is produced by an opponent with litigation funding. The margin of novelty the Act demands is therefore best understood as a distance requirement: a registrable design must stand significantly clear of what the eye already knows, and the nearer it stands to the known field, the more its registration is a hostage to the first determined challenger.

## Packaging, bottles, and get-up

Packaging is where design registration does some of its most routine and most valuable work, because a container is an article, its shape and surface are features judged by the eye, and the shelf is a place where appearance is the entire sales force. Bottles, jars, cartons, tins and moulded containers are registered in large numbers, and the registrations matter in the trade dress disputes of the fast moving consumer goods sector, where a rival's lookalike pack is often the first act of competition a successful product meets. Two cautions govern the sector's use of the register. The first is the function exclusion in its packaging form: the shape of a container dictated by the mechanics of filling, sealing, stacking or pouring is a mode of construction or a mechanical device feature, and only the surplus appearance, the contour or ornament that the function did not compel, is registrable subject matter. The second is the shape mark temptation already discussed: a bottle shape that succeeds will be pressed toward trade mark registration when the design term ends, and Section 9(3) of the Trade Marks Act will ask whether the shape gives substantial value to the goods, a question the proprietor's own design registration and marketing have spent fifteen years answering in the affirmative. The sector's registrations also illustrate the class discipline of the right: a design registered for the bottle does not cover the label as applied to other articles, and portfolios in this field are built as families of registrations, article by article and class by class, because the Act grants no general right in a house style. What it grants is a right per design per class, and the get-up that consumers recognise is usually the sum of several such rights, some registered as designs, some subsisting as trade marks, some protected only by the law of passing off, each with its own clock and its own conditions.

## The fifty reproduction count in practice

Section 15(2) of the Copyright Act is easy to state and treacherous to apply, because its trigger is a count that nobody in a busy factory is keeping. The text counts reproductions of any article to which the design has been applied, by an industrial process, by the copyright owner or with the owner's licence. Several features of that formula deserve a designer's attention. The count aggregates across articles: a pattern applied to curtains and to cushion covers is reproduced on both, and the fifty is reached on the combined production, not product by product. The count includes licensees' production: articles made by a job worker or licensee with the owner's licence count against the owner's copyright exactly as the owner's own production does, which is why a designer who licenses a pattern for a trial run may spend the copyright on someone else's press. The text does not confine the count to reproductions in India, and a designer whose articles are manufactured abroad for the Indian market should not assume the foreign run is outside the reckoning. Prototypes and samples occupy genuinely doubtful ground: a handful of prototypes made by hand may not be reproduction by an industrial process at all, while a pilot run of sixty on the production line plainly is, and the prudent course treats the pilot run as the cliff edge. Above all, the count is irreversible in effect: nothing in either Act restores a copyright that has ceased, and the design registration that might have replaced it is by then usually barred by the disclosure the sales themselves made. The rule's harshness is not a drafting oversight. It is the mechanism by which Parliament closed the long copyright route to industrial appearance, and its victims are chosen by a single characteristic: they learned of the rule after the count had run, rather than before.

## Refusal, hearing, and appeal

Registration is a grant, not a deposit, and the Act supplies the disappointed applicant with a path. Where the Controller is minded to refuse an application, because the design appears barred by Section 4 or outside Section 2(d), the applicant is entitled to be heard, and the practice of the Designs Office is to communicate objections in an examination report and to decide after hearing the applicant or the applicant's agent, with amendments to the representations or the statement of novelty sometimes answering the objection and sometimes conceding the substance of it. The Controller's orders under the Act, including a refusal to register and an order on a cancellation petition, are appealable to the High Court, and Section 36 supplies the framework for appeals the Act declares, including the period within which they must be brought. The appeal matters to the system beyond the individual case: it is on appeal and in transferred piracy suits that the High Courts have built the doctrine this article has drawn on, from the eye test to the burden on prior publication, and the Controller's office works in the knowledge that its register is supervised by courts that will compare designs for themselves rather than defer to the examiner's eye. For the applicant, the practical lesson of the appellate structure is that the filing record is the appeal record: representations, statements of novelty and hearing submissions made at the office stage are the materials on which the High Court will later judge the design, and a registration obtained on a narrow statement of novelty cannot be widened in the courtroom.

## The international route and the economics of the system

India has not joined the Hague system of international design registration, so there is no single international application that ripens into an Indian registration. The Indian designer's protection abroad, and the foreign designer's protection in India, are built the older way: a first filing in one country, then national filings in each market of interest within the six month convention priority period that Section 44 recognises, each examined under its own law. The economics of the Indian end of that route are a large part of the system's character. Official fees for a design application are modest by the standards of the patent system, and the fee reforms for natural persons, small entities and startups lowered them further for the applicants least able to pay; the professional cost of preparing representations and prosecuting an application is the larger item, and it is still small beside the cost of a patent application or a trade mark portfolio across classes. Examination, being confined to the register and the formalities, is correspondingly quick relative to patent prosecution. The system's cheapness and speed are not incidental virtues: they are what make the file-before-disclosure discipline practicable for small producers, and they are the strongest answer to the argument that registration burdens the small designer. The burden that remains is not the fee but the knowledge: the system is cheap for those who know it exists and ruinous only for those who meet it for the first time in a defendant's pleading or in the cold arithmetic of Section 15(2) of the Copyright Act.

## Design rights and the freedom to imitate

Underneath the Act's machinery sits a settlement about imitation that Indian courts state openly in the neighbouring fields and that design law presupposes: copying is the default condition of a market economy, and exclusive rights are exceptions carved out of it for defined subject matter, on defined conditions, for defined periods. The design right is one of the narrowest of those exceptions. It does not protect ideas, styles or schools of design: the designer who pioneers a style acquires no right against the followers who work in the style with different appearances, because the Act protects the particular features applied to the particular article and nothing larger. It does not protect the article's commercial concept, its price point or its function, each of which a competitor may take on the day of launch. What it protects is the one thing the pioneer alone contributed and a copyist alone can take without effort: the specific appearance, for the specific years, in the specific class. The brevity of the term is the settlement's second half. Fifteen years is long enough for the design's commercial premium to be earned and short enough that the appearance joins the common stock of forms within the working life of the designers who competed with it, available to be built on by the next generation of variations, some of which will clear Section 4's significantly distinguishable bar and earn registrations of their own. The system is best understood as a ratchet on the stock of appearances: novelty enters protected, imitation is delayed rather than forbidden, and the register records the queue. Critics of design rights as a category argue that appearance is cheap to vary and that the right mostly taxes the second comer for the pioneer's marketing; the Act's defenders answer with the sectors, textiles above all, where the copy is photographically exact, the pioneer's margin is the design itself, and a right that takes weeks to obtain is the difference between a design trade and a copying trade. The statute does not resolve that argument. It prices it, at ten years plus five, and Parliament has left the price where it set it in the year 2000.

## The design as an asset: assignments, licences, and portfolios

A registered design is property, and the register is its title deed. Section 30 governs the entry of assignments and transmissions: where a person becomes entitled to a registered design by assignment, transmission or operation of law, he may apply to the Controller to register his title, and the Controller enters him as proprietor on proof of title. Licences, by contrast, operate by contract: the proprietor may license others to apply the design, exclusively or non-exclusively, for the whole term or part of it, and the licensee's authority is the written consent that Section 22 makes the boundary of piracy. The practical asymmetries are worth noting. An unregistered assignment leaves the assignee's title unprovable from the register and, as this article has noted, the assignment deed may not even be admissible as evidence of title without the Controller's or the court's direction, so a purchaser of a design portfolio who skips the Section 30 entries has bought rights it may struggle to enforce. A licensee, conversely, has no title to enter and must rely on the proprietor to sue, unless the licence gives contractual rights of its own. In transactions, the design register is therefore a due diligence document of the first order: the purchaser's advisers read off the proprietor of record, the registration and extension dates, the lapse or restoration history and any entries of dealing, and price the portfolio on what the register shows, not on what the seller's schedules assert. The fifteen year ceiling gives design portfolios a wasting character that patent and trade mark portfolios lack: a portfolio bought in year twelve of its registrations is an asset with three years to run, and the register makes that arithmetic public. Businesses that treat design registrations as permanent brand assets, to be valued like trade marks, misread the instrument; the Act issues a wasting asset, and the register, unusually among intellectual property registers, tells the whole market exactly when the waste completes.

## The map of forums

The institutions of design law are few and their jurisdictions interlock in ways a litigant must hold in mind at once. The Controller of Designs and the Designs Wing at Kolkata are the registry: they examine, register, restore, record assignments and decide cancellation petitions. No design suit begins there. A piracy suit begins in a court not below a District Judge, the forum floor Section 22(2) prescribes, which takes design cases out of the lowest civil courts and into the district judiciary where commercial litigation ordinarily sits. The High Court stands over both tracks at once: it hears appeals from the Controller's orders under Section 19 and Section 36, and piracy suits are transferred to it under Section 22(4) when validity is put in issue by way of defence, a transfer that means the most consequential design litigation, the cases where the registration itself is at stake, is decided by the High Courts as courts of first instance for that question. The Supreme Court's interventions, of which Bharat Glass Tube is the standard example, have been on the meaning of the definition and the burden of proof rather than on the run of cases, which is consistent with a system whose questions are visual and factual and whose appellate doctrine was substantially settled early. A claimant choosing where and how to fight reads this map before filing: the district court for the copying claim, the expectation of transfer if validity is attacked, the Controller for a free-standing cancellation, and the register at Kolkata as the document all three forums will treat as the starting text.

## The proprietor's calendar

Design law is a law of deadlines more than of doctrines, and the proprietor's obligations can be stated as a calendar because the Act's forfeitures are almost all failures to act in time. Before anything else comes the filing, which must precede any disclosure of the design to the public anywhere in the world if Section 4 is not to destroy it. Behind the filing stand the two six month windows: six months from a first foreign filing to claim convention priority under Section 44, and six months from first exhibiting at a notified exhibition to file under the Section 21 route, with the Controller notified before the exhibition opens. Registration starts the longest clock, the ten year initial term, with the single five year extension to be applied for, with its fee, before the ten years expire. Miss the extension and a second, shorter clock runs: one year from the lapse in which restoration may be sought under Section 12 on the ground that the default was unintentional, after which the loss is beyond repair. Within litigation, the calendar's entry is the three month period Section 36 allows for an appeal to the High Court from the Controller's order. Set the calendar out in that order and the character of the right is plain: every protection the Act gives is on the far side of an act the proprietor must remember to perform, and the Act's severest consequences attach not to bad designs or bad faith but to missed dates. The well run design portfolio is therefore less a legal achievement than an administrative one, a diary kept by someone who understands that in this branch of the law the diary is the right.

## One product, two registers: the design beside the patent

A new product frequently carries both a patentable mechanism and a registrable appearance, and the manufacturer's filing strategy has to keep the two rights from destroying each other. The patent application will publish the invention, drawings and all, and publication is disclosure: a design application filed after the patent specification becomes public will meet the manufacturer's own disclosure under Section 4. The design application will display the product's appearance, and premature display of the mechanism's embodiment can compromise the patent's novelty position in the other direction. The disciplined sequence is therefore simultaneous or near-simultaneous filing, or design filing first where the appearance is launch-critical, with each application drafted in awareness of the other. The rights then run on their different clocks and different logics: the patent, if granted, protects the way the product works for twenty years and is indifferent to how it looks; the design protects the way it looks for fifteen and is indifferent to how it works; and a competitor remains free throughout to make a differently appearing product that works the same way once the patent expires, and a same-appearing product that works differently once the design expires, a division of the field that Section 2(d)'s function exclusion was written to produce. Products also illustrate the gaps between the registers. A feature that is both the mechanism and the look, a grille that is the ventilation system and the product's face, sits on the border: the design claim will be resisted as a mode or principle of construction, the patent claim will be resisted if the feature does not do inventive work, and the honest advice is that some product features are protected by neither register and are held, if at all, by speed to market and by the trade mark the product earns. The design right's place in the portfolio is the fast, cheap, certain layer: obtained in months, infringed by the eye, spent in fifteen years, and most valuable in the launch window when copying is easiest and the patent, if any, is still pending.

## The piracy suit in the season: interim relief as the real remedy

The account of remedies earlier in this article described the election Section 22 offers at the end of a suit, but design litigation is usually decided at its beginning, and a pillar's account of piracy should say so. A copied design harms its proprietor on the copyist's timetable, which is the selling season: for a textile range, a furniture line or a festival product, the season may be eight weeks long, and a decree eighteen months away is a remedy for a market that no longer exists. The proprietor's first application is accordingly for an interim injunction, and the court's decision on it, made on a prima facie view of the registration, the comparison of the articles by the eye, and the balance of convenience between the parties, determines the commercial outcome in most cases: the copyist restrained in week two withdraws the line, and the copyist not restrained has had the season whatever the final decree says. This is why the disciplines this article has repeated converge in the piracy suit's first fortnight. The registration must be valid on its face, because the defendant will open the Section 19 attack at the interim stage and a registration with an obvious prior publication wound may not carry even a prima facie case. The representations must make the comparison easy, because the interim judge compares as the customer compares, quickly and by impression. The marking record matters, because a defendant shown to have known of the registration is poorly placed to plead the equities. And the proprietor's own disclosure history must be clean, because the interim hearing is where the claimant's pre-filing sales are first priced. The damages suit and the contract debt, the remedies the statute's text foregrounds, are the litigation's second act, fought over the accounting for a season already won or lost on the injunction. A reader who understands that sequence understands why practitioners speak of the design right as strong in the doorway and modest in the decree, and why the Act's failure to update its money figures, though real, has not crippled the system in practice: the injunction was always the remedy that mattered, and the injunction was never capped.

## Drawings, photographs, and the discipline of the views

The representations deserve a fuller account than filing mechanics usually give them, because they are at once the application, the right and the evidence. The Designs Rules permit representations by drawings or photographs, and in suitable cases specimens, and the choice among them is a legal decision disguised as a presentational one. A drawing idealises: it presents the design as clean lines, free of the shadows, reflections and background clutter that a photograph cannot escape, and it claims the design at the level of form the draftsman selects. A photograph particularises: it captures the article as manufactured, surface finish and all, and may claim less than the proprietor supposes, because the court comparing a defendant's article with photographs will treat visible features of the photographed specimen as part of the claim's context. Multiple views are not decoration. A design is judged on the finished article as a whole, and a single view leaves the unseen sides unclaimed and the comparison at trial half equipped; the standard practice of front, back, side, top and perspective views exists because defendants copy the unseen side's differences into their defence. Endorsements on the representations perform the exclusions the definition requires: a disclaimer of any right in a mechanical device or in letters, words and numerals appearing in the design, and the statement of novelty, are entered so that the claim on its face respects Section 2(d) rather than relying on the court to read the exclusions in. The proprietor who files thin representations to save professional fees has economised on the document the entire right consists of, and the saving is recovered, with interest, by the first defendant's counsel. It is one of this system's quiet virtues that its central document is short, visual and public: the quality of a design registration can be judged by any reader in a minute, and portfolios reveal their drafting standard at a glance.

## Should the term be longer? The extension debate

Reform discussion around the Act returns periodically to the term, and the positions are worth stating because the article's verdict depends on them. The case for extension rests on the products whose lives the fifteen year ceiling cuts short: furniture, lighting, ceramics, vehicles and architectural hardware include designs that sell unchanged for thirty years, and their makers argue that an appearance still earning its premium in year sixteen is not spent merely because the statute says so, pointing to the European twenty-five year maximum as the settled practice of a comparable system. The case against rests on the ratchet this article described: the design stock becomes the common inheritance of the trade, variations build on expired designs, and a longer monopoly delays the cycle by which a pioneer's appearance becomes everyone's starting point, with the spare parts problem sharpening as the term lengthens, since every added year is a year in which repair parts remain the original maker's captive market. Between the positions stands the Indian compromise that already exists inside Section 11: ten years certain, which covers the commercial life of most designs entire, and five more on application, which sorts proprietors by whether the design still earns enough to justify a fee and a diary entry. A term debate conducted honestly should also concede what the piracy figures showed about this legislature: the Act's numbers are revised rarely and reluctantly, and a design statute whose remedy cap has stood unrevised since the year 2000 is unlikely to be reopened for its term. The practitioner's conclusion is the one this article has drawn throughout: plan on fifteen years, treat them as generous for most products and tight for the exceptional ones, and build the trade mark and portfolio strategies for the exceptional ones early, while the design clock still has years to run.

## Passing off: the fallback that rarely substitutes

Every account of the unregistered design's position in India must reckon with passing off, the common law action that protects goodwill against misrepresentation, because it is the only appearance-adjacent remedy the unregistered designer has. Its conditions explain why it is a fallback and not a substitute. The claimant must prove goodwill in the appearance as an indicium of source, so that buyers seeing the shape or get-up take it to come from the claimant; must prove a misrepresentation by the defendant calculated to lead the public to believe the defendant's goods are the claimant's; and must prove damage. A new design, however original, has no goodwill at launch: the shape indicates nothing to anyone until the market has learned it, and by the time the market has learned it, the design has been disclosed, sold far beyond fifty reproductions, and copied in the interval when no right covered it. Passing off therefore protects the established product's appearance late in its life, on evidence of recognition the design system would have made unnecessary, and it never protects the appearance as appearance: the defendant's honest statement of different origin, clearly made, may answer the misrepresentation while the copying continues. The two doctrines protect different things, goodwill against deception and the registered design against copying, and the designer's error is to mistake the existence of the first for coverage by the second. Indian courts have kept the boundary: a claimant who could have registered and did not will find no sympathy in equity for an attempt to obtain through passing off the monopoly the register would have given on conditions the claimant declined to meet. The conclusion completes the article's central teaching from the negative side. For product appearance in India there is the register, and outside the register there is, for practical purposes, the season in which the copying happens and the hope that goodwill grows faster than the copies spread.

## Why the Designs Wing sits in Kolkata

The geography of the register is a survival, and it explains features of practice that puzzle newcomers. The unified patents and designs administration of the 1911 Act was centred on Kolkata, then the seat of the Government of India and its oldest patent office, and when the Patents Act 1970 rebuilt the patent system it kept the head office of the organisation there while patent work spread to branch offices in the other metropolitan cities. Design administration, the smaller and steadier jurisdiction, remained concentrated: the Designs Wing of the Patent Office at Kolkata examines and registers designs for the whole country, and the Controller of Designs' register is a single national register rather than a set of regional ones. The single register has substantive consequences. There is one search to run before filing or suing, one record of title, one cancellation forum at the registry stage, and one body of examining practice, which gives Indian design law a uniformity that larger, multi-office systems have to work to achieve. Electronic filing through the intellectual property office's portal has dissolved most of the practical distance the geography once imposed: applications are filed online from anywhere, hearings are routinely conducted without travel, and the register is searchable remotely. What remains of the Kolkata settlement is institutional memory: the examining cadre, the manual of practice and the accumulated register itself sit in one place, and the appeals and cancellation practice of the system still orients itself to a single registry mind. For the reader of the cluster, the point worth keeping is the contrast with the trade mark system, whose five registries and regional jurisdictions produce a more dispersed practice, and with the patent system, whose four offices divide the country's filings by territory. Design law's centralisation is one reason its doctrine has stayed comparatively coherent: one office, one register, and a small appellate case law speaking to both.

## Assessment and verdict

The Designs Act 2000 does a small number of things and does most of them competently. Its definition is well drawn: the eye test, the function exclusion and the artistic work exclusion together sort product appearance into the right regimes with a clarity the case law has been able to operate for two decades without serious strain. Its registration system is fast, cheap and public, and its cancellation system gives the register a self-cleaning mechanism that the eye-based evidence makes workable. Its term is defensible on the theory the Act embodies, that appearance is a short-lived commercial asset and that a fifteen year ceiling balances the designer's investment against the competitor's freedom to imitate what the market has absorbed.

Measured against comparable systems, the Indian term sits at the economical end of the range without being an outlier. A registered design in the European Union can be renewed to a maximum of twenty-five years, and the United States design patent runs fifteen years from grant for applications filed after the American move to the Hague system, close to the Indian ceiling. India's ten plus five is shorter than the European settlement and equal at its ceiling to the American, and the difference is a policy choice about how long appearance deserves a monopoly, not an accident of drafting: a system that gives a decade certain and a further five on request is a system that expects most designs to be commercially spent well before the right is.

Against that, the statute has two defects that repetition has made structural. The piracy sum in Section 22(2)(a) is a dead letter for serious cases, and its decay signals a legislature that has not revisited the Act's economics since the year 2000. And the Act's silence on unregistered designs, combined with the Copyright Act's fifty reproduction rule, produces the harshest forfeiture in Indian intellectual property law: the designer who manufactures first and takes advice afterwards loses everything, not through any judgment on the merit of the design but through the sequence of their own steps. The series to which this article belongs has returned repeatedly to rights lost by a step the holder takes without realising its legal effect. The design system is the purest case. The step is manufacturing, which every designer must take, and the protection lies entirely in taking one administrative step first. A designer or manufacturer reading this article for guidance, rather than for examination, should leave with three working rules: file before any disclosure, anywhere; treat the fifteen years as a countdown that begins on the filing date, not on the product's success; and never let a production run pass fifty pieces on the assumption that copyright is holding the position, because at fifty it has stopped doing so. How this right compares in term, cost and difficulty of enforcement with the patent and copyright alternatives for the same product is the question [the patent, copyright and trade mark comparison](/2019/12/15/patent-copyright-trademark-comparison/) takes up, and the comparison is where the design right's combination of cheapness, speed and brevity can be weighed against its neighbours. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is a registrable design in India?

A registrable design is the features of shape, configuration, pattern, ornament or composition of lines or colours applied to an article, in two dimensional or three dimensional form or both, by an industrial process or means, which in the finished article appeal to and are judged solely by the eye, as Section 2(d) of the Designs Act 2000 defines it. The design must also pass Section 4: it must be new or original, not previously disclosed to the public anywhere in the world, significantly distinguishable from known designs, and free of scandalous or obscene matter. Registration is class-based, so the claim is the appearance on articles of the class in which the design is registered.

### Q: What cannot be registered as a design?

The definition itself excludes three things: any mode or principle of construction, anything which is in substance a mere mechanical device, and trade marks, property marks and artistic works as the neighbouring statutes define them. Function is therefore outside the design right from the start, however attractive the functional feature may be. Section 4 adds the subject matter bars: a design that is not new or original, one disclosed to the public anywhere before filing, one not significantly distinguishable from known designs or combinations of them, and one containing scandalous or obscene matter. A product's working principle belongs, if anywhere, to patent law, and its brand significance belongs to trade mark law.

### Q: How long does design protection last?

Copyright in a registered design lasts ten years from the date of registration under Section 11 of the Designs Act 2000. Before the ten years expire, the proprietor may apply, with the prescribed fee, for one extension of five years, giving a maximum term of fifteen years. There is no further renewal, and when the term ends the design enters the public domain. If the extension fee is missed and the design lapses, Section 12 allows restoration on application made within one year of the lapse where the failure was unintentional, on payment of the unpaid fee and an additional fee. Protection is short by design: compare copyright's term of the author's life plus sixty years.

### Q: What happens when a design is also an artistic work?

Section 15 of the Copyright Act 1957 arbitrates the overlap. Under Section 15(1), copyright does not subsist in a design that is registered under the Designs Act, so registration moves the subject matter wholly into the design regime with its short term. Under Section 15(2), copyright in a design capable of registration, but not registered, ceases as soon as any article to which the design has been applied has been reproduced more than fifty times by an industrial process by the copyright owner or a licensee. The Delhi High Court's Division Bench in Microfibres v Girdhar and Co held that the original artistic work, such as the drawing itself, keeps its copyright as a work, while the design derived from it for industrial use is governed by the fifty reproduction rule.

### Q: What remedies exist for design piracy?

Section 22 of the Designs Act offers the proprietor an election. Under Section 22(2)(a), the infringer is liable for each contravention to pay a sum not exceeding twenty-five thousand rupees, recoverable as a contract debt, subject to a total cap of fifty thousand rupees for any one design. Alternatively, under Section 22(2)(b), the proprietor may sue for damages and an injunction, and proprietors facing serious piracy ordinarily choose that route because the statutory sums have stood unchanged since the Act was passed. The suit must be filed in a court not below a District Judge, every Section 19 cancellation ground is open to the defendant as a defence, and an interim injunction is in practice the remedy that decides the commercial outcome.

### Q: Can a design registration be cancelled?

Yes. Section 19 allows any person interested to petition the Controller at any time after registration for cancellation on five grounds: the design was previously registered in India; it was published in India or any other country before the date of registration; it is not new or original; it is not registrable under the Act; or it is not a design within Section 2(d). The Controller hears the parties and may order cancellation, and an appeal lies to the High Court. There is no limitation period, so a registration is contestable for its whole life. Cancellation grounds also operate defensively: under Section 22(3) every Section 19 ground is available to a defendant in a piracy suit, which then transfers to the High Court under Section 22(4).

### Q: Is there any protection for unregistered designs?

No design right exists for an unregistered design under Indian law. The Designs Act protects only registered designs, and Section 15(2) of the Copyright Act withdraws copyright from a design that was capable of registration but never registered once the article has been reproduced more than fifty times industrially, so the unregistered designer who manufactures at scale holds neither right. What remains is peripheral: copyright in an underlying artistic work before the fifty reproduction threshold is crossed, passing off where the appearance has acquired goodwill and the defendant misrepresents source, and contract or confidentiality protection against those who received the design in confidence. Unlike the European Union, India grants no unregistered design right, not even a short one against copying, which makes filing before disclosure the system's central discipline.

### Q: How does design protection compare with a shape trade mark?

Both regimes can cover a product's shape, but they protect different things on different clocks. A design registration protects the appearance as such, on the articles of the registered class, for a maximum of fifteen years, and it requires no proof that buyers associate the shape with one maker. A shape trade mark protects the shape as an indicator of origin, potentially forever through ten year renewals, but it must be distinctive of one trader's goods, and Section 9(3) of the Trade Marks Act 1999 refuses shapes resulting from the nature of the goods, shapes necessary to obtain a technical result, and shapes giving substantial value to the goods. The practical sequence is design registration for the launch years, and a trade mark only if the shape earns distinctiveness before the design term expires.

### Q: Who owns a design created by an employee or commissioned from an outside designer?

Section 2(j) of the Designs Act defines the proprietor. The author of the design is the proprietor, but where the author executes the design for another person for good consideration, that other person is the proprietor, which covers both the staff designer employed by a manufacturer and the freelance designer commissioned by a client. The right also belongs to a person who has acquired the design or the right to apply it from the author, and to anyone on whom it devolves. Authorship still matters because originality under Section 2(g) is tested by whether the design originated from its author. Businesses should record proprietorship in the employment or commissioning contract, because the applicant before the Controller must claim as proprietor, and a title dispute is the first defence a piracy defendant will raise.

### Q: How is a design application filed and examined in India?

An application is made to the Controller of Designs at the Designs Wing of the Patent Office in Kolkata, filed electronically through the intellectual property office portal, in the prescribed form with the prescribed fee, identifying the article and the class in which registration is sought under the Locarno-based classification the Designs Rules adopt. It must be accompanied by representations of the design, usually drawings or photographs from several views, and may carry a statement of novelty. The examiner searches the register and the known field and raises objections where the design appears known, not significantly distinguishable, or outside Section 2(d). The applicant is heard on objections, and there is no opposition stage: registration follows if the objections are answered, and the date of registration is the date of application.

### Q: What is a statement of novelty in a design application?

A statement of novelty is the short statement filed with a design application identifying the features of the article in which novelty is claimed to reside. It matters because the right is read through the representations together with the statement: a proprietor may claim novelty in the shape as a whole, or in a particular feature such as the pattern on a chair's backrest, and the statement directs the comparisons the examiner, and later a court, will make. A well drawn statement confines the claim to what is genuinely new, which strengthens the registration against cancellation for want of significant distinguishability. It cannot rescue a design that is not new at all, and it cannot widen the claim later: a registration obtained on a narrow statement is enforced on that statement in a piracy suit.

### Q: Can a design be registered after the product has already gone on sale?

Ordinarily no. Section 4 bars registration of a design that has been disclosed to the public anywhere in India or in any other country, by publication in tangible form or by use or in any other way, before the filing date or the priority date. Selling the product, displaying it in a showroom or listing it in a catalogue is disclosure by use or publication, and it destroys registrability even though the designer is disclosing his own design, because the Act contains no general grace period for the designer's own disclosures. The narrow exceptions are the exhibition route in Section 21, which must be arranged before a notified exhibition, and disclosures made in confidence, which are not disclosures to the public. The safe sequence is file first, launch second.

### Q: Does exhibiting a design at a trade fair before filing destroy its novelty?

It can, and Section 21 of the Designs Act provides a narrow route through the danger. Display of a design at an exhibition to which the Central Government has extended that section, or publication of a description of it there, or display by another person without the proprietor's consent, does not prevent registration, but only if two conditions are met: the exhibitor gave the Controller previous notice in the prescribed form before exhibiting, and the application for registration is made within six months from the date of first exhibiting or first publishing the description. An exhibition outside that notification, or a failure to give the prior notice, leaves the display as an ordinary public disclosure under Section 4, which bars registration. Trade fair organisers' paperwork is not a substitute for the statutory notice.

### Q: What happens when a registered design lapses, and can it be restored?

A design lapses when its ten year term expires without the five year extension being applied for and paid under Section 11. Section 12 then gives the proprietor, or a legal representative or assignee, one year from the date the design ceased to have effect to apply for restoration, on the ground that the failure to pay the extension fee was unintentional. The Controller, if satisfied, restores the design on payment of the unpaid fee and an additional prescribed fee. Restoration is subject to Section 13, which protects a person who in good faith began using the lapsed design in the interval, by limiting the restored proprietor's rights against that person. After the one year window, the lapse is final and the appearance is in the public domain as a design.

### Q: Can a registered design be assigned or licensed to another person?

Yes. A registered design is transferable property. Under Section 30, a person who becomes entitled by assignment, transmission or operation of law may apply to the Controller to be registered as proprietor, and the Controller enters the assignment on proof of title; until entry, the assignment document may not be admitted as evidence of title in proceedings except by direction of the Controller or the court. A licence is the proprietor's written consent to another person applying the design, and it is the boundary of piracy under Section 22, since application with licence or written consent is lawful. Licences operate by contract and do not transfer title. Portfolios are priced in transactions on what the register shows, so assignments should be entered promptly.

### Q: Why should articles carry the design registration number?

Section 15 requires the proprietor, before delivery on sale of articles to which a registered design has been applied, to cause the articles to be marked with the prescribed mark, word or figures denoting that the design is registered, in practice the word Registered or its abbreviation with the registration number, alongside furnishing exact representations to the Controller where that was not done at filing. Marking is not a condition of the right's existence, but it conditions its full value: a proprietor who has not marked, and cannot show that all proper steps were taken to ensure marking, recovers no penalty or damages for an infringement unless the infringer knew or had notice of the proprietor's copyright in the design. Marking converts the infringer's ignorance from a shield into an irrelevance, and costs almost nothing.

### Q: Can a graphical user interface or icon be registered as a design in India?

In Designs Office practice, no. Although the Locarno classification contains a class for screen displays and icons, and the Designs (Amendment) Rules 2021 added Class 32 for graphic symbols and logos, the office has consistently declined to register graphical user interfaces and icons, reasoning that they do not answer the statutory definition: a design must be features applied to an article and judged on the finished article by the eye, and a screen display exists only when a device is in operation rather than as a feature of the finished article itself. Applicants have argued the contrary as interfaces became commercially central, and some foreign design systems accept such registrations. The Indian position rests on the text of Section 2(a) and Section 2(d), and unless the office practice or the Act changes, interface appearance is protected in India, if at all, through copyright and trade mark law.

### Q: How do courts decide whether a product copies a registered design?

By the eye. Piracy under Section 22 is established where the defendant has applied the registered design or a fraudulent or obvious imitation of it, and the comparison is between the defendant's article and the registered design as shown in the representations, informed by the statement of novelty. The Bombay High Court in Whirlpool v Videocon held that the imitation need not be an exact replica: what matters is whether the article produces the same overall visual impression, judged as a customer would judge it, because customers buy on general impression and imperfect recollection rather than by side by side inspection. Differences in detail that leave the impression untouched do not answer the claim, and the defendant's changes are disregarded if the features in which novelty resides have been taken. Fraudulent imitation covers colourable variations made to disguise the taking.

### Q: Does an Indian design registration protect the design in other countries?

No. Design rights are territorial, and an Indian registration is enforceable only in India. Section 44 provides the bridge: where India has reciprocal arrangements with a convention country, an applicant who filed first in that country may file in India within six months claiming the foreign date as the priority date, and an Indian applicant enjoys the corresponding facility abroad under the other country's law. India is not part of the Hague international registration system, so there is no single international application: protection in each market requires a national filing there, linked by the six month priority window running from the first filing. Because Section 4's disclosure bar is worldwide, the filing sequence must be planned globally from the start: a launch in one country before filing can destroy registrability in all of them.

### Q: Where is a design piracy suit filed, and why do some such suits move to a High Court?

Section 22(2) provides that no suit or proceeding for piracy shall be instituted in a court inferior to a court of District Judge, so the suit begins in the district court. The movement upward comes from the defence. Under Section 22(3), every ground on which a registration may be cancelled under Section 19 is available as a ground of defence, and under Section 22(4), where any such ground is availed of, the suit stands transferred to the High Court for decision, because the validity of a registration is treated as a question for the court that also hears appeals from the Controller. A defendant therefore chooses, in effect, the forum by choosing the defence: contest copying alone and stay in the district court, or attack the registration and litigate in the High Court.
