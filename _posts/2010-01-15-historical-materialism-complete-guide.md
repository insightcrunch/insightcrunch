Ask two well-read people whether historical materialism has been refuted and you will usually get a confident yes and a confident no, followed by an exchange in which neither position moves. The reason is not stubbornness. The critic is attacking a claim that technological development determines the whole of social life with something approaching necessity, and that claim is in serious trouble. The defender is holding a claim that material conditions constrain what is historically possible and that ideas do not float free of the arrangements that sustain them, and that claim is close to something most working historians accept. Both are describing something real. Neither is describing what the other is describing.

That gap is the subject of this guide. Historical materialism is not one thesis. It is a family of at least six claims of descending strength, each with a different textual warrant, a different set of defenders, a different list of things it forbids, and a different account of what would count against it. Arranged from the strongest to the weakest they form a ladder, and the single most useful thing a reader can learn about this topic is how to identify which rung any given argument is standing on. Call it the strong-weak ladder rule: nearly every refutation of historical materialism attacks the top rung while nearly every defence retreats to the bottom rung, and an argument only counts once both parties have named their rung.

![The strong-weak ladder of historical materialism, the 1859 preface explained clause by clause with criticisms - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-07.webp)

The ladder is not a diplomatic device for splitting the difference. It is a description of what the tradition actually contains, and the evidence for it is that the disputes about which rung is correct are conducted mainly between Marxists rather than between Marxists and their critics. G. A. Cohen and Robert Brenner disagreed sharply about whether productive forces or class relations carry the explanatory weight, and both were working inside the theory. E. P. Thompson attacked the structural version with a ferocity no external critic matched. The rungs are real positions with real holders, and treating them as one position is what makes the argument about this theory so unproductive.

## What historical materialism claims, stated precisely

Historical materialism is the claim that the fundamental explanation of the structure of a society and of large-scale change in that structure lies in the way the society organises the production of its material life. The proposition has three parts, and separating them at the outset prevents most of the confusion that follows.

The first part is a claim about the units of analysis. Societies are to be analysed in terms of their productive forces, meaning the means of production together with the labour power and technical knowledge that set them in motion, and their relations of production, meaning the effective control over those forces and over the labour that uses them. The relations of production in any class society are relations of appropriation: they determine who performs surplus labour and who lives on it. This pairing, forces and relations, is the analytical core, and everything else in the theory is built on it.

The second part is a claim about explanatory priority. The forces and relations of production are not one factor among several competing for causal weight in historical explanation. They occupy a distinctive position: the legal system, the political apparatus, the prevailing religious and philosophical outlook, and the forms of consciousness through which people understand their situation are to be explained by reference to the productive arrangements rather than treated as autonomous starting points. This is the claim that generates almost all the controversy, and it is also the claim that admits the widest range of strengths, from a rigid determination to a loose setting of limits.

The third part is a claim about the mechanism of change. Productive forces develop. At some point the relations of production that facilitated that development begin to obstruct it, and the resulting tension is resolved through a period of social transformation in which the obstructing relations give way to relations better suited to the forces that have grown up. This is the fetters argument, and it is the engine of the theory. Where the theory locates the agency that carries out the transformation, whether in class struggle, in the interests of a rising class, or in a quasi-automatic adaptation, is itself disputed, and the dispute maps onto the rungs of the ladder directly.

Two clarifications belong here before the argument develops. Historical materialism is not the claim that material motives govern individual behaviour; that is a psychological thesis and Marx does not hold it, since his account of the capitalist as a personification of a function rather than as a greedy individual makes exactly the opposite move. And it is not the claim that ideas have no causal power; the theory is about the explanation of durable social structures, not about the causal inertness of belief, and any reading that produces the latter has misread the fetters argument, which requires that people act.

### Is historical materialism just economic determinism?

Not in most of its versions. Economic determinism holds that economic factors are the sole determinants of social and political outcomes. Historical materialism in its middle and weaker versions claims explanatory priority for productive arrangements while allowing political and ideological forms genuine causal power, and Engels spent the last decade of his life saying so in print.

The distinction is worth drawing precisely because so much writing collapses it. Determination admits of degrees, and the tradition contains at least three readings of what determination means: that the productive base fixes the superstructural forms, that it selects among a limited range of possible forms, and that it sets outer limits and exerts pressures without fixing anything. The [full debate over economic determination](/2010/03/15/economic-determinism-debate/) is the property of its own article in this series, and this guide states the positions without adjudicating between them, because adjudication there would duplicate work that belongs elsewhere.

## Where the theory comes from in the texts

The theory is developed in three places of very different status, and knowing which is which separates a reader who can cite from one who can only assert.

The first extended development is in The German Ideology, written by Marx and Engels between 1845 and 1846 and unpublished in either author's lifetime. The manuscripts advance the argument that consciousness cannot be treated as an independent starting point, that human beings must be understood first as producers of their means of subsistence, and that the forms of ownership succeed one another as the division of labour develops. The text is indispensable and its status is complicated: what is read as its famous first part survives as separately paginated manuscript bundles whose ordering is an editorial decision, and no editor has succeeded in ordering them uncontroversially. A writer citing it should say what they are citing. The [reader's guide to The German Ideology](/2010/03/01/german-ideology-readers-guide/) treats the manuscript problem in the detail it deserves.

The second is The Poverty of Philosophy, published in 1847 as a polemic against Pierre-Joseph Proudhon, which contains the compressed illustration that has done more to shape and distort the popular understanding of this theory than any other sentence Marx wrote. The line pairs the hand-mill with feudal society and the steam-mill with industrial capitalism, and it is genuinely Marx's: "the hand-mill gives you society with the feudal lord" is his own phrasing in that work. What it is not is a statement of a causal law, and reading it as one produces the technological determinism that the theory's critics then attack. It sits inside a polemic, it is doing illustrative work in an argument about the historicity of economic categories, and Marx never developed it into a general principle. Any account that leads with this line has chosen its evidence badly.

The third and canonical statement is the preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy, published in 1859. It runs to a few pages, it was written as an autobiographical account of how Marx arrived at his research programme, and it contains the entire architecture of the theory in compressed form. Every subsequent argument about historical materialism, in the tradition and against it, is an argument about the propositions in this text. It is short enough to read three times in an evening and dense enough to require it, and the next section takes it clause by clause because no competing treatment does.

There is a fourth source of a different kind: Marx's own historical writing, principally The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte of 1852 and the material on so-called primitive accumulation in the first volume of Capital of 1867. These show the theory being used, and they are considerably more subtle than the programmatic statements. The Eighteenth Brumaire opens with the proposition that people make their own history but not in circumstances of their own choosing, which is the theory's own statement of the relation between agency and constraint and is the strongest available correction to any reading that makes the theory fatalistic.

### Which text contains the clearest statement of the theory?

The first extended statement is in The German Ideology, written between 1845 and 1846 and unpublished until the twentieth century. The canonical compressed statement, which every subsequent argument addresses, is the preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy of 1859. Neither text uses the phrase historical materialism, which is a later coinage.

That last point is not pedantry. Neither Marx nor Engels used the phrase historical materialism in the founding texts. Marx describes his approach as a guiding thread for his studies; Engels, in the 1890s, uses the phrase materialist conception of history and, in the introduction to an English edition of his own popularising pamphlet, uses historical materialism as a convenient name. The naming happened alongside the systematising, and a reader who knows that will treat any account presenting the theory as a finished doctrine with the scepticism it deserves.

## The canonical preface, clause by clause

The preface compresses six distinct propositions into a few paragraphs, and they are not equally strong, not equally supported elsewhere in Marx's work, and not equally binding on anyone who calls themselves a materialist about history. Reading them separately is the whole exercise.

### The clause on necessary and independent relations

Marx begins the theoretical passage by saying that in the social production of their life, people enter into relations that are definite, necessary, and independent of their will, and that these relations of production correspond to a definite stage of development of their material productive forces. Three things are being asserted at once and they are frequently run together.

The first is that productive relations are not chosen. A person born into a society does not select the property system, the labour market, or the technical division of labour they enter; they encounter these as facts. This is a claim about structural constraint on individuals and it is nearly uncontroversial. The second is that these relations form a system with its own requirements rather than a collection of individual arrangements. The third and much stronger claim is the correspondence: that the character of the productive relations is fixed by the stage the productive forces have reached. That third claim is where the ladder's top rung lives, and it is the claim that has to bear the weight of the entire theory in its strong form.

Notice what the correspondence clause does not say. It does not say that a given technology permits exactly one set of relations, which would make history a machine. It says that relations correspond to a stage, which is compatible with a range of relations being possible at any stage and with only some being viable. Whether Marx meant the narrow reading or the permissive one is disputed and the text alone does not settle it, which is one reason the strong and weak versions can both claim textual warrant.

### The clause on the real foundation

The relations of production, taken together, constitute the economic structure of society, which Marx calls the real foundation on which a legal and political superstructure rises and to which definite forms of social consciousness correspond. This is the base and superstructure formulation, and it is the single most quoted and most misapplied thing in the whole theory.

Two points are worth extracting now, though the boundary questions belong to the [dedicated treatment of base and superstructure](/2010/02/01/base-and-superstructure-explained/) rather than here. First, the base is not the economy in the ordinary journalistic sense of markets, prices, and money. It is the ensemble of relations of production, meaning the relations through which surplus labour is performed and appropriated, which is a narrower and more specific object. Second, the German original uses an ordinary architectural word for the superstructure, not a technical coinage, which is part of why the metaphor invites a rigidity the surrounding argument does not require. A superstructure in ordinary German building vocabulary rests on a foundation and is also a functioning part of the building, which is closer to the argument than the popular image of a passive reflection.

The clause also introduces a distinction that most summaries drop entirely. Marx distinguishes the legal and political superstructure, which rises on the base, from the definite forms of social consciousness, which correspond to it. Those are two different relations described in one sentence. Institutions are said to rise on the foundation; forms of consciousness are said to correspond. Whether that difference is deliberate is arguable, but it is in the text, and a reading that treats law, the state, religion, and philosophy as one undifferentiated layer has flattened something the original does not flatten.

### The clause on consciousness and social being

Marx then states the inversion that gives the theory its name: it is not the consciousness of people that determines their being, but their social being that determines their consciousness. The formulation is aimed at the idealist historiography he had been arguing with since the early 1840s, in which the development of ideas drives the development of institutions.

The claim is easy to caricature and the caricature is easy to refute, so precision matters. Marx is not asserting that individuals think whatever their economic position dictates, which is a psychological claim contradicted by the existence of Marx, a bourgeois intellectual who reached conclusions his position supposedly forbade. He is asserting that the forms of consciousness available in a period, the categories in which questions get posed, the range of the thinkable, are to be explained by reference to the social organisation of production rather than treated as self-generating. That is a claim about explanation in social theory, and it is the foundation of the whole tradition of ideology critique that followed.

### The fetters clause

At a certain stage of development, Marx writes, the material productive forces come into conflict with the existing relations of production, or, in legal expression, with the property relations within which they had been at work until then. From forms of development of the productive forces these relations turn into their fetters, and an epoch of social revolution begins.

This is the engine, and it is also the clause that competing summaries most often drop, because it complicates the determinism story they want to tell. Three features deserve attention. First, the conflict is between forces and relations, not between classes directly, which is why the relationship between the fetters argument and the class struggle argument is a genuine problem inside the theory rather than a synonym. Second, the parenthetical about legal expression is a considerable concession: it acknowledges that the relations of production are ordinarily encountered and described in legal vocabulary, which is exactly the fact that generates the circularity problem the theory has struggled with ever since, since law is superstructural and the base is supposed to explain it. Third, the clause says an epoch of social revolution begins, not that a revolution succeeds. Nothing in the sentence guarantees an outcome.

### The clause on the transformation and on self-understanding

Marx distinguishes the transformation of the economic conditions of production, which can be determined with the precision of natural science, from the legal, political, religious, artistic, and philosophical forms in which people become conscious of the conflict and fight it out. He then adds a methodological rule of unusual sharpness: just as one does not judge an individual by what they think of themselves, one does not judge a period of transformation by its own consciousness of itself.

This clause is doing two jobs. It is asserting an asymmetry between the two levels, with one being susceptible to something like scientific determination and the other not. And it is establishing the interpretive rule that governs Marxist historiography: the self-understanding of historical actors is evidence about the period, not the explanation of it. That rule is enormously productive and it is also the source of the charge of condescension that ideology critique attracts, since it licenses the analyst to know better than the participants.

The clause also contains the theory's clearest statement that the ideological forms are where the conflict is actually fought. People do not experience a contradiction between forces and relations; they experience a religious controversy, a constitutional crisis, a war over tariffs. The theory does not deny this and does not treat it as illusion. It claims that the material conflict is what explains why the religious controversy takes the form and the intensity it does.

### The restraint clause

The preface then supplies the qualification that its most confident readers ignore. No social order, Marx writes, perishes before all the productive forces for which it is sufficient have developed, and new higher relations of production never appear before the material conditions of their existence have matured within the framework of the old society. Humanity therefore sets itself only such tasks as it can solve, since the task arises only where the material conditions for its solution are present or at least in the process of formation.

This is the most restrictive thing in the theory and it cuts in two directions at once. Against voluntarist politics, it says that a revolution attempted before the conditions exist will not produce the society it intends, which is precisely the argument that Menshevik and later social democratic critics turned against the Bolsheviks in and after 1917. Against the charge of fatalism, it says that possibility is bounded rather than that outcome is fixed, since a task arising is not a task performed. And against the theory itself, it supplies a hedge so generous that any failed transformation can be explained by saying the conditions had not matured, which is the elasticity problem in its most concentrated form.

### The clause on the epochs

Finally, Marx names the Asiatic, ancient, feudal, and modern bourgeois modes of production as epochs marking progress in the economic formation of society, and describes the bourgeois relations of production as the last antagonistic form, with the productive forces developing within bourgeois society creating the material conditions for the solution of that antagonism.

Two things about this list matter more than anything else written about it. It contains four terms, not five, and Marx does not present it as an exhaustive universal sequence through which every society must pass. He presents it as marking epochs in a progress he is describing, and he does so in a single sentence at the end of a compressed autobiographical preface, having given the theoretical architecture the preceding pages. Everything the textbooks have made of this list is an extrapolation, and the section below on the five-stage schema shows exactly when and by whom the extrapolation was performed.

The other thing is the final clause, describing the bourgeois form as the last antagonistic form. This is the one point at which the preface says something about the future that the rest of the theory does not obviously license. A theory of how modes of production succeed one another does not by itself entail that the sequence terminates, and the claim that this one does is the closest the passage comes to the philosophy of history that Marx spent his last decade denying he had written.

## The strong-weak ladder of historical materialism

The six propositions extracted above do not all have to be held together, and the tradition's history is largely a record of people holding some and dropping others. The following table arranges the resulting positions from strongest to weakest. Each row is a version of historical materialism that someone has defended in print, with the textual warrant it draws on, the kind of theorist who holds it, what it forbids, and what evidence would count against it.

The ladder is the artifact this guide exists to supply, and its use is diagnostic. When someone says historical materialism has been refuted, ask which rung. If they mean the first, they are attacking a position with real defenders and a real problem, and the argument is worth having. If they are attacking the first while their opponent defends the fifth, the exchange is not an argument at all. The corresponding discipline applies to defenders: a defence that begins on the first rung and finishes on the fifth has conceded the dispute while appearing to win it, and this manoeuvre is common enough in the literature to deserve a name.

## The rungs examined

### Rung one and the primacy of the productive forces

The strongest version holds that the productive forces have explanatory primacy: relations of production obtain because they are the relations that develop the forces at the level the forces have reached, and the superstructure obtains because it stabilises those relations. The most rigorous statement of this position is not a nineteenth-century one. It is G. A. Cohen's reconstruction of 1978, which sets out to defend the old-fashioned reading against a century of qualification, and which does so by arguing that the theory's explanations are functional explanations of a legitimate kind.

Cohen's argument turns on a distinction that matters. He redescribes the relations of production not as legal ownership but as effective powers over productive forces and over labour power, which detaches the base from the legal vocabulary and answers the circularity problem the fetters clause creates. He then argues that the development thesis, that productive forces tend to develop through history, can be grounded in facts about human rationality and scarcity that do not themselves require the theory. The combination gives him a version of historical materialism that is strong, precise, and stated in a form that can be assessed.

What the rung forbids is substantial. It forbids explaining a durable set of production relations by the political preferences of a ruling group, by the content of a legal system, or by cultural inheritance alone. Applied to real historical questions, this is a live constraint: it rules out a large class of explanations of the persistence of serfdom, of the divergence between European and Asian economic trajectories, and of the survival of slavery in expanding market economies.

What would count against it is equally specific. Two societies at comparable technical levels sustaining persistently different production relations, without either converging, is the standard counter-case, and the comparative history of eastern and western Europe after the fifteenth century is exactly that case, which is why it became the terrain of the transition debates. A defender can answer that the technical levels were not in fact comparable or that the divergence was not stable, but the answer has to be made on the evidence rather than by definition.

### Rung two and the fetters argument

The second rung drops the primacy claim while keeping the mechanism. It holds that forces and relations periodically come into contradiction and that this contradiction drives epochal change, without committing to the view that the forces determine which relations arise. This is the version most people mean by historical materialism and it is the version examined in schools and universities.

Its advantage is that it accommodates the historical record more comfortably. Its cost is that the fetters argument without the primacy thesis has a gap where its explanation should be. If the forces do not determine the relations, then the fact that the relations have become fetters does not by itself explain their replacement, and something else has to do the work of getting from the contradiction to the outcome. That something else is normally class struggle, which is how rung two collapses into rung three under pressure.

### Rung three and the primacy of class relations

The third rung reverses the explanatory order. It holds that class relations are the fundamental structure, that the development of the productive forces is something that class relations permit or obstruct rather than something that generates them, and that transitions are explained by the outcome of conflicts between classes over the terms of surplus extraction.

This position has the strongest support in the actual practice of Marxist historians, and the debate opened by Robert Brenner in 1976 over the origins of agrarian capitalism in England is its most developed statement. Brenner's argument is that demographic and commercial explanations of the transition fail because identical demographic pressures produced opposite outcomes in different regions, and that the difference is explained by the balance of class forces on the land. Whatever the verdict on the specific historical case, the methodological point stands: a theory that predicts a uniform response to a uniform pressure is falsified by divergent outcomes, and a theory in which class conflict mediates the response is not.

The cost of rung three is that it weakens the connection between historical materialism and any general theory of history. If outcomes turn on the contingent balance of class forces, then history has no direction, the sequence of modes of production is not a sequence, and the theory becomes a framework for explaining particular transitions rather than a claim about the shape of the human past. Defenders regard this as maturity. Critics regard it as the theory dissolving into ordinary historiography with a distinctive vocabulary.

### Rung four and the structuralist reconstruction

The fourth rung is the attempt to keep determination while granting the political and ideological levels genuine effectivity. Its vocabulary comes from Althusser: overdetermination, structural causality, relative autonomy, and determination in the last instance. The claim is that a social formation is a structured whole in which the economic level determines which level is dominant, so that in a given formation the political or the ideological can be dominant while the economic remains determinant.

The position is more sophisticated than its detractors allow and more fragile than its defenders allowed. Its fragility is captured in Althusser's own remark that the lonely hour of the last instance never comes, which was intended as a warning against expecting a pure economic determination to be visible and has been received as an admission that the determination does no work. Once relative autonomy is granted without a specification of its limits, the position becomes difficult to distinguish from a general claim that everything affects everything, which is not a theory.

### Rungs five and six and what remains

The fifth rung, that material conditions set limits and exert pressures without determining outcomes, is Raymond Williams's reformulation and it is where a great deal of contemporary social and cultural history actually operates. Its strength is that it is almost certainly true. Its weakness is the same fact: a claim that almost no historian would deny is not doing much theoretical work, and the elasticity objection lands on it with full force.

The sixth rung is not a substantive claim at all but a research strategy: begin with the organisation of material production and see how far it takes you. Assessed as a strategy rather than as a law, it has been extraordinarily productive, and its products include most of what social history became after the middle of the twentieth century. Whether a research strategy is what the tradition set out to defend is a fair question, and the honest answer is no.

## The five stages that Marx did not write

Open almost any revision guide, textbook summary, or examination answer on this subject and you will find a sequence of five stages: primitive communism, slavery, feudalism, capitalism, and socialism or communism, presented as the path history takes. The sequence is presented as Marx's. It is not.

What Marx wrote, once, in a single sentence at the end of the 1859 preface, names four things: the Asiatic, the ancient, the feudal, and the modern bourgeois modes of production, described as epochs marking progress in the economic formation of society. There is no primitive communism in the list, though the concept exists elsewhere in the tradition and was developed principally by Engels in his 1884 work on the family, private property, and the state, drawing on the anthropological work of Lewis Henry Morgan. There is no slavery as a separate stage; the ancient mode covers that ground and Marx does not treat it as universal. There is no socialism in the list, because the list is a description of what has occurred rather than a prediction. And crucially there is no statement that every society passes through every term.

The rigid five-stage ladder was formalised much later. Its most influential codification is the section on dialectical and historical materialism that Stalin published in 1938 as part of the official history of the Soviet Communist Party, which presents five modes of production as a law-governed succession through which societies pass, and which became the compulsory framework for teaching across an enormous territory for decades. The removal of the Asiatic mode from the schema, following theoretical debates in the Soviet Union in the early 1930s, was the change that made the sequence tidy, and the reasons for the removal were as much political as scholarly, since a category describing a state-dominated agrarian formation with a bureaucratic ruling stratum had awkward applications close to home.

The consequence for a modern reader is direct. A summary presenting the five stages as Marx's own theory is reproducing a Stalin-era gloss, usually without knowing it, and the gloss reverses the meaning of the theory on the exact point Marx was most insistent about at the end of his life. This is not an obscure textual quibble. It is the difference between a theory of the constraints on historical possibility and a philosophy of history in which the future is written.

### Where does the five-stage schema actually come from?

No. Marx names four epochs, once, in the 1859 preface, and does not present them as a universal compulsory sequence. The five-stage schema was codified later, most influentially in a Soviet statement of 1938, and Marx explicitly rejected the conversion of his account of Western European development into a universal theory of history.

### The late letters that settle Marx's own view

Two documents in Marx's last decade settle his own view, and almost no competing treatment uses them.

The first is a letter Marx drafted in 1877 to the editorial board of a Russian journal, responding to a critic who had read his account of the genesis of capitalism in Western Europe as a general theory. Marx objects in terms that leave nothing to interpretation: his sketch of the origins of capitalism in Western Europe is not to be metamorphosed into a historico-philosophical theory of the general path every people is fated to tread, whatever the historical circumstances in which it finds itself. He goes further, offering a comparison from ancient Rome in which the dispossession of the peasantry produced not capitalist relations but a slave economy, on the grounds that similar events in different historical settings produce different results. The letter was found among his papers and published after his death, which is why it circulated late and why the schema it refutes had time to harden.

The second is the set of drafts Marx wrote in 1881 in reply to Vera Zasulich, who had asked him directly whether the Russian peasant commune was doomed to dissolve under capitalist development or whether it might serve as a starting point for a socialist development that skipped the capitalist stage. Marx wrote several long drafts and sent a short polite reply. The drafts take the possibility seriously, argue that the analysis in Capital concerning the expropriation of the agricultural producer is expressly restricted to Western Europe, and treat the commune's fate as an open question depending on political circumstances rather than on a law of development. The preface Marx and Engels wrote for a Russian edition of the Manifesto in 1882 makes the same point in public form.

Taken together, these documents establish that the author of the theory rejected the stagist reading in the strongest terms available to him. They do not establish that the stagist reading is wrong, since an author's intentions do not settle what their theory implies, but they do establish that attributing it to Marx is a factual error. For a student, this pair of documents is the single highest-value thing on the topic, because it converts a memorised list into an argument.

## Engels's later correspondence and what it corrected

After Marx's death, Engels spent much of his remaining energy correcting a reading of the theory that had taken hold among younger adherents, in which every historical development was to be derived directly from economic causes. Four letters carry the correction and they are cited constantly, usually without their dates or recipients being got right.

The letter to Joseph Bloch, written in 1890, is the most quoted. Engels states that according to the materialist conception the ultimately determining element in history is the production and reproduction of real life, that neither he nor Marx ever asserted more than this, and that anyone who twists this into the claim that the economic element is the only determining one turns the proposition into a meaningless phrase. He describes the various elements of the superstructure as exercising their influence on the course of the historical struggles and in many cases preponderating in determining their form, and he offers the image of history as a resultant of innumerable intersecting forces, a parallelogram of forces in which each individual will contributes to a result that nobody willed.

The letter to Conrad Schmidt, also of 1890, addresses the same problem from the side of method, warning against treating the materialist conception as a formula for constructing history rather than as a guide to studying it, and insisting that the whole of the relevant history must be studied afresh. The letter to Franz Mehring of 1893 introduces the idea that ideologists work with thought material they take as given without examining its origins, and it is in this letter that the phrase false consciousness appears, a phrase that appears nowhere in Marx and that subsequent literature attributes to him with remarkable consistency. The letter to Heinz Starkenburg, sometimes catalogued under the name Borgius, written in 1894, restates the reciprocal action of political and ideological forms on the economic base and adds the observation that economic conditions are the ultimately determining factor but not the only active cause.

The interpretive question is what these letters amount to. One reading treats them as a late softening, in which Engels retreats from a determinism the founding texts asserted, either because the theory had proved too rigid or because he was managing a political movement. The other reading treats them as a restatement of what the 1859 preface already contained, pointing to the preface's own distinction between the superstructure and forms of consciousness, to the fetters clause's insistence that the conflict is fought out in ideological forms, and to the opening of The Eighteenth Brumaire on inherited circumstances. The second reading has the better of the textual argument. What is not disputable is that the letters are the authorised statement of the theory's scope, that they postdate the founding texts by three decades, and that any account of historical materialism that omits them is presenting a version its surviving author explicitly disowned.

Engels's own contribution is more ambiguous than this suggests, and honesty requires saying so. His popularising works, particularly the 1880 pamphlet on utopian and scientific socialism and the longer polemic it was extracted from, present the theory with a confidence and a systematic completeness that Marx's own writing does not, and they extend materialism into a general philosophy of nature that Marx never wrote. The Second International orthodoxy that the 1890s letters were correcting was built substantially out of the 1870s and 1880s material Engels himself had produced.

## The misreadings, named and corrected

Five errors do most of the damage in general-audience writing on this subject, and each has a specific correction that a reader can apply immediately.

The first is the five-stage schema, treated above. The correction is that Marx names four epochs once and explicitly refused to universalise them, and that the compulsory ladder is a later codification.

The second is that historical materialism denies any role to individuals, ideas, religion, or accident. The correction is textual and abundant. The Eighteenth Brumaire is a study of how a specific individual exploited a specific conjuncture, and its opening proposition, that people make their own history but not under conditions they choose, is the theory's own statement of the relation between agency and constraint. The 1859 preface locates the ideological forms as the medium in which the conflict is fought out, which requires that they be causally efficacious. Engels's parallelogram image in the Bloch letter makes individual wills constituents of the historical resultant rather than epiphenomena of it. What the theory denies is that these things function as ultimate explanations, not that they function at all. A historian who explains the English civil war partly through religious conviction is not violating the theory; a historian who explains it entirely through religious conviction with no account of the material interests and property relations in play is.

The third is that historical materialism is the same thing as dialectical materialism. It is not, and the confusion is compounded by the fact that Marx used neither phrase. Dialectical materialism, in the form that entered the textbooks, is a general philosophy of nature and knowledge, holding that matter is primary, that development everywhere proceeds through contradiction, and that certain laws of dialectics govern nature as well as society. Its lineage runs through Engels's writing on nature, through Georgi Plekhanov, who supplied the name, and into the Soviet codification, where it acquired the status of compulsory doctrine and was taught as the philosophical foundation of which the historical theory was an application. That ordering, philosophy first and history derived from it, is exactly the reverse of how Marx worked, and it is one reason the Soviet exposition reads so unlike the primary texts. Historical materialism is a theory about the explanation of social structures and social change. One can hold the second without the first, and a great deal of twentieth-century Western Marxism did precisely that, regarding the extension of dialectics to nature as a category error. The [dedicated treatment of the determination question](/2010/03/15/economic-determinism-debate/) covers the relation between them in more depth.

The fourth is that the theory predicts revolution in the most advanced economies as a matter of necessity. The restraint clause of the 1859 preface does say that new relations do not appear before the material conditions have matured, which supports an expectation that the transformation comes where development has gone furthest. But an expectation grounded in a statement about necessary conditions is not a prediction of a sufficient condition, and the theory says an epoch of social revolution begins, not that it concludes successfully. The record, in which socialist revolutions occurred in agrarian economies and not in industrial ones, is a serious problem for the theory and should be stated as one. It is a problem about the theory's expectations, not a refutation of a prophecy the theory did not make.

The fifth is that Engels's 1890s letters were a retreat. The evidence for the alternative reading is set out above, and the practical consequence is that a writer should stop describing the letters as a concession, since doing so implicitly concedes that the founding statement was as crude as its critics say.

### Where does individual agency fit into the theory?

No. The theory holds that individuals act within circumstances they inherit rather than choose, and that their actions are explained by reference to those circumstances. The Eighteenth Brumaire is an extended study of individual political agency, and Engels's image of history as a resultant of intersecting individual wills makes those wills constituents of the outcome.

## The strongest objection: a function without a mechanism

The sharpest thing said against historical materialism in the modern literature is not that it is politically dangerous, not that it failed to predict, and not that it neglects culture. It is a problem about the logical form of its explanations, and it is stated here at full strength before any reply.

The theory explains the existence of a set of production relations by the fact that those relations develop the productive forces. It explains the existence of a superstructure by the fact that the superstructure stabilises the relations. Both explanations have the same shape: a thing exists because of a beneficial effect it produces. That is a functional explanation. Functional explanations are not automatically illegitimate; biology is full of them, and they are legitimate there because natural selection supplies a mechanism connecting the beneficial effect to the persistence of the trait, through differential reproduction over generations. Historical materialism specifies no such mechanism. It asserts the functional relationship and leaves the connection unexplained.

The objection was pressed hardest by Jon Elster, in his study of Marx published in 1985 and in a series of related papers, and it has two parts. The first is the logical point just stated: without a mechanism, a functional explanation is a promissory note rather than an explanation, and the appearance of explanatory power comes from the mind's willingness to accept purpose as a cause. The second is a charge about practice: Marxist historiography routinely slides from observing that an institution serves capital's interests to concluding that it exists because it serves them, without any account of the process by which capital's interests could have produced it. Elster's alternative is methodological individualism and the specification of intentional mechanisms, in which explanations must be cashed out in terms of the beliefs, interests, and actions of identifiable agents.

The most serious defence is Cohen's. He argues that functional explanations can be legitimate where a consequence law holds, meaning a law of the form that whenever a structure of a certain kind would have a certain beneficial effect, a structure of that kind appears. Such a law can be confirmed by evidence and can support explanation even where the mechanism connecting the effect to the cause is unknown, exactly as the theory of natural selection was confirmed and used before genetics supplied the mechanism. Cohen accepts that a mechanism must exist and offers sketches of what it might be, including the suggestion that agents with an interest in developing the productive forces will tend to select the relations that permit it, and that unfavourable relations will tend to be selected against through competition between societies.

Three things should be said about where this exchange leaves the theory.

The mechanism gap is real and defenders of the strong version concede it. Cohen's own defence is explicit that a mechanism is required and that the theory does not supply one, which is a considerable admission from the position's most rigorous advocate. A theory whose central explanations depend on an unspecified process is in a weaker position than one that supplies the process, and no amount of methodological argument about consequence laws changes that.

The objection's force varies sharply by rung, which is exactly what the ladder predicts. It bites hardest on rung one, where the functional form is explicit. It bites much less on rung three, where class struggle supplies precisely the intentional mechanism Elster demands, since classes are composed of agents with interests who organise and fight. This is a substantial part of why the class-primacy version has been the productive one in historical practice: it satisfies the demand for mechanism that the forces-primacy version cannot.

And the objection generalises further than its proponents sometimes acknowledge. Functional reasoning of the same form is pervasive in sociology, in institutional economics, and in evolutionary explanation outside biology, and a standard that condemns historical materialism condemns a good deal else. That is not a defence of the theory so much as a caution against treating the objection as uniquely damaging. The [full engagement with the case against historical materialism](/2010/04/15/case-against-historical-materialism/) works through this exchange and the others in the detail a summary cannot.

### Is historical materialism falsifiable?

It depends on the rung. The strong versions make specific claims that could fail, such as the prediction that comparable technical levels generate comparable production relations. The weakest versions, holding that material conditions constrain possibility, are close to unfalsifiable and are correspondingly close to propositions most historians already accept.

## What does the evidence show?

A theory of history cannot be tested the way a theory of combustion can, and pretending otherwise wastes everyone's time. What can be done is to identify the historical questions on which the theory's expectations differ from its rivals' and to ask what the research on those questions has established.

The transition from feudal to capitalist relations in Europe is the test case the tradition itself chose, and it has generated the most sustained empirical engagement. The exchange between Maurice Dobb and Paul Sweezy in the 1950s posed the question sharply: did feudalism dissolve because of contradictions internal to the relationship between lord and peasant, principally the struggle over rent and labour services, or because of the growth of trade and towns acting on it from outside? Dobb argued the internal case, Sweezy the external, and the disagreement is significant for this article because both are materialist explanations that make incompatible predictions. A theory that could accommodate either has not been tested by the exchange; a theory committed to one of them has.

The debate opened by Robert Brenner in 1976 sharpened the test further. Brenner's argument turned on a comparative observation: the demographic crises of the fourteenth century and the commercial expansion of the sixteenth affected large parts of Europe similarly, yet produced serfdom's decline in the west and its intensification in the east, and produced agrarian capitalism in England and peasant proprietorship in France. If demographic or commercial pressures were the driver, the outcomes should have converged. That they diverged is evidence that something mediating was doing the work, and Brenner's candidate was the balance of class power on the land. The responses, from historians working within and outside the tradition, contested the specific chronology, the characterisation of French and English property relations, and the treatment of the state, and the exchange remains unresolved in its particulars. What it established is more general: a mono-causal materialist explanation running directly from technical or demographic conditions to social relations does not survive comparative testing. That is evidence against rung one and evidence for rung three.

The second body of evidence concerns the relationship between technical change and social organisation. Here the record is genuinely mixed in a way that neither side finds comfortable. There are well-documented cases in which technical possibilities were available and were not adopted for reasons that look political rather than technical, which counts against the primacy thesis. There are equally well-documented cases in which the adoption of a technique reorganised social relations in ways that no participant intended or controlled, which counts for it. The literature in the sociology of work on how technologies of production are selected partly for their effects on managerial control rather than purely for productivity is directly relevant and is largely the product of researchers with no allegiance to the tradition, which makes it better evidence for this purpose than partisan work would be.

The third body of evidence concerns the prediction implicit in the restraint clause, that transformation comes where development has gone furthest. This is where the record is worst. The revolutions of the twentieth century that established states claiming this theory occurred in economies with small industrial sectors, large peasantries, and productive forces the theory itself would classify as immature. The advanced economies, where the theory expected the transformation, produced instead durable constitutional politics, an expanded welfare settlement, and no transformation at all. Defenders have answered with theories of uneven and combined development, with imperialism as the mechanism displacing the contradiction to the periphery, and with hegemony as the account of why consent held in the core. These are substantive arguments rather than evasions, and they are also exactly the kind of auxiliary construction that the elasticity objection targets. An honest summary records the failure of the original expectation and treats the auxiliaries as claims requiring their own evidence.

The fourth and least discussed body of evidence is comparative and archaeological, concerning the emergence of states and class divisions in the ancient world. The pattern here is that stratification and state formation appear repeatedly in agrarian societies that reached comparable levels of surplus production, which is a point in favour of the general materialist expectation, while the specific institutional forms vary enormously, which is a point against the strong correspondence claim. The convergence supports the weak version. The variation embarrasses the strong one.

### Which historical evidence bears on the theory most directly?

The strongest support is comparative: class stratification and state formation appear repeatedly across unconnected agrarian societies once surplus production reaches comparable levels, which is what a materialist expectation predicts and an idealist one does not. The strongest evidence against is the divergence of social relations across regions at similar technical levels.

## Rival accounts of historical change

A theory is assessed against its competitors rather than against perfection, and three rivals have done most of the work of framing the alternative.

The Weberian account, developed in Max Weber's work on the sociology of religion and on economic history, does not deny that material interests drive action. Weber's own formulation about ideas functioning as switchmen determining the tracks along which interests push action concedes the interests and disputes the direction. His argument concerning the relationship between ascetic Protestantism and the spirit of capitalism is frequently misdescribed as a claim that religion caused capitalism; the claim is narrower and concerns the origins of a specific economic ethic, and Weber explicitly denies the one-sided idealist thesis. The genuine disagreement is about explanatory priority and about whether the plurality of causes Weber insists on can be ranked at all. Weber also supplies a competing account of the modern state and of bureaucratic rationalisation as a process with a logic independent of class interest, which is a serious rival to the theory of the superstructure.

The demographic account, associated with M. M. Postan and later Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie, explains long medieval and early modern cycles through the relation between population and available land, with rising population depressing wages and raising rents until crisis intervenes. It has the virtue of specifying a mechanism precisely and the vulnerability that the Brenner critique exposed, namely that identical demographic movements produced divergent social outcomes.

The institutionalist account, in its modern form, explains long-run economic divergence through the security of property rights and the constraints on rulers, and treats institutional arrangements as causes of economic development rather than as effects of it. This is the direct inversion of the base and superstructure claim, and the debate between the two is live in economic history. Its weakness from a materialist standpoint is that it takes institutions as the explanatory starting point without a systematic account of why particular institutions emerge and persist, which is the question historical materialism was constructed to answer.

None of these rivals is an idealist theory in the sense the 1859 preface was attacking, which is a fact worth registering. The idealist historiography of the 1840s, in which the development of the spirit of an age drives its institutional history, has no serious contemporary defenders. Historical materialism won that argument so completely that its victory is invisible, and the live disputes are now between materialisms.

## Where the theory operates in scholarship now

The most honest account of the theory's current standing is that its strong version has few defenders, its weak version has been absorbed so thoroughly that it is no longer noticed, and its middle versions do real work in specific research areas.

In economic and social history the absorption is close to total. The proposition that the study of a period should begin with how its people made their living, that property relations structure political conflict, and that the self-descriptions of historical actors are evidence rather than explanation, are now methodological commonplaces held by historians who have never read the 1859 preface. The British Marxist historians of the mid-twentieth century, working on the English revolution, the eighteenth-century crowd, the formation of the working class, and medieval agrarian society, are the principal transmission route, and their influence on the discipline substantially exceeds the number of their followers.

In comparative and historical sociology, the framework survives in the study of state formation, of revolutions, and of long-run development, frequently in versions that give the state and international competition an autonomy the classical theory does not license. Theda Skocpol's structural analysis of revolutions is a case in point: it is materialist in method, it treats state structures and international pressures as causally primary in ways the classical theory does not, and it was written in explicit dialogue with the tradition.

In archaeology and anthropology, the materialist approach to the emergence of social complexity has been a major research programme, and the concept of articulation between modes of production supplied the framework for a generation of work on colonial and postcolonial economies. In geography, the analysis of uneven development and of the production of space is substantially a historical materialist programme and one of the few areas where the vocabulary is used unapologetically.

Where the theory has lost ground is in the general philosophy of history and in any claim about the direction of the human past. Very few working scholars now defend the proposition that history has a direction determined by the development of productive capacity, and the collapse of that claim is the largest change in the theory's standing over the past half-century. What has survived is the method rather than the philosophy, which is a substantial thing to survive but is not what the theory originally proposed.

### Do working historians use this framework today?

Yes, though usually without the name. The commitments that a period should be studied through how its people made their living, that property relations structure political conflict, and that actors' self-understanding is evidence rather than explanation, are standard methodological practice in social and economic history, including among historians with no interest in the wider theory.

## How the theory developed across Marx's own writing

Treating the theory as a single fixed position that Marx held from 1845 to 1883 produces bad readings, and the developmental story is one of the more useful things a reader can carry.

The earliest formulations, in the manuscripts of the mid-1840s, are framed through a philosophical anthropology. Human beings are distinguished from animals by the production of their means of subsistence, the division of labour develops through stages marked by forms of ownership, and the resulting alienation of the producer is the condition the argument is built to explain. The vocabulary is Feuerbachian and Hegelian, the argument moves through the critique of religion and philosophy to the critique of society, and the theory of history is doing service in a larger argument about human self-realisation.

By 1859 the anthropological framing has gone. The preface presents forces, relations, base, superstructure, and the fetters mechanism as an analytical apparatus with no explicit anthropology and no evaluative vocabulary. The change is real and it is what licenses the reading, argued most forcefully by Louis Althusser in the 1960s, that an epistemological break separates the early ideological writings from the mature scientific ones. Whether that reading survives is one of the long-running disputes in the literature, and the belated publication of the Grundrisse, the notebooks of 1857 and 1858, complicated it considerably, since those are mature texts using the early vocabulary freely.

The third phase is the one this guide has already treated: a marked retreat in the last decade from any universal claim. The 1877 letter, the Zasulich drafts of 1881, and the 1882 preface to a Russian edition of the Manifesto all point the same way, and the late ethnological notebooks, in which Marx read extensively in the anthropology of non-European societies, suggest a research programme that was becoming more comparative and less schematic rather than the reverse. Some scholars read this as Marx abandoning the developmental framework; others as him applying it more carefully. What cannot be defended is a portrait of a late Marx more confident about universal stages than the middle-period one, which is exactly the portrait the textbook tradition supplies.

A further complication deserves a sentence. The relationship between the theory of history and the critique of political economy is not one of general to particular. Capital is not an application of the 1859 preface. It is an analysis of one mode of production conducted with categories developed for the purpose, and it makes very few claims about historical sequence outside the material on primitive accumulation. A reader who expects the economics to confirm the theory of history will be disappointed, and a critic who treats a difficulty in the value theory as a difficulty for the theory of history has confused two components. The [complete guide to the first volume of Capital](/2010/06/01/capital-volume-one-complete-guide/) treats that analysis on its own terms.

## Technology and the productive forces

The question deserves its own treatment because the answer is more contested than any summary suggests, and because the technological reading is both the most influential popular version and the one with the weakest textual support.

The case for a central role is straightforward. The productive forces include instruments of production, and their development is what generates the tension with existing relations. The hand-mill line pairs a technique with a social form. Cohen's reconstruction makes the development of the forces the engine of the whole theory and grounds it in facts about human rationality under scarcity.

The case against is stronger than most readers expect. The productive forces in Marx's usage are not simply machines; they include labour power itself, meaning the skills, knowledge, and cooperative capacities of producers, and Marx repeatedly treats the organisation of labour as a productive force in its own right. On that reading, a change in how work is organised is a development of the forces even where no new machine appears, which drains the technological reading of much of its content. The analysis of cooperation and of the division of labour in the first volume of Capital argues explicitly that the productive power of combined labour exceeds the sum of its parts, which is a claim about social organisation rather than about technique.

More damagingly for the technological reading, Marx's own analysis of machinery treats the adoption of techniques as driven by the class relation rather than as driving it. Machinery is introduced, in his account, as a means of raising relative surplus value and of breaking the resistance of skilled workers, which makes the technical change an effect of the class struggle over the labour process rather than its cause. A reader who takes that analysis seriously cannot hold the strong technological version, and Marx wrote it himself, at length, in the book he considered his major work.

The honest conclusion is that the theory contains both a technological strand and a social-relational strand, that they pull in different directions, and that Marx did not resolve the tension. The subsequent tradition split along exactly this fault line, with the primacy theorists on one side and the political Marxists on the other. Recognising the tension as internal to the original rather than as a later corruption is what allows a reader to place any modern argument on the subject.

## Applying the theory: a worked demonstration

A framework is only usable if it produces determinate analysis, so it is worth showing the procedure on a case rather than asserting that it works.

Take the question of why chattel slavery expanded rather than contracted in parts of the Atlantic economy during a period when commercial and financial institutions were developing rapidly. An idealist explanation would look to the prevailing beliefs about race and to the moral outlook of the period. A demographic explanation would look to labour scarcity. An institutionalist explanation would look to the legal regime governing property in persons.

The materialist procedure asks a different opening question: what were the relations through which surplus labour was extracted, and what conditions made those relations viable and profitable relative to the alternatives available to those who commanded capital? That reframing immediately makes several things analytically central that the other approaches treat as background. The availability of land relative to labour matters because it determines whether free labourers can be induced to work for others at all, which is a structural rather than a moral question. The character of the crop and its processing requirements matters because it determines whether gang labour under supervision yields more than tenancy or smallholding. The relationship between the plantation economy and the metropolitan financial and shipping interests matters because it determines who bears the risk and who captures the return. The legal regime enters the analysis, but as something to be explained by the relations it secures rather than as their foundation.

Two disciplines follow from the procedure. The first is that the explanation must specify how the beneficial effect for the interested parties actually produced the institution, which is the mechanism demand the functional objection raises, and which in this case is answerable through identifiable agents pursuing identifiable interests through identifiable political channels. The second is that the account must be tested comparatively: if the relations are explained by the conditions, then regions with different conditions should show different relations, and regions with similar conditions should converge. A materialist account that cannot survive that comparative test has failed by its own standards.

What the procedure does not do is generate the answer automatically. It generates the questions and rules out a class of explanations, which is what a framework is for. A reader who finishes this article able to run those two steps on a case of their own has got what the theory is actually good for, and the sequence in which those categories are best introduced to a class is the business of the teaching articles in this series rather than of a concept pillar.

## For students and exam candidates

Historical materialism is the single most examined topic in this subject and it appears in at least four assessment contexts with different demands. Sociology papers want the theory as an explanatory framework, set against functionalist and Weberian alternatives, with the base and superstructure model as the centrepiece. Politics and political theory papers want it as a theory of social change and revolution, with the fetters mechanism and the question of inevitability doing the work. Philosophy papers want it as a thesis about explanation, with the functional-explanation problem and the falsifiability question as the evaluative material. Competitive examinations covering political science want the whole apparatus compressed, usually in a fixed word budget, with the ability to distinguish Marx's own statement from later codifications carrying a disproportionate share of the marks.

The one distinction that earns marks across all four is the ladder. An answer that states the theory as a single position and then evaluates it will be marked as competent. An answer that distinguishes the strong version from the weak version, states which version the standard criticism targets, and observes that the criticism's force varies accordingly, is doing what mark schemes call sustained analysis, and it does so in three sentences. Two supporting moves compound the effect: naming the four epochs of the 1859 preface rather than reciting five stages, and citing the 1877 letter as evidence that Marx rejected the universalising reading. Very few candidates do either, which is precisely why they signal command.

Three traps recur. The first is the five-stage schema, which is what most revision material supplies and which a well-read marker will recognise as a Soviet-era codification. The second is attributing dialectical materialism to Marx, which is a factual error that costs credit in any philosophy or theory paper. The third is using the twentieth-century record as an evaluative paragraph without connecting it to a specific claim in the theory, which reads as opinion rather than evaluation. The fix is to name the connective question explicitly: the record bears on the restraint clause, since revolutions occurred where the theory said the conditions had not matured, and that is a determinate criticism rather than a gesture.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The misconception that dominates a class on this topic is the five-stage ladder, and it is unusually hard to shift because the students did not invent it. It is in the textbook, it is in the revision site, and it was very likely in the previous year's teaching. Telling a class that the sequence is not Marx's tends to produce polite disbelief, since the assertion is competing against every other source they have consulted.

The question that surfaces it productively is comparative rather than textual. Ask the class to explain why two regions of Europe at similar technical levels in the same century developed opposite labour regimes, one moving away from serfdom and the other intensifying it. Students working with the ladder will attempt to place both regions on the same rung and will discover that they cannot. The failure is theirs to observe rather than the teacher's to announce, which is the only form in which it lands.

The extract that resolves it is the 1877 letter to the Russian editorial board, in which Marx himself refuses to have his account of Western European development converted into a universal path every people must tread, and offers the ancient Roman comparison in which similar dispossession produced a slave economy rather than a capitalist one. Handing a class the author's own repudiation of the schema they were taught changes the discussion in a way no amount of teacherly correction achieves. It also models the practice the course should be teaching, which is that a claim about what a thinker held is checkable against what the thinker wrote.

For a politically mixed room, the balance move that works is to teach the ladder of versions rather than a single position, and to ask students to identify which version a given criticism targets. A class that can see the theory has a strong form in trouble and a weak form that is nearly uncontroversial will not experience the session as advocacy in either direction, and both the committed and the sceptical students get something they can use.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Three verification habits prevent the errors that appear most often in professional writing on this topic.

Check the provenance of any statement of the theory before citing it as Marx's. The five stages are not his, dialectical materialism is not his phrase, and the systematic exposition most sources rely on descends from Engels's popularising works of the 1870s and 1880s and from the Soviet codification of 1938. Where a summary presents a tidy law-governed sequence, it is reproducing that lineage. Citing the 1859 preface while summarising the Stalin-era gloss is the specific error to watch for, and it is common enough in otherwise careful writing to be worth a deliberate check.

Check the recipients and dates of the Engels correspondence, because they are misreported constantly. The four relevant letters are to Joseph Bloch in 1890, to Conrad Schmidt in 1890, to Franz Mehring in 1893, and to Heinz Starkenburg in 1894, the last of which appears in some catalogues under a different addressee name. The phrase false consciousness belongs to the Mehring letter and to no text of Marx. Attributing it to Marx is the single most reliable indicator that a source has not checked its own citations.

Check the status of the late documents. The 1877 letter was not sent in the form found and was published after Marx's death; the Zasulich material consists of several drafts plus a short letter that was actually sent, and the drafts differ from one another in emphasis. Citing a draft as a settled position overstates the case, and describing the drafts as unpublished notes understates it. The accurate formulation is that Marx wrote several drafts entertaining the possibility and sent a brief reply, and that the 1882 Russian preface makes the point publicly over both his and Engels's signatures. Getting this right is what distinguishes a piece that a specialist will trust from one they will stop reading, and the check takes minutes rather than hours.

## Who developed the theory after Marx

The theory's post-Marx history is largely a history of people trying to fix a problem the founding statement left open, and knowing the sequence lets a reader place almost any modern argument.

Karl Kautsky, the dominant theorist of the Second International from the 1880s to the First World War, produced the evolutionary reading: capitalism's development would generate its own supersession with something close to natural regularity, and the movement's task was organisation and patience. This is the version the 1890s Engels letters were written against, and it is also the version that most later criticism of Marxism as fatalistic actually targets. Georgi Plekhanov, working in the Russian movement, supplied the philosophical framing and the phrase dialectical materialism, and his essay on the role of the individual in history is the classical attempt to reconcile structural determination with individual agency.

Antonio Gramsci, writing in prison in the late 1920s and 1930s, attacked the evolutionary reading directly. His notebooks include a sustained polemic against a popular textbook exposition of the theory, on the grounds that it converted a method into a mechanical sociology, and his concept of hegemony was developed to explain why the transformation the theory expected had not occurred in Western Europe. The answer he gave, that the ruling order is secured through consent organised in civil society as much as through coercion, gave the superstructure a weight and an autonomy that the classical statement did not.

Georg Lukacs, in essays collected in 1923, made a different move, arguing that the distinctive thing about the theory is not any substantive claim but the standpoint of totality, and that the working class is the subject that can grasp society as a whole because of its position within it. This is the founding text of Western Marxism and the beginning of the tradition's turn towards philosophy and consciousness.

Louis Althusser, from the early 1960s, attempted the opposite correction, purging the humanist and Hegelian vocabulary and reconstructing the theory as an analysis of structures, with the concepts of overdetermination, relative autonomy, and determination in the last instance. E. P. Thompson's reply, a book-length polemic published in 1978, is the sharpest attack the structural version received and it came from a Marxist historian defending the explanatory role of human agency and experience.

G. A. Cohen's reconstruction, also of 1978, opened the analytical current, and Robert Brenner's intervention of 1976 opened the political Marxist one. That these two positions, the strongest defence of the primacy of the productive forces and the strongest defence of the primacy of class relations, appeared within two years of each other and were both produced by scholars committed to the tradition is the clearest possible evidence that the theory contains a genuine internal fault line rather than a settled doctrine with external critics.

## Does the theory make history inevitable?

The question is the one general readers arrive with most often and it has a more precise answer than either the confident yes or the defensive no.

Nothing in the 1859 preface asserts that a given transformation will succeed. The fetters clause says an epoch of social revolution begins; the restraint clause says new relations do not appear before their material conditions have matured; and neither sentence contains a guarantee. What the passage does assert is a constraint on possibility, that the tasks a society sets itself arise only where the conditions for their solution are present or forming, which restricts what can happen without specifying what will.

Two things pull in the other direction and honesty requires naming them. The final clause of the preface describes bourgeois relations as the last antagonistic form, which is a claim about the future that a theory of successive modes does not by itself license. And the rhetoric of the Manifesto is unambiguously confident, describing the fall of the bourgeoisie and the victory of the proletariat as equally inevitable, which is a political document's language rather than the theory's, but it is Marx's language and it is what most readers have encountered.

The tradition split on this point almost immediately. The evolutionary reading treated the outcome as assured. The voluntarist reading treated the outcome as depending entirely on organisation and will. Rosa Luxemburg's formulation of the alternative between socialism and barbarism, offered during the First World War, is the most influential statement of the position that the transformation is possible rather than guaranteed and that failure has its own outcome. That formulation is now the mainstream position within the tradition, and it is worth noting that it is incompatible with the strong reading of the theory rather than a gloss on it.

The practical rule for a reader is that any source describing communism as inevitable in Marx is quoting the Manifesto's rhetoric or the Second International's orthodoxy rather than the theoretical statement, and any source describing the theory as making no claim about the future has dropped the last clause of the 1859 preface. Both simplifications are common and both are avoidable.

## Three further objections and where they land

The mechanism problem is the sharpest objection but it is not the only one, and a reader assessing the theory should know the shape of the others. Each is stated here in the critic's own terms and assessed briefly, with the full engagement belonging to the [dedicated critique article](/2010/04/15/case-against-historical-materialism/).

The first is the unfalsifiability charge, associated above all with Karl Popper, who treated the theory as his central example of a research programme that began as a genuine scientific conjecture and was rescued from refutation by auxiliary hypotheses until it forbade nothing. Popper's specific point is worth stating precisely, because it is usually reported as a blanket dismissal. He credits the original theory with being testable and with having been tested; his objection is to the reinterpretations that followed the failure of its predictions, which preserved the theory at the cost of its empirical content. Where the objection lands is on the practice rather than the doctrine, and the ladder shows why: the strong versions are testable and are in trouble, while the weak versions are safe because they claim little. Where it fails is in treating the auxiliary constructions as uniformly protective. Some of them, particularly the analyses of uneven development and of the mechanisms of consent, generated substantial independent research programmes with findings of their own, which is the mark of a productive auxiliary rather than a face-saving one.

The second is the plurality-of-causes objection, which is the Weberian position and is the most respectable alternative in the professional literature. It concedes that material interests shape action pervasively and denies that any single class of causes can be assigned general primacy. Religion, law, military organisation, bureaucratic form, and the accidents of political succession all shape historical outcomes, and the assertion that one of them is fundamental while the others are derived is, on this view, an unargued preference rather than a finding. The objection is strengthened by the observation that historical materialists disagree among themselves about which material factor is primary, since a school that cannot agree whether the forces or the relations do the explaining has not established that either does. The reply available is that a theory which assigns no priority makes no claims and generates no research, and that the productivity of the materialist starting point in historical practice is the evidence for it. That reply is pragmatic rather than demonstrative, and it should be offered as such.

The third is the political objection, which holds that the theory's account of the future licensed the treatment of moral constraints as ideological and of political opposition as historically obsolete, and that this contributed to the institutional outcomes of the twentieth century. This objection is different in kind from the other two because it concerns consequences rather than truth, and it requires care in both directions. A theory is not refuted by the uses made of it, and the same standard applied elsewhere would indict a great many frameworks. But the objection is not merely about misuse: it identifies specific features of the theory, namely the absence of any account of political accountability and the treatment of moral claims as forms of consciousness to be explained rather than reasons to be weighed, and argues that those features made a particular institutional trajectory more available. Stated that way it is a serious argument and the tradition's own critics, from within, have made versions of it. Answering it requires an argument about the theory rather than a recitation of either the achievements or the crimes of any state, which is why this pillar keeps that record in a separate component and treats it there rather than allowing it to stand in for an argument about the theory itself.

## Historical materialism and human nature

A recurring objection holds that the theory ignores human nature, and the objection misfires on the texts while pointing at something real.

Marx has an account of human nature and it is doing work in the theory rather than sitting beside it. The claim, developed in the writings of the 1840s and never repudiated, is that human beings are distinguished by producing their means of subsistence rather than merely appropriating what is found, that this productive activity is the medium through which capacities develop, and that the historically specific forms of that activity shape the people who perform it. This is not a claim that human beings are infinitely malleable. It is a claim that a fixed set of species capacities is realised differently under different productive arrangements, which is why Marx can say that people change themselves in changing their circumstances without asserting that anything at all is possible.

The theory needs this anthropology more than its later structuralist versions admitted. The development thesis, that productive forces tend to develop over historical time, requires a premise about human beings: that they are rational enough to adopt improvements, situated in conditions of scarcity that give them a reason to, and knowledgeable enough to retain what works. Cohen makes this explicit in his defence, grounding the development thesis in exactly such facts. Strip out the anthropology and the engine of the theory has nothing to run on.

Where the objection has force is on a narrower point. If human dispositions towards acquisition, competition, and status are more fixed than the theory allows, then the range of viable production relations is narrower than it supposes, and the projected supersession of antagonistic forms is correspondingly less plausible. That is a substantive empirical claim, evidence from behavioural and evolutionary research bears on it, and the evidence is genuinely mixed rather than settling the matter in either direction. What should be abandoned is the version of the objection asserting that the theory has no account of human nature at all, since it plainly does and the account is available in print.

## How to assess a historical materialist explanation

The final practical skill this guide can install is a procedure for judging whether a specific piece of materialist explanation is any good, since the theory is applied far more often than it is defended and most of what a reader encounters will be an application.

Ask first whether the explanation names the relations of production it is relying on, specifically and concretely, rather than gesturing at the economy. An account that refers to economic pressures, market forces, or capitalism as such has not identified the relations through which surplus was extracted from anyone, and the analysis has not begun. The test is whether you can say, after reading it, who worked for whom on what terms.

Ask second whether the explanation supplies a mechanism. If the account says an institution exists because it served the interests of a class, the follow-up question is how those interests produced that institution: which agents, acting through which channels, over what period. An account that cannot answer is offering a functional correlation dressed as an explanation, which is exactly the practice the mechanism objection identifies, and it is fair to withhold assent until the process is specified.

Ask third whether the explanation is comparative. A materialist claim that conditions of a certain kind produce relations of a certain kind is testable against cases with different conditions and against cases with similar conditions and different outcomes. An account resting on a single case cannot distinguish its own explanation from a dozen others that would fit the same evidence. This is the discipline the transition debates imposed on the tradition, and it is why those debates improved the historiography whatever their verdict.

Ask fourth whether the explanation states what would count against it. A materialist account of a transition should be able to say which finding would embarrass it, and the answer should be something an archive might contain rather than something no evidence could establish. Where the author has not supplied this, the reader can usually construct it, and the exercise is the fastest way to tell a serious application from a decorative one.

## Does the theory claim that history improves?

The 1859 preface describes its four epochs as marking progress in the economic formation of society, and that single word has carried a great deal of weight it may not be able to bear.

Progress in the passage plainly means development of productive capacity rather than moral improvement, and the distinction is one Marx observes elsewhere with some force. The chapters on the working day and on machinery in the first volume of Capital describe the extension of capitalist relations as accompanied by a deterioration in the conditions of the people subjected to them, and the material on the expropriation of the agricultural population is written as an account of dispossession rather than of advance. Marx's writing on colonial rule contains passages describing the destruction of existing social forms as brutal and as a precondition of development in the same breath, which is the same structure. A theory that treats productive development as the axis of historical progress while describing the human cost of that development without euphemism is not making a claim that things get better; it is making a claim about what changes and in which direction.

The difficulty is that the two senses are not fully separable in the argument. The fetters mechanism explains transformation by reference to the development of productive capacity, which means the theory has to treat that development as the thing that persists and accumulates across epochs, and once a single dimension is identified as cumulative it becomes very difficult to prevent it from functioning as a standard of evaluation. The tradition has not always tried to prevent it. The Second International reading treated development as unambiguously progressive and treated resistance to it as reactionary, a stance with consequences for how the movement regarded peasant societies and colonial ones. Walter Benjamin's theses on the concept of history, written in 1940, are the sharpest internal attack on exactly this, rejecting the identification of historical development with progress and the treatment of the defeated as historically superseded.

For a reader assessing the theory, the useful formulation is that historical materialism entails a directional claim about productive capacity and does not entail a directional claim about human welfare, freedom, or moral development, and that a great deal of the tradition has slid between the two. Sources that describe the theory as a doctrine of inevitable human progress are describing the Second International rather than the 1859 preface. Sources that deny any directional claim have dropped the development thesis, which is what the fetters mechanism runs on.

## What is settled and what remains disputed

Several things about this topic are not in serious dispute among people who have read the primary material, and stating them plainly is useful because public argument suggests otherwise.

It is settled that neither Marx nor Engels used the phrase historical materialism in the founding texts; that the 1859 preface names four epochs rather than five stages; that the compulsory five-stage sequence is a later codification with a specific and datable origin; that Marx explicitly rejected the universalising reading in 1877 and entertained a non-capitalist route for Russia in 1881; that dialectical materialism is a separate doctrine with a different lineage and is not Marx's term; that the fetters clause says an epoch of revolution begins rather than concludes; that the restraint clause imposes a serious constraint on voluntarist politics; that Engels's 1890s letters are the authorised statement of the theory's scope; and that the phrase false consciousness is Engels's, once, in correspondence. Each of these is contradicted routinely in general-audience writing, and a reader who holds all ten can identify an unreliable source within a paragraph.

It remains disputed whether the productive forces or the class relations carry explanatory primacy; whether the functional form of the theory's explanations can be legitimate without a specified mechanism; whether Engels's letters restate or revise the founding position; whether the early and mature statements represent continuity or a break; whether the theory retains any claim about the direction of history once the strong version is abandoned; whether the transition from feudal to capitalist relations is best explained by internal class conflict or by commercial expansion; and whether the middle versions of the theory are distinguishable from a general historical materialism that most non-Marxist historians would accept. These disputes are conducted largely inside the tradition, in its own literature, by people who disagree with each other far more sharply than they disagree with any external critic.

One structural observation about that list is worth carrying away. The settled items concern what the theory does not contain and what its founders did not write; the disputed items concern how societies actually work. That asymmetry is the signature of a subject whose popular exposition ran far ahead of its textual scholarship for the better part of a century, so that a reader's first task is subtraction rather than addition. The five stages, the compulsory ladder, the phrase Marx never used, the retreat Engels never made: clearing these away is not preliminary to the argument, it is most of what separates a reader who can conduct the argument from one who cannot.

A reader who has reached this point can state the theory in six versions, name the rung any argument is standing on, cite the clause of the 1859 preface that supports it, name what it forbids, and say what evidence would count against it. Those five moves are what this guide was built to install. The natural next step is the [treatment of base and superstructure](/2010/02/01/base-and-superstructure-explained/), which takes the metaphor introduced in the second clause above and works out the boundary problem that makes the definition genuinely hard, followed by the [guide to the mode of production](/2010/02/15/mode-of-production-explained/), which supplies the unit of analysis this theory operates on.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is historical materialism in simple terms?

It is the claim that the way a society organises the production of its material life is the fundamental thing to understand about it, and that large-scale historical change is driven by the tension between a society's productive capacities and the social relations organising their use. Put concretely, it says that if you want to explain why a society has the laws, politics, and prevailing beliefs it has, you should begin with who owns the productive resources, who works for whom, and how the surplus is extracted, rather than beginning with ideas or with the intentions of rulers. The theory comes in versions of very different strength, from the claim that technical development determines everything else to the much weaker claim that material conditions limit what is possible, and most disagreement about it is really disagreement about which version is being discussed.

### Q: Where did Marx first set out historical materialism?

The first extended statement is in The German Ideology, written with Engels between 1845 and 1846 and unpublished during their lifetimes, which argues that consciousness cannot be an independent starting point and that forms of ownership succeed one another as the division of labour develops. The canonical compressed statement, and the one every subsequent argument addresses, is the preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy of 1859, which sets out the correspondence of relations to forces, the base and superstructure formulation, the fetters mechanism, and the restraint on premature transformation in a few pages. Neither text uses the phrase historical materialism, which is a later coinage, and the manuscript status of The German Ideology means a careful writer specifies which bundle they are citing.

### Q: How does historical materialism explain social change?

Through a mechanism with three stages. Productive capacity develops within a given set of production relations. At some point those relations, which had been the framework enabling development, become obstacles to it, at which point the 1859 preface says an epoch of social revolution begins. The conflict is then fought out in legal, political, religious, and philosophical forms, and it is resolved through the establishment of relations better suited to the developed forces. The sequence sounds mechanical stated that way, and the texts resist the mechanical reading at each step. The development of productive capacity is not automatic and stalls for long periods in the historical record. The point at which relations become fetters is not marked by any observable threshold, which is why the tradition has never agreed on when a given set of relations became obstructive. And the fighting out of the conflict in ideological forms is where the participants live, so an account that reports only the underlying contradiction has described none of the history. What the theory does not supply, and what its critics press hardest on, is a specification of the process connecting the obstruction to its removal. The versions that give class struggle the leading role fill that gap with identifiable agents pursuing identifiable interests; the versions that give the productive forces primacy leave it more open.

### Q: Did Marx believe history moves through five stages?

No. The five-stage sequence of primitive communism, slavery, feudalism, capitalism, and socialism is not in Marx. The 1859 preface names four epochs, the Asiatic, ancient, feudal, and modern bourgeois modes, in a single sentence, and describes them as marking progress rather than as a compulsory ladder every society must climb. The rigid five-stage schema was codified later, most influentially in a Soviet statement of 1938, following theoretical debates in the early 1930s that removed the Asiatic mode from the sequence. Marx himself repudiated the universalising reading directly in an 1877 letter, refusing to have his account of Western European development turned into a general theory of the path every people must tread, and his 1881 drafts to Vera Zasulich entertain a Russian route that skips capitalist development.

### Q: Is historical materialism the same as dialectical materialism?

No, and Marx used neither phrase. Dialectical materialism is a general philosophy of nature and knowledge, holding that matter is primary and that development everywhere proceeds through contradiction according to certain laws. Its lineage runs through Engels's writing on nature, through Georgi Plekhanov, who coined the name, and into the Soviet codification where it became compulsory doctrine. Historical materialism is narrower: a theory about how to explain social structures and social change. One can hold the second and reject the first, and much of twentieth-century Western Marxism did exactly that, treating the extension of dialectics to nature as a category error. Sources that use the two terms interchangeably, or that attribute dialectical materialism to Marx, have not checked their lineage.

### Q: What evidence supports historical materialism?

The most robust support is comparative. Class stratification, surplus extraction, and state formation appear repeatedly across societies with no contact between them once agricultural surplus reaches comparable levels, which is what a materialist expectation predicts and what an explanation through ideas or through the accidents of rulership does not. Research on the selection of production techniques, showing that methods are adopted partly for their effects on control over the labour process rather than purely for output, supports the theory's claim that technical arrangements are shaped by social relations. Against this stands the divergence of social relations across regions at similar technical levels, which the transition debates documented in detail, and the failure of transformation to occur where development had gone furthest. The evidence supports the middle and weaker versions considerably better than the strong one.

### Q: Who developed historical materialism after Marx?

Karl Kautsky produced the evolutionary reading that dominated the Second International, in which capitalism's supersession would follow from its development with near-natural regularity. Georgi Plekhanov supplied the philosophical framing and the name dialectical materialism. Antonio Gramsci, writing in prison in the late 1920s and 1930s, attacked the mechanical reading and developed hegemony to explain why transformation had not occurred in Western Europe. Georg Lukacs, in essays of 1923, recast the theory around the standpoint of totality. Louis Althusser, from the 1960s, reconstructed it as a theory of structures with relative autonomy and determination in the last instance, and E. P. Thompson attacked that reconstruction on behalf of human agency. G. A. Cohen and Robert Brenner, both writing in the late 1970s, produced the strongest modern statements of the forces-primacy and relations-primacy positions respectively.

### Q: Does historical materialism deny the role of individuals in history?

No. The Eighteenth Brumaire, Marx's most sustained piece of historical analysis, is a study of how one individual exploited a specific political conjuncture, and it opens with the proposition that people make their own history but not in circumstances of their own choosing, which is the theory's own account of the relation between agency and constraint. Engels, in his 1890 letter to Joseph Bloch, describes history as a resultant of innumerable intersecting individual wills, each of which contributes to an outcome nobody intended. What the theory denies is that individuals, ideas, or accidents function as ultimate explanations of durable social structures. A historian who gives religious conviction a causal role is not violating the theory; one who explains a durable institutional order entirely through conviction, with no account of how it is materially reproduced, is.

### Q: Is historical materialism still used by historians?

Yes, though frequently without the label. The methodological commitments that a period should be approached through how its people made their living, that property relations structure political conflict, and that the self-understanding of historical actors is evidence about the period rather than an explanation of it, are now standard practice in social and economic history. The British Marxist historians of the mid-twentieth century, working on the English revolution, the eighteenth-century crowd, the formation of the working class, and medieval agrarian society, are the main transmission route, and history from below is a mainstream commitment held by historians with no interest in the wider theory. What has largely disappeared is the philosophy of history: very few working scholars now defend the claim that the human past has a direction fixed by productive development.

### Q: What role does technology play in historical materialism?

Less than the popular version suggests, and the tradition is split on the question. The strong reading treats the development of technique as the engine of the whole theory, and it can point to the correspondence clause of the 1859 preface and to the hand-mill line in The Poverty of Philosophy. The countervailing evidence is substantial and comes from Marx himself. The productive forces in his usage include labour power, skills, and cooperative capacities, so that a reorganisation of work counts as their development even where no new machine appears. More decisively, the analysis of machinery in the first volume of Capital treats technical change as adopted in order to raise relative surplus value and to break the resistance of skilled workers, which makes technique an effect of the class relation rather than its cause.

### Q: What does the fetters clause actually say?

It says that at a certain stage the material productive forces come into conflict with the existing relations of production, or, as Marx puts it in the same sentence, with the property relations that express those relations legally, and that these relations turn from forms of development into fetters, at which point an epoch of social revolution begins. Three features are routinely dropped in summaries. The conflict is between forces and relations rather than directly between classes, which leaves the connection to class struggle as a genuine problem inside the theory. The parenthesis about legal expression concedes that the base is ordinarily described in the legal vocabulary that the base is supposed to explain, which generates the circularity problem. And the clause says an epoch begins, not that it concludes successfully.

### Q: Did Engels change the theory after Marx died?

He clarified its scope in four letters of the 1890s, and whether that constitutes a change is the disputed question. Writing to Joseph Bloch in 1890, to Conrad Schmidt in the same year, to Franz Mehring in 1893, and to Heinz Starkenburg in 1894, Engels insists that neither he nor Marx ever claimed the economic element was the only determining one, describes superstructural forms as exerting real influence and often preponderating in determining the form of struggles, and warns against using the conception as a formula for constructing history rather than as a guide to studying it. One reading treats this as a retreat from an earlier rigidity. The better-supported reading treats it as a restatement of what the 1859 preface already contained, since that text locates the conflict as being fought out in ideological forms.

### Q: Is there a version of the theory that most historians would accept?

Yes, and this is both the theory's quiet success and its awkward position. The proposition that material conditions set limits on what social arrangements are possible and exert pressures within those limits, without determining which possibility is realised, would be accepted by a very large proportion of working historians, most of whom would not describe themselves as Marxists. So would the methodological commitment to begin with the organisation of production. The difficulty is that a claim nearly everyone accepts is not doing much theoretical work, and the elasticity objection lands on it with full force. The interesting versions of historical materialism are the ones with defenders and opponents, which are the stronger ones, and those are the ones facing serious difficulties.

### Q: Which version of the theory should a student defend in an essay?

Whichever one the question targets, stated explicitly, which is the move that separates a strong answer from a competent one. If the question asks whether historical materialism is deterministic, the productive answer distinguishes the primacy version from the constraint version and shows that the charge of determinism succeeds against the first and fails against the second. If the question asks whether the theory is falsifiable, the same distinction does the same work in reverse, since the strong version is testable and in difficulty while the weak version is safe and thin. Defending a single version throughout, without acknowledging that the tradition contains others, produces an answer that a well-read marker can dismantle with one counter-example.

### Q: What should a teacher do about the five-stage diagram in the textbook?

Use it as the object of the lesson rather than as its content. Present the diagram, then present the four epochs of the 1859 preface, then ask the class to account for the discrepancy. The gap opens the whole question of how a theory becomes a doctrine and who performs the conversion, which is a more valuable lesson than the sequence itself. The 1877 letter, in which Marx refuses to have his account of Western Europe turned into a universal path, is the extract that settles it, and handing a class the author's own repudiation of what they were taught is considerably more persuasive than teacherly correction. It also models the checking habit the course should be installing.

### Q: How should a researcher cite the canonical preface?

By the work and the passage rather than by a page number in whatever edition happens to be at hand, since the preface appears in numerous collections with different pagination, and by specifying which translation where the wording of the correspondence clause or the consciousness inversion is doing argumentative work. The greater risk is not citation format but contamination: summarising the Stalin-era five-stage gloss while citing the preface is a common error in otherwise careful writing, and a reader who knows the primary text will spot it. Where the argument depends on the fetters clause or the restraint clause, quote or paraphrase closely rather than relying on a secondary summary, since those are precisely the clauses that summaries drop.

### Q: Does historical materialism have anything to say about non-European societies?

It has a difficult history here and a live research programme. The Asiatic mode of production was Marx's category for agrarian formations he took to be organised around a state apparatus rather than around private landed property, and it was removed from Soviet orthodoxy after debates in the early 1930s, partly because its application uncomfortably close to home was awkward. Marx's own writings on colonial India contain passages that read as a developmental teleology the tradition later had to shed. Against this, his 1877 letter and the 1881 Zasulich drafts explicitly deny that his European account is a universal path, and his late ethnological notebooks suggest a comparative research programme rather than a schematic one. The theory's most productive applications outside Europe have come from confronting the misfit rather than assuming it away.

### Q: What is the difference between the productive forces and the relations of production?

The productive forces are the means by which a society produces: instruments, raw materials, technical knowledge, and the labour power and cooperative capacities of the producers themselves. The relations of production are the social arrangements governing effective control over those forces and over the labour that sets them in motion, which in class societies means the arrangements determining who performs surplus labour and who lives on it. The distinction is the theory's basic analytical move, and two errors follow from blurring it. Treating the forces as machinery alone produces the technological reading that Marx's own analysis of cooperation contradicts, since the organisation of labour is itself a productive force. Treating the relations as legal ownership produces the circularity problem, since law is superstructural and the base is meant to explain it, which is why Cohen redescribes them as effective powers rather than rights.
