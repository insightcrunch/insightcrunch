## Two Readers at the Tomb: The Quarrel This Article Settles

Every serious argument about Romeo and Juliet is, underneath its local subject, an argument between two camps. Popular accounts of the play mix the two approaches unawares, praising the design in one sentence and psychoanalyzing the lovers in the next, and the mixture is harmless in conversation; in criticism it produces verdicts that cannot be checked, because the evidence keeps changing kind. Restoring the divide is not pedantry. It is the precondition of adjudication: only when each camp's claim is stated in its own terms can a scene be asked which claim it supports. One camp reads the play through character: the lovers are persons, their catastrophe is the sum of their choices, temperaments and growth, and the critic's task is to know them as one knows people, from their speeches, their hesitations and their changes. The other camp reads the play through structure: form, imagery, genre and design carry the meaning, the catastrophe is announced in the Prologue's fourteenth line before any person has acted, and the critic's task is to map the pattern that the persons serve. The first camp descends from Coleridge and Anna Jameson through A. C. Bradley. The second descends from Caroline Spurgeon's imagery census through G. Wilson Knight, Harry Levin, Susan Snyder and Nicholas Brooke. Between them they have produced nearly every verdict the play has ever received, and they rarely agree about what kind of evidence a speech is.

The verdict argued here is that the quarrel is real as a division of labour and false as a rivalry of explanations. The play is constructed so that character is the mechanism by which structure executes itself. Its catastrophe needs both the stopped letter and Romeo's haste, both the feud's machinery and Juliet's resolve; remove either term and the tomb stays empty. The structure camp owns the play's causation: why the end arrives, and why it could not arrive otherwise once the design is set. The character camp owns the play's significance: why the end matters, why audiences grieve for these two dead children rather than merely admire the engineering. Criticism goes wrong when either camp claims the other's territory, when pattern-reading dissolves the lovers into functions or when person-reading forgets that the Prologue has already buried them. The strongest critics in the play's history, Levin and Brooke among them, are precisely those who treat character as structure's instrument, and the modern editors, who must print a text both camps can use, have quietly ratified that settlement.

What follows surveys the two camps whole: their founders, their evidence, their characteristic blindnesses, and four test cases where the quarrel can be adjudicated scene by scene rather than traded as slogans. The lovers' first meeting is the emblem of the whole dispute, because it is a sonnet. "If I profane with my unworthiest hand / This holy shrine, the gentle sin is this" (1.5.92-93): two strangers build a perfect formal pattern together, fourteen lines shared line by line, and fall in love inside it. Is that a form producing a feeling, or two persons discovering each other through the only language their world allows? The camps divide on the answer. This article's answer is that the question is the play in miniature, and that the play refuses to let either half of it go.

The divide also has an institutional geography, which explains its persistence. The character camp is the criticism of the theatre and the general reader: it speaks the language of rehearsal, where an actor must decide who Juliet is before deciding how her lines run, and of the classroom, where a first encounter with the play is an encounter with two young people whose fate feels unjust. The structure camp is the criticism of the study: it speaks the language of the edition, the concordance and the seminar, where the play is an object that can be taken apart and its workings demonstrated to a sceptic. Each camp suspects the other of a professional deformation. To the structure reader, character criticism looks like biography written about people who never lived; to the character reader, structural analysis looks like an autopsy performed on a living voice. Both suspicions contain enough truth to keep the quarrel funded, and neither contains enough to settle it. What settles a quarrel of this kind is not polemic but evidence handled scene by scene, which is the procedure the test cases below follow: state each camp's claim at full strength, then ask which claim the passage in question can actually carry.

Read down either column and a complete criticism emerges, coherent, evidenced and partial. Read across any row and the quarrel stands in its working form. The rows on the letter and the statues are the rows where the camps' scores differ most sharply, and the scene-by-scene tests later in this article return to them. First, each camp deserves its full hearing, beginning with the older one, the camp of persons, which owned the play for a century before the pattern-readers arrived with their counts and diagrams.

Two rows deserve a word before the hearings begin, because they mark the quarrel's outer limits. The first-question row is the only one that is purely methodological: it records not a disagreement about the play but a disagreement about what criticism is for, and every other row is downstream of it. A reader who has decided that the play is fundamentally a construction will find the lovers' psychology thin wherever it resists the pattern, and a reader who has decided that the play is fundamentally a pair of lives will find the pattern decorative wherever it resists the psychology. The blindness row is the table's confession that neither column is a complete criticism: each camp's characteristic failure is the other's founding observation. The structure camp saw first that the character camp could not see the frame; the character camp saw first that the structure camp could not hear a voice. A comparison that flattered either side would be useless as an instrument, and the rows are drawn to be used, tested against scenes and corrected where a scene refuses them.

## The Character Camp: Persons Before Patterns

The character camp begins with Coleridge, whose lectures gave the play its first criticism of the modern kind. Against the fault-finding of the previous century, which had marked the tragedy down for irregularity, Coleridge proposed organic unity: homogeneity, proportionateness and totality of interest, a whole in which every part answers to the others. That sounds like a structural principle, and the structure camp has often tried to claim him, but the unity Coleridge cared about was the unity of a living mind. His famous sentence on the play calls precipitancy "the character of the play", and the word character carries its double sense openly: precipitancy is the temperament of Romeo, of Juliet, of the feud's young men, and simultaneously the tempo of the work that contains them. For the character camp, that doubleness is not a confusion but the founding insight. A play's tempo is what its persons are like, felt from inside.

Coleridge's Juliet shows the method at work. He insisted that Juliet must not be shown already in love when the play opens: the growth of her passion, from the dutiful daughter who will "look to like, if looking liking move" (1.3.98) to the woman who stakes her life on a potion, is the drama, and a production or a reading that flattens the growth has lost the character. His Nurse, by contrast, he called a borrowing from observation rather than a creation of imagination, a person transcribed from life whose garrulous morality needs no pattern to explain it. Both judgments treat speeches as evidence of minds. When Juliet argues with the lark in the dawn scene, the character critic hears a young wife inventing reasons to keep her husband beside her; the verse is what a person in her situation would say, raised to poetry. The camp's evidence is the speech, its unit is the person, and its test is recognition: does this reading make the figure on the stage more knowable?

Coleridge's Romeo shows the same method working at the level of a single faculty. His distinction between fancy and imagination, the mechanical assemblage of images against the shaping spirit that fuses them, becomes in his hands a diagnosis of the hero: Romeo's love for Rosaline is fancy, a wardrobe of borrowed Petrarchan counters worn without conviction, while his love for Juliet is imagination, the same counters melted into a single living perception. The character camp treasured this reading because it made a formal observation do psychological work. The verse really does change at the feast; every reader can hear the stale oxymorons of the first act give way to the shared sonnet's living exchange. Coleridge's claim is that the change is a change in a mind, the moment a boy who was performing love begins to feel it. The structure camp would later claim the same evidence for the opposite moral, that the change is a change in the play's formal register, a gear engaging. The evidence is identical; the camps are the two names for its explanation. Hazlitt, lecturing in the same decades, read the play as the poetry of the passions themselves, a work whose power lies in the intensity with which feeling is rendered rather than in the machinery that conveys it, and his Romeo and Juliet are studies in what passion does to perception. Between them, the Romantic founders fixed the character camp's habits for a century: begin from the speech, infer the soul, judge the play by the truth of the inference.

### What does the character camp do with the Prologue's announced ending?

The character camp treats the announced ending as a framing convention inherited from the sources, a price of admission the audience pays once and sets aside, so that attention can settle where the camp believes the action lives: inside the lovers' choices, speeches and growth, where the end, however certain, still feels open while it is being watched and chosen.

Anna Jameson gave the camp its manifesto and its moral seriousness. Her Characteristics of Women, published in 1832 and later known as Shakespeare's Heroines, sorted the heroines into classes and placed Juliet among the characters of passion and imagination. The essay on Juliet remains the character camp's classic performance. Jameson calls Juliet "love itself", not a girl in love but the passion entire, embodied; she defends Juliet's frankness in the orchard against the charge of forwardness, answers the critics who found the wedding-night invocation indelicate, and stakes her reading on the potion soliloquy of Act 4, where the girl who had been all feeling reasons her way through terror, imagining the vault's horrors one by one and drinking anyway. For Jameson, that soliloquy is the crisis of a character: the point where passion has become courage, and courage has become a solitary act no other person in the play could perform. The essay is advocacy as much as analysis. Juliet stood accused before Victorian propriety of haste, disobedience and immodesty, and Jameson appears as her counsel, examining the evidence speech by speech and winning the acquittal.

It is worth noticing that Jameson's book is built on a classification, heroines sorted into kinds, intellect here, passion and imagination there, affection in a third division. The character camp's classic text is thus a taxonomy, a pattern imposed on persons before any person is discussed, and the structure camp is entitled to its smile: even the portraitists begin by arranging. The difference lies in what the arrangement is for. Jameson's classes are a gallery's hanging plan, designed to bring each portrait into its best light; the persons remain the end, the classes their frames. Spurgeon's classes, a century later, are a census, designed to bring the pattern into view; the pattern is the end, the persons its instances. Between the gallery and the census stands the whole distance the quarrel travels, and the fact that both founders reach for classification suggests the distance is one of emphasis more often than of kind. The camps were never as pure as their polemics, which is the historical form of this article's verdict.

The character camp also owned the stage, and the stage returned the favour. Through the nineteenth century the play lived in performance as a vehicle for its lovers, above all for its Juliet, and the acting tradition embodied the camp's premises nightly: that the tragedy's meaning is carried by the credibility of two persons, that an audience's tears are criticism's data, and that a reading which cannot be played is a reading refuted. The great Juliets of the century were, in effect, character critics with the house as their jury, and their promptbooks record interpretations, choices about the potion scene's terror or the balcony's frankness, that academic criticism would later rediscover as arguments. This alliance with performance gave the character camp a kind of evidence the structure camp could not match and could not dismiss: the accumulated testimony of audiences, across centuries and languages, that these figures are received as persons. A theory of the play that treats that testimony as a mass hallucination has some explaining to do, and the structure camp's explanations, that the pattern manufactures the impression of personhood, concede the impression's power even while disputing its source.

The camp's Romeo deserves a paragraph of his own, because he is its hardest case and its favourite exhibit. Is he constant? The character tradition divided against itself on the question. One line of reading, descending from the Romantics, takes the Rosaline episode as the record of a boy in love with love's vocabulary, whose transfer to Juliet is the transfer from recitation to feeling: the same intensity, educated. A sterner line, which the Victorian moralists favoured, counts the transfer against him, a fickleness the tragedy then punishes. Edward Dowden, whose critical study of Shakespeare's mind and art psychologized the author a generation before the method had a name, read the play as a stage in the poet's own development, the lovers' lyricism as the young Shakespeare's self-portrait in passion; the move is the character camp's characteristic temptation in its biographical form, the persons behind the persons, and it drew the same protest Knights would later formalize. What unites the camp's Romeos is the assumption that the question is a question about a someone: that Romeo has a disposition, stable or unstable, which the action reveals. The structure camp's counter-assumption, that "Romeo" names a position successively occupied by the Petrarchan lover, the sonnet partner, the bridegroom, the duellist and the suicide, explains the same scenes as a sequence of functions. The scenes themselves, as the test cases will show, keep voting for both descriptions at once.

The camp that Coleridge and Jameson founded reads the play as a double portrait. Its Romeo is a youth whose imagination outruns his experience, constant only in the intensity of each successive passion; its Juliet is the play's centre of growth, the one figure who changes in kind rather than in circumstance. Its criticism attends to motive, to development, to the moral weight of decisions: Romeo's intervention in the duel, Juliet's deception of her parents, the Friar's meddling, the Nurse's betrayal of counsel in Act 3. Its characteristic sentence is "she has become", and its characteristic failure is the mirror image of its strength. A camp that reads speeches as psychology can forget that the speeches were arranged: that the Prologue told the audience the ending before the lovers met, that the catastrophe's machinery includes a letter no character writes badly and a friar's timetable no motive explains. The character camp's Verona is a place where things happen because people are as they are. It took Bradley, the camp's greatest systematic mind, to state that premise as a criterion, and the criterion's failure on this play is the divide's most instructive event.

## Bradley's Criterion: The Character Camp States Its Terms

A. C. Bradley's Shakespearean Tragedy, published in 1904, built the character camp into a system, and Romeo and Juliet is the play the system could not house. Bradley's four tragedies are Hamlet, Othello, King Lear and Macbeth. The Veronese lovers are left outside, and the Introduction says why, in terms that define the camp: in a Shakespearean tragedy the catastrophe must issue from the actions of the persons, and the hero must contribute to his own disaster. Accident may assist, but it may not decide. Measured by that standard, this play fails at its climax. Romeo dies because a letter miscarried. Friar John is quarantined by plague officers, "the searchers of the town, / Suspecting that we both were in a house / Where the infectious pestilence did reign, / Sealed up the doors" (5.2.8-11), and no quality in Romeo, no error or virtue, has anything to do with the sealing of that door. The conflict, moreover, is outward: two houses at feud, a circumstance inherited rather than a struggle within a soul. Bradley grants the play its beauties and Romeo his precipitancy, an error at the mild end of his spectrum, but the disaster's deciding cause is chance, and chance is what his definition excludes.

The criterion's weakness is visible inside Bradley's own four tragedies, and fairness requires noting it. Bradley himself concedes that accident operates in the chosen plays: a handkerchief dropped at the wrong moment, a pirate ship, a storm. His defence is proportion and placement: in the four, accident assists a catastrophe already issuing from character, while in Verona accident decides one that character had, in a sense, already survived, since the marriage was made and the feud's heirs were reconciled in secret before the letter failed. The distinction is real but thinner than the exclusion it carries. It amounts to a judgment that in this play the accidental share crosses some threshold, and thresholds of that kind are exactly where definitions stop and tastes begin. The structure camp's whole later enterprise can be described as an attempt to move the letter back under the threshold, by showing that the play's world is one in which messages fail by design: a city of sealed houses, intercepted visits, unspoken names and overheard soliloquies, where the stopped letter is the largest instance of the play's universal condition rather than an intruder into it.

The exclusion matters beyond its verdict because it located the play's problem exactly. For the century that followed, the criticism of this play is a series of answers to Bradley, whether the answerers name him or not: the rehabilitation of the play's standing was achieved by critics who showed that the accident was prepared, the design deliberate, the catastrophe earned by structure if not by character. The hamartia question that Bradley modernized, whether a tragedy of this shape needs a flaw in its hero at all, has its own long ledger, traced in [the debate over tragic flaw and hamartia in the play](/2027/06/04/tragic-flaw-hamartia-debate/). What Bradley exposed, perhaps against his intention, is that the character camp's criterion is a definition disguised as a discovery. Define tragedy as character-caused catastrophe and this play is deficient by construction; define it otherwise and the same facts become evidence of a different tragic kind. His system did not find the play wanting. It found the play illegible, which is a different and more interesting result.

Bradley's verdict had heirs who hardened it. H. B. Charlton, lecturing in the late nineteen-thirties and publishing his Shakespeare studies across the following decade, kept the play in the demoted place Bradley had prepared for it: a lyrical experiment, glorious in its poetry, whose catastrophe by accident disqualified it from the company of the mature tragedies. The demotion is worth dwelling on because it shows what the character criterion did to the play's standing as a whole. A work can survive being called flawed; it struggles to survive being reclassified, moved from the shelf of tragedies to the shelf of beautiful failures, where its beauties are praised in the tone used for juvenilia. The play's twentieth-century rehabilitation, traced in this series' article on that recovery, was therefore not a change of taste but a change of criterion. The formalists did not discover that the play was better than Bradley thought. They disputed the court in which he had tried it, arguing that the accident he condemned was the design's instrument and that a tragedy of pattern is not a failed tragedy of character but a different kind. The quarrel over the play's rank is the camps' quarrel wearing institutional dress, and it was won, insofar as it has been won, by changing the question from "did the hero cause his end?" to "was the end made?" Those are the structure camp's terms, and the character camp has been answering on them ever since.

### What does Bradley's criterion actually measure?

Bradley's criterion measures the proportion of the catastrophe a hero's actions must answer for: where the deciding cause can be traced to a choice, an error or a trait, the play passes as tragedy in his sense; where the deciding cause arrives by accident, as the stopped letter does, the play falls outside the definition.

It should be recorded that the exclusion coexisted with admiration, since the caricature of Bradley as the play's enemy has distorted the quarrel's history. He praised the poetry with a warmth his system could not use, and in later impressions let the praise stand beside the verdict unchanged. The combination is not hypocrisy but the criterion's signature: a critic can find a work's beauties overwhelming and its construction inadmissible in the same hour, if his definition of the kind is doing the judging. The rehabilitation article in this series asks whether Bradley disliked the play he left out, and the answer the record supports is that he loved its music and distrusted its engineering, which is precisely the split the two camps would spend a century formalizing. Bradley is therefore not the character camp's villain but its most honest casualty: the reader whose loves and whose laws pointed in opposite directions, and who obeyed the laws.

The counter-attack came from inside the character camp's own house. In 1933 L. C. Knights published the essay whose title became a slogan, "How Many Children Had Lady Macbeth?", and aimed it at the habit Bradley's method had licensed: treating dramatic persons as real people with biographies offstage, childhoods, marriages and inner lives continuous with ours. Knights proposed that critics study instead the words on the page, the poem as a dramatic poem, since a Shakespeare play is not a novel with the narrative removed but a structure of language whose persons exist only in their speeches. The essay is usually filed as the character camp's obituary, and its effect was indeed to drive psychological portraiture out of respectable criticism for a generation. But notice what Knights actually did: he relocated character inside language. The persons survive, but as verbal constructions, patterns of image and rhythm that readers assemble into the impression of a mind. That relocation is the seed of every later bridge between the camps, and it was planted by the character camp's own executioner.

## The Structure Camp Answers: Image, Pattern, Atmosphere

The structure camp's founding act was a count. Caroline Spurgeon's Shakespeare's Imagery and What It Tells Us, published in 1935, tabulated the images of the whole canon with research assistants and card indexes, and for this play her census records 204 images across 2,989 lines. Her finding for Romeo and Juliet is the most quoted result of the method: the play is saturated with light. The lovers call each other suns, stars, torches and snowy doves among ravens; the imagery of fire and brightness runs against the imagery of the tomb's darkness from the first scene to the last. Spurgeon's own numbers require care, and her article in this series gives them exactly: the class she files as fire and light is not her largest, and the dominance of light is a claim about a leading motive gathered across classes, established by exhibition rather than won by arithmetic. That distinction is the method's honesty and its limit in one. The count proves recurrence, and recurrence is a fact no reader's impression can overturn; what the recurrence means, the count cannot say.

Spurgeon's revolution began in lectures before it became a book, and the lecture-hall origin matters to the quarrel: hers was a public method, demonstrated before audiences with charts, a professor's evidence offered to inspection. It also carried a second, quieter claim that her critics seized on. The book's subtitle promises what the imagery tells us, and part of what Spurgeon thought it told was the biography of the author, his reading, his countryside childhood, his distastes. That documentary ambition, the inference from pattern to person at the author's level, is the point where her method re-enters the character camp's territory from behind, and it is the point Knights's 1933 attack on documentary reading struck: patterns may be facts about plays without being facts about the man's soul. The structure camp kept the census and dropped the biography, which is why Spurgeon's light finding has outlived her claims about Shakespeare himself. The episode is a useful warning against camp purity. The founder of the count was also a portraitist manqué, and the method survived by amputating its founder's favourite inference.

The counting method drew its reckoning soon enough, and the reckoning refines rather than reverses her. Wolfgang Clemen, whose study of the development of Shakespeare's imagery placed this play early in the poet's growth as an image-maker, argued that images live inside dramatic situations and change their charge as they pass from speaker to speaker, so that a census, however accurate, photographs a river. A light image in Romeo's mouth in Act 1 is a convention; the same family of images in Juliet's mouth in Act 3 is a desperation. Frequency cannot register that difference, and Clemen's correction stands: the pattern is real, but its meaning is local, earned scene by scene. The digital afterlife of Spurgeon's method cuts the other way. Searchable texts have made her hand-counting repeatable by anyone, and the light pattern survives every recount, which is more than can be said for many impressionistic claims of her century. The structure camp's mature position is the union of the two: Spurgeon's evidence, Clemen's discipline. Count first, so that the pattern is a fact; then read closely, so that the fact becomes an interpretation.

What the structure camp built on Spurgeon's foundation was a new kind of evidence. An image pattern is public property: any reader can verify it, argue about its distribution, trace its thickening at the crisis. Where the character camp's evidence was the felt life of a speech, answerable finally to the reader's sense of a person, the structure camp's evidence could be checked like a collation. [Caroline Spurgeon and the imagery revolution](/2027/03/05/caroline-spurgeon-imagery-revolution/) is treated whole in its own article in this series; its place in the quarrel is that it changed what a claim about the play had to look like. After Spurgeon, a critic who said the play was about haste or love or fate could be asked: where is the pattern, and how does it run?

### Can counting images prove anything about a person in a play?

Counting can prove recurrence, and recurrence can discipline a claim about a person: if a figure's speeches gather one image family each time she chooses, the pattern is evidence about how the play builds that figure, though the count alone cannot say what the pattern feels like from inside, which remains the character camp's question to answer.

G. Wilson Knight supplied the structure camp with its metaphysics. Knight read a play as a spatial whole, an expanded metaphor apprehended entire rather than a sequence of events in time, and his treatment of this play stands in The Shakespearian Tempest of 1932, within his survey of the tempest and music pattern that runs across the canon: storm against harmony, the feud's noise against the lovers' concord. Knight's Romeo and Juliet is a pattern of oppositions, love and death interpenetrating, the tomb lit like a bridal chamber, Juliet's beauty making the vault "a feasting presence full of light" (5.3.85-86). Character, in Knight's readings, thins into correspondence; Romeo matters less as a youth than as the point where the play's light imagery and death imagery cross. The character camp's protest writes itself: a person is not a crossing point, and an audience does not weep for a correspondence. But Knight saw something the portrait-readers had missed, that the play's meanings are carried by recurrences no single character controls, that the design speaks over the persons' heads to the audience, and that any account of the tragedy which ignores the design has explained the speeches and missed the play.

The camp's quietest evidence is the calendar. The action occupies some five days, Sunday to Thursday, compressed from the nine months of Arthur Brooke's poem into less than a week, and the compression is a structural fact before it is anything else: no human courtship requires this speed, but this design does, since every delay the sources allowed would have let the feud cool, the letter arrive, the plot dissolve. The character camp reads the same calendar psychologically, as the objective correlative of the lovers' precipitancy, a world moving at the speed of their temperament. Here the two readings stand in a relation neither camp likes to acknowledge, reciprocal borrowing: the design's tempo is justified by the persons' haste, and the persons' haste is manufactured by the design's tempo. Coleridge's word precipitancy, the character of the play, turns out to name the hinge itself, the point where the calendar and the temperament are one fact wearing two descriptions. A critic who asks whether the haste belongs to the plot or to the boy has asked the question the play was built to make unanswerable.

Knight's method also attracted the structure camp's sharpest internal prosecution. When R. G. Cox reviewed Knight's later work in Scrutiny in 1947 under the title "Interpreter or Oracle?", the charge was that spatial reading lacks a discipline: correspondences are cheap, any image can be married to any other, and a method that finds the pattern it went looking for has explained nothing. The charge lands on Knight's weaker pages and stands permanently as the structure camp's necessary warning, the oracle problem. A pattern claim must be falsifiable to be evidence; it must survive counting, distribution and the test of the awkward scene that does not fit. Spurgeon's census, for all its limits, is the camp's answer to Cox, since a count can be wrong in a way a vision cannot. The quarrel's later history can be written as the structure camp slowly acquiring the disciplines its founders scorned: Levin's figures are checkable, Snyder's matrix is a genre claim that productions can test, Brooke's modes can be marked in a text. The camp that began with a count and a vision survived by becoming accountable.

## Levin: Formality as the Play's Subject

Harry Levin's "Form and Formality in Romeo and Juliet", published in Shakespeare Quarterly in 1960, is the structure camp's masterpiece on this play and the quarrel's turning point, because Levin's formalism refuses to stay formal. His subject is formality itself: Verona is a city of forms, the feud a ritual of inherited gestures, insults exchanged by rule, "Do you bite your thumb at us, sir?" (1.1.42), duels fought by the book, marriages arranged as treaties, grief performed in set laments. Against this world of empty form Levin sets the lovers, whose bond he calls the one organic relation in the play, and whose love is expressed, paradoxically, through the most artificial forms available: the shared sonnet, the aubade, the formal lament. The lovers do not escape form. They fill it with life, and the play's tragedy is that Verona's dead forms, the feud's etiquette and the marriage market's timetable, close over the one living form and crush it.

Levin's evidence is the verse itself, figure by figure: the ploce, the rhetorical doubling that thickens wherever the lovers speak; the antitheses that Romeo begins as decoration, "O brawling love! O loving hate!" (1.1.176), and that the action keeps converting into fact. His treatment of the laments in Act 4 shows the method at its most delicate. The discovery of Juliet's apparently dead body produces a set lament, the household's grief arranged in formal turns, which a character reading must either excuse as convention or strain to psychologize. Levin calls the passage an operatic quartet and means it as analysis rather than apology: grief in Verona is a public form before it is a private feeling, and the scene's artificiality is the city's truth, mourners performing sorrow by rota while the one person who knows the death is feigned stands inside the performance. When the musicians remain behind, joking over their instruments, form has outlasted the feeling it was hired to express, and Levin's essay closes there because the whole play is in that residue: the forms persist, beautiful and empty, after the lives that filled them are gone.

His scheme sets form against nature throughout the play's population: Mercutio and the Nurse stand on nature's side, improvisers whose speech overflows every container, prose and bawdy and digression, while the older generation and the Prince administer form's side, proclamation, contract and rite. The lovers begin inside form, as sonneteers, and are educated by the action toward a speech that keeps form's discipline while filling it with nature's urgency, which is why their exchanges tighten rather than loosen as the danger grows. Levin borrows Bergson's theory of comedy for the play's lower world, the mechanical encrusted on the living, and the borrowing shows his method's range: a philosopher of laughter conscripted to explain why the musicians and servants, comedy's residue, persist into the tragedy's final acts. His Paris is the terzo incommodo, the intrusive third at the feast of two. His essay ends, characteristically, not with the statues but with Peter and the musicians in Act 4, and the question "Why silver sound?" (4.5), form persisting as professional routine after feeling has left the room. [Harry Levin's 'Form and Formality' (1960)](/2026/12/04/harry-levin-form-and-formality/) receives its full treatment in this series; what matters for the quarrel is Levin's position between the camps. He is a structure critic whose structure is inhabited. The forms he maps are things persons do: insult, woo, marry, mourn. In Levin's hands the character camp's evidence, the speeches, becomes the structure camp's evidence, a system, without ceasing to be the record of minds. That is why his essay, more than any other single piece, made the old divide look optional.

Levin's afterlife confirms the bridge status. Modern editions present the play in his wake: introductions that once apologized for the play's rhetoric now exhibit it, teaching the sonnet, the aubade and the lament as the play's thinking rather than its decoration, and the essay is the conduit through which formality entered the editorial mainstream. There is a lesson in the timing. The essay appeared in 1960, a generation after Knights had driven character-talk out of the journals and a decade before the theory decades would redraw the map again. Levin's formalism was not a retreat from the human; it was a demonstration that the most rigorous attention to verbal structure could return the reader to the lovers with more, not less, feeling for what their speeches cost them. The structure camp's other founders had each paid a price for their rigour, Spurgeon her biography, Knight his discipline. Levin's price was different: by making form so hospitable to feeling, he left later critics unsure which camp to file him in, and the uncertainty is the measure of the bridge.

### How can a shared sonnet be both a form and a feeling?

A shared sonnet is a form because its fourteen lines, its rhymes and its pilgrim conceit are fixed in advance by convention, and a feeling because the two speakers must complete each other's lines in real time, so that the pattern only exists if each mind answers the other exactly, which is the play's definition of their love.

## Snyder and Brooke: Genre Is Structure Too

Susan Snyder widened the structure camp's territory from image and rhetoric to genre. Her essay "Comedy into Tragedy" appeared in 1970, and The Comic Matrix of Shakespeare's Tragedies, published by Princeton in 1979, set this play beside Hamlet, Othello and King Lear as tragedies built on comedy's foundations. Snyder's Verona runs for two acts on the machinery of romantic comedy: a hero in love with an unattainable lady, blocking fathers, a witty friend, a go-between nurse, a friendly friar, a secret marriage that should end the play in festivity. The feud, on her reading, is not even a passion; it is a reflex, hatred as mechanical habit in houses that have forgotten its cause, and comedy is the genre in which such machinery resolves. Then Mercutio dies, the matrix breaks, and the same devices return with their outcomes reversed: the potion that should produce a reunion produces a tomb, the letter that should explain produces the catastrophe, comic providence curdles into tragic accident. [Susan Snyder's comedy-to-tragedy reading](/2026/12/11/susan-snyder-comedy-to-tragedy/) is owned whole by its own article in this series; its force in the quarrel is that it explains the play's most famous oddity, the death of its tone at the midpoint, as a structural event requiring no psychology at all. Mercutio is, in her phrase, the incarnation of comic atmosphere, and when he dies the atmosphere dies with him.

Snyder's treatment of character deserves emphasis, because it is the structure camp's most deliberate demotion of the rival camp's object. If fate, or rather the matrix, drives the ending, then the lovers' characters are not the tragedy's cause but its medium: Romeo's haste and Juliet's daring are the qualities the design needs in its carriers, selected the way a dramatist selects any instrument. Snyder does not deny the traits; she reassigns their explanatory rank. What the character camp calls the cause of the catastrophe she calls its transmission system. The demotion clarifies a great deal that Bradley could not: why the lovers can be faultless in intention and still doomed, why the play's blame keeps sliding off persons onto arrangements whenever a reader tries to assign it. It also exacts a price the test cases will collect. A reading in which character is transmission cannot easily explain why the transmission, at the hinge, hesitates, argues with itself and names its own condition, as Romeo does when he calls his gentleness a softening of valour's steel. Snyder's later work extended the matrix across the tragedies, finding in King Lear a comic foundation driven to darker transformations; the Romeo and Juliet chapter remains the method's clearest case because the play's comic machinery is the most nearly intact.

Nicholas Brooke, whose Shakespeare's Early Tragedies appeared in 1968, differs from Snyder on the design's shape, and their disagreement is the structure camp's internal quarrel at its most useful. Brooke's play is a mixture rather than a sequence: lyric, comic and tragic modes present from the start, the Prologue announcing tragedy while the servants clown, the wedding at the design's centre with the modes changing hands around it. Snyder's play is a transformation, comedy successive, a modulation from one key to another at the duel. The adjudication delivered in this series' Brooke article holds, and this capstone adopts it: Brooke wins on construction, since the tragic mode is inscribed from line one and the comic devices are still functioning, terribly, in Act 5; Snyder wins on the felt hinge, since an audience experiences the duel as a door closing. The two accounts operate at different scales, mixture describing what the sequence is made of, sequence describing how the mixture is lived through in time. Frank Kermode's later survey of the play's language belongs to the same camp at a longer range: in Shakespeare's Language of 2000 the play stands in the early, patterned, rhetorical manner, its set forms the mark of a style not yet turned toward the compressed difficulty of the post-1600 work. Kermode's chronology survives his ladder of value: the patterning is a phase's instrument, not a young poet's failure.

Two exhibits test the Snyder-Brooke settlement, and both camps should be made to look at them. The first is the musicians' scene in Act 4, where Peter banters with the hired band over the corpse-strewn wedding morning. Snyder's reading faces its hardest moment here, since comedy has plainly not died with Mercutio; her answer, that the scene is comedy's ghost, its routines running on without its world, the musicians as professionals whose function has detached from any festivity the play can still imagine, converts the objection into evidence, though at some cost to the sequence's cleanliness. Brooke's mixture handles the scene without strain: the comic mode was never switched off, only outvoted, and its persistence among the servants is the mixture's ordinary weather. The second exhibit is the Chorus, who speaks the Prologue, returns once to usher in the orchard act, and then vanishes from a play that announced itself through him. For Brooke the disappearance is modal evidence, the framing voice absorbed into the action as the design stops needing a herald; for Snyder the Chorus belongs to the tragedy's frame from the start, the one figure who was never inside the comedy at all. Either way, the structure camp agrees on the fact's significance: a play that dismisses its own narrator at the midpoint is making an argument about who gets to explain events, and the explanation is thereafter left to persons who understand less than the audience. The character camp, which rarely discusses the Chorus at all, has no comparable account to set against that one, and the omission is characteristic: frames are the structure camp's home ground.

Rosaline, who never appears, is the same kind of test in miniature. The structure camp needs her: the matrix requires a false start, a rehearsal love whose Petrarchan machinery can be exposed as machinery when the true partner arrives to answer line for line, and her invisibility is the point, since a function does not require a face. Garber's account of the play's substitutions gives her a structural promotion, the first term in the series of replacements the design keeps performing. The character camp needs her too, as the evidence in its oldest case against Romeo: a passion professed in superlatives and dropped in an evening is either a boy's fancy, in Coleridge's forgiving vocabulary, or a warning the tragedy later cashes. Both camps thus agree that Rosaline is indispensable and disagree about what she is indispensable as, a gear or a symptom. The play withholds her from the stage with a completeness that looks deliberate, as if it were protecting the quarrel from settlement: produce Rosaline, and one camp's account would collapse; keep her absent, and both survive.

## Test Case One: The Duel, Where Both Camps Score

Act 3 Scene 1 is the quarrel's best laboratory, because the scene's causation is double and each camp can prove its half. The character camp's evidence is a sequence of decisions. Romeo, newly married, refuses Tybalt's challenge with a gentleness that is itself a character revelation: "I do protest I never injured thee, / But love thee better than thou canst devise / Till thou shalt know the reason of my love" (3.1.68-70). Mercutio, reading the refusal as shame, draws in his friend's place. Romeo steps between the fencers, and Mercutio takes the wound under his arm. Romeo's self-indictment is the character camp's exhibit entire: "O sweet Juliet, / Thy beauty hath made me effeminate / And in my temper softened valour's steel!" (3.1.113-115). Then the reversal, the killing of Tybalt, and the cry that hands the scene to the opposition: "O, I am fortune's fool!" (3.1.131). A man has chosen, erred, recognized and ruined himself in a hundred lines. Bradley's criterion, exiled in Act 5, is fully satisfied here.

The structure camp's evidence is the scene's placement and machinery. The duel stands at the play's exact centre, the hinge Snyder and Brooke both build on, and it is fought inside the feud's purest form: a quarrel with no content, Tybalt's grievance a formality about a gatecrasher, the code of honour executing itself through whoever stands nearest. Mercutio's curse, "A plague o' both your houses!" (3.1.99), is the design naming itself, the mechanism's victim identifying the machine. Romeo's gentleness, the character camp's trait, is itself structurally produced: it exists because the secret marriage, comedy's device, has made Tybalt his kinsman an hour before. The scene's deepest fact is that neither score cancels the other. A structural slot, the centre duel the feud requires, is filled by a character's decision, the refusal and the intervention; and the decision is only possible because the structure has secretly rewritten Romeo's relations. Character is not competing with structure here. Character is how the structure gets itself performed.

Two further witnesses sharpen the adjudication. Tybalt, the character camp tends to flatten into a temper, is on inspection the structure camp's purest product: a man whose entire being is the feud's form, honour as reflex, so completely the code's creature that his death is the code consuming its own instrument. Yet the play gives him one flash of something like wounded status, his rage at the feast restrained by Capulet's public courtesy, "I will withdraw, but this intrusion shall, / Now seeming sweet, convert to bitt'rest gall" (1.5), a line in which humiliation is stored as fuel. A person is there, briefly, inside the function. And above the scene stands the Prince's edict from the first act, the death penalty proclaimed against further brawling, which converts the duel from a private catastrophe into a legal machine: Romeo's killing of Tybalt is banishment by statute before it is anything else, its consequence fixed by proclamation in advance. The edict is the structure camp's quiet masterpiece of evidence, a rule announced in Act 1 that executes itself in Act 3 regardless of anyone's intentions, while the intentions, Romeo's gentleness, Mercutio's shame, Tybalt's stored gall, decide the precise human shape the execution takes. Law supplies the trap; character supplies the path into it.

The play then does something the camps should both find embarrassing and instructive: it stages its own criticism of the scene. Benvolio's narration to the Prince is the character camp's method inside the fiction, a witness's account of who began, who pleaded, who fell, assembled into a story of persons and intentions, and it is, in the play's own terms, a partial account, shaped by loyalty to his house. The Prince's response is the structure camp's method enthroned: he does not refine the story, he applies the edict, converting narrative into sentence by rule, while Lady Capulet's demand, "Romeo slew Tybalt, Romeo must not live" (3.1), supplies the feud's own summary, a verdict in search of a hearing. Between Benvolio's telling and the Prince's sentence stands the whole quarrel, enacted by the play upon itself. Every later critic of the scene is re-performing one of these two offices, the friend's or the magistrate's, and the play has already shown the cost of each: the friend's account bends toward mercy, the magistrate's toward mechanism, and the truth of the scene requires a court that can hold both, which Verona does not possess and criticism, at its best, can.

Romeo's word "effeminate", spoken over Mercutio's body, has become a small battlefield of the camps in its own right. The character camp reads it as self-knowledge arriving on time: Romeo recognizes that love has unmanned his function in the feud's economy and corrects the error in blood, a tragic recognition in the strict sequence of error, insight and irreversible act. Readings in the queer line, whose account of Mercutio this series gives elsewhere, hear the word as the code speaking through him, the honour system's own vocabulary reclaiming a deserter; on that account the sentence is not Romeo's insight but his repossession. The structure camp notes the word's placement, the exact hinge of the scene, where the lover's register gives way to the duellist's in a single speech. Three camps, or two camps and a bridge, divide over eight syllables, and the division reproduces the master quarrel faithfully: is a speech the record of a mind, the voice of a system, or the joint in a design? The play, characteristically, has arranged for it to be all three in succession.

### Whose act turns the duel scene: Romeo's, Mercutio's, or the feud's?

The feud supplies the occasion, Mercutio supplies the sword, and Romeo supplies the fatal variation, stepping between the blades at the one moment his body can turn a wound into a death; the scene is built so that all three answers are true in sequence, which is why both camps can prove their readings from the same hundred lines.

## Test Case Two: The Letter, Where Structure Wins the Cause

Act 5 is the character camp's hardest ground and the structure camp's citadel. Romeo in Mantua has done nothing in the act but wait; the catastrophe is assembled entirely from information failures. Balthasar outruns the truth, Friar John is stopped by the plague searchers, the Friar arrives at the vault minutes late, Romeo arrives minutes early, and the timing, not any trait, distributes the deaths. "Then I defy you, stars!" (5.1.24) is a magnificent speech, and the structure camp is right to note that it changes nothing: the stars Romeo defies are the Prologue's, the design announced before his birth in the fiction, and his defiance is a line inside the pattern it protests. Substitute a patient Romeo, a cautious Romeo, a Romeo of any temperament, and the tomb still fills, because what has failed in Act 5 is Verona's system for moving truth between people, and systems do not have character. On causation, the verdict must go to structure outright. Bradley saw the fact clearly; his error was to call it a defect rather than a design.

The design reading has answers at every point where Bradley pressed. Harley Granville-Barker's preface to the play, written from the rehearsal room, answers the accident objection from the stage: preparation is everything, and a production that has built the play's speed honestly makes the mishap land as the last term of a sequence the audience has felt gathering, not as a coin toss. Brooke's account names the mechanism: the devices of the comic movement, the feigned death, the token, the message, return in the final acts inverted, comedy's debt collected in tragedy's currency, so that the letter's failure is the pattern completing itself rather than an intrusion into it. Even the play's last distribution of blame is structural in form. The Prince sentences not a person but a city: "See what a scourge is laid upon your hate, / That heaven finds means to kill your joys with love" (5.3.292-293), and closes with "All are punished" (5.3.295), fathers, prince and houses together, a verdict on a system delivered over the bodies it processed. What character still explains in Act 5 is not the outcome but its temperature. Romeo's speed in the apothecary's shop, his courtesy to the man he is about to kill, his tenderness over the body he believes dead: these decide how the catastrophe feels, and feeling, in a theatre, is not a decoration on the event. It is half the event.

Paris at the vault is Act 5's control experiment, and both camps should be made to publish their readings of him. Structurally he is Levin's terzo incommodo completing his arc, the intrusive third who has shadowed the lovers' pattern from the feast to the tomb, arriving with flowers to perform the bridegroom's office at the one wedding the feud's timetable could still arrange, and dying as the design's toll on everyone who touches the pattern. Yet the play gives this function a mourner's interiority at the moment of his use: "Sweet flower, with flowers thy bridal bed I strew, / O woe! thy canopy is dust and stones" (5.3.12-13). The lines are formal, a set lament, and they are also, unmistakably, a young man grieving at a grave he believes real. Romeo's apology over his body, and his promise to bury him with Juliet, extend the same doubleness to the act's end: the design consumes Paris, and the persons, even his killer, insist on treating the consumption as a death. An account of Act 5 that registers only the mechanism has missed what the mechanism costs, which is the only thing the act is about.

The act even contains a small parable of the two camps in Romeo's dream. He wakes in Mantua in unaccountable cheer: "I dreamt my lady came and found me dead, / Strange dream, that gives a dead man leave to think! / And breath'd such life with kisses in my lips, / That I reviv'd, and was an emperor" (5.1.6-9). The character camp reads the dream as a mind's weather, hope rising in Romeo for no reason the situation supplies, the last flare of the temperament that has misread every sign since the feast. The structure camp reads it as the design's irony in its purest form, a true prophecy inverted, since Juliet will indeed come and find him dead, and her kiss will not revive him; the audience, who hold the Prologue's knowledge, watch a man rejoice at the exact image of his catastrophe. Both readings are correct about different layers of the same four lines, and the layers do not compete. The dream is a character's consolation and a structure's signature at once, and a criticism that must choose between those descriptions has misunderstood how the play writes.

The statues close the act, and the quarrel, with an exhibit both camps must read in the other's presence. Montague promises a statue of Juliet in pure gold; Capulet answers that Romeo's shall lie as rich beside his lady; the Prince converts the exchange into the city's epitaph. The structure camp reads the moment as the design sealing itself: two bodies of passion become two effigies of metal, symmetry restored, the feud's houses joined at last in the only marriage the play allows them, a double monument. Gold is the feud's own element, the language of jointure and purchase in which the marriage market spoke, and the lovers end as its most expensive artifacts. The character camp reads the same lines as the fathers' first honest speech: men who priced everything discovering the one price that cannot recover what it buys, grief arriving in the only vocabulary they own. Neither reading cancels the other, because the lines are built double, a transaction that is also a breakdown. The play's last gift to the quarrel is to make its closing image unusable as a trophy for either side.

Act 5 also contains the play's own official account of itself, and its verdict is a draw. Friar Laurence, brought before the Prince, narrates the entire plot in a single sustained speech, the nearest thing the play offers to a structural summary: marriages, potions, letters and timings laid out as a chain of events. Yet the chain's narrator is a character pleading for his life, and he frames the chain with a surrender no structural summary would need: "A greater power than we can contradict / Hath thwarted our intents" (5.3.153-154). The play's most complete structural account is thus delivered as a personal apologia, by the one man who stood inside both the design and the decisions, and it satisfies neither camp's theory of the whole. The Prince accepts the narrative and distributes the blame across the city, persons and system sentenced in one breath. Shakespeare, at the end of his most formally announced tragedy, declines to let either the pattern or the persons have the last explanatory word, and criticism's two centuries of quarrel are the sound of that refusal echoing.

## Test Case Three: Capulet's Two Voices, the Crux Neither Camp Owns

Lord Capulet presents the quarrel with its most domestic puzzle, because he appears to be two men. In Act 1 he is the indulgent father, counselling Paris to patience: his child is a stranger in the world, not yet fourteen, and "my will to her consent is but a part" (1.2.16), a formula that makes Juliet's agreement a condition of the match. In Act 3, days later in stage time, he is a tyrant who answers his daughter's refusal with "An you be mine, I'll give you to my friend; / An you be not, hang, beg, starve, die in the streets" (3.5.191-192), and threatens to drag her to church on a hurdle. The character camp must explain how one man holds both voices, and its explanation is psychological pressure: grief for Tybalt, the family's honour suddenly precarious, a father's authority challenged at the moment of his maximum investment, the jointure agreed and the day appointed. On this reading the two voices are one character under changing loads, and the change is the point: Capulet is what the feud's world does to ordinary paternal affection when the timetable tightens.

The structure camp's explanation is colder and, on the evidence of the scenes' construction, stronger. Capulet in Act 1 is comedy's father, the blocking figure whose function is delay, and delay in comedy is benign because comedy has time. His courtesy to Paris is the senex performing his role in a genre where daughters marry at the end. In Act 3 the genre has turned, and the same figure performs the tragedy's version of the role: the father as the feud's property system in person, disposing of an heiress as the houses dispose of everything, by contract. The structure camp notes what the character camp tends to skip, that Capulet's Act 1 indulgence is itself a negotiation, conducted with the suitor over the daughter's absence, her consent priced as a part of his will rather than acknowledged as a will of her own. The tyranny is not a reversal of the courtesy but its truth with the gloves off, the marriage market showing its mechanism when the commodity resists. New historicist readings of the two voices, which this series treats in their own place, build on exactly this observation: the father's register changes because the transaction's stage has changed, from courtship to enforcement.

### Is the Capulet of Act 3 the same man as the Capulet of Act 1?

Yes in the text and no in the function: the play gives him motives, grief, honour and investment, that a psychological reading can join into one man, while his scenes are built to comedy's pattern in the first half and to tragedy's in the second, so that the figure an audience meets is one person performing two offices successively.

The household's other voice confirms the pattern from the women's side. Lady Capulet in Act 1 conducts the marriage suit as an audit, presenting Paris to her daughter in the language of a book to be studied, gold clasps locking in a golden story; in Act 3 she wishes the fool were married to her grave (3.5) and withdraws a mother's intercession in a sentence. The character camp must build a psychology capacious enough for both registers, a courtier-mother whose affection was always administrative. The structure camp reads the two registers as the market's two phases, display and enforcement, and notes that the women of the house administer the system that consumes them, as the Nurse's counsel in Act 3 administers it from below. Once again the camps divide the labour of explanation between them: psychology supplies the continuity of the woman, structure the discontinuity of her office, and the scene needs both to be as chilling as it is.

The adjudication favours structure on the cause and character on the cost. The cause of the change is best explained by the design: Shakespeare needed the marriage accelerated, the pressure on Juliet raised past endurance, and the father converted from delay to engine, and he made the conversion at exactly the joint where the genre turns. But the scene's horror is a character effect. A pure function would not frighten; Capulet frightens because the audience has met the man at his feast, watched him protect Romeo from Tybalt's rage, heard him call his hall's guests welcome, and must now watch that host disown his child in the vocabulary of property. The play spends Act 1 building a person so that Act 3's function can wound. That expenditure is the synthesis in its domestic form: structure commissions the turn, character pays for it.

## Test Case Four: Juliet's Growth, Where the Divide Collapses

Juliet is the character camp's strongest evidence and, properly examined, the synthesis's clearest proof. The growth Jameson staked her essay on is unmistakable in the text. The daughter of Act 1 who promises to look to like, "But no more deep will I endart mine eye / Than your consent gives strength to make it fly" (1.3.98-99), becomes the bride who commands the night, "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds" (3.2.1), becomes the strategist who out-reasons the Friar, becomes the solitary of the potion soliloquy, who imagines waking among the bones, fears the poison is a trick, fears Tybalt's ghost, and drinks to Romeo through every fear in sequence. By Act 5 she needs three lines and one dagger: "O happy dagger! / This is thy sheath. There rust, and let me die" (5.3.169-170). No reading of the play that cannot account for this trajectory deserves the name, and the trajectory is a fact about a person, or about the impression of a person that language can build.

The trajectory has a severance at its centre, and the camps divide over that too. When the Nurse counsels Juliet to marry Paris, on the ground that Romeo is banished and the second match is the better bargain, "I think it best you married with the county" (3.5.217), Juliet's reply is a courtesy that closes like a door: she commends the counsel and leaves to make her confession, meaning the opposite of what the Nurse hears. The character camp reads the moment as the tragedy's true coming of age, the point where Juliet's last counsellor is dismissed and her solitude, the condition of the potion soliloquy, is established by her own act. The structure camp reads it as a function expiring: the go-between, comedy's indispensable machine, has no further use in the tragedy's economy, and the play retires her with a line of dialogue the way it retires the Chorus. The readings converge on the fact and diverge on the pathos. What retires, for one camp, is a device; for the other, the only mother Juliet has effectively had, and the audience's knowledge of which description is true is precisely the play's method of making structure felt as loss.

Growth's darkest proof comes in Act 3 Scene 5, where Juliet, on her knees to the father who has just threatened her with the streets, performs submission while meaning defiance: "Good father, I beseech you on my knees, / Hear me with patience but to speak a word" (3.5.158-159). The girl of Act 1 could not have sustained the performance; the woman of Act 3 dissembles fluently, praises the match she intends to escape, and keeps two meanings running in every sentence she speaks to her parents from this point on. The character camp claims the doubleness as maturity's price: Juliet has learned Verona's own skill, the management of surfaces, and turned it against Verona. The structure camp notes that dissimulation is the intrigue plot's native device, comedy's heroine surviving by wit inside a household plot, and that Juliet's doubleness arrives exactly when the design transfers her from the romance plot to the intrigue plot, from the orchard to the Friar's cell. Her character, once again, is the form's requirement experienced as a soul's education, and the audience's unease at her fluency is the feeling of the two descriptions grinding against each other.

The orchard scene is where the growth is transacted, and it rewards a slow look from both camps. Juliet is the scene's realist: she extracts the confession Romeo did not know he was making, refuses his oath by the inconstant moon, "O, swear not by the moon, th' inconstant moon" (2.2.109), demands the form that will make the feeling enforceable, "If that thy bent of love be honourable, / Thy purpose marriage, send me word tomorrow" (2.2.142-143), and sets the terms, the time and the messenger. The character camp claims the scene as proof that Juliet's intelligence, not her passion alone, is the play's fastest-growing faculty: she is negotiating while Romeo is rhapsodizing, and the asymmetry predicts the whole tragedy, in which she will plan and he will improvise. The structure camp counters that the scene is built as a formal duet, an overhearing, a name meditation, a vow exchange, its beats fixed by the conventions it inherits, and that Juliet's realism is the design's requirement, since someone must convert the sonnet's rapture into the plot's contract before the act can end. Both claims survive contact with the text. The scene works because the design's need and the girl's mind are the same event described twice.

But notice how the growth is measured. It is measured in verse: in the loosening of set forms into soliloquy, in Juliet's share of the lovers' exchanges rising from respondent to initiator, in her imagery widening from the borrowed terms of the feast sonnet to a private lexicon of night, poison and the grave. Edward Snow's account of the lovers' language, which this series' Kahn article sets beside its subject, describes the same ascent in formal terms: a shared idiom being built, line by line, out of the Petrarchan script both lovers inherited. The character camp calls the ascent growth and the structure camp calls it formal development, and they are describing one phenomenon with two instruments, like surveyors measuring the same field in different units. Coleridge's founding insight returns here with its doubleness intact: precipitancy is the character of the play because the play's persons and the play's pattern share a single tempo. On Juliet, the divide does not need bridging. It evaporates, and what remains is the recognition that her character is the structure, experienced from inside.

## The Editors' Tribunal: Where the Camps Must Share a Page

Editors are the quarrel's unwilling arbiters, because an edition cannot suspend judgment. Every variant in the early texts forces a decision that one camp or the other will claim, and every introduction must tell a new reader, in effect, which kind of play this is. Brian Gibbons, whose Arden Second Series edition appeared in 1980, registered the formalist settlement in his introduction: the play's mixture of modes presented as design, its conventions burned into sincerity, the reading Brooke had argued given an editor's authority. Jill Levenson's Oxford edition of 2000 built its text on an archive of some 170 promptbooks, performance's evidence made load-bearing inside an edition, and framed the feud for its readers as a system of household obligations, a documentary ground on which the structure camp's mechanism and the character camp's household both stand. René Weis, in the Arden Third Series edition of 2012, pressed the first quarto's claims and argued an edition whose centre of gravity is Juliet herself: the character camp's heroine installed by the structure camp's own methods, collation and stage history.

The tribunal has a longer bench than three. G. Blakemore Evans's New Cambridge edition drew the play's stage history into its apparatus, treating performance as a class of evidence beside the documents; the Folger edition's facing-page format made the text and its explanations share every opening, an architecture that quietly instructs its schoolroom readers that a play is both an object of study and a script for voices. Each format is an argument about what kind of thing the play is, made in paper and type before a word of introduction is read. The camps, which conduct their quarrel in essays, sometimes forget that most readers meet the play inside an editor's decisions, and that the quarrel's terms reach them pre-sorted by a tribunal that has already voted.

The tribunal's hardest evidence is the existence of two early texts. The first quarto of 1597, a shorter, theatrically inflected version its title page calls an excellent conceited tragedy, and the second quarto of 1599, longer, more literary, "newly corrected, augmented, and amended", present editors with two shapes of the play, and the choice between their readings at hundreds of points is a choice between conceptions: a playing text's speed against a reading text's fullness, the company's Verona against the study's. A character-minded editor leans toward the readings that deepen persons, a structure-minded editor toward the readings that clarify design, and since no editor can print both texts in one line, every edition is a quiet verdict in the quarrel, delivered without argument in the apparatus. Weis's willingness to take the first quarto seriously as a version rather than a corruption is the tribunal's boldest recent act, because it concedes that the play existed, from the beginning, in more than one legitimate shape, and that structure and character may have been differently proportioned in the playhouse and the bookshop from the start.

The tribunal's lesson is that the text precedes the camps. Before any reading can begin, someone must decide what Romeo and Juliet says, and those decisions, the dawn quatrain's assignment, the staging of the orchard, the status of the bad quarto, are made with evidence neither camp owns: paper, type, theatrical record. The editors' practice is the working model of the synthesis this article argues. An introduction may lean formalist, as Gibbons's does, or documentary, as Levenson's does, or toward the heroine, as Weis's does, but the edition as a whole must keep both camps' evidence in print, the pattern and the persons on facing pages of the same apparatus. Criticism can afford camps. A text cannot.

## The Bridges: Character as Structure's Instrument

The generation after Levin and Brooke produced readings that refuse the divide by dissolving its terms, and each bridge, examined closely, crosses at the same point. Brooke's modes of utterance are carried by persons: the lyric mode is not an abstraction but Juliet at a window, and a mode changes when a character grows into it. Coppélia Kahn's reading of the feud as a patriarchal rite of passage manufactures its characters: Verona's sons become men by the feud's script and its daughters are denied a script altogether, so that Romeo's tears and Juliet's resolve are the system's products before they are anyone's traits. Catherine Belsey's essay on the rose speech argues that the name precedes the subject and is not at the disposal of the person who bears it: character, in her account, is an effect of the language that names it, the structure camp's premise stated as a theory of the self. René Girard's triangle does the same work for desire: what the character camp calls Romeo's passion is, for Girard, a mediation effect, desire copied from rivals and obstacles, the feud and the love sharing one root. Girard's reading of this play famously survives as a verdict rather than a finished essay, the chapter withheld from A Theatre of Envy, but the verdict's logic is complete. Marjorie Garber's account of the play's names and doubles likewise treats persons as positions in a verbal system that keeps substituting one figure for another, Rosaline for Juliet, Paris for Romeo, the letter for the voice.

Dympna Callaghan's feminist materialism crosses with a heavier cargo, because her target is the character camp's founding celebration itself. Where Jameson defended Juliet as love itself, Callaghan reads the lovers' passion as an ideology, a historically specific formation of desiring subjectivity that the play does not merely express but helps to manufacture, its very inwardness a cultural product. The move dissolves the character camp's prize exhibit into the structure camp's evidence: Juliet's interiority, the growth, the soliloquies, the frankness Jameson acquitted, becomes the effect of a discourse rather than the property of a soul. Yet Callaghan's reading, as this series' article on her argues in adjudicating her against Kahn, wins the theory of the desire and not the theory of the catastrophe: the orchard evidence shows a subject being formed, but the tomb is filled by machinery her account shares with the formalists. The bridge she builds thus lands on the far bank at two different points, which is why her work belongs in this section rather than in either camp's roster. She demonstrates that a critic can take the character camp's central datum, a young woman's inner life, more seriously than the character camp ever did, and still conclude that the datum is a structure's product.

Psychoanalysis crosses the divide from the opposite bank, and its crossing exposes the banks. The school's premise is the character camp's premise deepened: the lovers have inner lives, and those lives obey laws their speeches betray, the desire that fastens on obstacles, the haste that is a wish, the tomb that completes a pattern laid down in the nursery of the feud's households. Yet the laws the school applies are structures, impersonal geometries of the psyche that any bearer would enact, so that its persons are at once more inward than Jameson's and more determined than Snyder's. Freud himself, as this series' psychoanalytic article establishes, published no reading of this play; the school's work on the lovers is instruments in search of their case, and the case, when critics such as Kristeva took it up, produced accounts in which the couple's passion is a system for exporting hatred into the feud that surrounds it. Character as the symptom of a structure: the formula could stand as the bridge generation's motto, and it was reached from the camp of persons by digging down rather than from the camp of patterns by building up.

Performance criticism builds a bridge of a different material, evidence. Its claim, developed across this series' article on the living text, is that a production is a reading executed in public, and that four centuries of productions constitute a laboratory in which the camps' claims have been tested nightly. When Peter Brook cut the final reconciliation in 1947, the production was a structural argument, that the design's irony curdles the fathers' statues; when the same century's great Juliets made the potion scene a summit of acted psychology, the productions were character arguments with the house voting. Neither camp can dismiss the laboratory's results, and both ransack it. The performance bridge matters because it relocates the quarrel from the page to the event, where structure and character are not rival theories but the two things a rehearsal must separately solve: the shape of the evening and the life of the figures inside it.

One absence in the bridge traffic deserves recording. The new historicism, the method that redrew Shakespeare studies in the nineteen-eighties, never gave this play its set-piece reading; its founder's engagements with the play are an introduction's appreciation and a biographer's use, as this series' Greenblatt article establishes from the contents of his books. The omission is itself a datum for the quarrel. A method that reads plays as negotiations of social energy found its exhibits elsewhere, perhaps because this play's energies run so visibly through its forms, its sonnets, rites and rituals of honour, that the anecdotal method had less to add to what the formalists had already mapped. The camps' map, in other words, predicted where a later method would find its marginal returns, which is what a good map is for.

These bridges share a direction of travel: each begins from a system, a rite, a language, a geometry, and derives the persons. Yet none of them can afford to arrive at persons who do not feel real, because the systems they describe are only interesting as pressures on lives an audience can imagine. Kahn's rite needs Romeo's shame at his own tears; Belsey's name needs Juliet's wish to unname him; Girard's triangle needs Mercutio's wit to make mediation visible. The bridge readings therefore confirm the verdict from the far side. Character is structure's instrument, but an instrument that sounds: the pattern exists only as it is suffered, chosen and spoken by figures the audience receives as persons. A criticism that keeps the system and loses the suffering has explained the play's machinery and abandoned its subject, which is what the machinery is for.

## Verdict: A Division of Labour, Not a War

The claims can now be settled one by one. On the catastrophe's causation, the structure camp wins: the Prologue announces the end, the genre supplies the machinery, and Act 5's outcome survives every substitution of temperament, so that blame-centred readings of the whole play fail on the evidence of the letter. On the catastrophe's meaning, the character camp wins: the grief audiences carry out of the theatre attaches to persons, to a girl who reasoned herself through the vault's terrors and a boy who mistook haste for honour, and a pattern that no one suffers would not be a tragedy but a diagram. On the hinge, the honours are shared by construction: the duel is a structural slot filled with a character's choices, each camp's proof-texts drawn from the same hundred lines. On the lovers' speeches, the divide collapses entirely, since the growth the character camp celebrates is measured by the formal changes the structure camp catalogues. Bradley's exclusion stands as the quarrel's most valuable error: it proved, by the play's refusal to fit, that the character criterion was a definition, and it set the terms in which the structure camp's century of answers became necessary.

A verdict that cannot say what would overturn it is a slogan, so this article's falsification conditions should be stated, in the spirit of the discipline Cox demanded of Knight. The synthesis would fail if a scene could be produced in which a character operates wholly outside the play's forms, a speech with no pattern, no convention, no echo, whose power is unmediated personality; the orchard's improvisations are the usual candidates, and they turn out, on inspection, to be built from the sonnet, the aubade and the oath, forms all the way down. It would fail from the other side if a passage could be produced whose pattern signifies completely without persons, a stretch of design that audiences receive as pure structure; the Prologue is the usual candidate, and it turns out to be a voice, a presenter with a tone, already a person in embryo. The play keeps refusing both purity tests, and the refusal is systematic enough to count as its poetics. Character is never found unformed; form is never found uninhabited.

The settlement also specifies what each camp should stop claiming. The character camp should stop claiming the catastrophe: no distribution of blame among Romeo's haste, the Friar's meddling, Capulet's tyranny and the Nurse's counsel will sum to the tomb, because the sum omits the letter, and the letter is not a person. It should likewise stop treating the Prologue as packaging, since a frame the author built is evidence of the same order as a speech. The structure camp should stop claiming the grief: a pattern does not weep, and criticism that cannot say why this audience, on this night, feared for a girl with a vial in her hand has left the tragedy's effect outside its theory. It should also stop treating intention as a solvent, as if demonstrating the design dissolved the persons the design requires. These renunciations are not politeness. Each is dictated by a scene the renouncing camp cannot read, and a camp defined by what it cannot read is a camp with its borders honestly drawn.

The settlement has a practical form, a protocol for reading that any student of the play can apply. Take the scene twice. First pass, structure's questions: what does the frame announce, which forms are operating, what pattern thickens or breaks here, what would this scene's machinery do to any occupants? Second pass, character's questions: who wants what, what does this speech cost its speaker, what has changed in this person since the scene before? Then the adjudicating pass, the one this article has practised: ask where the two accounts compete for the same fact, and where they divide it. Where they compete, judge by the passage's weight of evidence, as the duel judges for partnership and the letter for structure. Where they divide it, refuse the temptation to declare a winner, since a fact about causation and a fact about significance cannot defeat each other. The protocol is laborious, and it is meant to be: the camps persist because each is the other's shortcut refused.

So the camps survive, and should, as a division of labour. Read structure to learn how the end is made: the announced design, the comic matrix breaking, the forms closing over the one organic relation. Read character to learn why the end wounds: the growth, the choices, the persons who might, in a world with a faster friar, have lived. The play's own construction enforces the partnership, because its persons are built from the very forms that destroy them. The lovers meet inside a sonnet, marry inside a rite, conspire inside a intrigue plot borrowed from comedy, and die inside a tomb the Prologue described before they had names. To read only the forms is to miss that the forms are inhabited; to read only the inhabitants is to miss that their house was condemned before they moved in. The thirty articles of this run, each owned by a school, a critic or an editor, are the quarrel's evidence assembled; the play they assemble around is large enough for both camps, and exact enough to correct each by the other.

The quarrel will continue, and the verdict here predicts its future form. Each new method that arrives in Shakespeare studies presents itself as the divide's abolition and is sorted, within a decade, into the column it secretly extends: the depth psychologies into character's, the systems and discourses into structure's, the performances into whichever column the production chose. This is not a failure of the methods. It is the play's own shape reasserting itself in its readers, a work built from persons and patterns in equal necessity reproducing its construction in every instrument brought to measure it. The reader who knows the quarrel in advance is not disarmed by it. They are equipped: able to say, of any new reading, which camp's evidence it has mastered, which camp's questions it cannot hear, and what the other column of the comparison would answer. That equipment, more than any single verdict in this article, is what the camps comparison is for.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch camps comparison, and what does it set side by side?

The InsightCrunch camps comparison is the two-column table in this article that sets the structure camp and the character camp side by side across the points where they collide: the first question each asks, the evidence each trusts, the feud, the duel, the letter, the statues and the verdict on the lovers. It compresses two centuries of criticism into rows so that a reader can watch the same fact, the stopped letter for instance, change its meaning as it crosses from one column to the other. The pattern it exposes is that the camps rarely dispute the evidence itself; they dispute what kind of thing the evidence is evidence of.

### Q: What question does each camp ask first when it opens the play?

The structure camp asks how the play is built: what the Prologue announces, how the acts are proportioned, which images recur and where the genre's machinery sits. The character camp asks who these people are: what Romeo wants, how Juliet changes, what the Friar fears and the Nurse wants. The first question governs everything downstream, because a critic who begins with construction will treat speeches as components, while a critic who begins with persons will treat construction as the scenery of decisions. Most quarrels between the camps are this difference of starting point, prolonged.

### Q: Why do structure critics start from the Prologue instead of from Romeo?

Because the Prologue is the one place where the play speaks in its own voice about its design, announcing "a pair of star-cross'd lovers" and their deaths before any character has acted. For a structure critic that announcement is the author's signature on the pattern: proof that the end precedes the choices, and that the persons move inside a frame built before them. Starting anywhere else, from this view, mistakes the contents for the container. The character camp replies that audiences forget the spoiler within a scene, but the structure critic's claim is about construction, not about what audiences remember.

### Q: What does the character camp find in Juliet's potion soliloquy that a structural reading misses?

A mind at work under terror. The character camp reads the soliloquy of Act 4 as Juliet reasoning: she imagines the poison is a trick, imagines waking among the ancestral bones, imagines Tybalt's ghost, and answers each fear in turn before drinking to Romeo. A structural reading registers the speech as a set piece, a formal pause before the feigned death, and can miss the soliloquy's essential drama, which is deliberation. For the character camp this is the play's centre of consciousness, the scene where Juliet stops being acted upon and becomes the tragedy's most decisive agent.

### Q: How does the structure camp explain the play's habit of announcing its ending in advance?

As a change of question. By surrendering suspense about the outcome, the play transfers the audience's attention to the manner of the arrival: not whether the lovers will die, but how each step toward the known end is taken, delayed and nearly averted. Structure critics from Levin onward treat the announcement as the design's honesty, a tragedy of process rather than of surprise, in which dramatic irony replaces curiosity as the engine. The repeated near-misses, the letter written, the wedding advanced, the watch set, become the pattern's visible workings, each one measured against the end the audience already holds.

### Q: What happens to Mercutio in each camp's account of the tragedy?

The character camp's Mercutio is a person: the play's wittiest mind, loyal past discretion, whose bawdy energy and contempt for dreams define a whole way of being young in Verona, and whose death is a friend's catastrophe. The structure camp's Mercutio is a function: for Snyder he is the incarnation of comic atmosphere, and his death is the moment the comic matrix breaks and the play's weather changes. The two accounts explain different facts. The first explains why audiences miss him; the second explains why the play after his exit feels airless, its jokes curdled and its pace turned funereal.

### Q: Which camp claims the golden statues as its evidence, and what does the other answer?

The structure camp claims them first: two figures of pure gold are the design's closing emblem, the lovers converted into pattern, symmetry and civic monument, their story sealed into the feud's official architecture. The character camp answers that the statues are the fathers' grief made metal, the price two particular men pay for two particular children, and that the Prince's closing couplet speaks of persons, not patterns: "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3.309-310). The exchange is the quarrel in miniature, an emblem to one reader and a bereavement to the other.

### Q: Why does the stopped letter trouble the character camp more than the feud does?

The feud can be absorbed by the character camp as circumstance: an inherited enmity that persons choose, scene by scene, to honour or refuse, so that blame and credit still attach to agents. The letter cannot be absorbed, because no one chooses its failure. Friar John is quarantined, Balthasar rides fast with false news, and Romeo's part in Act 5 is only to believe what he is told. A criticism built on the premise that catastrophe issues from character must either call the play defective, as Bradley did, or concede that its deciding cause lies outside character altogether.

### Q: What did L. C. Knights propose critics study in place of characters' imagined lives?

The words on the page. Knights argued that a Shakespeare play is a dramatic poem, and that its persons exist only in their speeches, so the critic's proper object is the language itself: its imagery, rhythm and tonal life, from which the impression of character is built. The proposal did not abolish character; it relocated it inside verbal structure, which is why Knights's attack on Bradley became an unlikely bridge between the camps. A reader who studies how Juliet's sentences change is studying both her growth and the play's formal development, under a single discipline.

### Q: Does the structure camp have any account of why audiences grieve for the lovers?

It has an account of the grief's engineering. Structure critics point to proportion and delay: the audience is given the wedding at the centre, the consummation, the dawn parting, each happiness formally completed before its destruction, so that the catastrophe repossesses things the audience has watched being built. Grief, on this account, is the felt cost of a pattern completing itself against the lives inside it. The character camp finds the explanation thin, since audiences grieve for the persons and not for the proportion, but the structural account does explain why this play's grief feels fated rather than merely sad.

### Q: How does each camp locate the play's turning point?

The structure camp locates it in the design: for Snyder, the duel of Act 3, where the comic matrix breaks; for Brooke, distributed through the mixture, the wedding at the centre where the modes change hands. The character camp locates it in decisions: Romeo's choice to intervene and then to kill, and Juliet's choice at the Friar's cell to accept the potion, are the hinges on which its readings turn. The disagreement is less about the scenes than about what a turning point is, a joint in the construction or a moment when a person becomes answerable for the end.

### Q: What do sonnets and set speeches become in the character camp's hands?

Evidence of minds meeting and changing. The shared sonnet of the feast becomes, for the character camp, the record of two intelligences discovering each other in real time, each completing the other's thought; the formal laments of Act 4 become the sound of grief performed by people who have no private language for it; even the aubade is read as a young wife's invention, reasons manufactured to delay a parting. The camp does not deny that these are set forms. It insists that forms are what persons in this world have instead of privacy, and that character shows precisely in how a form is inhabited.

### Q: How does the word flaw change its meaning between the two camps?

In the character camp's usage, inherited from Aristotle through Bradley, a flaw is a trait inside a person, haste or pride or jealousy, from which the catastrophe can be derived. In the structure camp's usage, what looks like a flaw is a gear in the design: Romeo's haste is the tempo the plot requires, the quality that gets him married in a day and to Mantua and the vault on schedule. The same word thus names either a moral fact about a man or a mechanical fact about a play, and much of the quarrel is the two camps declining to notice that they have changed the subject.

### Q: Which camp reads the first scene better, and on what evidence?

The structure camp, on the evidence of the scene's construction. The opening brawl is a mechanism in miniature: servants exchanging ritual insults whose meaning they litigate as they go, citizens with clubs, two patriarchs calling for swords their wives mock, and a prince imposing form on chaos by proclamation. Its persons are barely persons; Sampson and Gregory are functions of the feud's etiquette. The character camp can explain the old men's ridiculousness, but the scene's meaning is its demonstration that Verona's violence is procedural, a liturgy of honour performed by whoever is standing in the street. Form reads it best because form is what it is about.

### Q: What would a production have to choose between the camps in Act 3 Scene 1?

Emphasis, which in a theatre is destiny. A production leaning to character will build the duel from relationships, Romeo's new-wed gentleness played as conviction, Mercutio's intervention as loyalty, the killing of Tybalt as a man's collapse into the feud he had renounced. A production leaning to structure will choreograph the scene as the feud's ritual reasserting itself, the fighters interchangeable inside the code, Romeo's body placed by the blocking where the wound becomes possible. Granville-Barker's stage criticism suggests the choice is real but not total: preparation can make the machinery visible while the persons carry its cost.

### Q: How do modern editors hold both camps inside a single edition?

By division of the page. The edited text and its apparatus belong to neither camp: collation, variant readings and stage history are evidence both must use. The introduction is where an editor leans, Gibbons toward the designed mixture, Levenson toward the documentary household and the promptbook record, Weis toward Juliet as the play's centre. An edition therefore holds the quarrel in suspension, printing the pattern's evidence and the persons' evidence in one volume, and letting each generation of readers re-argue the case from the same pages. Editing is the synthesis practised as a trade.

### Q: Why does Friar Laurence split the camps: failed person or misfiring device?

Because the Friar is where their explanations trade places. To the character camp he is a person whose meddling, timidity and self-protection, culminating in his flight from the vault, make him the tragedy's most answerable adult. To the structure camp he is comedy's standard device, the helpful friar of the intrigue plot, whose schemes work exactly as such schemes work until the genre turns under him, so that his failure is the matrix failing, not a man. Each reading exposes the other's gap: the device account cannot judge him, and the play does judge him; the personal account cannot explain why his plan was sound until the world's rules changed.

### Q: Where does the character camp find its strongest evidence in Act 5?

In Romeo's conduct inside a catastrophe he did not cause. The character camp concedes the letter to structure and claims everything Romeo does with the false news: the instant resolve, the apothecary scene's terrible courtesy, the killing of Paris conducted almost as a rite of apology, and the long farewell over Juliet's body, where the speeches slow the design to a standstill. On this evidence the act's meaning is personal even though its outcome is mechanical. Romeo cannot change what happens; he can only decide who he will be while it happens, and the camp holds that this decision is what the audience remembers.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch camps comparison to argue that character serves structure in this play?

Begin from the rows where the columns collide hardest, the letter and the duel, and show the same fact changing its explanation as it crosses the table. Then argue the mechanism: the play's design requires specific traits, haste in Romeo, resolve in Juliet, wit in Mercutio, and supplies the occasions that convert those traits into the catastrophe, so that character is not the design's rival but its moving part. The comparison gives the essay its fairness, since each camp's best evidence is set out before the verdict, and its shape, since the verdict follows the rows where one column demonstrably cannot carry the scene alone.

### Q: Can the two camps be reconciled, or must a reader choose between them?

They can be reconciled as methods and not as slogans. A reader must choose, scene by scene, which question the passage in front of them answers better, how the end is being made or why it wounds; but the choice is local and reversible, not a allegiance. The reconciled criticism this article argues for treats character as structure's instrument: the design supplies the frame, the feud, the timetable and the stopped letter, while the persons supply the choices that make the frame's completion feel like loss. A reader who holds both has not split the difference. They have described the whole play.
