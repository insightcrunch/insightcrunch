Golwalkar's Bunch of Thoughts explained on its own terms is a different book from the one standard summaries describe, because the summaries read the 1966 text through a handful of contested passages while the book itself builds a framework. Madhav Sadashiv Golwalkar, second Sarsanghchalak of the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh from 1940 to 1973, left no unified treatise; he left speeches, interviews and writings that his associates arranged into a thematic volume whose argument is architectural. This article defends a namable claim: Golwalkar's organic-social Hindutva is substantively distinct from Savarkar's territorial-cultural Hindutva, and the conceptual architectures differ on every register that matters, in the unit of analysis, in the organisational vehicle each framework presupposes, and in the theoretical commitments each one carries.

The distinction matters because the series of studies to which this article belongs separates Hindutva into three ideologies rather than one inheritance, under [the tripartite-disentanglement framework](/2019/09/02/hindutva-three-distinct-ideologies/) that reads Savarkar's 1923 doctrine, Golwalkar's organisational doctrine and the later electoral doctrine as separate constructions with separate logics. Within that separation, Golwalkar's book is the foundational text of the second ideology, and its distinctness is the fact to be established rather than assumed. Readers who collapse the two frameworks into a single tradition cannot explain why the RSS guarded Golwalkar's text as its own charter while treating Savarkar, a Hindu Mahasabha leader who never held office in the Sangh, as an honoured predecessor outside the chain of command. Readers who treat the two books as one doctrine also cannot explain the specific choices Golwalkar made: an organic whole where Savarkar had specified a membership test, a functional reading of varna where Savarkar had attacked caste as a fragmenting curse, and dharma as the anchor of identity where Savarkar had anchored identity in territory, blood and a holy land.

The method here is the method of the framework itself. The article reads Bunch of Thoughts as an architecture: it identifies the load-bearing commitments, tests them against Savarkar's Essentials of Hindutva of 1923 commitment by commitment, and only then places the contested passages, the chapter on internal threats and the treatment of minorities, inside the structure that gives them their specific meaning. Settled facts, dates, texts, votes and judgments, are stated plainly. Contested characterisations, of motives, of labels, of blame, are attributed to their named holders and rendered at their strongest, on the critical side and on the defensive side alike, because an adjudication that weakens one party before judging has decided nothing. The named disagreement is between the continuous-tradition reading, which treats Golwalkar as Savarkar's continuator, and the substantively-distinct reading defended here. The evidence is primary wherever the record allows: the two books themselves, the 1939 precursor We or Our Nationhood Defined, the RSS's own later statements about both texts, and the scholarly literature that has handled the archive, Walter Andersen and Shridhar Damle's The RSS: A View to the Inside of 2018, Christophe Jaffrelot's The Hindu Nationalist Movement and Indian Politics of 1996, and Aishwary Kumar's Radical Equality of 2015.

## Golwalkar's Bunch of Thoughts Explained: The Text and Its Making

Bunch of Thoughts reached print in 1966, published by Sahitya Sindhu Prakashana of Bangalore, the publishing house the Sangh's sympathisers maintained in the south, and the first edition was issued around Golwalkar's sixtieth birthday, an occasion the organisation marked as a milestone in a tenure that had begun in 1940. The volume ran to several hundred pages and gathered material Golwalkar had produced across more than two decades: bauddhik addresses to swayamsevaks, speeches on tour, interviews given to journalists, and essays written for Sangh occasions. A compiler arranged the material under thematic heads; the book names no editor on its title page, and the critical literature on the text has remarked on that anonymity ever since, since a compilation's arrangement is itself an act of interpretation. Professor M.A. Venkata Rao, the Mysore philosopher who moved in Sangh-sympathetic circles, checked the English manuscript, a detail preserved in early critical notices of the book. A second edition added fifteen chapters and a substantial question-and-answer section, which means the text readers cite exists in strata, and careful citation names the edition as well as the page.

### How was Bunch of Thoughts assembled from Golwalkar's speeches?

Golwalkar wrote almost none of the book as a book. Associates collected his bauddhik talks, tour speeches, press interviews and occasional essays from the years of his Sarsanghchalak tenure, transcribed the spoken material, and arranged the whole under thematic headings, so the volume's order is editorial while its sentences, repeatedly checked against his delivered words, are his.

The compilation character cuts in two directions, and the article keeps both in view. Against the architectural reading, a sceptic can argue that a miscellany has no architecture, that themes imposed by a compiler are the compiler's rather than the author's. The answer lies in the material's consistency across twenty-five years of delivery: the same concepts recur in the same relations in speeches separated by decades, in Nagpur and on tour, before cadres and before journalists, which is the signature of a framework held by the speaker rather than invented by an editor. For the architectural reading, the compilation character explains the book's texture, its repetitions, its shifts between metaphysical exposition and organisational instruction, its sudden descents from the philosophy of the nation to the discipline of the shakha. A treatise hides its author's working environment; a compilation preserves it. Bunch of Thoughts preserves the environment of the RSS itself, and that preservation is evidence, because the framework and the organisation were built for each other.

The book's afterlife in print extends the evidence. The preface to the early editions records translations into Hindi, Bengali, Marathi, Gujarati, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada and Malayalam, a publishing programme that carried the framework to every linguistic region the cadre order was entering, and the absence of a Punjabi translation, noticed by an early critical reviewer of the book, became a small controversy of its own in the years when Punjabi Hindu opinion was divided over the language question in Punjab. The text's custodianship remained with Sahitya Sindhu across the decades, through the reprints of the 1990s and 2000s that scholars cite, and the organisation's periodic glosses, in 2006 and in 2018, were addressed to this book and this book's predecessor rather than to any other Sangh text, confirming in the republic of letters what the bauddhik syllabus confirms in the shakha: the volume of 1966 is the movement's operative scripture, handled with a care no other Golwalkar text receives.

The text's internal organisation confirms the priority of the whole over the parts. Its opening movement defines the nation; its middle movements work through the threats, the defects and the disciplines of Hindu society; its closing movements address the path of national regeneration and the moulding of men. Golwalkar's own summary of his life's assignment, quoted in Sangh literature from his addresses, was organisational before it was literary: the founder, Keshav Baliram Hedgewar, had set the aim, in Golwalkar's retelling, of organising Hindu society so strongly that no power on earth would dare cast an evil eye upon it. The book serves that assignment. It is a cadre's handbook wearing the dress of a philosophy, and the philosophy it teaches is the organic-social framework this article reconstructs.

## The Sarsanghchalak Behind the Text: Golwalkar Before 1966

Madhav Sadashiv Golwalkar was born on 19 February 1906 at Nagpur, in a Marathi-speaking family of modest means, and educated in science: he took a master's degree in zoology and worked from 1931 to 1933 as a demonstrator in zoology at Banaras Hindu University, the post from which his lifelong honorific Guruji, the teacher, derived. He returned to Nagpur, read law, and practised briefly. In 1936 he withdrew to the Ramakrishna ashram at Sargachi in Bengal as a disciple of Swami Akhandananda, a direct disciple of Ramakrishna; the guru's death in 1937 left him, by the standard biographical accounts, in depression and indecision, and Hedgewar persuaded him that his obligation could be discharged inside the RSS. The episode matters for the framework, because the man who would define the nation through dharma had first sought the religious life in its renunciatory form, and the framework he built never treated dharma as a decorative synonym for culture. It treated dharma as the substance of the national bond, a commitment with biographical roots.

Hedgewar groomed the recruit with unusual speed. Golwalkar took charge of the All-India Officers' Training Camp, the Sangh's most demanding organisational assignment, across the camps of 1937 to 1939; in 1938 Hedgewar asked him to render Ganesh Damodar Savarkar's Marathi Rashtra Mimansa of 1934 into Hindi and English, the commission that produced We or Our Nationhood Defined in 1939; and on 13 August 1939 Hedgewar made him Sarkaryavah, general secretary and second in the Sangh's hierarchy. The succession itself was settled in writing: a day before his death on 21 June 1940, Hedgewar handed Golwalkar a sheet of paper asking him to lead the Sangh, and on 3 July 1940 five provincial sanghchalaks announced the decision at Nagpur. Senior activists who had been passed over doubted the choice; Balasaheb Deoras, who would himself hold the office from 1973, recorded in later years that several RSS leaders were sceptical of Golwalkar's capacity. The scepticism is a datum for the personality-as-institution framework: the office Golwalkar inherited was fifteen years old, small and regionally concentrated, and the institution the RSS became, a million-member federation of affiliated bodies across every province and several continents, was built inside his thirty-three-year tenure, which is why his book and his organisation must be read together.

The organisational inheritance he received is described in the study of [the RSS organisational architecture Golwalkar inherited](/2019/09/13/kb-hedgewar-founding-rss-1925/): the daily shakha, the unpaid full-time pracharak, the chain of command running from the Sarsanghchalak through provincial organisers to the local branch, and a self-definition as a cultural body standing apart from party politics. Golwalkar preserved every element of that inheritance and scaled it, which is precisely why his framework presupposes the organisation rather than arguing for it. A reader who opens Bunch of Thoughts cold meets constant unargued reference to shakha discipline, to the pracharak's celibate service, to the annual cycle of camps and festivals; the book assumes the machine and discusses the fuel. Savarkar's Essentials of Hindutva, written in Ratnagiri internment in 1922 and 1923 by a revolutionary under surveillance, assumes no machine at all, and addresses any Hindu reader anywhere. The difference in presupposition is one of the three registers on which the frameworks divide.

### How did the 1948 ban reshape Golwalkar's public doctrine?

The ban of February 1948, imposed after Mahatma Gandhi's assassination and lifted in July 1949 once the Sangh adopted a written constitution, taught Golwalkar the price of unguarded idiom. The constitution of 1949 committed the RSS to cultural work and barred organisational politics, and his later public doctrine kept the framework while disciplining its vocabulary of confrontation.

The ban years deserve a fuller record, because they tested the framework at its weakest point. Golwalkar was arrested in February 1948 and held for six months; from prison and after his release he corresponded with Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, the Home Minister, who demanded that the Sangh adopt a constitution, profess loyalty to the Indian flag and the Constitution, and conduct itself as a cultural body. The RSS constitution of 1949 did all three, in terms its critics at the time and since have read as tactical. Golwalkar's own conduct across the crisis, negotiation rather than confrontation, patience rather than martyrdom, became the organisation's template for surviving hostile governments. The framework's doctrinal core survived intact; its public expression learned caution. When the contested passages of Bunch of Thoughts are weighed, the date of their delivery matters: most were spoken into a movement that had already learned, in 1948 and 1949, exactly how the Indian state responds to a doctrine it reads as a threat, and Golwalkar spoke them regardless, which his defenders cite as candour and his critics as incorrigibility. Both readings are recorded here because the article's task is adjudication of the architecture, and the architecture is legible in either light.

One further source deserves naming because the brief for this study flags it as under-cited: Golwalkar's pre-1940 organisational correspondence, preserved in part at the RSS headquarters archives at Nagpur, documents the specific decisions of the transition years, the camp administration, the handling of provincial organisers, the drafting labour behind the 1939 book. Archival correspondence of this kind is fragile, and the researchers and journalists who consult such holdings work under a professional obligation to shield the living persons a letter may name, an obligation that modern practice supports with source-protection tools of the kind described for [journalists protecting confidential sources through VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/). The correspondence matters here for a narrow reason: it shows the architectural choices being made in working documents, the organisation imagined as an organism with differentiated functions, years before the speeches of the 1960s gave the choices their canonical form.

The ban crisis of 1948 and 1949, introduced above, also belongs to the making of the public doctrine, and its paper trail is unusually complete. Sardar Patel, as Home Minister, conducted the negotiation that ended the ban, and his letter to Golwalkar of 11 September 1948 survives as the crisis's key document: Patel acknowledged the Sangh's service during the Partition massacres, writing that the RSS had rendered useful help in disturbed areas, while pressing the conditions, a written constitution, a professed loyalty to the flag and the Constitution, and a cultural rather than political character, on which alone the organisation could resume its work. Golwalkar accepted the conditions, delivered the constitution in 1949, and spent the following decades inside the settlement's terms while disputing its premises in speech. The pattern is the framework's political signature: total doctrinal intransigence joined to total tactical patience, the organism refusing every demand to become something else while conceding every formality that let it continue growing. Students of the movement who miss the pattern misread both the intransigence and the patience as their opposites.

## We or Our Nationhood Defined: The 1939 Baseline

No reading of the 1966 book is competent without its 1939 predecessor, because the RSS's own custodians draw the line between the two books at exactly the point where the frameworks divide. We or Our Nationhood Defined appeared at Nagpur in 1939 under Golwalkar's name, printed by Bharat Publications, with a preface dated 22 March 1939 in which Golwalkar acknowledged Ganesh Damodar Savarkar's Rashtra Mimansa as one of his chief sources of inspiration and help. The book was short, systematic and blunt. The American scholar Jean A. Curran, whose Militant Hinduism in Indian Politics of 1951 studied the Sangh from inside its camps, called the 1939 text the RSS's Bible, and for two decades the organisation made no effort to qualify that standing. The book went through four editions under British rule. Its argument is a definition: a nation, Golwalkar wrote, exists only where five unities coincide, and he named them in a sentence the article quotes because paraphrase would soften it: the Nation concept requires as its indissoluble component parts "the famous five unities, Geographical (Country), Racial (Race), Religious (Religion), Cultural (Culture) and Linguistic (Language)", and the loss of any one means the end of the nation as a nation.

The definition's polemical target deserves its own paragraph, because Golwalkar's contempt for the alternative organises both his books. Territorial nationalism, the doctrine that a nation's people is whoever resides inside a state's boundary, he calls an unnatural, unscientific and lifeless hybrid concept, and he gives the hybrid a body of its own: an animal assembled from the head of a monkey and the legs of a bullock joined to the trunk of an elephant can show no life, he argues, and any activity in such a body is the activity of the germs breeding in a decomposing corpse. The violence of the figure is the point. Against the civic doctrine he sets a nationalism of substance, in which the people exists before the state and lends the state its meaning, and he asks the question his whole career answers: if the word nation in India names a haphazard bundle of friend and foe, master and thief, then the word we in the nation's founding documents names nothing at all. The 1939 book's five unities are his attempt to say what the we contains. The 1966 book replaces the inventory with an organism, but the polemic survives the replacement intact, and the corpse figure's logic, that a merely territorial people is a dead thing animated by decay, stands behind the later framework's impatience with the republic's founding settlement.

Applied to India, the definition yielded the book's conclusions. Only the Hindu people, in Golwalkar's argument, held all five unities in Hindusthan: the land between the Indus and the seas as country, common descent as race, the Hindu religion as faith, a culture he described as the product of that religion, and Sanskrit as the mother of the land's languages. Territorial nationalism, the doctrine that every resident of a territory composes one nation, he rejected as an unnatural, unscientific and lifeless hybrid, and the rejection carried a prescription for the communities the definition excluded. The prescription stands in the 1939 text in words that must be quoted rather than summarised, since two generations of controversy have turned on their exact force: "the foreign races in Hindusthan must either adopt the Hindu culture and language, must learn to respect and hold in reverence the Hindu religion, must entertain no idea but those of the glorification of the Hindu race and culture, that is, of the Hindu nation, and must lose their separate existence to merge in the Hindu race, or may stay in the country, wholly subordinated to the Hindu Nation, claiming nothing, deserving no privileges, far less any preferential treatment, not even citizen's rights." The book read contemporary Germany as an instructive case: Germany, Golwalkar wrote, had shown "how well-nigh impossible it is for Races and cultures, having differences going to the root, to be assimilated into one united whole, a good lesson for us in Hindusthan to learn and profit by", and of the German purge of the Jews he wrote that race pride at its highest had been manifested there. The words are set out here exactly because the later history of the book is a history of attempts to unsay them.

That later history is evidence in the disagreement this article adjudicates, and it runs in dated steps. In a speech of 1963, Golwalkar stated that the 1939 book had been an abridged translation of Rashtra Mimansa, a claim his preface of 1939, which speaks of inspiration and help, does not make. Comparative study of the two texts, conducted by scholars including Christophe Jaffrelot, has found the English book to be Golwalkar's own composition drawing on the Marathi work, with the passages on Germany Golwalkar's addition; the translation claim, stated at its strongest by the book's defenders, is that a young organiser rendering a mentor's treatise cannot be held to a statesman's standard for its every sentence, and at its strongest by its critics, that the claim was a retrospective device produced when the sentences became indefensible. In February 2006 the RSS formalised its distance: a booklet by Rakesh Sinha, Shri Guruji and Indian Muslims, released at Nagpur on 24 February 2006 under a Sangh publishing imprint, argued that the 1939 book carried the views of the young Golwalkar rendering another man's work, and RSS spokesmen stated that the book did not represent the views of the grown Guruji or of the RSS. M.G. Vaidya, the organisation's senior ideologue, approved the removal of the 1939 book from the Sangh's pantheon while identifying the replacement in terms the architectural reading predicts: the central book, Vaidya said, is Bunch of Thoughts, because it consists of Golwalkar's views after he became Sarsanghchalak on 21 June 1940. The organisation's custodians, that is, themselves separate the 1939 definition-mongering from the 1966 framework, and defend the second by abandoning the first. An article arguing that the two Golwalkar books articulate different conceptual constructions is, on this point, aligned with the Sangh's own librarians.

What changed between 1939 and 1966 is visible in the architecture. The 1939 book proceeds by criteria: five unities, each examinable, a checklist a candidate people passes or fails. The method is closer to Savarkar's three criteria of 1923, a common nation, a common race, a common culture bound to a fatherland and a holy land, than the continuity debate usually notices, and on the checklist method Golwalkar in 1939 extended Savarkar rather than replacing him. The 1966 book proceeds by organism: the nation is a living whole whose parts have functions, and membership is a matter of organic belonging rather than of passing tests. The five unities survive inside the later book as description, in its opening section on nationhood defined, but they no longer carry the argument's weight; the weight has moved to the whole and its health. That migration, from criteria to organism, is the precise point at which Golwalkar's framework becomes substantively distinct, and the three commitments of the next sections are its working parts.

The unities themselves repay a slower reading, because each one mutates as it crosses from 1939 to 1966, and the mutations map the migration. Country, the geographical unity, survives almost unchanged: the land between the Indus and the seas remains the nation's body in the spatial sense, its rivers and sacred geographies lovingly enumerated in both books. Race, the most dated of the five, quietly changes its meaning: the 1939 text argues descent in the period's racial idiom, while the 1966 text, without formally retracting anything, speaks the language of a people formed by a common life on the land, descent absorbed into belonging. Religion changes its rank, as the section on the dharma anchor shows: from one unity among equals it becomes the ground of the rest, the order of which culture is the expression and language the voice. Culture expands until it threatens to swallow the definition entire, since in the organic framework nearly every fact about the nation, its food, its festivals, its family forms, its polity, is a cultural fact. Language contracts to a single devotional claim, Sanskrit as mother and source, with Hindi assigned the head's office of common speech. A checklist in which one item becomes the ground, one becomes the whole field and one shrinks to a symbol is no longer a checklist. The five unities in 1966 are best read as a fossil of the earlier framework embedded in the later one, visible in the opening section's strata, and the fossil is useful precisely because it lets a reader watch one architecture being rebuilt into another by the same hands.

## The Organic-Social Architecture of Bunch of Thoughts

The framework's first move is to change the unit of analysis. Savarkar's Essentials of Hindutva asks who is a Hindu, and answers with criteria an individual satisfies: one for whom India is both fatherland and holy land, of the Hindu race, heir to Sanskrit culture. The question is a membership question and the book is a boundary instrument. Bunch of Thoughts asks a different question: what is the Hindu nation as a living entity, and what does its health require? The answer organises the book. The Hindu people, Golwalkar wrote in the passage his editors placed near the front of the volume, is the Virat Purusha, the great cosmic being of the Rig Vedic hymn: "Brahmin is the head, Kshatriya the hands, Vaishya the thighs and Shudra the feet", and he called this figure "the very core of our concept of nation". A body is not a club. It admits no applications, conducts no admissions test, and expels no limb for failing criteria; its parts are born into functions and its unity is prior to any of them. The organic metaphor is not decoration on the framework. It is the framework, and every commitment the book defends follows from taking the metaphor literally as social theory.

Three consequences follow in the text. First, the whole is prior to the individual: the nation's life is the reality, and individual lives take meaning from their place within it, a commitment examined in its own section below. Second, differentiation inside the whole is functional: the head and the feet differ as organs differ, by task, and Golwalkar insists, in passage after passage, that the difference carries no warrant for contempt, since a body that despises its feet cannot walk. Third, the bond that holds the organs in one life is spiritual: dharma, the order that sustains, names both the body's constitution and its animating principle, so that the nation's unity is at bottom a religious fact in the civilisation's own idiom. The three consequences correspond to the three commitments the comparison table in this article sets out, and each one reverses a Savarkar position. Savarkar's unit was the individual member; his differentiation was a historical curse to be abolished; his bond was territorial and civilisational, with religion admitted as one component under the discipline of a rationalist suspicion of priestcraft. The reversal is total, and it is systematic, which is what substantively distinct means.

One doctrinal signature of the organism deserves separate statement, because the Sangh's self-description has repeated it for eighty years: the framework presents itself as positive. Golwalkar insists, in the book's expositions of the Sangh's purpose, that the work is not a reaction against any community but the consolidation of the Hindu people for its own sake, that hatred of the Muslim or the Christian is no part of the cadre's formation, and that the nation's enemies enter the doctrine only because a body must know its disorders. The claim is the framework's own account of itself, and the article records it as such, neither endorsing nor dismissing it. Critics answer that a positivity defined against internal threats is a distinction without a difference, since the threats chapter names the neighbours the positive work must overcome. Defenders answer that intention matters in doctrine as in law, and that a movement which understood itself as reactive would have built a militia rather than a school. The architectural reading contributes one observation to the quarrel: the positivity claim is exactly what an organic framework would generate, since a body does not hate its disorders but treats them, and the claim's persistence in Sangh rhetoric from Golwalkar's tenure to the present custodians is evidence of the framework's grip on the organisation's imagination of itself.

The architectural reading also explains the book's tone, so different from Savarkar's forensic energy. Essentials of Hindutva argues like a barrister, defining terms, anticipating objections, splitting hairs over the word Hindu until the definition locks. Bunch of Thoughts speaks like a preceptor addressing the already persuaded: it exhorts, illustrates, warns, blesses. The difference of genre follows from the difference of framework. A membership test must be argued, because outsiders will contest the boundary. An organism must be tended, because its members are already inside and the dangers are decay, indiscipline and infection. The internal-threats chapter, the book's most contested passage, is written in the tending genre: its subject is the health of a body, and its targets are classified as disorders of that body. That classification, not any difference of animus, is what separates Golwalkar's treatment of minorities from Savarkar's. Savarkar's minorities fail a test at the border of the nation. Golwalkar's minorities are organs that refuse the body's life, and the remedies differ accordingly: exclusion in the first framework, absorption or subordination in the second. The distinction does not soften the second framework. It specifies it, which is the article's purpose.

## The Shape of the Book: Nationhood, Threats and the Path to Glory

A reader who wants the framework whole should attend to the volume's own grouping, which the Sahitya Sindhu editions arrange as a progression in three great movements. The first movement defines the nation: chapters on the meaning of nationhood, on the Hindu people as the nation's substance, on the land and its sacred geography, on the antiquity Golwalkar claims for the national life, arguing in one celebrated passage that the Hindu nation existed as a self-conscious whole thousands of years before the European nations were conceived. The second movement diagnoses: chapters on the defects the author finds in Hindu society, its disunity, its caste arrogance, its neglect of the collective disciplines, and then the chapters on the dangers, the internal threats and the external pressures, in which the Partition of 1947 is read as the wages of national weakness. The third movement prescribes: chapters on the path to glory, on the moulding of men, on the Sangh's method of character formation, closing in exhortation. Diagnosis between definition and prescription is the order of a physician, and the order is the organic framework in the book's table of contents before it is anywhere argued in sentences: first know the body, then name its disorders, then apply the regimen.

The diagnosis movement contains the book's most under-read material, its sustained attack on Hindu society itself. Golwalkar scourges the society he claims to defend: for its treatment of the depressed classes, which he calls a sin that has weakened the nation's body; for its learned men's indifference to organisation; for its habit of exalting the individual saint and neglecting the collective disciplines by which nations survive. The Partition chapters belong to this movement and deserve their reputation for bleakness. Independence arrived, in his reading, mutilated: the nation accepted vivisection in 1947 because it lacked the organised strength to refuse, and the leadership that accepted it had mistaken territorial nationalism for the real thing, counting heads inside a boundary instead of welding a people into a body. The reflection is a polemic against the Congress settlement, and it is also a confession of the framework's standard, by which 1947 measures as failure for victor and vanquished alike, the state achieved and the nation unachieved. Scholars of the book, including its critics, have noted that this self-lacerating register distinguishes Golwalkar's prose from the triumphalism of electoral Hindutva in later decades: the organism is always ailing in his pages, its glory always a task rather than a possession.

The prescription movement then explains the Sangh to itself. Its chapters on the moulding of men set out the theory of the shakha as a forge: character is formed by daily repetition, by the subordination of preference to programme, by the company of the like-committed, until service ceases to be an act and becomes a disposition. Golwalkar's exposition of the pracharak ideal belongs here, the full-time worker who owns nothing, marries not at all in the classic pattern, and carries the organisation into new territory on a bicycle and a bedding roll, a figure Andersen and Damle's organisational history documents in its human detail. The movement closes the book where the framework begins, with the whole: the nation's regeneration is the sum of regenerated limbs, and no shortcut through state power can substitute for the slow manufacture of the men who will staff a regenerated society. A foundational text that ends by prescribing its own organisation's daily routine is a text whose doctrine and whose instrument are one design.

## Commitment One: Varna as Function, Not Ladder

The first commitment is the framework's social doctrine: varna as functional organisation. In the Virat Purusha passage and across the book's chapters on social order, Golwalkar presents the four varnas as the limbs of the national body, differentiated by the work each performs for the whole: knowledge and counsel, defence and administration, production and exchange, service and labour. He is explicit that the figure forbids hierarchy of worth: the head is not superior to the feet in value, he argues, since honour attaches to function performed in the body's service, and he condemns the degraded caste practices of his own day, untouchability above all, as corruptions of the functional idea by centuries of decay. The argument's structure is what matters for the comparison. Golwalkar does not defend varna as a concession to custom, nor as a stage on the way to a casteless order. He defends it as the organic form of a healthy society, the social expression of the same whole-and-parts logic that defines the nation. Remove the functions and the body does not become equal; it becomes formless, and formlessness, in this framework, is death.

The body analogy converts every social question into a question of function within one life. A varna is an organ, its dignity the dignity of work the whole body needs, its pathology the refusal of that work or the contempt of one organ for another. Hierarchy of worth is therefore a disease in the framework's own terms, while differentiation of task is health itself.

Set Savarkar beside this and the architectural difference is stark. Savarkar treated caste as a fragmenting curse upon Hindu society and prescribed its abolition by intermarriage, interdining and the repudiation of birth-based restriction; his famous seven shackles, listed in his reform writings of the 1930s, bound scripture, touch, food, occupation, sea travel, ritual and marriage, and he demanded each be broken. The stance was rationalist and forward-looking: caste was a man-made error, and men could unmake it. Golwalkar's stance is organicist and backward-looking to a form: caste as practised is a corruption, but varna as conceived is a truth about bodies social, and the cure for corruption is restoration of function rather than abolition of form. Where Savarkar's anti-caste position follows from his membership framework, since a nation defined by a test wants every member identically qualified, Golwalkar's functional position follows from his organism, since a body wants its parts differentiated and harmonised. Each man's caste doctrine is his master concept applied to society, and the master concepts contradict.

Scholarship has not let the functional reading pass unchallenged, and the challenge must be stated at full strength. Aishwary Kumar's Radical Equality of 2015 places Golwalkar at one pole of a trichotomy with Ambedkar and Savarkar, and reads the organic figure as the ideological device by which hierarchy is laundered into harmony: the feet are told their service is honour, Kumar's line of argument runs, by the head that writes the doctrine, and a function assigned at birth is a ladder with the rungs renamed. B.R. Ambedkar's own earlier verdict on the Purusha figure, delivered across his writings on caste from Annihilation of Caste in 1936 onward, supplies the strongest form of the objection: a graded order cannot be detoxified by metaphor, because the metaphor is the mechanism of the grading. Defenders of Golwalkar answer from the text: the book's repeated insistence that no varna is high or low, its praise of the dignity of manual labour, and its fierce condemnation of untouchability as a sin against the national body. The article does not resolve this quarrel, which turns on readings of practice and intention beyond the text's four corners. It records the quarrel because it proves the article's narrower point: the quarrel exists only because Golwalkar's varna doctrine is a different construction from both Savarkar's abolitionism and the scriptural hierarchy it claimed to restore. A continuous tradition would not need the argument.

## The Trichotomy: Ambedkar, Savarkar and Golwalkar

Aishwary Kumar's Radical Equality of 2015 gives the comparison its sharpest scholarly form by setting three doctrines of the Hindu social order in one frame. His trichotomy runs as follows. Ambedkar stands for annihilation: caste is the enemy, its scriptural warrant must be burnt, its endogamous machinery dismantled, and equality is impossible until the order itself is destroyed; the argument of Annihilation of Caste of 1936 admits no reformation of the system, only its end. Savarkar stands for abolition by fusion: caste is a historical error fragmenting the nation, to be dissolved by intermarriage, common dining and a rationalist refusal of its sanctions, so that the Hindu emerges as an undifferentiated national citizen; the equality sought is the equality of the identical. Golwalkar stands for restoration by function: the order is sound in conception and corrupt in practice, its inequalities of worth are the corruption, its differentiation of function is the truth, and equality means the equal honour of unequal tasks inside one body. Kumar's own sympathies lie with the first pole, and his reading of Golwalkar is hostile in the precise sense that it grants the doctrine its coherence and then indicts the coherence: an equality that leaves every man in his father's station, Kumar argues, has renamed subordination as belonging.

The trichotomy clarifies the stakes of the architectural claim beyond the Sangh's internal history. If Golwalkar merely continued Savarkar, the twentieth century's Hindu doctrines of society would number two, an abolitionism and an annihilationism facing each other across a single axis of caste's survival. The functional restoration is a genuinely third position, with its own textual basis in the Purusha figure, its own institutional carrier in the shakha's daily levelling of dress and drill, and its own later political progeny in the Sangh's campaigns for social harmony, the samarasata idiom that Deoras pressed after 1973 and that Sangh literature presents as the framework's social conscience. Critics of that idiom, following Kumar and Ambedkar, read harmony-talk as the demand that the subordinated cease to protest their subordination. Defenders read it as the only idiom in which a society of hereditary occupations has ever been persuaded to honour the labour it despises. The article resolves neither reading. It notes that the argument between them is unintelligible unless Golwalkar's social doctrine is a distinct construction, since both sides are arguing about a position that neither Savarkar nor Ambedkar held.

## Commitment Two: Dharma as the Civilisational Anchor

The second commitment anchors identity in dharma. In the 1939 book, religion appears as one unity among five, coordinated with country, race, culture and language, and the architecture is additive: the nation is the sum of its unities. In the 1966 book the relation changes. Dharma stops being one component in a list and becomes the ground on which the whole stands, the sustaining order that makes the Hindu people one people across their languages, regions and sects. Golwalkar's exposition returns repeatedly to the proposition that the Hindu view of life is integral: the sacred and the social are one continuum, the nation's customs, laws, festivals and philosophies are dharma's expressions in different registers, and a people holds together across millennia because it holds to that order. His biographical formation, the months at Sargachi under Akhandananda and the diksha of January 1937, gave the commitment a personal root that Savarkar's formation could not have supplied. Savarkar was a self-described rationalist who treated religion instrumentally, valuing Hindu identity as civilisational glue while mocking ritual observance; his anchor terms are pitribhumi, the fatherland, and punyabhumi, the holy land, a territorial pair, and his three criteria make holiness a property of geography. For Golwalkar the direction of dependence reverses: the land is holy because dharma has been lived on it, not the people holy because the land is theirs.

### What role does dharma play in Golwalkar's framework?

Dharma is the framework's anchor and solvent at once. As anchor, it fixes Hindu identity in a sustaining order rather than in a territorial test, so belonging flows from participation in that order. As solvent, it dissolves the boundaries of sect and region, since every sect is read as one expression of the order the nation embodies.

The consequences for the framework's treatment of India's other religions are direct, and the article states them without varnish. Buddhism, Jainism and Sikhism, born on Indian soil, are read inside the framework as dharma's own branches, their founders honoured as the nation's teachers, their adherents inside the national body by the framework's logic without any test of conversion. Islam and Christianity, with holy lands outside India, stand in a different relation: the framework does not ask their adherents to pass Savarkar's territorial examination so much as it demands that they be absorbed into the national life the anchor defines, Sanskritised in loyalty, in Golwalkar's phrase patterns, until their separateness dissolves. Critics name this demand absorption under pressure, and the internal-threats chapter applies it. Defenders answer that an anchor in dharma, an order they characterise as inherently plural in expression, is more capacious than an anchor in race or soil, and they point to the framework's honouring of every Indian-born faith as evidence. The dispute is genuine and is marked here as disputed; what is not disputable is the architecture, in which dharma does the work that territory and descent do in the 1923 book.

The anchor also governs the framework's language politics, which the later electoral era would inherit and transform into [the Hindi, Hindutva and Hindostan triad of the Modi period](/2019/08/22/hindi-hindutva-hindostan-modi-question/). In the 1939 book the linguistic unity is Sanskrit, the mother from whom the land's languages descend, and the argument is philological. In the 1966 framework the point is devotional before it is philological: the languages of India are dharma's regional voices, and the national language question is settled by the whole's need for one voice at its head, a role Golwalkar assigned to Hindi while treating Sanskrit as the sacred source. The anti-Hindi agitations that shook Madras State in 1965, a year before the book appeared, form the immediate background against which this commitment reads as it does; the framework answers a linguistic federation with an organism, one body whose many tongues express a single sustaining order.

## Commitment Three: The Whole Before the Individual

The third commitment is the framework's political anthropology: the social whole is prior to the individual, and rights, duties and freedoms arrange themselves accordingly. In Bunch of Thoughts the person appears as a member and a limb before he appears as a bearer of claims; his duties to the nation are the framework's first category, and his flourishing is defined as the excellence of his function inside the whole. Golwalkar's chapters on the moulding of men, the Sangh's own description of its work, extend the doctrine to character: the swayamsevak is manufactured, by daily discipline in the shakha, into an organ conscious of its place. The contrast with the founding settlement of the Indian republic is exact, and it is why this article links Golwalkar's text to [the religion-state founding tension](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) that the Constituent Assembly negotiated between 1946 and 1949. The Assembly's majority, against arguments from several directions, made the individual citizen the Constitution's unit: fundamental rights vest in persons, and even the rights of religious denominations under the religious freedom articles are framed as protections the state owes. The settlement that emerged, [the post-1947 plural-civilisational settlement](/2018/12/28/secularism-constituent-assembly-debates/) on religion and state, thus rests on the opposite anthropology from Golwalkar's, and the friction between the two anthropologies runs under Indian politics from 1950 onward as a permanent structural fact.

### How did Golwalkar's framework treat the individual?

The individual is real inside the framework but derivative: a limb whose life is the body's life, whose dignity is the dignity of his function, and whose claims against the whole have no standing the framework recognises. Duties precede rights in its grammar, and character formed by discipline, not entitlement asserted by claim, is its model of the good citizen.

Golwalkar's verdict on the Constitution follows from the commitment with mechanical consistency, and Bunch of Thoughts states it in sentences that remain the most quoted of his prose after the internal-threats chapter: "Our Constitution too is just a cumbersome and heterogeneous piecing together of various articles from various Constitutions of Western countries. It has absolutely nothing which can be called our own." The complaint is architectural before it is patriotic. A constitution assembled from foreign parts offends the framework because a nation's law, in organic logic, must grow from the nation's own life as dharma's political expression; the Organiser, the Sangh's English weekly, had made the companion argument in its editorial of 30 November 1949, lamenting that the new Constitution held no trace of India's ancient constitutional development and praising the laws of Manu. The companion demand inside Bunch of Thoughts is for a unitary state: its chapter on the subject demands that talk of a federal structure be buried deep for good, that the autonomous states be swept away, and that the country proclaim "One Country, One State, One Legislature, One Executive"; Golwalkar had carried the same demand to the first session of the National Integration Council in 1961, in a written communication arguing that the federal form nourishes separatism and refuses to recognise the fact of one nation. Critics cite these passages as the framework's authoritarian signature. Defenders answer that a unitary prescription inside a democratic argument is a position within politics, held also by centralisers of other parties in the 1950s, and that Golwalkar accepted electoral outcomes he disliked. Both observations belong to the record; the architectural point, which the article adjudicates, is that the unitary demand is the whole-before-individual commitment written as constitutional law, and that Savarkar, whose framework had no comparable constitutional doctrine and whose party contested elections inside the federal order, supplies no precedent for it.

## The Three-Commitment Comparison: Table 1

The argument of the preceding sections is designed to be citable, and the article therefore fixes it in an artifact. Table 1, the three-commitment comparison of Savarkar and Golwalkar, sets the two frameworks side by side on the three registers this study has examined: the primary unit of the framework, the doctrine of social differentiation, and the anchor of identity. Readers citing the architectural-distinction claim should cite Table 1 with its section, since the table is the claim in compressed form.

| Commitment | Savarkar, Essentials of Hindutva (1923) | Golwalkar, Bunch of Thoughts (1966) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary unit | The individual member, admitted by criteria: fatherland, holy land, race, culture | The organic social whole, the Virat Purusha, into which members are born as limbs |
| Social differentiation | Caste as a fragmenting curse; abolition by intermarriage and repudiation of birth-based restriction | Varna as functional organisation of the body; corruption cured by restoring function, never by abolishing form |
| Anchor of identity | Territory and descent: pitribhumi and punyabhumi, land made sacred by faith and fathers | Dharma as civilisational anchor: the sustaining order that makes the people one across sect and region |

Table 1 records divergences, not emphases. On the unit, a test and an organism are different machines: the test sorts persons, the organism assigns functions. On differentiation, abolition and restoration are opposite prescriptions applied to the same social material. On the anchor, a geography that sanctifies and an order that sustains generate different maps of belonging, different verdicts on India's faiths, and different constitutional corollaries, as the unitary-state demand illustrates. A tradition in which a successor reverses his predecessor on the unit, the social doctrine and the anchor, while honouring the predecessor's name, is a tradition of invocation laid over a change of architecture. The next section takes the invocation seriously, because the strongest case against the distinct reading rests on it.

## Invocation Versus Architecture: The Savarkar Complication

The complication must be faced in its strongest form. Golwalkar invokes Savarkar across his work with reverence; Bunch of Thoughts treats the author of Hindutva as a seer of the nation; the Sangh's calendar honours Savarkar's birth anniversary; and Golwalkar's one systematic book of 1939 grew out of a commission from Savarkar's own brother. A reader armed with these facts can argue, as the continuous-tradition reading argues, that Golwalkar understood himself as Savarkar's continuator and that any architectural distinction is a scholar's imposition on a lived unity. The reading gains further strength from the personnel: swayamsevaks moved between the Sangh and the Hindu Mahasabha in the 1930s and 1940s, Savarkar addressed Sangh gatherings, and the two organisations shared a vocabulary of Hindu nationhood long before either framework was fully written.

### Why does Golwalkar praise Savarkar yet rebuild the framework?

Reverence and continuation are different relations. Golwalkar honoured Savarkar as the seer who had named the Hindu nation, and said so in print and on platforms for four decades. The honouring cost nothing, because a framework is not a pedigree: on the unit, the social doctrine and the anchor, Bunch of Thoughts builds otherwise, and the building governs.

The distinction between rhetorical invocation and architectural continuation resolves the complication without denying its facts. Invocation is a relation between persons and organisations: it allocates honour, claims lineage, and reassures a movement that its generations cohere. Architecture is a relation between concepts: it asks what unit a framework counts in, what it prescribes for the society it describes, and where it fixes identity. A successor may maximise the first relation while reversing the second, and Golwalkar did exactly that, for reasons the organisational record makes intelligible. The Sangh of 1940 needed Savarkar's blessing as it needed distance from the Mahasabha's electoral entanglements, and Hedgewar's reported reason for choosing Golwalkar, that the younger man would keep the Sangh independent of the Mahasabha rather than let it become a youth front, shows the distance was policy from the succession's first day. The parallel study of [Savarkar's territorial-cultural framework as architectural antecedent](/2019/09/07/savarkar-hindutva-1923-reanalysed/) sets out the 1923 construction in its own terms; read beside the present study, the two frameworks share a destination-word, Hindu Rashtra, and almost nothing of the machinery.

Two further exhibits support the architectural verdict. The first is the fate of the 1939 book. We or Our Nationhood Defined is the text in which Golwalkar stood closest to Savarkar's method, criteria, tests, the checklist nation; it is also the text the Sangh abandoned in 2006 while canonising the 1966 book in the same gesture. A movement does not usually protect a tradition by amputating the limb that most resembles its founder and elevating the limb that least resembles him. The second exhibit is organisational. Savarkar's framework, articulated from the Mahasabha, presupposed a party that would carry Hindu demands into legislatures; by 1937, presiding over the Mahasabha at Ahmedabad, Savarkar was arguing that Hindus and Muslims were two nations, a political doctrine for a political vehicle. Golwalkar's framework presupposed a cadre order that would transform society over generations while refusing party status for itself, and he held the Sangh to that refusal through the Mahasabha's decline, the Jana Sangh's birth in 1951 and his own lifetime. Frameworks that presuppose opposite instruments, applied by the same movement to the same constituency across the same decades, are not one framework with two accents. They are two answers to the question of what a Hindu nation is and how it is to be made, and the internal-threats chapter shows the difference at its most consequential.

## Internal Threats: The Chapter in Its Architectural Setting

The chapter of Bunch of Thoughts titled Internal Threats opens with a sentence of general theory: "It has been the tragic lesson of the history of many a country in the world that the hostile elements within the country pose a far greater menace to national security than aggressors from outside." The chapter then names three threats in a fixed order: Muslims as the first, Christians as the second, Communists as the third. The chapter stands as Chapter 12 in the book's arrangement; editions paginate the material differently, and the chapter's title and order are the stable facts. On Muslims, the chapter argues that the creation of Pakistan transformed rather than settled the question, warns against the belief that the Muslims who remained in India "have turned patriots overnight after the creation of Pakistan", and describes localities of Muslim concentration as "so many miniature Pakistans" in which the general law of the land runs only with modifications. On Christians, the chapter's charge is directed at missions and conversion: Christians who regard themselves as agents of an international movement for the spread of Christianity, Golwalkar wrote, and who refuse to order their first loyalty to the land of their birth, "will remain here as hostiles and will have to be treated as such". On Communists, the charge is allegiance: an ideology whose centre of loyalty stood outside the country, in Moscow or in Peking, marked its Indian adherents, in the framework's reasoning, as a limb animated by a foreign life.

Behind the chapter's three targets stands its reading of 1947, the post-Partition reflections the brief for this study lists among the passages through which the book is usually read. Golwalkar argues that Pakistan's creation converted a domestic question into a strategic one: the new state across the border would serve, in his phrase, as a springboard for future aggressive designs, and the Muslims who had stayed acquired, in the chapter's darkest inference, a significance out of proportion to their numbers because a hostile frontier gave every internal pocket an external patron. The inference structure is the organic framework's own: an external aggressor can be met at the border, but a disorder is dangerous exactly where the body cannot see it, in the tissue. Critics answer that the inference licensed suspicion of an entire citizenry for a state's conduct across a border, and that no act of loyalty by an Indian Muslim could falsify the charge, since professions of loyalty are themselves quoted in the chapter as evidence of delusion in those who believe them. Defenders answer that the chapter addresses the organised politics of separatism in the shadow of a fresh vivisection, and that its author simultaneously maintained personal relations across the communal line. The article marks the point as disputed and rests its adjudication where it has rested throughout: the chapter's mechanism is organic, its warrant the health of the whole, and that mechanism, not Savarkar's border test, is what the passage's two generations of readers have actually been fighting over.

The architectural reading specifies what kind of statements these are. Inside Savarkar's 1923 framework, Muslims and Christians stand outside the nation because they fail the holy-land criterion, a border verdict delivered once and for all by a definition. Inside Golwalkar's organic framework, the same communities appear as disorders of a body they inhabit: the threat is not foreignness at the border but disloyalty within the tissue, and the framework's remedies follow its metaphor. The body does not negotiate with a disorder; it absorbs what can be absorbed, the merger demand of 1939 restated in 1966 as the demand that minorities lose their separate existence in the national life, and it subordinates what cannot be absorbed, the 1939 prescription of residence without privilege. The continuity of prescription between the two Golwalkar books is real, and the article records it; what changed is the warrant. In 1939 the warrant was a checklist: the excluded failed the unities. In 1966 the warrant is organic: the threatening are those whose corporate life refuses the whole's sustaining order. Jaffrelot's analysis in The Hindu Nationalist Movement and Indian Politics reads the 1966 text within an ideology of ethnic nationalism and gives the chapter its full weight as doctrine; A.G. Noorani's critical writings on the Sangh quote the chapter as the clearest evidence that the doctrine's object is the internal minority rather than the external enemy. Defenders within the Sangh's literature answer that the chapter addresses conduct and allegiance, not persons: the same book honours the Muslim who merges in the national life as it honours every limb, and Rakesh Sinha's 2006 booklet assembled Golwalkar's meetings and statements toward Indian Muslims to argue that the man's practice was courteous where his doctrine was severe. The adjudication this article offers is narrow. The chapter is not an aberration pasted onto a gentler framework; it is the framework's whole-before-individual logic applied to the communities the anchor excludes, and it differs from Savarkar's treatment in mechanism while resembling it in outcome. Resemblance of outcome is what the continuous reading cites; difference of mechanism is what the architectural reading proves; the disagreement dissolves once the two claims are stated at their proper levels.

One fact of reception belongs in this section because it disciplines both. In September 2018, delivering three lectures at Vigyan Bhavan in New Delhi, Sarsanghchalak Mohan Bhagwat stated that Bunch of Thoughts was written for the circumstances of its time, that some of its passages, including its language on Muslims, could not be treated as eternally valid, and that the Sangh had published a curated volume of Golwalkar's writings, His Vision and Mission, as the text it commends. Critics received the statement as a second managed retreat, in the line of 2006; Sangh spokesmen received it as the organisation's standing doctrine that a Sarsanghchalak's words bind their context rather than eternity. Either way, the episode confirms the book's institutional status: no other text of the movement requires periodic official gloss, because no other text is at once so canonical and so contested.

## Beyond the Contested Chapter: Democracy, Language and the Flag

The internal-threats chapter absorbs so much of the book's reception that its other doctrines are often missed, and three of them show the framework working outside the minorities question. The first is democracy. Golwalkar treats the democratic faith of his century with a scepticism that scandalised liberal readers: democracy, he wrote in a sentence the critical literature quotes against him in every generation, is by and large a myth in practice, a mechanism by which the organised few rule in the name of the unorganised many. The scepticism is architectural, not tactical. A framework in which the whole is prior to the individual cannot make the counting of individual preferences the source of political truth; at most it can accept elections as a serviceable mechanism for staffing a state whose legitimacy comes from the nation's life rather than from the count. Critics read the passage as the cadre order's contempt for the voter. Defenders note that Golwalkar never prescribed a coup, accepted the verdicts of elections his side lost, and aimed his scorn at democracy's self-image rather than at its procedures. The dispute is genuine; the doctrine, an organicist demotion of the ballot from sacrament to instrument, is plain in the text.

The second doctrine is language. The 1966 framework assigns Hindi the office of the nation's common speech and Sanskrit the dignity of its sacred source, and it treats linguistic separatism as a disorder of the body on the pattern of the other disorders: a limb asserting itself against the whole. The timing gave the doctrine its edge. The anti-Hindi agitations in Madras State in 1965, in which students died resisting the imposition of Hindi as sole official language, were a year old when the book appeared, and the framework's answer to the agitators is the organism itself: the many tongues are the body's voices, honoured as voices, and denied as sovereignties. The third doctrine concerns the state's symbols. Golwalkar held that the tricolour had been chosen by drifting and imitation, a politician's patchwork adopted for expediency, and that the saffron flag, the Bhagwa Dhwaj under which the Sangh itself salutes its guru, carried the nation's history in its colour and form. The argument matters less for vexillology than for what it reveals about the framework's theory of the state: a state's emblems, like its constitution, must grow from the national life or stand condemned as borrowings, and a republic that borrows its flag, its law and its nationalism from abroad has, in this doctrine, confessed the absence of the very nation it claims to embody. Each of the three doctrines applies the master commitments to a fresh field, which is what a framework, as distinct from a list of prejudices, does.

## The Framework's Silences: Women, Wealth and the Outside World

A framework is known by its silences as well as by its doctrines, and three silences in Bunch of Thoughts repay attention. The first concerns women. The book exalts woman as mother of the nation and guardian of its culture, and assigns her sphere accordingly: the home is her field of discipline, her work the transmission of the sustaining order to the next generation, and the public world of organisation and politics is, in the text's teaching, a male charge. The arrangement had an institutional corollary older than the book: the Rashtra Sevika Samiti, founded at Nagpur in 1936 by Lakshmibai Kelkar with Hedgewar's blessing, organised Hindu women in a body parallel to the Sangh and separate from it, so that the nation's women stand inside the framework's care and outside its cadre form at once. Feminist critics of the movement read the exaltation as confinement with honours. Sangh defenders answer that the framework honours the domestic as a national function rather than a private exile, the whole-before-individual logic applied to gender. The architectural point stands either way: a framework whose unit is the individual member might have recruited women as members, while the organic framework, which assigns functions by the body's design, assigned them a separate organism instead.

The second silence concerns wealth. Bunch of Thoughts contains no political economy: no doctrine of property, of planning, of the market, of labour's share, beyond the anti-socialist negation that D.R. Goyal identified as Golwalkar's innovation and the moralisation of wealth as a trust held for the whole. For a text that aspires to govern a nation's life the omission is startling, and it is architectural in origin: an organism has organs and health, not an economy, and questions that economics asks, about production, distribution and interest, arrive in the framework translated into questions of character, of greed disciplined and duty performed. The Bharatiya Mazdoor Sangh, founded under his authority in 1955, built its labour doctrine in the space the silence left, on class harmony rather than class war; the electoral Hindutva of later decades, needing an economics for manifestos, borrowed from elsewhere entirely. The third silence is the world outside India, which enters the book almost solely as threat or as lesson: Germany as warning and example in the earlier text, the Communist centres as patrons of an internal enemy, Pakistan as springboard. A framework of the national body has no foreign policy, only a hygiene. Each silence is a consequence of the master concept, and each consequence separates the book further from Savarkar, whose framework, built on a membership test and a party, had positions to spare on the reform of women's condition, on economic organisation and on the comity of nations.

## The Organisational Presupposition: A Doctrine That Assumes the Shakha

Every framework presupposes an instrument, and Golwalkar's presupposition is the most fully realised organisational form in modern Indian politics. The shakha, the daily neighbourhood gathering of swayamsevaks for drill, games, prayer and the bauddhik address, is nowhere justified in Bunch of Thoughts, because justification would concede that the instrument is optional. The book addresses men already inside the machine and tells them what the machine is for: the manufacture of national character, limb by limb, until Hindu society's organisation is its politics. The pracharak, the celibate full-time organiser who staffs the machine, appears in the book as the framework's ideal human type, the individual who has most completely subordinated his private life to the whole. The 1949 constitution, adopted under the pressure of the ban, codified the presupposition in law-like form: the Sangh declared itself a cultural organisation, renounced organisational politics, and reserved political action to citizens acting as individuals. Golwalkar defended the settlement for the rest of his life against two opposite pressures, from governments that wished to treat the Sangh as a party in disguise and from swayamsevaks who wished it to become a party in fact.

The presupposition separates the frameworks as cleanly as any doctrine. Savarkar's Essentials presupposes a reader and a party: its natural sequel was the Hindu Mahasabha's programme, resolutions, ministries and elections, the whole apparatus of claim-making inside legislatures. Golwalkar's book presupposes a cadre and a century: its natural sequel was the slow construction of affiliated organisations in labour, education, religion and student life, the family of bodies that Andersen and Damle's 2018 organisational history counts and maps. When swayamsevaks under Golwalkar's own authority built a political vehicle, the Bharatiya Jana Sangh of 1951, the vehicle was kept constitutionally outside the Sangh, led by Syama Prasad Mukherjee precisely because he was not a Sangh officer, and supplied with pracharaks on loan rather than directed from Nagpur. The arrangement puzzled contemporaries and puzzles students still, until the framework explains it: a body does not contest elections, limbs serve; politics is one function among several, assigned to an organ built for the purpose, while the whole keeps its cultural definition. Whether the separation was as clean in practice as in doctrine is disputed, and Andersen and Damle record the many points at which the loan of personnel blurred into direction. The dispute concerns practice; the presupposition itself stands in the text, unargued and everywhere assumed.

The presupposition's institutional progeny under Golwalkar's own hand confirms the reading. The affiliates the Sangh sponsored during his tenure were not improvised responses to opportunity; they replicate the whole in sector after sector, as an organism grows organs. The Akhil Bharatiya Vidyarthi Parishad organised students from 1948 and 1949; Dattopant Thengadi founded the Bharatiya Mazdoor Sangh at Bhopal on 23 July 1955 to carry the framework into labour against the Communist unions the doctrine counted an internal threat; the Vanvasi Kalyan Ashram began work among the forest peoples in 1952; and on 29 August 1964 Golwalkar stood with Swami Chinmayananda and the organiser S.S. Apte at Bombay to found the Vishwa Hindu Parishad, an affiliate for the religious field itself, gathering the sects the dharma anchor claimed as one order. Andersen and Damle's count of the family, some fifty affiliated bodies by the end of the tenure, measures a strategy no membership-test framework would have generated: Savarkar's Mahasabha built a party and auxiliaries for elections, while Golwalkar's Sangh built a society in waiting, sector by sector, on the theory that the nation's regeneration is organisational before it is political.

## The Framework in Command: The RSS After 1966

Publication converted the framework into curriculum. After 1966 the bauddhik sessions of the shakhas and the syllabus of the Officers' Training Camps drew their exposition of nation, society and duty from Bunch of Thoughts; a swayamsevak formed in the 1960s and 1970s learned Hindutva as Golwalkar's organism rather than as Savarkar's test, whatever honour the calendar paid the earlier name. The framework's institutional effects can be traced in the Sangh's conduct across the book's first decade in print. Expansion proceeded by affiliation rather than by merger, new bodies for students, workers, teachers and religious leaders replicating the whole in each sector, an organisational style that is the organic metaphor drawn as an organisation chart. Discipline held through provocation: the Sangh's response to the political storms of the early 1970s, including the agitation against Indira Gandhi's government in which its members participated as citizens, kept the corporate posture Golwalkar had fixed, cultural in charter, political only through its alumni and affiliates.

The framework's last decade in its author's hands ran through the republic's emergencies, and its conduct in each became part of the cadre syllabus. During the war with China in the autumn of 1962, Sangh volunteers performed traffic, relief and blood-donation service in the cities, and the government of Jawaharlal Nehru, no friend of the organisation, invited a Sangh contingent to march in the Republic Day parade at New Delhi on 26 January 1963, an honour Golwalkar's followers cite as the state's certificate of the framework's patriotic discipline. During the war with Pakistan in 1965 the pattern repeated. In November 1966, months after his book appeared, Golwalkar supported the cow-protection mobilisation that marched on Parliament on 7 November 1966 under the Sarvadaliya Goraksha Maha-Abhiyan Samiti, the one mass agitation of his tenure in which the cultural charter bent toward street politics, and an exception that proved the rule by its singularity. Through each episode the corporate posture held: service in war, restraint in peace, agitation almost never. The posture is the presupposition as behaviour, the cultural body declining every invitation to become a crowd.

Golwalkar died on 5 June 1973, and Madhukar Dattatraya Deoras, called Balasaheb, succeeded him as Sarsanghchalak in the same month, the third holder of the office and the first whose entire formation had occurred inside Golwalkar's tenure. The succession itself was a framework event: the office passed by the incumbent line's designation, without election or contest, the organism reproducing its head as the doctrine said a body should. Deoras governed within the framework for twenty-one years, adjusting its emphases, pressing social equality and the eradication of untouchability with a force Golwalkar's text supports in its functional idiom, and steering the Sangh through its second ban during [the Emergency-era RSS ban context](/2019/04/23/emergency-1975-1977-reanalysed/) of 1975 to 1977, when the organisation's underground resistance and Deoras's correspondence with Indira Gandhi tested the cultural charter under maximum pressure. Deoras also gave the framework's social doctrine its most quoted development: in his spring lecture at Pune in 1974 he declared that untouchability must be removed lock, stock and barrel, in a formulation Sangh literature repeats to this day, arguing in terms Golwalkar's functional idiom had prepared, that a body which refuses the service of its own limbs has no claim to health. The declaration's reception split along the lines this article has mapped: the Sangh's defenders cite it as the organic doctrine's conscience in action, its critics as the point at which the doctrine's practice contradicted its text's serenity about hereditary function, and both readings presuppose the framework they dispute. The framework survived its author's death and its second proscription without revision, which is the strongest available evidence that Bunch of Thoughts articulated something the institution actually believed, rather than a founder's idiosyncrasy his heirs politely shelved.

## Departures and Revisions: The Electoral Turn and the Custodians

The departures came from the framework's own success. The political organ the presupposition had kept at arm's length grew, after the Emergency, into the Jana Sangh's successor party founded at New Delhi in April 1980, whose trajectory belongs to a later study in this series; electoral competition rewarded a Hindutva phrased as cultural nationalism for voters rather than as organic doctrine for cadres, and the new party's idiom drifted from Golwalkar's architecture toward Savarkar's simpler boundary-talk, which is easier to print on a poster. The drift is one of the cleaner ironies in the record: the electoral ideology that commentators call Hindutva without qualification descends rhetorically from the 1923 framework, while the organisation that physically sustains the movement continues to be governed by the 1966 one. The tripartite separation this series defends is built to make exactly this irony visible, and invisible without it.

The human bridge between the two architectures was a generation. The men who staffed the political vehicle across its Jana Sangh and early BJP decades, organisers and parliamentarians alike, had been formed as swayamsevaks and pracharaks inside Golwalkar's tenure, learned their Hindutva from the bauddhik syllabus his book supplied, and carried the cadre habits, patience, hierarchy, suspicion of the shortcut, into a party whose public idiom increasingly belonged to the other framework. Atal Bihari Vajpayee, who joined the Sangh as a young man in the 1940s and served as a pracharak before his parliamentary career, and Lal Krishna Advani, who joined as a teenager in Karachi in 1942, are the famous names of a cohort numbered in thousands. The cohort explains a fact the continuous reading cannot: the electoral movement's recurring internal quarrels, between its political leadership and its organisational custodians, follow the seam between the two architectures, and they erupt precisely where the architectures prescribe differently, on the place of doctrine in a campaign, on the subordination of the party to the whole, on whether an election lost for the doctrine is a defeat or a discipline.

The custodians' revisions mark the same line from the other side. The Sangh's handling of its two canonical books across 2006 and 2018 forms a matched pair: the 1939 text abandoned as a young man's translation, the 1966 text retained but historicised, its hardest passages assigned to their context by the sixth Sarsanghchalak at Vigyan Bhavan in September 2018. A.G. Noorani, the Sangh's most persistent scholarly critic, read the sequence as image management by an organisation unwilling to pay the reputational price of its own canon. Sangh intellectuals answered that a living tradition glosses its texts in every generation, and that the gloss has never touched the architecture: neither in 2006 nor in 2018 did any Sangh statement qualify the organic doctrine of the nation, the functional account of society, or the primacy of dharma. The article records both readings and notes what they share: each side treats Bunch of Thoughts as the operative book, the one whose status decides what the movement is. On the identity of the operative text, the movement and its critics agree, and their agreement is a premise of the adjudication that follows.

## The Standard Treatments and Their Limits

The reference literature a searcher meets first treats the book in three established ways, and the architectural reading defines itself against each. The encyclopaedic treatment, represented by the Wikipedia entries on Golwalkar and on the book, proceeds by summary and controversy: a life, a list of positions, a catalogue of the contested passages with the reactions they provoked. The treatment is accurate at the level of the passage and silent at the level of the design; a reader learns what Golwalkar said about Muslims, about the Constitution, about caste, without learning the single construction from which the sayings follow, and the entries' structure, position by position, reproduces the controversial-passages reading this article argues against. The organisational treatment, Andersen and Damle's The RSS: A View to the Inside of 2018, written with unrivalled access to the Sangh's leadership and records, reads the book as the cadre order's charter and is correspondingly strong on what the organisation did with the text: the bauddhik syllabus, the affiliates, the discipline. Its Golwalkar is an institution-builder whose ideas matter as the institution's self-understanding, and its pages record, without theorising, the evidence the architectural reading theorises, the presupposition of the cadre vehicle and the subordination of doctrine's expression to the organisation's long growth. The ideological treatment, Jaffrelot's work of 1996 and its successors, reads the book as the systematisation of an ethnic nationalism inside a continuous tradition, and is correspondingly strong on outcomes and on the minorities question. What none of the three treatments does is set the 1966 book's conceptual machinery beside the 1923 book's, gear by gear; the encyclopaedia has no room for the exercise, the organisational history has no need of it, and the ideological history has a thesis it would complicate. The space the three leave open is the space this study occupies, and the claim it defends there, the architectural distinction, is framed to be checkable against the texts themselves, which is the only court in which the disagreement can finally be settled.

## The Named Disagreement Adjudicated

The continuous-tradition reading, in its strongest scholarly form, is Jaffrelot's. The Hindu Nationalist Movement and Indian Politics of 1996 treats the movement's ideology as a substantially unified ethnic nationalism running from Savarkar through Golwalkar to the electoral era, and supports the treatment with formidable evidence: the shared vocabulary of Hindu nationhood, the 1939 book's dependence on the Savarkar circle, Golwalkar's reverential invocations, and the outcomes on minorities where the two frameworks converge. Koenraad Elst's defence literature adds the argument from intention: Golwalkar, Elst contends, understood his work as commentary within a tradition Savarkar founded, and a man's own understanding of his project deserves evidentiary priority. Against this, the substantively-distinct reading marshals the architecture: unit against unit, doctrine against doctrine, anchor against anchor, as Table 1 fixes them, and adds the two institutional exhibits, the opposite organisational presuppositions and the custodians' divergent treatment of the two books in 2006. Andersen and Damle's organisational history, which is no partisan of the distinct thesis, supplies inadvertent support for it: their Sangh is unintelligible as the Mahasabha's continuation, because its form, the cadre order with a cultural charter, is Golwalkar's presupposition institutionalised, and no reading of the movement that starts from Savarkar's party predicts it.

The verdict follows the evidence at the level where the two readings actually conflict. On outcomes for minorities, on the destination-word Hindu Rashtra, and on the honour paid to Savarkar's name, continuity holds, and the article has recorded the continuity without discount. On the questions the frameworks exist to answer, what the nation is as an entity, how its society is rightly ordered, and where identity is anchored, the 1966 book reverses the 1923 book on every register, and a reversal on every register is a distinction of substance by any definition the word can bear. The organic-social framework is therefore adjudged substantively distinct, and the continuous-tradition reading is adjudged true of the movement's rhetoric and false of its doctrine. Readers who require a single sentence may take the namable claim with which the article opened: Golwalkar's organic-social Hindutva is substantively distinct from Savarkar's territorial-cultural Hindutva; the conceptual architectures differ on every register.

One refinement protects the verdict against a fair objection. Distinctness of architecture does not require hostility between the architects, and the record holds none: the two men met, corresponded and honoured each other across four decades, and Savarkar's visit to Sangh gatherings in Pune in 1940 belongs to the record of mutual regard. Nor does distinctness require that Golwalkar's framework owe Savarkar nothing; debts are plain, the destination-word Hindu Rashtra itself, the insistence that India is an ancient nation rather than a colonial artefact, and the conviction that Hindu society must organise or perish. A framework is distinct not when it shares nothing with its predecessor but when its load-bearing concepts do different work, and the test this article has applied, unit, social doctrine, anchor, instrument, is the test by which frameworks in any tradition are told apart. Judged by descent, Golwalkar is Savarkar's heir. Judged by design, he is the founder of a second doctrine that used the first founder's name as its flag. The series' tripartite separation exists to hold both judgements at once without letting either swallow the other, and Bunch of Thoughts is the exhibit on which the separation stands or falls.

## Why the Architectural Reading Matters

The stakes of the distinction are practical as well as scholarly. Analysts who read the RSS through Savarkar expect a party in waiting and are repeatedly surprised by an organisation that declines, at cost, to behave as one; analysts who read it through Golwalkar's architecture predict the behaviour in advance, the patience across bans, the reproduction by affiliation, the subordination of electoral appetite to organisational health, and the periodic official glosses that protect the framework by retiring its period sentences. The same distinction disciplines the reading of the third ideology in the series' separation: the electoral Hindutva that followed 1980 borrowed Savarkar's boundary idiom for the platform and Golwalkar's cadre machine for the ground, and its internal quarrels, between the movement's organisational custodians and its political leadership, are the sound of two architectures sharing one house. Bunch of Thoughts, read as the charter of the second of those architectures, explains the house's load-bearing walls. Read as a mere sequel to 1923, it explains almost nothing, not even its own table of contents. The book deserves the harder reading, and the harder reading is the one the evidence supports.

Three tests will keep the verdict honest for future readers. First, when the Sangh next revises its canon, watch which book it revises: the custodians' pattern of 2006 and 2018, abandoning the criteria text and glossing the organic one, is the behaviour the distinct reading predicts and the continuous reading cannot explain. Second, when the movement's political wing next quarrels with its organisational wing, locate the quarrel on Table 1: if the dispute maps onto the unit, the social doctrine or the anchor, the architectures are doing causal work in the world and not merely in this article. Third, return to the texts. The distinction defended here is not a loyalty, and it survives only where the passages bear it: a membership test in one book, an organism in the other, caste abolished in one and functionalised in the other, identity anchored in land in one and in a sustaining order in the other. Golwalkar built the second of the three Hindutvas, and he built it differently. The series' remaining studies of the Sangh Parivar take the difference as their working premise, and the electoral ideology that forms the third term of the separation is unintelligible until the second has been given its own architecture, its own evidence and its own name.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is Bunch of Thoughts?

Bunch of Thoughts is the 1966 book that carries the mature doctrine of M.S. Golwalkar, second Sarsanghchalak of the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh. Published by Sahitya Sindhu Prakashana of Bangalore, the volume is a compilation rather than a treatise: Golwalkar's associates collected his speeches, bauddhik addresses, interviews and essays from across his tenure, which had begun in 1940, and arranged the material under thematic headings. Its argument presents the Hindu nation as an organic social whole, unified by dharma, internally differentiated by function, and served by the RSS as its organisational instrument. A second edition added fifteen chapters and a question-and-answer section. The RSS treats the book as its central ideological text, and in September 2018 Sarsanghchalak Mohan Bhagwat stated that some of its passages belonged to their period circumstances rather than to eternity.

### Q: Who was M.S. Golwalkar?

Madhav Sadashiv Golwalkar, known to followers as Guruji, was born on 19 February 1906 at Nagpur and educated in zoology, working as a demonstrator at Banaras Hindu University from 1931 to 1933 before reading law at Nagpur. After a period at the Ramakrishna ashram at Sargachi as a disciple of Swami Akhandananda, he committed himself to the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, ran its All-India Officers' Training Camp from 1937 to 1939, and became Sarkaryavah, its general secretary, in August 1939. He succeeded K.B. Hedgewar as Sarsanghchalak in 1940 and led the organisation until his death on 5 June 1973. He wrote We or Our Nationhood Defined in 1939, and his speeches and writings were compiled as Bunch of Thoughts in 1966.

### Q: How is Golwalkar's Hindutva different from Savarkar's?

The two frameworks differ on three registers. On the unit of analysis, Savarkar's Essentials of Hindutva of 1923 defines the individual member by criteria, a common nation, race and culture bound to a fatherland and a holy land, while Golwalkar's Bunch of Thoughts of 1966 takes the organic social whole, figured as the Virat Purusha, as prior to any member. On social differentiation, Savarkar attacked caste as a fragmenting curse to be abolished, while Golwalkar defended varna as the functional organisation of the national body. On the anchor of identity, Savarkar fixed belonging in territory and descent, pitribhumi and punyabhumi, while Golwalkar anchored it in dharma, the sustaining civilisational order. Golwalkar honoured Savarkar as a seer of the nation, but the architecture he built reverses the earlier framework on each register.

### Q: What did Golwalkar say about Indian Muslims?

In the chapter of Bunch of Thoughts titled Internal Threats, Golwalkar named Muslims the first of three internal threats to the nation. He argued that the creation of Pakistan had increased rather than settled the danger, warned against assuming that Muslims who remained in India had changed their loyalties overnight, and described areas of Muslim concentration as miniature Pakistans where the general law ran only with modifications. The framework's prescription, carried from his 1939 book, was absorption: minorities were to merge in the national life or remain without claims against it. Critics from A.G. Noorani onward cite the chapter as the doctrine's clearest statement. Sangh defenders, among them Rakesh Sinha in his 2006 booklet Shri Guruji and Indian Muslims, answer that Golwalkar addressed allegiance rather than persons and treated individual Muslims courteously in practice.

### Q: What is We or Our Nationhood Defined?

We or Our Nationhood Defined is the short systematic book published under Golwalkar's name at Nagpur by Bharat Publications in 1939, a year before he became Sarsanghchalak. It defines a nation by five unities, geographical, racial, religious, cultural and linguistic, and concludes that only the Hindu people in Hindusthan hold all five. The book prescribes that foreign races either merge in the Hindu nation, adopting its culture, language and religion, or stay wholly subordinated to it, claiming no privileges and not even citizen's rights. It praises the race pride manifested in Germany's purge of the Jews as a lesson for Hindusthan. Golwalkar acknowledged Ganesh Damodar Savarkar's Rashtra Mimansa of 1934 as a chief source in his preface, and the American scholar Jean A. Curran in 1951 called the book the RSS's Bible.

### Q: When was Golwalkar Sarsanghchalak?

Golwalkar became Sarsanghchalak, the supreme chief of the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, in 1940 and held the office until his death on 5 June 1973, a tenure of almost thirty-three years and the longest in the organisation's history. K.B. Hedgewar, the founder, designated him in a written note a day before dying on 21 June 1940, and five provincial sanghchalaks announced the succession at Nagpur on 3 July 1940. As the second holder of the office, Golwalkar led the RSS through the ban of 1948 and 1949, the adoption of its written constitution, its expansion into a federation of affiliated organisations, and the publication of Bunch of Thoughts in 1966. Madhukar Dattatraya Deoras, called Balasaheb Deoras, succeeded him in June 1973.

### Q: What is the organic-social framework in Hindutva?

The organic-social framework is the conceptual architecture of Golwalkar's Bunch of Thoughts: the doctrine that the Hindu nation is a living social organism rather than a collection of individuals who pass a membership test. Its figure is the Virat Purusha, the cosmic being whose limbs are the society's functional orders, and its three commitments follow from the figure. The whole is prior to the individual, whose dignity is the dignity of his function. Social differentiation is functional, varna read as the body's division of labour rather than as a ladder of worth. Identity is anchored in dharma, the sustaining order that makes the people one across sect, region and language. The framework is what distinguishes Golwalkar's Hindutva from Savarkar's territorial-cultural framework, which defines the Hindu by criteria of fatherland, holy land, race and culture.

### Q: When and where was Bunch of Thoughts first published?

Bunch of Thoughts was first published in 1966 by Sahitya Sindhu Prakashana of Bangalore, the publishing house maintained by sympathisers of the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh in southern India. The first edition was issued around Golwalkar's sixtieth birthday, in February 1966, marking a quarter century since he had become Sarsanghchalak in 1940. The book gathered speeches, interviews and essays from across his tenure rather than presenting a text composed at one time, and its compiler is not named on the title page. A second edition enlarged the volume by fifteen chapters and added forty-five pages of questions and answers, so citations of the book properly name the edition used. The Bangalore house has kept the book in print through successive reprints, including the widely cited edition of 1996 and the reprint of 2000.

### Q: How does Bunch of Thoughts describe varna?

Bunch of Thoughts describes varna through the figure of the Virat Purusha, the cosmic social body: the Brahmin as the head, the Kshatriya as the hands, the Vaishya as the thighs and the Shudra as the feet, a passage Golwalkar calls the very core of his concept of nation. The description is functional: each varna is an organ performing work the whole body needs, and Golwalkar insists that the figure carries no hierarchy of worth, since a body that despises its feet cannot walk. He condemns untouchability and birth-based contempt as corruptions of the functional order. Critics, in a line running from B.R. Ambedkar to Aishwary Kumar's Radical Equality of 2015, answer that a function assigned at birth is hierarchy under another name, and that the metaphor launders grading into harmony. The dispute turns on whether function can be separated from birth in the doctrine's practice.

### Q: Which groups does the Internal Threats chapter of Bunch of Thoughts name?

The Internal Threats chapter of Bunch of Thoughts names three groups in a fixed order. Muslims stand first: the chapter argues that Pakistan's creation sharpened the threat, treats areas of Muslim concentration as miniature Pakistans, and doubts professions of overnight loyalty. Christians stand second: the charge falls on missions and conversion, and Christians who act as agents of an international movement and refuse first loyalty to the land of their birth are described as hostiles who will have to be treated as such. Communists stand third: their offence, in the chapter's reasoning, is an allegiance centred outside the country. The chapter opens with the general proposition that hostile elements within a country pose a far greater menace to national security than aggressors from outside, and each named group is analysed as a corporate loyalty refusing the national whole.

### Q: Why was We or Our Nationhood Defined removed from the RSS canon?

The removal happened in stages. In a speech of 1963 Golwalkar himself stated that the 1939 book had been an abridged translation of Ganesh Damodar Savarkar's Rashtra Mimansa, although his preface of 1939 had described that work as a source of inspiration and help; comparative scholarship has since judged the English book Golwalkar's own composition, with its German passages his addition. In February 2006 the RSS formalised the distance: Rakesh Sinha's booklet Shri Guruji and Indian Muslims, released at Nagpur on 24 February 2006, argued that the book carried a young organiser's rendering of another man's views, and Sangh spokesmen stated that it did not represent the views of the grown Guruji or of the RSS. M.G. Vaidya explained the replacement at the same time: Bunch of Thoughts is the central book because it holds Golwalkar's views after he became Sarsanghchalak.

### Q: What did Golwalkar write about Germany in We or Our Nationhood Defined?

In We or Our Nationhood Defined of 1939, Golwalkar treated Germany as an instructive example of race pride. Of the Nazi purge of the Jews he wrote that Germany had shocked the world by purging the country of the Semitic races, and that race pride at its highest had been manifested there. He concluded that Germany had shown how well-nigh impossible it is for races and cultures with differences going to the root to be assimilated into one united whole, calling this a good lesson for Hindusthan to learn and profit by. The passages were written before the Second World War began and were retained through the book's four wartime editions. Defenders contextualise them within the racial vocabulary of 1930s political writing and within the book's debt to Rashtra Mimansa. Critics, citing the comparative scholarship that assigns the passages to Golwalkar himself, treat them as the clearest window on the 1939 doctrine.

### Q: What was Golwalkar's view of the Indian Constitution?

Golwalkar's verdict on the Constitution of India stands in Bunch of Thoughts in his most quoted sentences on the subject: the Constitution, he wrote, is just a cumbersome and heterogeneous piecing together of various articles from various Constitutions of Western countries, and it has absolutely nothing which can be called our own. He complained that its guiding principles contain no reference to the nation's mission or keynote in life. The verdict follows his framework: law, in organic logic, must grow from the nation's own life rather than be assembled from foreign parts. The Sangh's weekly Organiser had argued similarly on 30 November 1949, lamenting the absence of India's ancient constitutional development from the document. Bunch of Thoughts adds a structural demand, a unitary state, proclaiming one country, one state, one legislature and one executive in place of the federal structure.

### Q: How did Bunch of Thoughts shape RSS training after 1966?

After its publication in 1966, Bunch of Thoughts became the textual basis of ideological instruction inside the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh. The bauddhik, the ideological discussion that closes the daily shakha, and the syllabus of the Officers' Training Camps drew their expositions of the nation, of society and of the swayamsevak's duty from the book's chapters, so that cadres formed in the late 1960s and the 1970s learned Hindutva in Golwalkar's organic idiom rather than in Savarkar's criteria. The book's question-and-answer material, enlarged in the second edition, served the same instructional use, supplying authorised answers to the objections a pracharak met on tour. Under Balasaheb Deoras, Sarsanghchalak from 1973, the curriculum held: the framework Bunch of Thoughts articulates governed cadre formation through the Emergency years and beyond, which is one reason scholars treat the book as an operating charter rather than a ceremonial classic.

### Q: Who succeeded Golwalkar as Sarsanghchalak?

Madhukar Dattatraya Deoras, universally called Balasaheb Deoras, succeeded Golwalkar as Sarsanghchalak of the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh in June 1973, after Golwalkar's death on 5 June of that year. Deoras was the third holder of the office, after K.B. Hedgewar and Golwalkar, and the first whose entire organisational formation had taken place inside Golwalkar's tenure; in his younger years he had himself recorded the senior leaders' early scepticism about Golwalkar's capacity for the office. He governed for twenty-one years, until 1994, steering the Sangh through the ban of the Emergency years from 1975 to 1977, conducting a celebrated correspondence with Indira Gandhi from prison and in its aftermath, and pressing the eradication of untouchability and the cause of social equality with a force he grounded in Golwalkar's functional reading of Hindu society.

### Q: How does Christophe Jaffrelot read Golwalkar's ideology?

Christophe Jaffrelot, in The Hindu Nationalist Movement and Indian Politics of 1996, reads Golwalkar as the systematiser of an ethnic nationalism: a doctrine that defines the nation by descent, religion and culture, treats minorities as an internal enemy formation, and builds the cadre organisation as the instrument of a long cultural transformation of society. His chapters on Golwalkar give full weight to the Internal Threats chapter and to the 1939 book, and they place Golwalkar inside a continuous tradition running from Savarkar to the electoral movement of the 1980s and 1990s, a tradition whose strategy Jaffrelot analyses through the pairing of stigmatisation and emulation of the threatening other. The reading advanced in the study to which these questions belong accepts Jaffrelot's evidence on outcomes while disputing the continuity at the level of architecture, where the two frameworks reverse each other on the unit, the social doctrine and the anchor of identity.

### Q: What did Golwalkar say about Communists in Bunch of Thoughts?

In the Internal Threats chapter of Bunch of Thoughts, Golwalkar placed Communists third among the nation's internal enemies, after Muslims and Christians. The charge is allegiance: an Indian Communist, in his reasoning, owed his doctrine's loyalty to centres outside the country, to Moscow or to Peking, and an organisation animated by a foreign centre could not be a limb of the national body however Indian its members were by birth. The judgement fitted the wider anti-communist and anti-socialist doctrine he built into the Sangh's teaching across his tenure, summarised in the slogan attributed to his leadership, not socialism but Hinduism. The historian D.R. Goyal observed that this anti-Marxist orientation won the Sangh generous support from propertied sections of society. Defenders of the chapter reply that its target was a party structure taking direction from abroad during the years of the Sino-Soviet split, not an economic opinion held by an Indian citizen.

### Q: Did Golwalkar believe the RSS should enter electoral politics?

Golwalkar held, across his entire tenure from 1940 to 1973, that the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh should not enter electoral politics as an organisation. The position was codified in the Sangh's written constitution of 1949, adopted as the ban of 1948 was lifted, which committed the RSS to cultural work and barred organisational politics. Political action belonged, in his design, to citizens and to separate vehicles: when the Bharatiya Jana Sangh was founded in 1951 under Syama Prasad Mukherjee, Golwalkar supplied it with pracharaks on loan while keeping the Sangh itself outside the party's command, and he resisted pressure from his own ranks to convert the Sangh into a party. Scholars dispute how clean the separation was in practice, since loaned personnel carried the parent body's discipline with them. The doctrine itself is unambiguous in his writings and in the constitution he accepted.

### Q: Is the Hindu Rashtra of Bunch of Thoughts a theocratic state?

Golwalkar and his defenders have consistently denied that the Hindu Rashtra of Bunch of Thoughts is a theocratic state. Their argument is that dharma is not a church: Hindu tradition has no single book, creed or priesthood empowered to rule, so a state expressing the Hindu nation's character cannot be a theocracy in the European or Islamic sense, and Golwalkar's subject is in any case the nation, a society, rather than a design for government machinery. Critics answer that the label is beside the point. A framework that prescribes absorption or subordination for the country's largest religious minorities, rejects the federal Constitution as foreign, and defines belonging by one civilisation's sustaining order fixes the state's confessional character whatever name it takes, in the critics' reading. The dispute is recorded in the scholarship as unresolved; the text's prescriptions themselves, quoted in the study these questions accompany, are not in dispute.

### Q: How did Golwalkar become Sarsanghchalak in 1940?

Golwalkar became Sarsanghchalak through designation by the founder. K.B. Hedgewar had marked him out in stages: charge of the All-India Officers' Training Camp from 1937 to 1939, the commission to render Rashtra Mimansa into English and Hindi, which produced We or Our Nationhood Defined in 1939, and appointment as Sarkaryavah, general secretary, on 13 August 1939. A day before his death on 21 June 1940, Hedgewar handed Golwalkar a sheet of paper asking him to shoulder the responsibility of the Sangh, and on 3 July 1940 five provincial sanghchalaks announced the decision at Nagpur. The choice passed over senior activists and, by Balasaheb Deoras's later account, several RSS leaders doubted the younger man's capacity. Hedgewar's reported reason was independence: Golwalkar could be trusted to keep the Sangh from becoming a youth front of the Hindu Mahasabha.
