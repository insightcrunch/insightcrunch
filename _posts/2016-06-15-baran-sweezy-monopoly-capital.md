The monopoly capital school is a tradition of American Marxist economics that formed around a journal, Monthly Review, and around a book, Monopoly Capital, published in 1966 by Paul A. Baran and Paul M. Sweezy. A reader arriving from a search about corporate power or about stagnation should grasp one thing before anything else, because everything in the school follows from it. The school's decisive act was not a new policy, a new prediction, or a new history. It was a change in the basic category of analysis. Where the Marxist tradition it inherited reasoned in terms of surplus value, a magnitude defined through labour time, Baran and Sweezy reasoned in terms of the economic surplus, a magnitude defined as the difference between what a society produces and the cost of producing it. The substitution looks like a technicality. It is the whole school.

Changing the category changed what the theory could see and what it could no longer say. A category defined without reference to labour values can be estimated, however roughly, from published accounts, and a school armed with it can argue about the size of the surplus, its growth, and its uses with evidence in hand. But a category that no longer passes through the labour process carries no built-in account of where the surplus originates, and critics inside Marxist economics were quick to press exactly there. The quarrel that followed, conducted principally in the nineteen seventies, is the reason the school's standing among Marxist economists is contested to a degree that surprises readers who know only its influence outside that circle. This article treats the substitution itself as the subject: the two founders and their separate books, the definition and its variants, the defence the school offered, the criticism at full strength, and the later tradition that grew from the journal, including the school's account of monopoly and finance combined in the phase that followed the joint book.

## The monopoly capital school in one view

A school, in the usage this series follows, is a durable company of writers who share a category, an institution, and a set of problems, and who transmit all three to successors. By that test the monopoly capital school qualifies more completely than most traditions that carry the label. Its institution was Monthly Review, founded in 1949 and edited by Sweezy, with Leo Huberman, for the rest of their working lives, and continued after them. Its shared problem was the character of advanced capitalism once the giant corporation had displaced the competitive firm as the economy's typical unit. Its shared category was the economic surplus, and the sharing was deliberate: Baran had built his book on underdevelopment around that category in 1957, and the joint work of 1966 rebuilt the theory of the advanced economy on the same foundation. Whoever reads the school through its category reads it as its members intended.

### What is the monopoly capital school?

A tradition of American Marxist economics built around the journal Monthly Review and the work of Paul Baran and Paul Sweezy, which analyses advanced capitalism through the category of economic surplus rather than surplus value, and takes the giant corporation, not the competitive firm, as its typical unit.

The map of the school has three storeys, and the article that follows climbs them in order. On the ground floor stand the two founders' separate achievements, each larger than the collaboration for which the pair is remembered: Sweezy's treatise of 1942, which served for decades as the standard English-language introduction to Marxist economics and carried the transformation problem to anglophone readers; and Baran's growth book of 1957, which founded the study of underdevelopment as a system rather than a stage and stands at the head of the dependency tradition. On the middle floor stands the joint construction: the surplus defined, its tendency to rise as monopoly spreads, and the problem of its absorption, the last of these being the school's crisis argument and the property of a companion article. On the top floor stands the tradition after the founders: the journal's work on imperialism at the end of the nineteen sixties, the long collaboration between Sweezy and Harry Magdoff, and the account of monopoly and finance combined that the school's successors built when finance grew too large for the older picture.

Two disciplines govern the telling. First, the neighbouring territories are linked rather than reoccupied: the stagnation thesis and the machinery of absorption belong to the series article on [monopoly capital stagnation theory](/2014/02/01/monopoly-capital-stagnation-theory/), and dependency theory belongs to the article on [dependency theory explained](/2016/07/01/dependency-theory-explained/), which traces the lineage from Baran's growth book in full. Second, the category that this school replaced is itself treated at length elsewhere, in the [surplus value complete guide](/2010/11/01/surplus-value-complete-guide/), and is linked here whenever the argument turns on what was given up. The present article keeps for itself the school, the substitution, and the dispute, since those are the parts of the record that general coverage most often flattens into a single book and a single thesis.

## A school formed around a journal

Monthly Review began publication in May 1949, edited by Leo Huberman, a popular writer on economics whose books had taught a generation of lay readers, and by Sweezy, who supplied the theoretical weight. The founding was an institutional decision before it was an editorial one. The American universities of the period were not open territory for Marxist economics: Sweezy had already resigned his Harvard assistant professorship after the war rather than wait out a tenure decision he had concluded was foreclosed, and Baran, though he held a chair at Stanford, worked at a distance from any community of like-minded economists. A journal owned by its editors answered to no appointments committee, no dean, and no advertiser, and it could hold a school together across a continent. Its first issue carried Albert Einstein's essay on why he was a socialist, an acquisition that announced the journal's ambitions and its reach more plainly than any manifesto could have done.

### Why did the school organise itself around a journal?

Because its founders stood outside the universities that might have housed the work: one had resigned a Harvard post in the certainty of being denied tenure, and the other, though tenured, worked in isolation. A journal they owned gave the school an institution no appointment committee could withdraw.

The journal was joined in 1952 by Monthly Review Press, and the pairing of periodical and press mattered to the school's method. Books that no commercial publisher would risk and no university press would sponsor, including Baran's growth book and the joint work of 1966, appeared under the school's own imprint, while the journal carried the argument forward month by month in essays, exchanges, and reviews. When later sections of this article describe the school's decline and revival, the institutional fact should be kept in view: a tradition lodged in departments dies when the departments close ranks, whereas a tradition lodged in a journal and a press can survive decades of professional cold weather on subscriptions and stubbornness. The school outlived both founders, which is the practical proof that the institution, and not merely the two men, was the school's skeleton.

## Paul Baran: the outsider with a chair

Paul Alexander Baran was born in 1909 in Nikolaev, in the Russian Empire, and was educated in Moscow and in Germany, where he moved in the orbit of the Frankfurt Institute for Social Research before emigrating to the United States in 1939. He took a master's degree at Harvard, served in wartime government work, and spent time at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York before joining Stanford in 1949, where he was promoted to full professor in 1951 and taught until his death from a heart attack in March 1964. In the economics profession of his adopted country he occupied a position without parallel: he was widely described as the only tenured Marxist economist at a major American university, a distinction that brought students to Palo Alto from across the world and brought him a corresponding measure of public attack, which a later section records in its place.

### What did Baran bring that Sweezy did not?

A formation in the economics of Russia, Germany, and Poland, wartime government service, research at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, and above all the surplus concept in its development form: his growth book had already distinguished the surplus a poor economy actually generates from the larger surplus it could generate.

Baran's formation gave the school its outward face. Where Sweezy's questions were formed by the American economy and its trusts, Baran's were formed by the backward economies of Europe's east and by the colonial world, and his early reputation rested on an essay on the political economy of backwardness published in the Manchester School in 1952, in which the surplus framework first appeared in print. His intellectual manner differed from his partner's as well. Sweezy wrote as an expositor even when he was innovating, patient with the reader and scrupulous with opponents. Baran wrote as a polemicist of systems, impatient with piecemeal reform and drawn to the largest units of analysis, whole social orders and their historical fates. The joint book bears both signatures: its architecture is Sweezy's, much of its reach and severity is Baran's. The partnership worked because each man supplied the discipline the other lacked, and any account that treats the two founders as interchangeable misses the division of labour that made the school.


## Paul Sweezy: the insider who walked out

Paul Marlor Sweezy was born in New York City in 1910, the son of a vice president of the First National Bank, and was educated at Phillips Exeter Academy and Harvard, where he edited the Harvard Crimson and graduated in 1932. A year at the London School of Economics converted him to Marxism, and he returned to Harvard for doctoral work, taking his degree in 1937 with a dissertation on monopoly and competition in the English coal trade across two and a half centuries, published as a book the following year. He taught at Harvard as an assistant professor, served during the war in the research and analysis branch of the Office of Strategic Services, and came home in 1945 to a department in which, by his own account, agreement on granting him tenure was unattainable. He resigned, settled in New Hampshire, and for the remaining fifty-eight years of his life he held no regular academic post, an exile from the profession he had been trained to lead that shaped everything the school became. He died in 2004, having edited the journal he co-founded for fifty-five years.

### What did Sweezy bring that Baran did not?

The interpreter's craft and the American materials: a Harvard training in the orthodox literature, a dissertation on monopoly in the English coal trade, the treatise that taught two generations of English readers their Marxist economics, and an editor's patience that kept a journal alive for over half a century.

Sweezy's authority inside the school rested on a double competence that few Marxist economists of his generation matched. He knew the classical and Marxian texts closely enough to expound them line by line, and he knew the orthodox economics of his own time from inside its best department, having been formed by the Harvard of Schumpeter, with whom he maintained a celebrated intellectual friendship across every political difference. The combination made him the tradition's interpreter to the English-speaking world: when economists outside the Marxist circle wanted to know what the theory said, it was Sweezy's formulations they met. John Kenneth Galbraith, no ally of the school's conclusions, called him the most noted American Marxist scholar of the century's second half, and a Wall Street newspaper once named him the dean of radical economists, tributes that register the standing from which the category substitution was later launched. The substitution carried weight precisely because it came from the man who had taught the orthodox categories to everyone else.

Baran and Sweezy met within the small world of American Marxist scholarship before the war, and their collaboration deepened through the nineteen fifties as the journal gave them a common workshop. The division of labour between them was real but never rigid: Sweezy's hand is visible in the analytical chapters of the joint book, Baran's in its treatment of the underdeveloped world and in the surplus framework itself, which had been Baran's invention for a different purpose. Readers of the school should therefore resist the habit, common in summaries, of crediting the pair with a single mind. The school's two founding books were written a decade apart, on different continents of the subject, by men whose tempers and trainings differed sharply; the joint book is a confluence, and confluences are explained by the rivers, not by the delta.

## The world the school addressed: the giant corporation

Every school of economics quietly elects a typical unit, the firm or household whose behaviour the theory treats as normal, and the monopoly capital school announced its election in its name. The typical unit of the advanced economy, on its account, is the giant corporation: a firm large enough to administer the prices it charges rather than accept prices from a market, diversified across products and borders, financed substantially from its own retained earnings, and managed by a salaried hierarchy distinct from its legal owners. The separation of ownership from control in such firms had been documented for American scholarship a generation earlier, in the study of the modern corporation published by Adolf Berle and Gardiner Means in 1932, and the school took the finding as a premise rather than a discovery. What followed from the premise was a different mechanics of competition. Giants do not compete by undercutting one another's prices, a weapon each knows would injure all; they compete through product differentiation, advertising, salesmanship, and the race to introduce new lines, while price itself is set by markup over cost according to the firm's judgement of what its market will bear.

### Why does the school begin from the giant corporation?

Because its whole analysis hangs on how prices are made: where a handful of giant firms administer prices and avoid price competition, the mechanism that was supposed to discipline costs and distribute the social product in the older theory has been replaced by discretion, and the theory must start from that discretion.

The election of the unit was also an election of evidence. A theory centred on the giant corporation can be argued from the public record of corporate behaviour: annual reports, concentration in industry after industry, the visible apparatus of the sales effort, the internal financing of investment. A theory centred on labour values must argue from magnitudes that no statistical office publishes. The school's preference for the first kind of evidence was not an accident of taste; it was the methodological commitment that later produced the category substitution, and the two choices should be understood as one choice made twice. When critics charged that the school had traded theory for bookkeeping, the school could reply, and did, that a theory of an economy run by giant corporations must begin from the magnitudes those corporations themselves generate and report. Whether that reply is sufficient is the dispute at the article's centre, and it cannot be judged until the category itself has been stated exactly.

## The Theory of Capitalist Development and the interpreter's craft

Sweezy's treatise of 1942, The Theory of Capitalist Development, began as doctoral work and became something else in the writing: a systematic exposition of Marxist economics, principle by principle, that assumed no prior allegiance in its reader and conceded difficulties where it found them. Its chapters worked through the labour theory of value, the falling tendency of the rate of profit, crisis theories in their rival forms, and the economics of imperialism, and it closed with an account of fascism and the prospects of socialism. For decades it was the book from which English-speaking students, including many who became the school's critics, learned the subject, and its presence on reading lists across the anglophone world gave Sweezy a pedagogical authority that no other American Marxist possessed. The point matters for the school's later history: the man who would propose abandoning the value category in 1966 was the man who had explained it, more clearly than anyone writing in English, in 1942.

### How did the transformation problem reach English readers?

Through Sweezy twice over: his treatise expounded the objection raised by Bortkiewicz and the attempted solutions in a chapter English readers could follow, and his edited volume of 1949 placed the classic German texts on the problem, by Böhm-Bawerk, Hilferding, and Bortkiewicz himself, before an anglophone audience in translation.

The transformation problem, the difficulty of reconciling prices of production with labour values that runs through this cluster of the series and is treated in full in the article on [marxist economics the complete guide](/2016/04/01/marxist-economics-complete-guide/), entered the school's bloodstream through that early work. Sweezy never hid the problem, and his exposition conceded more to Bortkiewicz than orthodox readers liked. A line runs from the young expositor's candour about the weakest joint in the value theory to the older theorist's willingness to rebuild without it: the substitution of 1966 was not a sudden apostasy but the end of a long dissatisfaction, documented in print for a quarter of a century. Critics of the school sometimes write as though the value theory was dropped thoughtlessly. The record shows the opposite, a considered judgement by the economist who knew the theory's difficulties most intimately, which is precisely why the school's defence of the substitution, when it came, took the form it did: not that value theory was false in its own competitive world, but that its world had been superseded by the world of the giant corporation.

## The Political Economy of Growth: the surplus turned toward the poor countries

Baran's book of 1957 was addressed to a question the advanced economies did not ask about themselves: why do poor countries stay poor? Its answer turned on the surplus. Every society, Baran argued, produces more than it consumes in the course of staying alive, and the size, form, and use of that excess govern its possibilities. He distinguished the actual economic surplus, the difference between a society's actual output and its actual consumption, from the potential economic surplus, the difference between the output the society could produce with its resources rationally used and the consumption that could reasonably be counted essential. In the underdeveloped world the potential surplus was large and the actual surplus small, and the gap between them was not a technical accident. Land lay idle or grew export crops for distant tables; labour stood unemployed or was spent on retainers, guards, and luxury services; capital fled abroad or was frozen in speculation and display. The waste was organised by a social order: an alliance between domestic propertied classes, whose incomes depended on the existing arrangement, and foreign capital, whose claims on the surplus drained it outward, blocked every road by which the potential surplus might have been mobilised for development.

### What did Baran mean by potential surplus?

The output a society could achieve if its land, labour, and equipment were fully and rationally employed, minus the consumption reasonably counted as essential: a standard against actual performance, designed to expose how much of a poor economy's poverty was produced by its social order rather than its resources.

The book's influence ran far beyond its first readership. It treated underdevelopment not as an original condition awaiting the arrival of capital and technique, but as a product, actively manufactured by the same world economy that had developed the wealthy countries, and in that move it founded the dependency tradition in English-language scholarship. André Gunder Frank, whose studies of Latin America carried the argument to a wide audience in the following decade, acknowledged the descent openly, and the lineage from Baran's surplus to the dependency school's account of metropolis and satellite is traced in the series article on dependency theory, already linked above. For the school's own development, the growth book mattered in a second way: it proved the surplus category could organise a whole field of inquiry. What Baran had done for the poor countries with actual and potential surplus, the joint book would attempt for the rich ones, adding the problem that the rich countries' surplus raised not by its smallness but by its size.

## The joint book and its completion

Monopoly Capital appeared in 1966 under both names, with the subtitle An Essay on the American Economic and Social Order, but the book the world received was a survivor. Baran died in March 1964 with the manuscript unfinished, and Sweezy completed, arranged, and saw through the press the work the two had planned and partly drafted together, a completion he described in his preface with characteristic plainness. The book's argument, in outline, ran as follows. Monopoly, meaning the displacement of price competition by the administered pricing of giant corporations, changes the laws of motion of the system: the surplus tends to rise, both absolutely and as a share of output, because costs fall with technical progress while prices are held. A rising surplus poses the system a problem of absorption, since the normal outlets, the consumption of the owners and new investment, cannot be relied on to take up a swelling excess, investment itself being governed by the demand it is supposed to create. The book then surveyed the counteracting outlets, above all the sales effort and the state, civilian and military, and drew from the whole a picture of stagnation as the normal state of the monopoly economy and of irrationality as its everyday texture. The crisis machinery of that argument belongs, as noted, to the companion article; what belongs here is the foundation on which the machinery stands.

### How was Monopoly Capital finished after Baran's death?

Sweezy took the jointly planned and partly drafted manuscript, completed the unfinished portions in the idiom the two had agreed in years of collaboration, and prepared the whole for publication by the school's own press in 1966, so that the book appeared as the partnership's work although its senior partner had died two years earlier.

The foundation was the category. The joint book announced, early and without embarrassment, that it would work with the concept of economic surplus rather than with surplus value, presenting the choice as a simplification suited to its subject: the giant corporation does not keep its books in labour values, and an analysis of the American economic order should take its magnitudes from that order's own accounting. Reviewers friendly and hostile alike registered the substitution, though the argument about it ripened slowly. The book was taken up first as a theory of stagnation and of militarism, and its sales carried Monthly Review Press through years when the journal alone might not have sufficed. Only later, when the value tradition within Marxist economics reasserted itself in the nineteen seventies, did the category become the explicit bone of contention, and by then the school had a decade of work built on the foundation the critics proposed to dig up. The article now turns to the definition itself, since every subsequent section depends on its exact shape.

## The economic surplus defined

The definition must be given the way the school's own policy requires, in words and by procedure, since the brief of this cluster forbids formulas and the school itself presented its category verbally. Take a society's total output for a year, valued in money as the national accounts value it. Subtract the cost of producing that output: the wages of the labour employed, the materials consumed, the wear of the equipment used up, the whole bill of inputs that production itself necessitated. What remains is the economic surplus, the fund out of which property incomes are paid, the sales apparatus is maintained, the state is financed, and new investment is made. Nothing in the operation mentions labour time as a measure. The magnitudes are the ones accountants and statistical offices handle, and that is the point of them.

### How does economic surplus differ from surplus value?

Surplus value is defined in labour terms as the value produced beyond the value of labour power. Economic surplus is defined as the difference between what a society produces and the cost of producing it, without reference to labour values, which makes it measurable but detaches it from the theory of value.

The school worked with three forms of the category, and the distinctions among them carry much of the analysis. The actual surplus is the excess a society's output shows over its consumption as things stand, the fund visibly at the disposal of owners and states. The potential surplus, Baran's invention from the growth book, measures the output forgone through idle capacity, unemployed labour, unproductive employment, and irrational organisation, against the consumption a rational order would treat as essential. The planned surplus belongs to the school's writings on socialist construction: the excess a planned economy consciously designates for accumulation and collective purposes, freed from the forms waste takes under private ownership. A single thread runs through the three forms. Each is a social judgement rendered as a magnitude: the definition requires someone to decide which activities count as costs of production and which count as uses of the surplus, and the school never pretended that decision was mechanical. Advertising executives, financiers, and soldiers appear in its accounting as absorbers of surplus rather than as producers whose pay is a cost, a classification that states a theory of the social order in the guise of a bookkeeping rule.

### What separates actual surplus from potential surplus?

Actual surplus is measured on the economy as it runs, with its idle plants and occupied armies of salespeople taken as given; potential surplus is measured against the economy as it could run, with resources fully employed and only essential consumption deducted, so the second magnitude indicts the first one's organisation.

The definitional character of the category deserves emphasis, because friend and critic alike have mishandled it. The surplus is not offered as a physical heap, a pile of goods left over when production ends. It is an accounting construction built from the flow of output and the flow of costs, and its size moves with the classification rules the constructor adopts. Baran was explicit that the concept of potential surplus in particular embodied a standard of rationality, which is to say a judgement about what a society is for. That candour cuts both ways, as the critical sections below will show. It arms the school against the charge of false precision, since no estimate was ever presented as more than the working out of stated conventions. It also hands the critic a lever, since a magnitude whose boundaries are set by judgement cannot claim the objectivity of a measurement like temperature. The school accepted both consequences and wrote accordingly, giving procedures rather than constants, and this article follows its sources in stating no surplus estimate, concentration ratio, or markup figure as fact.

## The category substitution table

The substitution can be exhibited whole in a single instrument, and the instrument is the working centre of this article. The category substitution table arrays the two magnitudes the school moved between, surplus value as the inherited theory defines it and economic surplus as the school redefined it, across the questions a basic category must answer: how it is defined, what it can measure, what it says about where the excess originates, what it says about how the excess is absorbed, and what lies beyond its reach. Read down either column and a complete method appears; read across any row and the trade at the heart of the school appears, priced in both currencies.

| Aspect | Surplus value | Economic surplus |
|---|---|---|
| How defined | The value labour power adds beyond the value of its own upkeep, reckoned in labour time | A society's output minus the cost of producing it, reckoned in money from published accounts |
| What it can measure | The rate of exploitation within any single capital and the aggregate fund of profit, interest, and rent | The surplus of a whole economy in national-account terms, and its division among uses |
| What it explains about origin | Exploitation at the point of production, where unpaid labour time is embodied in the product | Nothing directly; origin is set aside in favour of size, tendency, and disposal |
| What it explains about absorption | Little on its own; disposal is handled by later parts of the theory, in competition and crisis | The central problem of the system: which outlets, from sales effort to state spending, take up a rising surplus |
| What it cannot do | Be estimated from public statistics, or handle administered prices set by discretion | Derive the surplus from the production relation, or distinguish exploitation from a mere transfer |

The row that decides the argument is the third. The value tradition's category is an explanation before it is a measure: it locates the origin of the excess inside the labour process, in the gap between what labour power costs and what labour performs, and every other magnitude in the system is then derived from that location. The school's category reverses the priority. It is a measure before it is an explanation: it sizes the excess for the whole economy and follows its disposal, while the question of origin is deliberately left to the side, treated as settled in general terms by the existence of a property-owning class but not pursued into the accounting. Everything the two camps said to each other for fifty years is a commentary on that row. The critics held that an economics which cannot say where the surplus comes from has surrendered the core of Marxism. The school held that an economics which cannot measure its central magnitude has surrendered the world to the statisticians of the other side.

### What does each category fail to see?

Surplus value cannot be read off any ledger an economy keeps, and it presupposes the competitive price formation that monopoly displaces; economic surplus cannot see the labour process that generates the excess it counts, and so it registers exploitation and unproductive transfer in the same column without distinction.

## The measurability trade

The school's signature claim, which this article names the measurability trade, holds that the substitution was an exchange with two sides, each honestly priced. What was bought was empirical purchase. A surplus defined in money terms can be estimated, with stated conventions, from the national income accounts that every advanced state publishes; its movement across decades can be traced; its division among capitalist consumption, the sales effort, civilian government, military spending, and investment can be argued about with documents in hand. Baran had already shown the style of work the category permits in his growth book, where the potential surplus of a poor economy was exhibited by inventory: so much labour idle, so much land misused, so much income consumed in luxury or drained abroad. What was sold was the origin account. A theory built on the surplus can demonstrate that an economy generates more than its reproduction requires and can follow the excess through its uses, but it cannot show, from inside its own category, why the excess accrues to the owners of property rather than to the producers, beyond pointing to the property relation itself. The value tradition regards that showing as the entire purpose of the theory of surplus value. The school regarded it as a metaphysical surcharge on an empirical programme, and therein lies the trade.

### What exactly was traded in the category substitution?

An explanation of origins for a programme of measurement: the school surrendered the derivation of the excess from unpaid labour time, and received in return a magnitude it could estimate from national accounts, track across decades, and divide among its uses in public argument.

The metaphor of a trade should not be allowed to soften the disagreement, because the two sides valued the goods differently to the point of incommensurability. For the value tradition, explanatory derivation is not one virtue among others; it is what makes the economics a Marxism, the thread that connects the critique of political economy to the theory of exploitation and so to the politics of the whole tradition. A category severed from that thread is, on this view, a category of bourgeois statistics however radical the hands that wield it. For the school, the derivation had become a scholastic exercise: prices in the monopoly economy are administered, labour values are nowhere observed, and a theory that insists on reasoning through unobservable magnitudes forfeits the argument about the actual economy to whoever will discuss its published figures. Between a Marxism that explains but cannot measure and a radical economics that measures but cannot derive, the two camps chose their losses, and the talking past one another that followed was structural rather than personal. Later sections of this article return to the trade in its institutional and political settings; its analytical form is the same everywhere, which is why the series treats the pattern as kin to the tractability divide in the reconstructions of the transformation problem, where rival schools likewise purchased formal consistency at differing explanatory prices.

## Generation and absorption: a distinction the argument needs

One distinction must be fixed before the dispute can be stated fairly, because the school's vocabulary joins two questions that its critics delighted in confusing. The generation of the surplus is a matter of production: how much excess over the cost of production an economy's organisation squeezes out, and in whose hands it accumulates. The absorption of the surplus is a matter of disposal: through what channels the generated excess is spent, invested, or wasted, and what happens to employment and output when the channels run short. The school's central contention joins the two: under monopoly, generation strengthens while reliable absorption weakens, since the giant corporation's pricing raises the surplus share even as its investment outlets are governed by the demand that investment is meant to serve. The detailed working of that contention, the rising tendency and the outlets that counteract it, from the sales effort to military expenditure, is the stagnation argument proper, and it is expounded in the companion article on the stagnation theory, to which readers are directed for the crisis side of the school.

### Why must generation and absorption be kept apart?

Because the school's claims differ in kind on the two sides: the claim that monopoly raises the share of surplus generated is an argument about pricing and costs, while the claim that the economy struggles to absorb what it generates is an argument about spending channels, and each fails or survives on different evidence.

Holding the distinction firm also clarifies the school's account of waste, which casual summaries garble. In the surplus framework, waste is not a moral epithet but a classification within absorption: expenditure that uses up part of the surplus without enlarging the economy's productive capacity and without meeting a human need the school's standard counts as essential. The sales effort is the paradigm case in the advanced economy, an apparatus of advertising, packaging, model changing, and salesmanship whose cost the corporations themselves treat as a necessary expense of business while the school reclassifies it as a use of surplus, one among several that keep the system running by consuming its excess. The reclassification is contestable, and critics contested it, but it is not arbitrary: it follows the definition, and the definition's judgement about which activities are socially necessary is worn on its face. A reader who grasps that the same wage bill can be a cost under one classification and an absorption under another has grasped both the school's method and the precise point at which its method invites attack.

## The value-theoretic criticism at full strength

The criticism arrived in force in the nineteen seventies, when a generation of Marxist economists formed by the revival of value theory took up the joint book and its category, and it deserves to be stated as its authors would state it, at full strength and without caricature. The critics' case has four movements. First, derivation: Marx's categories form a chain in which surplus value is derived from the analysis of the commodity and of labour power, so that profit, interest, and rent appear as distributed forms of a magnitude whose origin has been demonstrated. The economic surplus enters Baran and Sweezy's system underived, defined by an accounting operation; it cannot be obtained from Marx's categories by any transformation, and it transforms into none of them. Second, origin: because the surplus is defined as output over cost, it registers every excess alike, whether the excess arises from the exploitation of wage labour, from monopoly rent, from the pillage of a colony, or from a fortunate harvest sold dear. A theory that cannot distinguish exploitation from transfer, the critics concluded, has lost the specific object of Marxist economics, which is not excess in general but the capital relation. Third, tendency: the claim that the surplus tends to rise, detached from the value analysis of the organic composition of capital and the rate of profit, collapses in the critics' hands into a sophisticated underconsumptionism, an argument that capitalism ails from generating more purchasing-power claims than its spending can honour, dressed in Marxist vocabulary. Fourth, method: the judgement built into the definition, about which activities are necessary and which are waste, is a moral and political judgement wearing the costume of measurement, so that the vaunted empirical purchase is partly counterfeit, an estimate whose largest components were decided by the estimator's politics before the counting began.

### What is the core of the value-theoretic objection?

That the school's category is underived and origin-blind: it cannot be reached from Marx's value analysis, it cannot say whether an excess arose from exploitation or from any other transfer, and its central tendency therefore belongs to the theory of effective demand rather than to the critique of political economy.

The criticism had an institutional edge that should be recorded as part of its substance. The seventies critics were contesting the leadership of anglophone Marxist economics, which the Monthly Review tradition had exercised by default for two decades through the sheer absence of rivals, and their attack on the category doubled as a changing of the guard. Journals and circles formed around the value-theoretic restoration, and in several countries the school found its books reclassified on reading lists from foundation to foil. Nor was the fire all incoming. The school's house had its own internal critics, and the debate was carried in the open, in review essays and exchanges, rather than in whispers: the record shows a tradition arguing about its foundations with the gloves off, which is one reason the dispute remains the best single introduction to what a basic category does in an economic theory. Nothing in the school's defence, stated next, should be read as reducing the criticism's force; the parent instruction for this article is that the criticism stands at full strength, since it is the reason the school's standing among Marxist economists is what it is.

## The school's defence in its own terms

The defence begins by refusing the tribunal. The value categories, the school argued, were forged by Marx for an economy of competitive capitals, in which prices gravitate around values through the ceaseless movement of capital between branches, and in which the labour theory therefore describes a real mechanism of price formation, however mediated. That economy no longer exists. In the economy of the giant corporation, prices are administered: set by cost-plus calculation, held against rivals by tacit discipline, and changed by decision rather than by gravitation. A theory whose first magnitude cannot be connected to any price anyone pays, and whose defence requires an ever more elaborate machinery of transformation between the value system and the price system, has in the school's judgement mistaken fidelity to a text for fidelity to its author's method. Marx himself, Sweezy argued across his career, was a scientist of his own economy's surface forms as much as of its depths, and the surplus category is an attempt to do for the monopoly economy what Marx did for the competitive one: begin from the magnitudes the system itself produces and reports, and reason about its tendencies from there.

### Why did the school say value categories could not do the work?

Because those categories presuppose competitive price formation: where giant firms administer prices by markup and suppress price competition, labour values are neither observed nor approximated by the prices actually charged, while the surplus can be followed through the accounts the economy itself keeps.

The defence adds two reinforcements. The first is the record of the transformation problem itself, which Sweezy had expounded to English readers in his youth: if the bridge between values and prices requires conventions that different bridge-builders set differently, then the explanatory gold the critics claim for derivation is already alloyed, and the school's plain money magnitudes lose less by comparison than the critics allow. The second reinforcement is Baran's: the surplus concept had demonstrated its fertility in the study of underdevelopment, where it organised facts about land, labour, and foreign claims that value-theoretic treatments had left scattered, and a category with one proven field of application was a reasonable foundation for a second. The school's writers also answered the underconsumptionism charge directly, and the answer is worth isolating since the charge recurs in every summary. Their argument, they insisted, is not that wages are too low to buy the product, the classic underconsumptionist claim, but that the surplus rises as a share of output while the outlets for its profitable investment narrow, a claim about the composition of demand and the logic of monopoly pricing rather than about the poverty of the consumer. The distinction is real, though critics replied that it is thinner in practice than in principle, and the reader who wants the demand tradition in full should consult the series article on [overproduction and underconsumption](/2013/11/01/overproduction-and-underconsumption/), where that territory is mapped on its own terms.

## The strongest objection, and the answer the school could give

One objection stands above the rest because it survives the defence, and the series pattern requires it to be stated at full strength. A framework that works with a surplus detached from value theory, the objection runs, has no account of the origin of profit. It can observe that output exceeds cost and that the excess accrues to owners, but it cannot distinguish the surplus squeezed from exploited labour from a windfall, a rent, or a swindle, since all four swell the same column. Lacking that distinction, the framework cannot explain why the excess exists at all, beyond the bare fact of property, and its economics reduces to a radical version of the theory of effective demand: Keynes with a conscience and a longer historical memory. On that reading the school did not revise Marxism; it vacated the premises and hung a Marxist sign on a Keynesian building.

The school's answer, given across the founders' later writings and the journal's exchanges, has two parts. The first is the methodological argument already stated: value categories presuppose the competitive price formation of Marx's century, and a science that refuses to rebuild its foundations when its object changes has chosen scripture over inquiry. The second part is a concession, and the concession is the answer's honest core. The objection identifies a real theoretical loss, and the school accepted the loss knowingly. What the surplus framework says about origin is thin: that a class owning the means of production appropriates the excess their ownership commands, and that the labour process within the corporation is where the producing happens. The founders judged that thinness a price worth paying for a theory that could measure, date, and divide its magnitude in public. Readers must judge the judgement. What can be said without partisanship is that the loss was neither hidden nor accidental, which distinguishes the school's substitution from the quiet drifts and unexamined borrowings that mark lesser traditions, and which is why the school's critics, even at their most severe, treated its founders as opponents to be answered rather than as confusion to be cleared away.

## Kalecki, Steindl, and the school's acknowledged debts

No school invents its instruments unaided, and this one acknowledged its creditors with unusual care. The Polish economist Michal Kalecki, who had arrived at a theory of effective demand independently of and partly before Keynes, supplied two elements the founders absorbed early: the degree of monopoly as a determinant of the distribution of income between wages and profits, and a dynamics of investment in which the decisions of firms, financed from their own profits, drive the cycle. Sweezy had championed Kalecki's work to English readers from the nineteen thirties, and the kinship shows throughout the school's writing: where Kalecki measured distribution by the markup firms could sustain, Baran and Sweezy measured the surplus a monopoly economy generates by the same administered pricing, viewed from the side of the whole social product. The second creditor was Josef Steindl, whose Maturity and Stagnation in American Capitalism, published in 1952, argued that the oligopolistic restructuring of industry had impaired the economy's capacity to absorb its own savings, since giant firms financing investment internally no longer needed the capital market's outlets and would not invest beyond the growth of their markets. Steindl's book reached few readers on its first appearance; the joint book of 1966 built on its foundations and said so, and the school's later reissue of Steindl's work under its own press was an act of scholarly restitution as much as of publishing.

### Which debts did the joint book acknowledge openly?

Two above all: Kalecki's account of the degree of monopoly and of distribution, which taught the school how administered pricing governs the split of income, and Steindl's analysis of maturity and stagnation in an oligopolised economy, whose absorption problem the joint book generalised into its central argument.

Behind the named creditors stood a climate. Alvin Hansen, the Harvard Keynesian, had made secular stagnation a respectable phrase in American economics at the end of the nineteen thirties, arguing that declining population growth and the closing of the frontier had thinned the investment opportunities of a mature economy. The school shared Hansen's symptom while rejecting his diagnosis and his remedies: for the stagnationists of the Keynesian mainstream, the deficiency was a shortage of outlets that wise policy might repair; for Baran and Sweezy, the deficiency was the normal working of monopoly itself, removable only with the system that generated it. The contrast is a useful instrument for placing the school on the map of postwar economics. It drank from the same stream of stagnationist thought as the Keynesian establishment it opposed, and its distinctiveness lies not in noticing that advanced capitalism could stagnate but in assigning the cause to the monopoly form of the capital relation rather than to demography, technology, or policy error. Critics who file the school as radical Keynesianism are working from this neighbourhood; the school's writers answered that the neighbourhood is where any theory of effective demand must live, and that the difference between a theory of the system's tendencies and a technique for managing them is not erased by a shared map.

## The school under pressure: the nineteen fifties on the record

The professional pressures under which the school formed are part of its documentary record and are stated here as record, without embroidery. Sweezy's resignation from Harvard has been described: an assistant professor home from war service, assured by colleagues that the department could not agree on his retention with tenure, who declined to wait for the formal refusal. In July 1953, Leo Huberman was called before the Senate committee chaired by Joseph McCarthy and questioned about his views and associations; he answered that his views were in his books and declined to go further. In January 1954, Sweezy was subpoenaed by the attorney general of New Hampshire, Louis Wyman, acting as a one-man commission investigating subversive activities under state law, and was questioned about his associations and his politics; called again in June of that year, he was ordered to answer questions about a lecture on Marxism he had delivered at the University of New Hampshire in March, including its contents and the names of persons connected with it. He refused, on grounds of the First Amendment and of academic freedom, and was convicted of contempt. His case ascended through the state courts, which sustained the conviction, to the Supreme Court of the United States, which reversed it in June 1957 in a decision, Sweezy v. New Hampshire, that became a landmark of academic freedom doctrine. Baran's exposure took a different form: as the holder of a chair, he could be attacked but not simply removed, and attacks came in campaigns by newspapers, alumni, and political figures disturbed by his teaching and by his travels and writings on Cuba and other subjects, which Stanford's administration withstood. He remained, throughout, the only tenured Marxist teaching economics at a major American university, a fact his publisher could print on his book jacket in 1957 as a plain statement of the profession's condition.

### What did the New Hampshire case decide?

That Sweezy's contempt conviction could not stand: the Supreme Court reversed it in 1957, holding that the state had not shown the legislative interest that could justify interrogating a scholar about the content of a university lecture, in a ruling that entered the constitutional protection of academic freedom.

Three features of the episode matter for understanding the school rather than merely honouring its veterans. The first is timing: the journal was founded in 1949, before the worst of the pressure, so the school possessed its fortress before the siege, an accident of foresight that decided its survival. The second is geography: the school's independence was purchased by distance from the profession's centres of certification, and the price was real, since the school's writers held almost no power over appointments, curricula, or the training of successors inside the universities; its students were formed in its pages rather than its classrooms. The third is doctrinal: the experience of the nineteen fifties confirmed the founders in the belief, visible throughout the joint book, that the state in the monopoly economy is not a neutral arbiter standing outside the class structure but an institution whose coercive and fiscal machinery serves definite social functions, a conclusion they had reached from evidence supplied by their own biographies. The reader should register that inference as the school's, stated here without endorsement or dissent, in the manner this series observes toward the politics of its subjects.

## The word monopoly, and what the school counts as one

A preliminary confusion haunts the school's name, and clearing it costs a section. Monopoly, in the school's usage, does not mean a single seller confronting a market alone, the textbook case of the lone monopolist. It names a condition of an industry and then of an economy: the condition in which a few giant corporations, each large relative to its market, recognise their mutual dependence and conduct themselves accordingly, abstaining from price competition while competing in every other register. Sweezy had worked on oligopoly theory in his Harvard years, contributing an account of the kinked demand curve that entered the textbooks of the orthodox, and the school's usage descends from that expertise. Monopoly capital is thus a stage designation, kin to the usage in the Marxist literature on imperialism, where monopoly names the epoch of the giant firm, the bank, and the trust, rather than a market structure with a population of one. The joint book's opening chapters spend their care on exactly this clarification, insisting that the competitive economy of the classical textbooks had been a real historical formation, roughly the capitalism of the nineteenth century, and that the theory appropriate to it, including Marx's own value analysis as the founders read its presuppositions, belonged to that formation. The claim that a stage boundary runs through the theory itself, so that categories expire with the formations that made them true, is among the school's most radical methodological commitments and among the least discussed. It also explains the founders' serenity before the charge of abandoning value theory: on their premises, they had not abandoned a living instrument but declined to embalm one.

### Is monopoly in the school's usage a single seller?

No: the word names the condition of an economy dominated by a few giant firms in each branch, firms that recognise their interdependence, refrain from cutting prices against one another, and compete instead through advertising, product variation, and salesmanship, a condition the school treats as a historical stage.

The stage claim did quiet work elsewhere in the system. It fixed the school's periodisation, in which competitive capitalism gives way to monopoly capitalism around the turn of the twentieth century, and it thereby fixed the school's reading of Marx: Capital analyses the competitive stage with unmatched depth, and its categories, derived for that stage, apply to the successor stage only through the mediation the school attempted to supply. Readers who miss the stage claim tend to misread the substitution as a judgement that Marx was wrong, whereas the founders' own presentation was that Marx was right about an economy that had since been replaced. Whether a theory's categories can expire in that fashion is a genuine philosophical question about the historical sciences, and the value critics answered it in the negative with some force, holding that the labour theory of value is a theory of the capital relation as such, in whatever stage, and not a local theory of Victorian competition. The article records the disagreement here because it underlies the louder quarrel about measurement: beneath the dispute over what can be counted lies a dispute over whether categories have dates.

## Administered prices in the school's workshop

The mechanics deserve a closer look, because the school's plausibility stands or falls with its account of how a giant corporation actually sets a price. The firm, on this account, does not discover a price by trial in a market of anonymous rivals. It calculates its costs at a standard rate of operation, adds a margin intended to yield a target return on its capital, and posts the result, adjusting the margin slowly in light of demand, of rivals' parallel calculations, and of the danger of inviting entry or public attention. Gardiner Means, whose name attaches to the very phrase administered prices, had documented for the nineteen thirties how industrial prices held rigid through the depression while output collapsed, the opposite of the textbook's flexible auction. The school's contribution was to make the administered price do theoretical work: if price is a decision, then the division of the social product between wages and surplus is also, in the first instance, a decision, constrained by the strength of unions, the state of demand, and the corporations' collective interest in not provoking resistance. Distribution becomes politics conducted by accounting means, which is why the school's economics reads, at times, like institutional description raised to theory.

Two consequences follow that the school drew explicitly. The first concerns profit. In the competitive story, profit rates equalise across branches as capital migrates toward the highest return, and the equalisation is the market's discipline. Under administered pricing, the giants of each branch protect their margins, entry is slow and expensive, and the equalising flow is dammed: what remains is a structure of durably unequal returns, with the commanding heights of industry collecting the surplus their market power secures. The second consequence concerns investment. A giant firm finances expansion mainly from retained earnings, the surplus it has already appropriated, and therefore does not face the capital market as a supplicant; its investment is decided by its reading of future demand for its products, not by the cost of borrowing. Saving and investment, which the older theory imagined as two acts performed by different parties and reconciled by the interest rate, collapse into one act performed inside the corporation, and the economy loses the mechanism that was supposed to guarantee their balance. Here the school stands closest to Kalecki, whose dynamics it absorbed, and the reader can see the absorption problem being manufactured inside the firm's finance office before it ever appears as a statistic of stagnation.

## The journal's middle decades: Magdoff and the turn to imperialism

A school is tested by succession, and the first succession came with grief. Huberman died in 1968, and Harry Magdoff, an economist formed in government statistics during the New Deal and war years, joined Sweezy as co-editor, beginning a partnership that lasted until Sweezy's death more than three decades later. Magdoff brought to the school a statistician's memory and a new centre of gravity: the international system. His book The Age of Imperialism, published in 1969, restated the Marxist theory of imperialism for the age of the American ascendancy, and through the end of the nineteen sixties and into the seventies the journal made the economics of empire, war, and the underdeveloped world its signature territory, publishing analyses of the war in Vietnam as an economic as well as a political event and keeping Baran's development questions at the centre of its pages. Readers interested in that corpus should follow the series article on the [marxism and imperialism guide](/2017/02/01/marxism-and-imperialism-guide/), where the later imperialism work of the tradition is surveyed in its own right.

### How did the school's attention widen beyond the domestic economy?

Through succession and circumstance together: Magdoff's arrival brought a statistician of the world economy into the editorship just as war in Asia and revolt across the poor countries made the international hierarchy of capital unavoidable, and the journal's analyses of empire joined Baran's development questions to the joint book's domestic theory.

The widening had a theoretical root inside the school's own founder. Baran's growth book had treated the advanced and backward economies as one system whose surplus flowed uphill, and the joint book had bracketed that system to analyse the metropolis alone. The Magdoff years rejoined the two halves of Baran's vision: the surplus generated in the poor world and drained by foreign claims reappeared as a standing topic, and the dependency debates of the seventies, in which the school's alumni and critics argued over whether development within the world market was possible at all, were conducted substantially in and around the journal's pages. A school that had begun as two men's answer to American conditions had become, by its third decade, one of the addresses at which the economics of the whole capitalist world was argued. That breadth is part of the record the value dispute sometimes obscures: whatever the standing of its category among theorists of value, the tradition's empirical and historical writing on the world economy formed part of the education of a generation of scholars across the social sciences.

## Finance enters: the school's account of its own later phase

The tradition's most consequential self-revision began in the nineteen eighties, as Sweezy and Magdoff watched the financial sector of the American economy swell beyond anything the joint book had contemplated. Their joint writings of that decade, including the essays collected as Stagnation and the Financial Explosion in 1987, drew the conclusion in stages. Finance had once been a servant of production, a machinery for gathering savings and directing investment. It had become an arena in its own right, in which the surplus was absorbed, multiplied on paper, and speculated with, while productive investment stagnated: an explosion of financial claims standing over a stagnating base. Sweezy returned to the theme near the end of his life in an essay of 1994 on the triumph of financial capital, arguing that the centre of gravity of the whole system had shifted. The school's successors, above all John Bellamy Foster, who became the journal's editor in 2000, gathered these threads into a named account of a new phase, monopoly finance capital, in which the monopoly corporation and the financial institution operate as a single fused structure, and stagnation and financialisation appear as two faces of one condition.

### Why did finance move to the centre of the school's later work?

Because the facts of its own framework demanded it: a rising surplus that could not find productive outlets flowed into speculation and debt, swelling the financial sector decade by decade, until the school's writers concluded that the phase itself had changed and named the fusion of monopoly and finance as its object.

The phase concept does specific work inside the school's system, and readers should see the joinery. In the joint book, the absorption problem was the system's standing difficulty, and finance was a minor channel. In the later account, finance becomes the absorption mechanism of first resort and simultaneously a new source of fragility, since claims pyramided on a stagnant base must eventually be tested against the income the base can pay. The 2008 crisis, arriving four years after Sweezy's death, was read by the school's writers as the phase's signature event, and the reading carried the tradition to its largest audience since the nineteen sixties. Whether the phase designation will endure as the school's second founding idea, beside the surplus itself, is a question for the historians of the tradition; what is documented is that the school generated it from its own premises, by taking its absorption problem seriously for forty years until the problem changed shape. The seeded question in this article's FAQ, which asks what monopoly finance capital is, receives in its answer the school's own construal rather than the crisis-theory construal the same phrase carries elsewhere in this series, and the difference between those construals is itself an exhibit of the school's distinctiveness: where crisis theory sees a regime of accumulation driven by finance, the Monthly Review tradition sees the furthest development of its founding problem, a surplus too large for its world.

## Decline and revival

The school's fortunes moved against the long rhythm of the economy it studied. In the decades of postwar expansion, a theory whose centre was stagnation looked refuted by the morning's headlines, and the school settled into the role of a respected dissent: its books in print, its journal subscribed to by libraries and by the faithful, its founders ageing in public honour and professional exile. The stagflation of the nineteen seventies briefly returned its questions to the centre of debate, since an economy that could stagnate with inflation was an embarrassment to the reigning synthesis, but the ascendancy that followed, of monetarism in policy and of a purified market theory in the academy, pushed the whole Marxist economics of the period to the margin, and the school with it. Inside the Marxist camp, the value-theoretic restoration had already demoted the school's category; outside the camp, the profession's gatekeepers had little use for any Marxist category at all. By the century's end the tradition survived in the manner it had been built to survive, on its journal and its press, awaiting weather.

### What brought readers back to the school after the long quiet?

The return of its questions: a financial crisis generated inside the most advanced financial system, decades of wage stagnation beneath rising productivity, and mainstream studies of market power and markups that made the school's themes discussable again in rooms where its name had long been unmentionable.

The revival, when it came, had three sources, and the school's writers were candid about all of them. The crisis of 2008 discredited the finance-led growth model in public and sent readers toward any tradition that had treated finance as a symptom rather than an engine; the school had forty years of such treatment in print. The stagnation of wages and the hollowing of the postwar settlement made the joint book's picture of a wasteful, sales-driven, militarised prosperity read less like period polemic and more like field notes. And a body of mainstream empirical research on industrial concentration, market power, and markups, pursued with the profession's own methods, corroborated substantial parts of the picture the school had drawn from the outside, a convergence the series treats in the stagnation article and therefore links rather than repeats here. The school's standing rose on the strength of its questions returning, which is the most durable kind of revival a heterodox tradition can have. Its category, notably, rose more slowly than its reputation: the revival read Baran and Sweezy for their diagnosis of the system's tendencies more than for the surplus as an instrument, and the measurability trade remains, within the revival itself, the unfinished argument this article has placed at the centre.

## The necessary and the wasteful: judgement inside the measurement

Every application of the surplus category turns on a classification, and the classification deserves its own examination because it is where the school's economics and its social criticism fuse. To estimate the surplus, one must decide which expenditures are costs of production, socially necessary under any rational order, and which are uses of an excess that a different order would not require. The school's lists are well known: the apparatus of selling, from advertising to the annual model change; the apparatus of finance beyond its bookkeeping functions; the military establishment; the labour of supervision and guarding; the duplicated facilities of competing giants. None of these is dismissed as useless to the firms that pay for it; each is necessary to the reproduction of the existing order and unnecessary, in the school's standard, to the reproduction of society. The standard is avowedly a judgement about what human labour is for, and Baran, who had formed the distinction in the growth book's contrast between actual and essential consumption, never disguised its character. Potential surplus is not found in nature. It is computed against a picture of a rational society, and the computation is a critique in arithmetic form.

### Where does judgement enter the measuring of the surplus?

At the classification of expenditure: the estimator must rule on which activities count as necessary costs and which as absorption of an excess, and since no ledger draws that line by itself, the school's standard of social rationality enters the number before the counting starts.

Critics seized the opening, and their pressure on it was the most practically damaging of all, because it threatened the school's proudest claim, the empirical one. If the largest items in the estimated surplus are there by the estimator's ruling, then the measurement programmes of the school and its opponents will never meet on neutral ground, and the surplus will persuade only those who already accept its classifications. The school's reply conceded the premise and denied the conclusion. Every aggregate in economics, its writers observed, embodies classifications fixed by judgement: the national accounts themselves decide what counts as investment, what as intermediate cost, what as a transfer, and the decisions are conventions defended by argument, not discoveries. The school's classifications differ from the statisticians' in being explicit about the social theory they serve. A student approaching the school's empirical work should therefore separate, as the FAQ below puts the discipline, the accounting identity from the social ruling that fills it, and should grade the school's estimates as arguments with numbers attached, which is how their authors presented them. The discipline generalises: it is the same separation the measurability trade demands everywhere, between what a category can count and what it must assume before counting can begin.

## The procedure in practice: Baran's inventory of a poor economy

Abstract definitions persuade slowly; the school's method is best seen at work in the growth book's procedure, which the joint book's authors later adapted to the advanced economy. Baran took a concrete underdeveloped country, India above all, and proceeded by inventory. Begin with the labour force and ask how much of it produces anything the society needs: a large share, he argued, was absorbed in occupations that a rational order would not reproduce, in domestic service for the wealthy, in petty intermediation layered between producer and consumer, in the guard labour of a property regime, in underemployed agriculture where holdings too small to equip stood beside estates too large to cultivate. Turn to the land and ask what it grows: export crops and plantation products for foreign tables on soil that could feed the population that worked it. Turn to the income of the propertied and ask where it goes: to luxury consumption, to hoarding in gold and jewellery, to speculation in land and urban property, to accounts abroad, each destination a leakage from the accumulation the economy needed. Sum the inventory and the potential surplus stands revealed, not as a statistic the government published but as a total the investigator constructs, item by item, each item defensible or attackable on its own evidence.

The procedure's strength is its transparency: a critic who rejects the total can be walked to the exact line of the inventory he disputes, and the argument proceeds by evidence about land use, employment, and trade flows rather than by authority. Its weakness is the one the critics of the category identified in general form, visible here in miniature: the standard of rationality that decides which occupations are redundant and which consumption is essential is the investigator's own, informed by a picture of the planned economy Baran held in reserve as the contrast case. Indian economists of several schools engaged the argument on exactly those terms, disputing particular items, and the debate improved the inventory without dissolving the disagreement about the standard. The episode is the measurability trade in its most concrete historical dress. The surplus framework bought a research procedure of genuine power, usable by scholars who shared none of the author's politics, and it paid with the permanent contestability of its totals, a price Baran, to judge by the care with which he documented his items, understood and accepted in advance.

## Which Marx the school kept

The substitution has so dominated accounts of the school that its fidelity is easy to miss, and a section of inventory is owed on the other side. The school kept the class structure of Marx's analysis entire: an economy divided between those who own the means of production and those who must sell their capacity to work, with the state, the culture, and the legal order read in that division's light. It kept the historical method, the insistence that capitalism is a dated formation with a beginning in primitive accumulation and no guarantee of perpetuity, and it kept the theory of imperialism as the outward face of the monopoly stage, which Magdoff's work renewed. It kept, above all, the ambition of totality: the joint book's subtitle announces an essay on an economic and social order, and its chapters reach from the corporation to the family, from the sales effort to the condition of Black Americans and the culture of automobile and suburb, in a manner faithful to the founders' conviction that an economy is a civilisation and must be criticised as one. What the school surrendered was specific: the labour theory of value as the analytical engine, and with it the derivation of the system's categories from the commodity form. The surrender was argued, dated, and signed, which is why the school's profile in this series is filed not under apostasy but under revision, the category of traditions that change a doctrine's centre while declaring the change.

The founders' own testimony supports the filing. Sweezy, who had the most to lose by the substitution since his reputation rested on the exposition of the theory being set aside, returned to the question across his long career and never treated it as a fall from grace: his consistent position was that the theory of value was a theory of a competitive economy's price formation and that honouring Marx meant extending his method to the monopoly economy, not reciting his conclusions. Baran's essays, gathered after his death in the volume The Longer View, show the same temperament applied to development, to socialist planning, and to the intellectual's vocation, a Marxism held as a research programme rather than a creed. The school's severest critics granted the founders' seriousness even while denying their conclusion, and the school's defenders rested much of their case on the same ground. A tradition whose central act was a deliberate, declared, and defended revision occupies a different place in the history of Marxist economics from one that drifted, and the series' comparative articles rely on that difference: the measurability trade could only be struck by men who knew the price of both goods.

## The state in the school's account: the fiscal face of the surplus

The joint book's treatment of government is where its category meets its politics most visibly, and the argument is often summarised too crudely to be recognised. The state, on the school's account, confronts the absorption problem as a standing condition of its budgets. Civilian public spending is hemmed in from several sides: it must not compete with the private production it would displace, must not build the public capacity that would make private suppliers redundant, and must not trespass on the prerogatives that the owners of the giant corporations regard as theirs. Military spending suffers from none of those inhibitions. Weapons production competes with no private product, threatens no corporation's market, employs labour and absorbs materials on the largest scale, and can be expanded under the banner of security without the ideological resistance that greets civilian programmes. The military budget therefore appears in the school's analysis as the absorption channel of least resistance, the form of state spending most compatible with the monopoly order, and the book's chapters on the subject connected the scale of American armament to the economy's need for outlets rather than to the external dangers invoked to justify it. The argument was an application of the category, not an excursion from it: military expenditure is surplus absorbed by the state in a form the system's dominant interests can accept, and its size is therefore evidence about the surplus's size and about the constraints on every other channel.

Two clarifications keep the argument from being misread as a conspiracy theory of the budget. First, the mechanism the school proposes is selection, not instruction: no committee of executives dictates appropriations, but proposals for spending are filtered, session after session, by their compatibility with the monopoly order, and the channels that pass the filter repeatedly are the ones that grow. Second, the argument is comparative: the claim is not that military spending is uniquely large in the school's America by the standards of empires, but that among the possible absorbers available to a rich economy, armament is the one whose expansion meets the least structural resistance, which predicts its durability across administrations of both parties. Later writers in the tradition extended the analysis to the carceral and surveillance apparatuses and to the fiscal politics of the states and cities, and critics replied that the filter story explains everything and nothing, since any observed budget can be redescribed as the survivor of a selection. The exchange is recorded here as part of the school's reception; the analytical point for this article is narrower. The state sections of the joint book are the category substitution performing its characteristic work, following the surplus into an institution and reclassifying its accounts, and they persuaded readers who remained unmoved by the book's price theory, which is one route by which the school's influence travelled far beyond the readership of its economics.

## The school's socialism: the Sweezy and Bettelheim exchange

A school that had broken with the value theory might have been expected to break with the politics the theory underwrote, and the school's record on the actually existing socialist states is therefore part of its profile. The founders' position was critical and committed at once, a combination their milieu called being inside the movement and outside the churches. Baran, formed partly in Moscow and Berlin, wrote about the Soviet Union with a knowledge of its economy that few Western Marxists matched, and he treated its planning apparatus as a real, if deformed, attempt to mobilise a surplus for development, an analysis continuous with his growth book's category of planned surplus. Sweezy travelled to Cuba after its revolution and wrote two books with Huberman reporting the experiment sympathetically and urging its lessons on American readers. The school's independence showed most sharply in the exchange of the early nineteen seventies between Sweezy and the French economist Charles Bettelheim, published by the school's press as a book on the transition to socialism, in which the two men disputed the character of the Soviet system and the meaning of planning itself, Sweezy defending the possibility that the Soviet order remained, beneath its deformations, a society in transition away from capitalism, and Bettelheim pressing the contrary case from within a Maoist framework. The exchange is remembered as one of the last occasions on which the character of the Soviet system was debated at full length, in public, between Marxists of the first rank.

The episode bears on the category dispute in a way commentators rarely notice. Sweezy's defence of the transition thesis rested on the same methodological instinct as the surplus substitution: attend to the actual institutions and their movement, distrust the application of finished categories to unfinished formations, and let the accounting of a real economy discipline the theory brought to it. His critics in the value debate made the mirror-image charge in both arenas, that an empiricism this supple could find its conclusions wherever it looked. The school's writers accepted the description and rejected the sneer: flexibility toward formations in motion was, for them, the Marxist method itself, against a scholasticism that sorted societies by checklist. A reader weighing the school's standing should hold the two disputes together, because they are one dispute about what kind of theory Marxism is, conducted once over prices and once over the Soviet Union, and the school's answers are consistent enough that accepting or refusing them tends to happen as a package.

## The joint book's America: waste as a social order

The last part of the joint book turned the category on the quality of the civilisation the surplus built, and it is the part that carried the school's name into sociology, cultural criticism, and the common reader's vocabulary. The argument, stripped to its frame, is that an economy which must absorb a rising surplus will shape its citizens' lives around the absorption: it will sell them goods designed for replacement rather than use, house them in suburbs whose form multiplies the consumption of automobiles, fuel, and road space, and organise leisure as a market in packaged excitement. The automobile system received the book's most famous development analysis in miniature: a transport order rebuilt around the private car, with public alternatives allowed to decay, locks in an enormous standing demand for vehicles, fuel, tyres, insurance, highways, and suburban construction, a whole civilisation of absorption poured in concrete. The sales effort's cultural wing, the advertising industry's manufacture of wants, is treated by the series in its own article, already noted in the disambiguation of territories; here the point is the frame. In the school's hands, phenomena that other critics treated as failings of taste became entries in an account, the irrationality of the whole appearing as the rational working of the surplus's disposal problem at the level of everyday life.

The book also joined the economics of the surplus to the racial order of its country, arguing that the position of Black Americans, confined by discrimination to the worst housing, the worst schools, and the margins of the labour market, was not an anomaly within the monopoly economy but one of its load-bearing arrangements, supplying a reserve of cheap labour and a standing object of division within the working population. The treatment was among the earliest in American Marxist economics to give racial oppression structural rather than decorative place, and it influenced the generation of radical scholars who built the study of racial capitalism in the following decades. Critics of the school from the left have noted, in turn, that the joint book's framework subsumes race under the surplus's requirements more smoothly than the historical record of slavery and its afterlives comfortably allows, a criticism the tradition's later writers have partly absorbed. The balance sheet of the book's social chapters is therefore double, like everything else in this profile: a pioneering integration of the economic and the social within one category's reach, purchased at the price of making that category bear explanatory weight its critics judge it cannot carry.

## Why the substitution happened in America

Ideas have native soils, and the surplus category could only have been proposed where it was. American economics in the founders' lifetimes carried a strong institutionalist inheritance, descending from Thorstein Veblen, whose theory of business enterprise had distinguished, two generations earlier, between the making of goods and the making of money, and whose account of absentee ownership treated the corporation as a machinery for appropriating a social surplus by legal and financial means. Baran and Sweezy wrote in Veblen's long shadow and acknowledged the kinship; the surplus is in part the institutionalists' intuition rebuilt with Marxist materials. American conditions supplied a second ingredient: the world's most developed apparatus of national income accounting, built by Simon Kuznets and the federal statistical services in the nineteen thirties and forties, which made the whole social product and its division into a published object that an economist could hold. A category meant to be estimated from accounts could hardly be invented in a country without accounts. The third ingredient was negative: the distance of the American Marxist milieu from the European scholastic traditions, in which fidelity to the letter of Capital was policed by parties and their academies. The founders answered to no party's theoretical bureau. Their heresy was proposed by a journal's editors to a readership of subscribers, and it survived or fell by persuasion, which is perhaps why it was proposed in the form of a tool rather than a doctrine: tools are adopted, doctrines are obeyed or anathematised.

The American soil also explains the school's characteristic blind spots, which its critics in Europe and Latin America identified from the first. A theory formed on the richest economy's accounts tends to take that economy as the type of the system, and the joint book's subtitle confesses the limitation with pride: an essay on the American economic and social order. The dependency writers who descended from Baran had to correct the metropolis-centred picture from within the tradition, insisting that the surplus of the centre and the poverty of the periphery are one process, and the school's journal, to its credit, printed the correction. The tradition's history thus recapitulates its category: what the surplus framework made visible depended on where the accountant stood, and the school's later development can be read as a series of relocations of the accountant, from Palo Alto and New Hampshire to the world economy entire.

## The falling rate of profit in the school's handling

No account of the substitution is complete without the law it displaced from the centre, the tendency of the rate of profit to fall, which Marx derived from the rising organic composition of capital and which the older Marxist economics treated as the system's deepest contradiction. Sweezy's treatise of 1942 had given the law a respectful and critical exposition, noting the counteracting tendencies Marx himself listed and the logical pressure points his critics had found, and the young Sweezy's verdict was that the law, properly hedged, survived. The joint book of 1966 handled the inheritance by substitution in the strictest sense: where the falling rate of profit had stood, it placed the rising tendency of the surplus, arguing that monopoly reverses the sign of the old tendency, since the giant corporation's pricing power and its cheapening of its own inputs raise the surplus share even as mechanisation proceeds. The move was presented as an application of Marx's method to changed conditions, and it directly served the absorption argument: a system whose problem is a falling return on capital needs a different crisis theory from a system whose problem is an excess return it cannot place.

The value critics treated the displacement as the substitution's confession. The rate of profit in Marx, they argued, is not a share read off accounts but a value magnitude whose movement registers the self-expansion and self-limitation of capital itself; replacing it with an accounting share mistakes the surface for the mechanism, and the school's rising surplus is, on inspection, the mirror image of the orthodox law viewed through national accounts, equally consistent with several mechanisms and therefore evidence for none. The school's writers answered that an observable tendency with a contestable mechanism serves science better than an unobservable law with a perfect one, and there the matter rested, as it rests across this cluster of the series: the same divide between derivation and measurement, wearing the dress of the profit rate. Readers pursuing the rate of profit in its own terms will find its crisis-theoretic career treated in the cluster's earlier articles; the present point is biographical and methodological. The founders did not overlook the law. They weighed it, in print, across twenty-four years, and exchanged it for the rising surplus with their eyes open, which is why the school's profile must record the exchange among its deliberate acts and not among its oversights.

## Two kinds of evidence: the school's empiricism examined

The school's claim to empirical virtue rests on a distinctive diet of evidence, and an examiner should know its courses. The first course is the aggregate account: national income and product figures, corporate profit reports, the budgets of the federal government, from which the surplus and its division are estimated by the procedures already described. The second course is the case study: the history of an industry, a corporation, a weapons programme, a colonial territory, narrated in detail and made to bear the weight of a general claim. The journal's pages are full of both, and the school's characteristic essay moves between them, opening with the aggregate picture, descending to the documented case, and returning with the case's moral priced into the aggregate. The method's strength is rhetorical as well as analytical: a reader unpersuaded by the category can be detained by the dossier. Its weakness is the one its critics generalised from the classification problem: cases are chosen, and the choosing is guided by the theory the cases are meant to test, so the school's evidence persuades most reliably those whose classifications it already shares.

A fair assessment must add what the school's empiricism achieved outside its own camp. The tradition's documentary habits, its insistence that a claim about the economy should be able to point at the accounts, trained two generations of heterodox economists in a discipline that the more exegetical Marxisms of the same decades neglected. When the value restoration of the nineteen seventies rebuilt Marxist economics as a theoretical enterprise, some of its own leaders conceded, in retrospect, that the empirical thinness of the restored orthodoxy was the price of its rigour, and that the Monthly Review tradition had kept a different and necessary virtue alive through the wilderness years. The measurability trade, seen from the standpoint of the whole field's division of labour, looks less like an error than like a specialisation: one tradition kept the derivation and lost the data, the other kept the data and lost the derivation, and the history of Marxist economics since the founders can be written as the long attempt, still unfinished, to hold both ends of the rope at once.

## The missing workshop: Braverman and the labour process

The sharpest internal evidence about the surplus category's limits is a book the school itself published. By the early nineteen seventies, the tradition's own press issued Harry Braverman's Labor and Monopoly Capital, a study of the degradation of work under the monopoly corporation: the separation of conception from execution, the march of scientific management, the reduction of craft skill to supervised routine. Braverman, a former metalworker who had become an editor at the press, wrote the book in the school's idiom and aimed it at the school's own silence. The joint book had analysed the corporation from the boardroom and the ledger; Braverman analysed it from the bench, and the surplus whose disposal the founders had traced so carefully reappeared in his pages at the moment of its generation, in the managed expenditure of human effort. The work became one of the most influential products of the entire tradition, founding the modern study of the labour process and arming a generation of sociologists of work.

The episode deserves its place in this profile because it shows the school's institution correcting the school's category. The value critics had argued, as theory, that a framework without an account of origin would leave production unexamined. The tradition's own publishing house demonstrated the point as practice, by finding its most famous later book precisely in the territory its founding category could not see. Braverman's study is compatible with the surplus framework, and its author worked comfortably inside the school's world, but its explanatory engine is the labour process itself, the very site the substitution had bracketed. A balanced verdict on the measurability trade must count this among the trade's costs, and also among the tradition's virtues: the school had built an institution capacious enough to publish its own corrective, and the corrective's success is part of why the school's name survived the decades when its category was out of fashion. Traditions are judged by their ecosystems as well as by their axioms.

## The school's Keynes problem

The charge of radical Keynesianism, pressed in the objection section above, draws its plausibility from a real proximity, and the school's handling of Keynes across its history is the record of that proximity being managed. Sweezy was among the earliest American economists to expound Keynes seriously, and his treatise treated the theory of effective demand as a genuine, if incomplete, advance: Keynes had shown that a capitalist economy could settle below full employment, but had taken the distribution of income and the investment decisions of a propertied class as data rather than as the system's own products. The school's lifelong amendment can be stated in one sentence: where Keynes asked why spending falls short, the school asked why the surplus rises and why the channels open to it are the ones a monopoly order can tolerate. Military Keynesianism, the phrase the tradition helped circulate, names the junction of the two theories: a state that can absorb the surplus through armaments indefinitely, achieving the demand Keynes prescribed by the means his liberal conscience would have declined.

The difference in remedy is where the school's writers located their Marxism when the Keynesian charge was pressed. A Keynesian technique is indifferent to the social form of the spending it engineers; the school's analysis predicts that the form will be selected by the structure of power, and that the selection, running repeatedly through the same filter, produces the militarised, sales-driven, wasteful civilisation the joint book described. Policy, on this account, is not an external lever applied to the economy but one of the economy's own outputs, and a theory that treats it as a lever mistakes the machine's exhaust for its steering wheel. Critics answered that the prediction is loose enough to survive any budget, and the exchange remains open. What the record establishes is that the school's Keynesianism, if the word is to be used, was of a specific and argumentative kind: it absorbed the theory of effective demand into an account of class power and monopoly pricing, and it never accepted the technocratic premise that the system's managers could, by better technique, repeal its tendencies. The distance between that position and the Keynesianism of the treasuries is the distance the school's whole career was spent measuring.

## The third founder: Huberman and the school's popular voice

Histories of the school are written, including this one, around two names, and the compression wrongs a third. Leo Huberman was a teacher and a popular writer before he was an editor: his Man's Worldly Goods, published in 1936, retold the economic history of Europe as a story any reader could follow, and it sold in quantities no academic Marxist approached. When he and Sweezy founded the journal, Huberman supplied more than business management, though he supplied that too, steering the enterprise through its dangerous decade. He supplied the school's popular register, the conviction that the analysis existed to be understood outside the profession, and his own books with Sweezy on Cuba carried the school's reporting to the wide audience the treatises never reached. The pairing was structural. A tradition lodged in a journal lives by its readers, and readers are kept by prose that respects them; the school's famous lucidity, its preference for the stated definition and the documented case over the formula and the footnote war, descends from the partnership between a theorist and a populariser who edited each other for nineteen years.

The popular voice also shaped the school's doctrine in one respect that theorists overlook. Categories meant for a general readership must be graspable as procedures: the surplus can be explained at a kitchen table, as the founders repeatedly explained it, while the transformation of values into prices cannot. The measurability trade was therefore also a trade in audiences, exchanging the seminar of value theorists for the republic of readers the journal had assembled, and the school's writers knew it. When critics accused the founders of vulgarisation, the accusation landed on a choice the founders had made with open eyes and would have defended as democratic as well as methodological: an economics of the monopoly age that its victims cannot follow has, on the school's premises, failed at the first task of a critical science. The defence does not settle the theoretical question, and this article has not pretended otherwise; it explains the temper in which the question was answered, and why the school's books remain readable when the polemics against them require a commentary.

## The wider shelf: the founders beyond the famous books

The One Test of this article holds that the founders' separate contributions exceed the collaboration, and the shelf beyond the famous titles is the evidence. Sweezy's Socialism of 1949 set out his systematic position between the treatise and the journal; The Present as History, in 1953, collected the essays in which his reading of the postwar settlement took shape. His two Cuba books with Huberman, in 1960 and 1969, applied the school's reporting method to a revolution in progress and were read across Latin America as documents of the debate about the island's road. The essays gathered late as Modern Capitalism and Other Essays show the theorist of the seventies, still revising. Baran's shelf is shorter and stranger: the 1952 Manchester School essay that first carried the surplus into print; the essays on underconsumption's history, on Marxism and psychoanalysis, on the intellectual's duty, gathered after his death in The Longer View of 1969, a book whose range, from the economics of backwardness to the ethics of commitment, exhibits the mind the joint book's architecture domesticated. Magdoff's shelf extends the tradition's: The Age of Imperialism of 1969, the essay collections written with Sweezy through the seventies and eighties, and a body of statistical commentary on the American economy that the journal's readers treated as a serial in itself.

The shelf matters to the standing question because schools are misjudged when they are read through one book. The joint work's category looks, in isolation, like a simplification adopted for a single argument. Across the shelf it appears as one instrument in a workshop: Sweezy expounding value theory better than its defenders in one decade, Baran measuring a poor economy's forgone output in another, the two editing a journal that published Braverman's restoration of the labour process in a third. The value critics' case is strongest against the single book's foundations and weakest against the tradition's whole output, which repeatedly supplied, from its own resources, the analyses the founding category omitted. Whether a school should be graded by its axiom or by its ecosystem is a question this profile has posed more than once, and the FAQ below carries the grading rule the series applies: the measurability trade names the axiom's price, and the ecosystem is what the price bought.

## The boundary of the countable: unpaid labour and the surplus

A category defined on national accounts inherits the accounts' boundaries, and one boundary drew a criticism from inside the Marxist camp that the school's defenders found harder to answer than the value objection. The accounts count marketed production. The labour that reproduces the workforce itself, the cooking, cleaning, nursing, and child rearing performed unpaid in households, overwhelmingly by women, stands outside the boundary, and therefore outside the surplus's arithmetic: it appears neither as a cost of producing the economy's output nor as a use of its excess, although on any material reckoning it is among the largest inputs the economy consumes. Feminist economists, several of them formed in the same heterodox milieu as the school, pressed the point from the nineteen seventies: a surplus defined on the market's perimeter silently ratifies the market's valuation of whose work counts. The criticism generalises the classification problem. The school's writers had insisted that classification is judgement and had worn their judgements openly; the feminist critics replied that the inherited boundary was itself a judgement, and not one the school had examined merely because it had inherited it from the statisticians whose conventions it otherwise delighted in exposing.

The school's responses were partial, and the profile should record them as partial. The tradition's pages carried feminist work and the journal's later decades treated the household economy with increasing seriousness, but the founding category was never rebuilt around the objection, and the surplus in its canonical form remains a magnitude of the marketed economy. The episode refines the measurability trade rather than overturning it. Measurement is never innocent of its perimeter: every magnitude a theory can estimate arrives with the exclusions of the apparatus that does the estimating, and the school's history exhibits the rule twice over, once in what its category could not derive and once in what its accounts could not see. A reader grading the school should set the two blindnesses side by side, since they spring from the same root, the decision to take the economy's own accounting as the theory's data, with all the illumination and all the occlusion that decision carries.

## The life cycle of an industry in the school's account

The school's picture of monopoly is dynamic in one respect that summaries miss: monopoly is a destination industries travel toward, not a birthmark. A new branch of production begins, on the joint book's telling, in something like the competitive condition of the textbooks, with many firms, unsettled techniques, and prices that bite into costs. Success sorts the field: the firms that grow acquire the advantages of scale, of finance raised on their own earnings, of distribution networks and brand recognition, and the branch matures into oligopoly, its giants settling into the administered peace. The pattern does double duty in the system. It explains why the competitive past of the theory was real rather than mythical, since the economy's older branches had passed through it within living memory, and it supplies the founders' answer to the objection that monopoly is an economist's exaggeration: the tendency is visible in any industry's biography, and the economy as a whole is the sum of biographies at different ages. New industries still arrive in competitive dress, the newest technologies among them, and the school's later writers watched successive waves of innovation pass through the same maturation, each cohort of entrants consolidating into the structure the founders described.

The life-cycle picture also disciplines the school's stagnation claim from within, since the young industries are precisely the sites of the investment outlet whose narrowing the absorption argument laments: a monopoly economy rich in young branches still finds places for its surplus, and the school's writers treated the great waves of innovation, railways in one century, automobiles and electronics in the next, as the historical counterweights that had postponed the stagnation their theory predicted as the norm. Readers who follow that argument into its crisis form should again consult the companion article on the stagnation theory; the point for the school's profile is that its central concept was historical through and through. Monopoly names a phase in the biography of branches and of the system, and the surplus is the magnitude whose behaviour changes as the phase advances, which is why the school's economics cannot be summarised as a static model with a political gloss. Its statics are a photograph of a motion.

## Baran against the development establishment

The growth book's stature is clearer against the orthodoxy it opposed. The development economics that the wealthy world's governments and foundations carried to the new states of Asia and Africa in the nineteen fifties taught that poverty was an original condition: the poor country lacked capital, technique, and the habits of enterprise, and would grow by receiving them in stages, as the wealthy countries were imagined to have done before it. Walt Rostow's stages of growth, published in 1960 with a subtitle advertising itself as a non-communist manifesto, gave the doctrine its canonical form. Baran's book, three years earlier, inverted every element. The poor country's poverty was not original but produced, the counterpart of the wealth accumulating elsewhere in the same system; its ruling classes were not laggards awaiting education but beneficiaries of the arrangement that kept it poor; and the capital it lacked was being generated on its own soil every year, in a potential surplus its social order misused or exported. Development, on this account, was not a technical infusion but a political rupture, since the classes in possession of the surplus would not volunteer it for accumulation that dissolved their position.

The contrast decided the terms of a generation's argument. Development studies as an academic field was built substantially in the space between Rostow's stages and Baran's surplus, and the dependency school radicalised Baran's side of the space while the modernisation school administered the other. The measurability trade appears here in its international dress: Baran's category let investigators compute what a poor economy was losing, in idle land and exported income and luxury waste, and the computation was an indictment that finance ministries and their advisers had to answer with inventories of their own. The growth book's author did not live to see the dependency debates, the import-substitution experiments, or the eventual counter-revolution in development policy, but the questions his book set, whose surplus, used by whom, under whose veto, remained the questions even of those who answered them against him. The lineage into the dependency tradition is the property of the companion article linked earlier; the profile records the founder's originality here because it is the clearest proof of the article's opening claim, that the founders' separate works outsize the famous collaboration.

## Reading a corporate report with the school's eyes

The category's practical meaning can be fixed by a small exercise the school's teachers used in spirit if not in form: take the published accounts of any giant corporation and sort its year into the school's boxes. Revenue arrives at the top. Against it stand the costs the school counts as production: materials, energy, the wages of the workers who make and move the product, the year's wear on plant. What remains is the firm's gross surplus for the year, and the report then exhibits its absorption in the firm's own handwriting. Part is paid out as dividends and interest, the property incomes of the owners and creditors. Part is consumed by the apparatus of selling: the advertising budget, the dealer network's margins, the design studio's annual restyling. Part goes to the state as taxes, the firm's contribution to the public absorption. Part swells the executive payrolls and the headquarters establishment, the supervisory layer the school counts among the surplus's uses when it exceeds coordination's real needs. And part is retained, the famous retained earnings, which the firm will invest or hold as financial assets, converting the surplus into future capacity or into a claim on another firm's.

The exercise teaches two things the definitions alone cannot. The first is the collapse of saving and investment inside the giant firm: the surplus is appropriated before it is allocated, by the same board, in the same meeting, so the economy's balance between the two is struck within corporate planning departments rather than between separate classes of savers and investors in a market. The second is the visibility of the school's classification problem at ground level. Every line of the sorting can be disputed: the research laboratory's budget is a cost of future production or a use of surplus for prestige; the brand campaign is waste or the maintenance of a real asset. The school's writers never denied the disputable character of the exercise; they proposed it because the disputes are the economics. A reader who has sorted one real report has learned the category better than a reader who has memorised three definitions, and has also learned why the value critics' demand for a magnitude that needs no sorting struck the founders as a demand for an economics without judgement, which is to say without content.

## The editorials: the school arguing in real time

A journal-edited school leaves a kind of evidence department-based traditions cannot: a monthly record of its mind meeting events. For over fifty years the journal opened with an editorial survey, the Review of the Month, written principally by Sweezy and after him by his successors, in which the school's categories were applied, in public and on deadline, to wars, elections, crashes, and revolutions as they arrived. Read in sequence, the editorials amount to a parallel history of the postwar world written from inside one framework, and they served the school as its seminar and its recruitment office at once. Students who never read the treatise learned the method from the editorials' example: begin from the surplus and its disposal, locate the class interests a policy serves, and distrust any account of events that cannot say who appropriates what.

The editorial habit also imposed a discipline the founders' books escape: the framework had to survive contact with the unforeseen. A theory of categories can wait for its historian; an editorial must answer for the month that has just happened, and the school's long run of answers constitutes a natural experiment in its framework's elasticity. Readers of the revival years often arrived through the archive, finding the editorials of the nineteen seventies on stagnation and finance more prescient than the profession's journal literature of the same months, and the school's defenders made the comparison a standing exhibit. The same archive records the misses and the misjudgements, including enthusiasms of the Maoist years that the tradition's own later editors treated with candour, and a balanced profile notes the whole run rather than the vindications alone. The institution, once again, is the point: a school with a monthly voice cannot quietly revise its past, since its past is in the libraries, and the Monthly Review tradition's willingness to argue with its own back pages in public is among the reasons its history can be written with unusual confidence about what it actually claimed.

## The world audience

The school's geography widened early and never contracted. The growth book was debated in India within years of its appearance, its inventory method taken up and disputed by economists of the planning debate there; the joint book circulated in translation across Europe and Latin America, where its account of the giant corporation met readers whose economies lived in the corporation's shadow; and the dependency debates of the seventies were bilingual affairs in which the journal's positions were answered from Santiago, Mexico City, and Frankfurt in the same season. Sweezy travelled and lectured across the socialist and non-aligned worlds, and the journal's subscription list, throughout the cold decades, read like an atlas of the century's dissidence. This audience mattered to the doctrine's shape: a school read in the poor countries could not rest with a metropolitan theory, and the pressure of its own readership pushed the tradition from the joint book's American frame toward the world-economy frame of its later decades, a development its historians treat as the audience editing the school.

There is a reciprocal point about the school's American critics. The profession that declined to house the founders nevertheless educated their readers: the treatise was assigned, quietly, in courses whose lecturers disagreed with it, because no rival exposition matched its clarity, and the joint book entered the syllabi of sociology and political science when the economics departments would not have it. The school's influence in its home country therefore ran underground, through the formation of scholars in neighbouring disciplines, and surfaced in the labour-process studies, the sociology of the corporation, and the political science of the state that neighbouring fields produced in the seventies and eighties. When the revival came, it found the tradition's ideas already naturalised in the adjacent disciplines, awaiting only the economists' permission to be cited as economics. The founders, one suspects, would have counted that route of influence a vindication of their institutional bet: what the appointments committees withheld, the republic of readers supplied.

## The texts: a reader's apparatus

A school transmitted by a press leaves a tidy archive, and the reader who wants the primary record can be directed with unusual precision. The foundation documents are three: Sweezy's treatise of 1942, available in the reprints the school's press and others have kept in circulation; Baran's growth book of 1957, most widely read in the Penguin edition introduced by R. B. Sutcliffe, whose foreword remains a serviceable map of the book's reception; and the joint work of 1966, whose preface by Sweezy is the authoritative account of the book's completion after Baran's death. Around them stand the satellite texts in order of the profile's argument: Sweezy's edited volume of 1949, which assembled the classic statements of the transformation problem by Böhm-Bawerk, Hilferding, and Bortkiewicz and served anglophone students as the debate's sourcebook for a generation; the exchange with Bettelheim on the transition to socialism, printed by the school's press in the early nineteen seventies; Braverman's labour-process study of 1974, the tradition's great internal supplement; Magdoff's Age of Imperialism of 1969 and the Sweezy and Magdoff essay collections of the eighties, among them the volume on stagnation and the financial explosion; and Baran's posthumous essays, The Longer View. Foster's biographical essay on Sweezy, written at the founder's death for the journal he had joined as editor, is the standard short life, candid about the achievement and the limits alike.

Editions carry arguments in this tradition, and two cautions belong in the apparatus. The first concerns translation: the school's key terms travel badly, since surplus in the founders' sense collides in several languages with the ordinary word for profit or for a trade balance, and readers of translations should check the renderings of the category against the definitions given in this article. The second concerns the treatise of 1942: its author's views moved, and citing its formulations as the school's position confuses the school's prehistory with its doctrine, a caution the school's critics and defenders both observe in their careful moments. The archive's centre is the journal itself, bound in the libraries of five continents, and no selection of books replaces its monthly record for the reader who wants the school as it was, arguing in real time with its century.

## The school's standing among Marxist economists

The standing this profile has circled can now be stated directly. Among Marxist economists, the school is a major tradition under a standing objection: major, because its books formed two generations, its journal outlasted every rival institution, and its questions, about monopoly, stagnation, finance, and the world hierarchy, became the field's questions even for those who rejected its answers; under objection, because its basic category is, by the value tradition's lights, a defection from the theory that makes Marxist economics a single science rather than a family of radical opinions. Both halves of the verdict are stable, and the school's writers learned to live inside the conjunction, neither readmitted to orthodoxy nor expelled from the tradition's history. Among economists at large, the standing is simpler and colder: the school is treated as a chapter in the history of heterodox thought, honoured in histories, absent from the models. Among the neighbouring disciplines, the verdict reverses again: sociologists, historians, and development scholars cite the founders as classics of their own literatures, and the growth book's place in development studies is closer to canonical than anything the value restoration achieved in economics.

The pattern of the standings is itself evidence about the measurability trade. The audiences that grade by derivation place the school lowest; the audiences that grade by documented reach place it highest; and the school's own chosen audience, the republic of readers assembled by a journal, has kept it in print for three quarters of a century, through the founders' deaths and the profession's long indifference. A tradition's survival is not proof of its truth, and this series does not grade by survival. But a profile of a school, as distinct from a theorem, must record what the school was for as faithfully as what it claimed, and the monopoly capital school was for keeping a Marxist analysis of the American and world economy in continuous public existence, at monthly intervals, under professional conditions designed to prevent exactly that. Its category made the keeping possible, by giving the analysis a magnitude it could argue from in public; the category's price, the origin account, is what the keeping cost. The trade and the institution are the same fact read at two levels, which is why this article has insisted on treating them together from its first paragraph.

## A short genealogy of the surplus idea

The school's category has ancestors, and its novelty is misstated when they are ignored. The notion that a society produces a surplus over its necessary consumption, and that the disposition of the surplus decides the society's fate, is the oldest organising idea in political economy. The physiocrats of eighteenth-century France built their system on the net product of agriculture, the excess of the harvest over the costs of the farming year, and treated every other class's income as a share of it. The classical economists generalised the form: an economy's surplus, over the replacement of its inputs and the subsistence of its labourers, is the fund from which rent, profit, and accumulation are drawn, and the great classical questions were questions about the surplus's size and division. Marx inherited the schema and transformed it by locating the surplus's production in the labour process and measuring it in labour time, converting a magnitude of account into a relation of exploitation. The twentieth century's other great revival of the classical schema, the work around Sraffa treated elsewhere in this cluster, kept the surplus at the centre while discarding the labour measure for a system of physical quantities and prices.

Baran's category belongs to that longer lineage, and the school's writers knew it. The economic surplus is the classical net product rebuilt for an economy of national accounts: the excess of output over the costs of producing it, with the costs and the excess reckoned in the money magnitudes a statistical state publishes. The genealogy cuts both ways in the dispute, and both cuts should be exhibited. For the school, the ancestry dignifies the category: the surplus approach is not an improvisation but the recovery, under monopoly conditions, of political economy's founding question, which the marginalist century had suppressed in favour of allocation at the margin. For the critics, the genealogy is the indictment in historical dress: Marx's transformation of the schema was the science's decisive advance, the step that explained the surplus instead of merely exhibiting it, and a school that returns to the pre-Marxian form of the question has surrendered the advance while keeping the vocabulary of the tradition that made it. The reader who holds the genealogy steady can see the whole dispute as an argument about whether Marx's step beyond the classical schema was a completion or a detour, which is a finer statement of the quarrel than either camp usually permits itself.

## The road not taken: monopolistic competition

The founders were not the first economists of their generation to declare the competitive model obsolete, and the company they declined to join illuminates their choice. In 1933, Edward Chamberlin at Harvard and Joan Robinson at Cambridge had independently rebuilt price theory around monopolistic and imperfect competition, in which each firm faces a downward-sloping demand for its differentiated product and the economy's resting state is a tangle of small monopolies. Sweezy worked inside that literature as a young Harvard economist, contributing its most durable device, the kinked demand curve of 1939, which explained the rigidity of oligopoly prices by the asymmetric expectations of rivals. The school's later doctrine can be read as that literature's radicalisation by other means: where Chamberlin kept the individual firm and its demand curve and treated monopoly as a friction on a competitive norm, Baran and Sweezy inverted the norm itself, treating the administered price of the giant as the economy's normal fact and competition as the survival. The difference is not emphasis. Monopolistic competition is a theory of prices with the social structure left untouched; the school's monopoly capital is a theory of a social order with prices as one of its expressions, and the surplus category is what the theory needs once the unit of analysis moves from the firm to the whole economy's excess.

The comparison also fixes the school's distance from the Marxist orthodoxy in a second direction. Orthodox Marxist economics, no less than Chamberlin, kept the competitive economy as its reference formation, since Marx's value analysis was read as the theory of that formation's successor in an unbroken line. The school alone among the Marxist traditions of its time built its economics on the proposition that the reference formation itself had changed character, which is why it found itself arguing on two fronts, against a mainstream that thought monopoly a special case and against a Marxism that thought it a surface. Two-front wars are the usual fate of stage theories, and the founders' willingness to fight on both is a large part of the school's distinct flavour in the literature: it is the only Marxist economics of its century that the orthodox of both camps accused, in the same decade, of betraying their respective founders.

## The transition debate: Sweezy as a controversialist

Between the treatise and the journal's maturity stands an episode that displays Sweezy's powers in the mode his critics most respected, open controversy on a question of pure theory and history. In 1950 he reviewed Maurice Dobb's Studies in the Development of Capitalism, the standard Marxist account of the passage from feudalism, and dissented at the root: where Dobb located the solvent of the feudal order inside it, in the inefficiencies of production for use and the flight of the serfs, Sweezy assigned the decisive role to the growth of trade and of production for distant markets, which corroded the old relations from outside. The exchange that followed, in the pages of Science and Society across the nineteen fifties, drew in Dobb's reply, the Japanese scholar Kohachiro Takahashi, the English historian Rodney Hilton, and others, and it remains the classic statement of the internalist and externalist cases in the feudalism debate, assigned to students two generations later as the transition debate. Its relevance here is temperamental and institutional. The man who conducted it was no populariser manqué but a controversialist whom the leading Marxist historian of the British school treated as the opponent to beat; and the debate's venue, a small Marxist quarterly arguing at full scholarly stretch, was the world the founders inhabited before the journal they owned made them independent of such venues.

The episode also complicates the school's portrait in a way the profile should welcome. Sweezy's externalism in the transition debate, his stress on trade and the market as solvents of an old order, sits in visible tension with the joint book's later stress on production relations and monopoly structure, and the critics of the surplus category have occasionally used the early essay as evidence that its author's Marxism was always more Ricardian and market-centred than the doctrine he inherited. The school's defenders read the same essay as proof of intellectual honesty: a theorist willing to rule against the emerging orthodoxy of his own camp, on evidence, in the camp's own journal, at the height of the cold war's disciplines. Either reading supports the profile's central finding, that the substitution of 1966 was the act of a mind with a lifelong record of revising inherited doctrine where it judged the doctrine wrong, announced in the open and defended in the open. Schools founded by such minds are not refuted by being labelled unorthodox; they are answered, as this one was, in kind.

## Managers and owners: the school's refusal of a new ruling class

One further doctrine of the joint book's world deserves record, because it separated the school from a fashionable thesis of its century. The separation of ownership from control in the giant corporation had persuaded many observers, most famously James Burnham in his book on the managerial revolution of 1941, that a new class of managers was displacing the capitalists as the economy's rulers, and that the categories of ownership and profit were dissolving into administration. Sweezy had reviewed Burnham's argument at its appearance and rejected it, and the school never wavered: the manager of a giant corporation, however secure his tenure and however grand his salary, is a functionary of capital whose career is governed by the growth and profitability of the capital in his charge, and whose consumption, power, and outlook are those of the appropriating class he serves. The legal owners' retreat from the boardroom changes the personnel of appropriation, not its structure, and the surplus flows to the same destinations under new signatures.

The school's refusal mattered to its category. A theory that had accepted the managerial thesis would have had to rebuild its account of the surplus's destination, since managers who rule in their own right appropriate the excess for ends of their own, growth, security, empire, rather than for property income. The school held the older line: executive salaries beyond the price of the managerial labour itself are a share of the surplus, distributed inside the appropriating class, and the corporation's drive to accumulate is the capital relation reproducing itself through salaried hands. Later scholarship on executive pay and on the financialisation of the firm has kept the question alive, and the school's successors have had to argue it again against each generation's rediscovery of the manager as a free-standing power. The founders' answer, unglamorous and structural, is among the least dated parts of their system, and it preserves the continuity the value critics denied them: on the class anatomy of the corporation, if on nothing else, the school stood exactly where the classical Marxism it revised had stood.

## How an estimate of the surplus is assembled

Since the school's empirical claim is a procedure rather than a number, the procedure itself should be exhibited once, in the abstract form its practitioners used. The investigator begins from the published national product, the whole output of the economy in money for a chosen year. From it she deducts the costs she rules to be necessary: the materials and energy consumed, the year's wear on the capital stock, the wages of the workers engaged in producing and moving goods and in the services every society requires. The remainder is the actual surplus in its money dress, and the investigator then exhibits its disposal by following the accounts outward: the property incomes recorded, the outlays on selling and advertising traced through the industries that supply them, the budgets of the state divided between the functions the investigator counts as social services and those she counts as absorption, the investment that enlarges capacity. Potential surplus demands a second pass against the standard of full and rational use: the output lost to idle plant and unemployed labour is added by estimate, the production of the ruling's unproductive sectors is reclassified, and the distance between the economy as found and the economy as it might run is stated as a sum of identified items, each open to challenge.

The genre's discipline lies in the itemisation. An estimate in the school's manner is a list of argued decisions with figures attached, and its scholarly use was always as an agenda for dispute line by line, not as a verdict. The tradition's own exercises of the kind were typically partial, aimed at one component, the sales effort's growth or the military budget's weight, rather than at a single total for the whole economy, and the school's writers were correspondingly cautious about the totals that journalists extracted from their pages. This article follows their caution exactly, in conformity with the protocol for contested magnitudes that governs the series: no surplus share, no concentration ratio, and no markup figure is stated anywhere in these pages as fact, because the literature's figures differ by the classifications that produced them, and a reference work that prints one school's conventions as nature's constants has taken sides while pretending to report. What can be reported is the procedure and its logic, and those the reader now has.

## Debt: the household drafted as an absorber

The tradition's later decades added a channel of absorption its founders had treated as minor, and the addition shows the framework learning from its object. A stagnating economy whose wages are held down by the same monopoly structure that raises its surplus faces a demand problem at the household level: the population whose consumption must clear the market is paid too little to clear it from income. The resolution the system found, across the last decades of the twentieth century and the first of the next, was household debt on a mass scale: mortgages, credit cards, student loans, and the whole apparatus of consumer finance, which allowed spending to run ahead of wages for a generation while transferring a growing share of household income to the financial sector as interest. The school's later writers, Magdoff among them in his final essays and the journal's contributors after him, read the debt expansion as the absorption problem's domestic form: the surplus, unable to find sufficient outlets in investment, was lent to the households whose demand the system required, and the claims accumulated in the financial sector whose explosion Sweezy and Magdoff had already chronicled.

The reading welded the school's two late themes, stagnation and financialisation, into a single mechanism, since the debt that absorbed the surplus in the spending stream simultaneously fed the financial explosion in the claims stream, and the crisis of 2008 arrived, in the tradition's telling, as the moment the two streams collided: households could no longer service the claims, and the claims' collapse revealed the stagnation beneath. Economists of other schools dispute every joint of that telling, and the mainstream's own accounts of the crisis assign the causes elsewhere, to monetary policy, to global imbalances, to regulatory failure. The profile records the school's reading as its reading, noting only that a framework built in 1966 on the difficulty of absorbing a surplus proved, in its successors' hands, capable of narrating the greatest crisis of the following century without changing its premises, a feat of elasticity that its admirers count as vindication and its critics as the elasticity of a framework that can narrate anything. The reader who has followed the measurability trade this far will recognise the double edge without prompting.

## Reading the joint book: its architecture

Reference readers often meet the school's name before its text, and a walk through the joint book's architecture is the quickest cure for the summaries. The book opens by clearing the ground: its first movement sets the competitive economy of the classical theory beside the monopoly economy of the giant corporation and announces the surplus as the instrument for the second. The middle movement develops the instrument. A chapter on the corporation examines the giant firm as the system's typical actor, its pricing, its internal finance, its drive to grow. Then comes the book's analytical hinge, the argument that the surplus tends to rise under monopoly, developed through the corporation's cost discipline and its pricing power together. Around the hinge the book arranges its account of absorption: the owners' consumption, inherently limited; investment, inherently self-defeating as an outlet since it enlarges the capacity that must be fed; the sales effort, swelling to a second economy of persuasion; and the state, whose civilian and military budgets close the argument's economic half. The final movement leaves the ledger for the civilisation: chapters on the society the surplus builds, on its racial order, its culture of the automobile and the suburb, and on the irrationality that the authors present as the system's deepest product, a social order whose waste is not a malfunction but a requirement.

Two features of the architecture repay attention. The first is the distribution of weight: the purely economic machinery occupies less than half the book, and any reading that stops at the stagnation chapters has read a pamphlet inside a larger work. The founders intended a critique of a civilisation with an economics at its centre, in the manner of the Marx whose Manuscripts and whose chapters on the working day join the analysis of value to the description of a world, and the book's afterlife in sociology and cultural studies is a response to that intention rather than a misunderstanding of it. The second feature is the argument's candour about its instrument: the surplus is introduced as a concept to be judged by its fruits, the founders explicitly invite the reader to test it against the economy's record, and the book's confidence is of the kind that expects examination. Examiners of the school, favourable and hostile, have taken the invitation for sixty years, which is the plainest measure of the book's success as a scientific act whatever the verdict on its category.

## Tendencies and countertendencies: the school's method of law

The school's characteristic claims are tendency statements, and the form deserves a section because it is the founders' most direct inheritance from Marx and the point at which hostile methodologists press hardest. A tendency, in the usage Marx bequeathed, is a pressure the system's structure exerts continuously, whose visible outcome at any date is the resultant of the pressure and of everything pushing the other way. The rising surplus is such a pressure: monopoly pricing and cost discipline push the surplus share upward always, while wars, innovations, new industries, and the state's improvisations push the outcome around it. The form is indispensable to the school's long arguments, since it lets a tendency be confirmed by the direction of the confounders' exertions as well as by the outcome: that the economy required a world war's mobilisations, a suburban building boom, and a permanent arms budget to keep its surplus absorbed is, for the school, the tendency's fingerprint rather than its refutation.

The methodological objection writes itself, and the school's critics wrote it: a tendency compatible with every outcome explains none, and a framework whose failures are always the countertendencies' fault has insured itself against evidence. The founders' reply, scattered through the editorials where the framework met events monthly, amounts to a demand for symmetric treatment. Every economics of a complex system traffics in pressures and offsets, from the price mechanism's own tendencies, which no recession is allowed to falsify, to the demographic transitions of the growth textbooks. What distinguishes science from insurance, on the school's account, is whether the countertendencies are named in advance with their mechanisms, as the joint book named the sales effort, the state's budgets, and the epoch-making innovations before the outcomes they were summoned to explain. The article records the exchange without a verdict, noting only its consequence for readers: the school's claims should be graded as a theory of pressures is graded, by the specificity of its mechanisms and the honesty of its ledger of offsets, and not by the courtroom standard of the single decisive fact. That grading rule is the same one the measurability trade applies to the category, and it is fitting that the school's method and its magnitude should be acquitted or convicted together.

## The school's progeny inside the academy

The founders never held the posts from which academic lineages are usually founded, yet the tradition reproduced itself inside the universities by a side door, and the progeny is part of the profile. In 1968, the year of Huberman's death and of the student movements the journal covered with sympathy, radical economists in the United States founded the Union for Radical Political Economics, an association that gave the heterodox a professional address, a journal, and a job market of their own. The founders of the school stood to the new association as elders rather than officers, but its formation marked the moment the milieu the journal had sustained in exile acquired institutional form inside the academy the founders had been refused. A second progeny followed in the research programmes the tradition seeded. The social structure of accumulation framework, developed by David Gordon with Richard Edwards and Michael Reich, organised the postwar American economy into long phases of institutional stability and decay, an architecture whose debts to the Monthly Review tradition's periodisation and to its stagnation problematic were acknowledged by its authors. Labour studies after Braverman, dependency studies after Baran, and the political economy of the media after the journal's cultural criticism compose a wider diaspora of the same kind.

The progeny's character confirms the profile's running finding. What the tradition transmitted most successfully was not its category, which each successor programme modified or replaced, but its questions and its institutional example: the insistence that the economy be studied as a social order in history, that the accounts be made to answer, and that a heterodox science needs presses and journals of its own if it is to outlive the profession's weather. Academic traditions usually reproduce through students who cite the founders' theorems; this one reproduced through scholars who cited its founders' example while rebuilding its theorems, a looser and perhaps hardier form of descent. The school's historians have noted the irony without embarrassment. A tradition founded by men the universities excluded ended by colonising the universities' margins so thoroughly that its absence from the economics curriculum now requires more explanation than its presence in all the neighbouring ones.

## The commitment of an intellectual: Baran's self-portrait

Baran's most personal text explains the founders to themselves, and a profile should end its biographical work there. In a lecture delivered in 1961 and published in the journal, Baran defined the intellectual by a single obligation: to tell the truth as he sees it about the society he lives in, regardless of the cost to his comfort and standing, and he measured his contemporaries in the profession against the definition with a severity that spared neither the silent nor the careerist. The lecture became a small classic of its genre, quoted for decades by scholars deciding whether to sign, to speak, or to keep their posts, and it supplies the missing link between the school's economics and its biography. The surplus framework's candour, its insistence on naming the waste and the class destinations of the social product, is the lecture's obligation translated into method: an economics that classified the sales effort as productive and the arms budget as beyond question would be, by Baran's standard, not a technical error but a failure of the intellectual's defining duty.

Sweezy's parallel self-portrait is the biography itself: the resignation letter never sent because he resigned first, the refusal to answer at Concord's successor proceedings in New Hampshire, the fifty years of monthly deadlines met from a farmhouse rather than a faculty office. The founders did not ask to be admired, and this series does not admire on request; it records that the school's doctrines and its founders' conduct form an unusually legible unity, and that the unity is evidence a historian must weigh in explaining the tradition's hold on its readers. Men who paid for their analysis with their careers were believed when they wrote that the economy's arrangements were also paid for, by others, in other coin. The connection is rhetorical rather than logical, and the school's critics have always been entitled to insist on the difference. But the history of a school is not a logic seminar, and the authority the founders exercised over two generations of students they never taught was compounded of the category, the institution, and the example in proportions no reader of the period's memoirs can honestly separate.

## Five misreadings to correct

The school's reception has generated a standard set of errors, and a reference profile should correct them in one place. The first misreading treats the school as a variant of orthodox Marxist economics, one more dialect of the doctrine descending from Capital. The whole record refutes it: the school deliberately replaced the doctrine's central category, announced the replacement, and was denounced for it by the orthodoxy's guardians for fifty years. Whatever the school is, orthodoxy is the one thing it declined to be. The second misreading takes economic surplus as a synonym for surplus value, a loose modernisation of the same idea. The substitution table in this article exists to kill that error: the two magnitudes differ in definition, in what they can measure, in what they say about origins, and in what they cannot do, and conflating them dissolves the dispute that defines the school's standing. The third misreading reduces the school's contribution to the stagnation thesis, its most famous prediction, when the thesis is one application of a category with a wider career, in development economics, in the labour process debates the school's press enabled, and in the finance phase its successors named. A school is its instruments and its institution, not its best-known forecast.

The fourth misreading treats the two founders as interchangeable, a corporate author named Baran-and-Sweezy. The biographical sections of this article were written against it: the Russian-formed development economist with the chair and the polemicist's temper, and the Harvard-formed expositor with the editor's patience, produced separate books that stand separately in the literature, founded different subfields, and are still cited separately by the scholars who use them. The fifth misreading, the subtlest, supposes that the abandonment of the labour theory of value was an oversight, a gap the founders would have repaired had it been pointed out. It was pointed out, at length, in their lifetimes, in their own journal's wider milieu, and the founders answered with the argument this article has stated as their defence: the categories of value belonged, on their reading, to the competitive formation, and the monopoly economy required a magnitude of its own. To disagree with that answer is legitimate and common. To mistake it for an oversight is to have read neither the joint book's opening chapters nor its authors' replies, and a reader who corrects the five errors holds the school in the shape its friends and its enemies, at their most careful, agree it has.

## The measurability trade as a series pattern

The series files this article's claim under a pattern, and the filing deserves a closing word because it governs how the whole cluster should be read. Again and again in the economics written after Marx, a school gains empirical purchase by adopting a category that can be measured, and loses in the same transaction the explanatory content the original category carried. The reconstructions of the transformation problem exhibited the pattern as a tractability divide: rival solutions purchased formal consistency at prices denominated in fidelity to the text or in the theory's explanatory ambitions. The monopoly capital school is the pattern's boldest instance, because its purchase was the most public and its price the most frankly acknowledged. It bought the ability to argue about the actual economy, in the economy's own figures, before the widest audience any Marxist economics of its century commanded, and it paid by surrendering the derivation that made the surplus an accusation rather than a statistic. Whether the bargain was wise depends on what a critic believes a Marxist economics is for, and the school's long reception is best read as that referendum conducted over sixty years, in reviews, in reading lists, in the founding of rival journals, and in the return of readers whenever the economy itself restaged the school's questions. The referendum has never closed. This article's duty has been to state the terms of the bargain exactly, and on those terms, the category substitution table, the reader may now vote with full knowledge of the price on both sides.

## The category's birth certificate: Baran's backwardness essay

The surplus concept has a datable first appearance, and the date matters to its interpretation. In an essay published in the Manchester School in 1952, on the political economy of backwardness, Baran set out the distinction that became his signature: an underdeveloped economy's actual surplus, the saving it manages under its existing order, is characteristically tiny beside its potential surplus, the saving available if the economy's resources were organised for accumulation rather than for the reproduction of its ruling classes' position. The essay was written from the poor countries' side of the world economy, before the growth book expanded it into a system, and its analysis of why the gap persists, idle resources beside luxury imports, landlords beside landless labourers, local wealth beside foreign drains, remains the school's founding document in its purest form. Read against the treatise Sweezy had published a decade earlier, the essay also marks the exact point at which the school's two halves can be seen approaching each other: the interpreter of Marxist categories for the advanced economy, and the analyst of the poor economies quietly replacing the categories with one of his own. The joint book is unintelligible unless the essay is kept in view, since the surplus arrives in the collaboration fully formed from Baran's workshop, and the division of intellectual labour the profile has insisted on is nowhere clearer than in the concept's birth certificate.

## The joint book's racial order

Among the joint book's chapters, the one on the position of black Americans has had the longest independent life, and it belongs in this profile because it shows the surplus framework doing work no rival Marxist economics of the time attempted. The founders argued that the racial order was not a survival decorating the economy but a mechanism inside it: a stratum of the population held by law, custom, and violence in the worst-paid and least protected employments supplied the system with labour at a discount, divided the working class against itself, and furnished the possessing classes with a reserve whose existence disciplined the wages of all. The surplus analysis framed the mechanism exactly: discrimination raised the excess the economy extracted by lowering the cost at which a portion of its labour was reproduced, and the same order then consumed part of the excess in the apparatus of its own enforcement. Later scholarship on the economics of racial division, in several disciplines, has argued with the chapter's details for sixty years, which is the reception accorded to founding texts. The chapter also fixed the school's distance from a purely economic Marxism: the founders treated the racial order as co-constitutive of the American capitalism they analysed, not as an external prejudice awaiting the economy's verdict, and the tradition's subsequent historians have counted that treatment among the joint book's permanent contributions.

## The school's dialogue with Lenin

The school's relation to Lenin's theory of imperialism is the clearest case of its general method toward the classics: retain the stage, revise the mechanism. Lenin had built his account of the monopoly stage on the fusion of bank and industrial capital into finance capital, taking the banks' dominance as the epoch's signature. Sweezy, already in the treatise, treated that mechanism as a historically specific German pattern mistaken for the general case, and the joint book completed the demotion: the giant corporation finances itself from its own surplus, the banks serve rather than command it, and the epoch's signature is the surplus's absorption problem rather than any institution's supremacy. The school's successors restored finance to the centre, as this article has recorded, but on the school's own terms, as the surplus's claimant rather than industry's master, so the distance from Lenin's formulation survived the restoration. The argument generalises. Toward every classic the school adopted the posture its founders had adopted toward Marx's value theory: the text is a method for analysing a formation, not a mechanism to be worshipped after the formation changes, and fidelity consists in redoing the analysis for the new stage. Orthodoxy's guardians read the posture as lèse-majesté toward the entire canon at once, which in a sense it was, since a licence to revise Marx's categories on historical grounds cannot stop at Marx's categories. The school lived with the implication openly, and its debates with the Leninist parties of its time, conducted in the journal across decades, are the record of a tradition that preferred its method to its membership.

## The permanent arms economy: a rival absorber

The absorption doctrine met its sharpest rival inside the heterodox camp, and the exchange refined both sides. The permanent arms economy thesis, developed by Michael Kidron in the rival socialist tradition of the International Socialists, held that arms expenditure solved the system's surplus problem in a stronger sense than the school allowed: by withdrawing resources into products that neither enlarge capacity nor raise consumption, military spending counteracted the tendency of profit rates to fall, and the postwar boom rested on the arms budget as its keystone. The school's writers treated the mechanism with respect and the conclusion with resistance. Arms spending was, in their accounts, one absorber among several, privileged by the state's readiness to fund it but sharing the limits of the genus: it could employ the surplus without enlarging the surplus-generating capacity, which is precisely its attraction, yet its scale was set by politics and rivalry rather than by the economy's need, and no single absorber could carry a permanent explanation of a boom with many props. The debate's residue in the literature is a shared vocabulary of unproductive expenditure and a permanent question about which expenditures stabilise and which destabilise, and it stands in this profile as evidence that the school's categories were the common property of a wider argument, disputed most seriously by the rivals who had most use for them.

## The fiscal crisis: the school's state theory grows a successor

The state side of the absorption doctrine produced the tradition's most influential single successor work, and its lineage runs straight through the surplus. James O'Connor's The Fiscal Crisis of the State, published in 1973 from within the journal's wider milieu, argued that the capitalist state must perform two functions that expand against each other: it must underwrite accumulation, by funding the infrastructure, training, and research the monopolies require, and it must underwrite legitimation, by funding the welfare that makes the order tolerable, while the surplus that might pay for both is privately appropriated and politically defended against taxation. The structural gap between the state's expenditures and its revenues is then not a policy error but the fiscal expression of the surplus's private appropriation, and the crisis arrives as the state's budget rather than as the market's glut. The argument translated the school's framework into the era's idiom of public finance and carried it into sociology and political science, where O'Connor's categories organised a generation of research on the welfare state's strains. For this profile its significance is genealogical: a doctrine born in a category substitution, and dismissed by the value orthodoxy as an accounting trick, proved fertile enough to restate the theory of the state for neighbouring sciences, the exact form of influence, transmission by fertility rather than by fidelity, that the school's institutional history keeps exhibiting.

## Mandel's engagement: the strongest Marxist answer

The weightiest Marxist engagement with the school came from Ernest Mandel, the Belgian theorist of the rival Trotskyist tradition, whose Late Capitalism subjected the joint book to the compliment of a full-scale alternative. Mandel accepted much of the school's description: the monopoly stage, the weight of unproductive expenditure, the arms economy's role, the surplus's disposal as the system's governing problem. He refused the premise. Working within the value theory the founders had set aside, he reconstructed the same phenomena as expressions of the rate of profit's movements and of the long waves of accumulation, arguing that the school's surplus, for all its descriptive power, was a surface magnitude whose motions only the value framework could explain. The exchange between the traditions, Sweezy's reviews and Mandel's chapters answering each other across the nineteen seventies, is the intra-Marxist debate at its highest level in that generation, and students are still sent to it because both parties state the other's case recognisably before refusing it. Its bearing on this article's namable claim is direct: Mandel's reconstruction is the measurability trade read from the other side, a demonstration of what the retained category could still buy in explanatory depth, priced in a formalism that confined its readership to initiates. The two traditions' subsequent fortunes, the school's public persistence and Mandel's school-founding influence within a narrower world, restage the trade at the level of whole oeuvres.

## The ecological turn: the surplus meets the rift

The tradition's most unexpected later growth grew from the same rootstock, and a complete profile must register it. Under John Bellamy Foster's editorship, the journal developed an ecological Marxism built on the concept of the metabolic rift, Marx's own term for the rupture in the material interchange between society and nature that capitalist agriculture and industry produce. The connection to this article's category is structural rather than verbal: the surplus framework had always counted the economy's outputs against its real costs, and the ecological writers pressed the count further, asking what the accounts omit when nature's throughput is treated as free. The waste the joint book had catalogued as irrational expenditure reappeared, in the ecological literature, as material throughput, the physical burden of an absorption-driven economy on soils, climates, and bodies, and the founders' question, what the surplus is used for, acquired a planetary ledger. Critics of the school found the turn further evidence of a framework elastic enough to absorb any content; its historians read it as the tradition's third life, after the development debates and the finance revival, and as proof that the institution's continuity could still generate new research programmes rather than merely curate old ones. The profile records the turn neutrally, as evidence about the school's reproductive powers, which are the subject of a school's profile in a way they cannot be for a theorem's.

## Huberman's other book: the school's popular prehistory

The third founder's fame once exceeded the economists', and the fact explains the journal's instincts. Leo Huberman's Man's Worldly Goods, published in 1936, retold the economic history of the world from feudalism to the present as a story of who gets what and why, in prose a newspaper reader could follow, and it sold in quantities no Marxist treatise approached, carrying the rudiments of a materialist history to a mass audience a decade before the journal existed. Teachers assigned it, unions reprinted it, and its author's name on the new journal's masthead in 1949 told the founding readership what register the enterprise intended. The school's prose conscience descends from that book: the conviction that economic argument earns its authority in the open, that the reader's intelligence is the tribunal, and that a category which cannot survive translation into plain speech has failed a test no seminar can waive. The surplus concept itself bears the mark, since it is the only central category of twentieth-century Marxist economics that a reader of Man's Worldly Goods could carry away in an afternoon and apply the same evening to a newspaper's budget report. Orthodoxy's categories were designed for adequacy to a text; the school's were designed, by the populariser who co-founded its institution, for adequacy to a public, and the measurability trade was struck under his influence as surely as under the treatise-writer's.

## The correspondence: the collaboration in its own words

The joint book's making is unusually well documented, because the founders' letters survive and have been published. The correspondence between Baran and Sweezy across the years of their collaboration, issued by the school's press in a selected edition under the title The Age of Monopoly Capital and edited by Nicholas Baran, the founder's son, with Foster, shows the substitution being negotiated in real time: drafts of chapters travelling between Palo Alto and New Hampshire, disagreements about emphasis argued out in long letters, the older interpretive project's materials gradually reorganised around the growth economist's category. Historians of economics rarely receive so complete a record of a school's founding act, and the volume has become the first source for the questions this profile has had to decide: which founder carried which argument, how the joint voice was manufactured from two very different ones, and what the dying Baran knew of the book's completion. The letters also document the friendship, its courtesies and its strains under the pressure of the nineteen fifties' politics, and a researcher who reads them after the joint book reads the book differently, hearing the two voices the published text had fused. The school's transparency about its own archive is of a piece with everything else in this profile: a tradition conducted in public can be studied in public, and the study, as the revival showed, is one of the mechanisms by which a school outlives its founders.

## Luxemburg's bequest: the realisation problem

The absorption problematic has a grandmother, and the school acknowledged her. Rosa Luxemburg's Accumulation of Capital had argued in 1913 that the surplus value of a closed capitalist system cannot be fully realised by the consumption of its own classes, so that capitalism survives by trading with and devouring the non-capitalist world beyond its borders. Sweezy's treatise examined her argument with a respect unusual among Marx's editors, accepting its question while rejecting its arithmetic: the realisation difficulty she had overstated into an impossibility became, in the school's hands, the chronic absorption problem of the monopoly stage, a tendency to be managed rather than a wall to be struck. Joan Robinson, whose introduction carried Luxemburg's book to English readers, occupied the adjacent ground from the Keynesian side, and the three names mark the century's great triangle of underconsumptionist thought. The lineage matters for grading the school. Its stagnation doctrine was not an improvisation of the nineteen sixties but the third generation of a question posed before the First World War, each generation armed with better accounts and a larger surplus to explain. It also explains the school's confidence: a problematic that had survived its author's murder, its refutation by Bukharin, and its translation into two successor frameworks had earned its inheritors' patience when the profession pronounced it dead for the fourth time in 1990.

## The year's other prophecy: Sweezy and Schumpeter

The treatise appeared in the same year as the century's other great prophecy of capitalism's end, and the coincidence joins two Harvard lives. Joseph Schumpeter's Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy argued that capitalism would perish of its successes, bureaucratised by its own corporations and delegitimised by the intellectual class it bred. Sweezy had served as the older man's assistant at Harvard in the nineteen thirties, and the two maintained a documented mutual regard across every political line the century drew: the Austrian theorist of the entrepreneur and his Marxist former assistant agreed that the competitive economy of the textbooks was gone, that the large corporation had replaced its heroics with administration, and that the system's future would be decided by politics and culture as much as by markets. Where they differed was the valuation of the corpse. Schumpeter mourned the civilisation that monopoly administration was dissolving; the school indicted the dissolution's price list, in wasted surplus and stunted lives. The pairing disciplines a common misreading of the school's founders as provincial agitators: Sweezy's formation ran through the strongest non-Marxist theory of capitalist development his university possessed, and the surplus framework's stress on the corporation's internal finance and administrative pricing is Schumpeter's corporate landscape surveyed from the other camp. Schools, like armies, are known by the enemies they were trained against, and the school's first enemy was never ignorance.

## The school's vocabulary, held steady

A school that argues for three quarters of a century accumulates a vocabulary whose terms drift unless a reference work pins them, and the founders' usage can be stated as a short discipline. Surplus unqualified means the economic surplus of the definitions, never a trade balance and never a firm's profit, though profits are among its components. Generation names the surplus's production in the economy; absorption names its disposal through consumption, investment, waste, and the state, and the pair must never be blended, since the school's stagnation claim lives entirely on the absorption side. Waste, in the school's usage, is a technical term before it is an insult: it names expenditure that neither reproduces the economy's capacity nor serves ends the expenditure's defenders can state to the society that bears it, and the term's contested edge, where one reader's waste is another's civilised amenity, is the classification problem the article has exhibited rather than a blemish to be hidden. Administered price names the giant corporation's posted price, held across demand's swings and altered by decision, as against the market-clearing price of the textbooks. Potential surplus, finally, is Baran's term of comparison and must keep its if: it measures an economy against its own possibilities, and citing it as a flow anyone received is the reader's error the growth book's critics most enjoyed attributing to its author. A reader who holds the five terms at these settings can read the journal's whole run without anachronism.

## The school among American Marxisms

The school's singularity is clearest against its domestic competition. American Marxism in the founders' century offered three other institutional forms: the Communist Party's theoretical apparatus, disciplined to a line set elsewhere; the Trotskyist movement press, brilliant and schismatic, for which economics took second place to the Russian question; and the academic Marxism of Science and Society and its circle, scholarly, embattled, and dependent on the profession's shrinking tolerance. The journal defined itself against all three by the fact of its independence: owned by its editors, answerable to its subscribers, free to print the Chinese revolution's defenders and its critics in adjacent issues, free to lose arguments with its own readers in its own correspondence columns. Sweezy and Huberman were not party members, and the journal's refusal of every discipline but the evidence became its trademark and its vulnerability alike, since it could call on no organisation when the subpoenas came. The party's theoreticians returned the compliment with polemics against the school's economics that matched the value orthodoxy's in tone, and the school wore the double hostility as the founders wore their careers, as the price of the only condition under which they were willing to work. American intellectual history has few institutions of comparable duration founded on so simple a refusal, and the school's historiography, a cottage industry of its own by now, returns to the refusal as the explanation of everything else, the category's freedom included: only editors whom no bureau could instruct could have let a growth economist retire Marx's central concept in their flagship book.

## The accounts' politics: what the surplus taught the statisticians' critics

The school's category had a half-life in a neighbouring argument that deserves record, since it carried the founders' instincts to readers who never opened the joint book. The national accounts count every marketed activity as product: the arms budget and the hospital, the advertisement and the meal enter the totals by the same door, at their prices. Baran's distinction between the costs of production and the uses of a surplus was, among other things, an early licence to read the accounts against the grain, separating the output a society needs from the output its arrangements impose, and the licence was taken up wherever the century's accounting came under moral scrutiny. The later debates on measuring welfare beyond the gross product, the feminist exposure of the accounts' blindness to unpaid labour, and the environmental accountants' insistence that depletion is not income all reinvent, in their own vocabularies, the growth book's founding gesture: the refusal to let the official total define the economic reality it purports to summarise. The school did not cause those debates, and this profile does not claim a parentage the record will not bear. It claims a kinship of method, and notes that the kinship was visible to the participants: the critics of the accounts cite Baran across the decades with a regularity that the economists who dismissed him might find instructive. A category built to be argued with has been argued with far beyond the boundaries of the school that built it, which is perhaps the most literal vindication the measurability trade can receive: the measure escaped its makers and went to work in other people's arguments.

## The stagflation decade: the editorials meet their hardest month

Every long framework meets a decade that does not fit, and the school's was the nineteen seventies, when stagnation arrived married to inflation in a combination its categories had not forecast. The editorials of those years are the tradition's most instructive documents precisely because the fit failed: month by month the journal's writers worked the surplus framework against rising prices and idle capacity together, assigning the inflation to monopoly pricing power and the state's accommodation, and the stagnation to the absorption problem in its classic form, an analysis that treated the decade's puzzle as the two halves of the monopoly economy asserting themselves simultaneously. Rival readings assigned the decade to oil, to unions, to monetary error, and the school's version convinced few outside its readership. Yet the episode belongs in the profile as the framework's probity test. A monthly journal cannot wait for history to choose its facts, and the school's willingness to publish its working, errors included, in real time is the same transparency that makes its archive valuable and its historiography possible. The revival's readers, arriving after 2008 with the editorials of the seventies on the screen before them, could grade the school's honesty for themselves; traditions that revise their back pages quietly forfeit that examination, and this one never did.

## The functionalism objection

A methodological charge against the joint book recurred too often, and from too many quarters, to omit from the strongest-objection ledger. The book explains the sales effort, the arms budget, the suburban form, and much else by the service each performs for the surplus's absorption, and the objection holds that explaining an institution by its function is not explaining it at all: no board votes to absorb the surplus, no ministry designs the system that the argument describes as designed. The founders' implicit reply runs through their editorials' practice. Their claims were never that anyone intends the absorption, but that institutions which fail to absorb are selected against, by bankruptcy, electoral defeat, or crisis, while institutions that absorb persist, so that the economy's shape is the resultant of a blind filtration rather than of a plan. Whether that reply satisfies is a genuine question in the philosophy of social science, and the Marxist critics who pressed the charge, Mandel among them, were pressing a point the value framework answers more cleanly, since the rate of profit's movements give its institutions a motor with named drivers. The profile records the objection as the methodological form of the measurability trade: the school's category, purchased for its empirical reach, arrives without the causal engine the older theory supplied, and its explanations must borrow motors, selection, class veto, state accommodation, from the surrounding sociology. Readers who find the borrowed motors sufficient will grade the trade a bargain; readers who demand the engine will grade it a mortgage. The school lived on the mortgage for sixty years, and its house is still standing, which settles the question of payments rather than of title.

## Wages in the surplus framework

The school's account of wages follows from its premises and separates it from both orthodoxies once more. In the value framework, the wage is the price of labour power, tending to the cost of the worker's reproduction, and exploitation is measured at that margin. In the marginalist framework, the wage tends to the worker's contribution to output. The school treated the actual wage as a political resultant: set by the balance between the giant employers' interest in disciplined costs and the organised working class's capacity to defend and raise its share, with the racial and gender divisions of the labour force, and the reserve of the unemployed and underemployed, entering as the mechanisms by which the employers' interest usually prevails. The consequence for the surplus is direct. Wage restraint raises the excess at the economy's centre while compressing the consumption that must absorb it, so the system's wage policy manufactures its own absorption problem, the contradiction the household-debt section of this article found the tradition's later writers exploiting. The school's wage doctrine never received the formal development its category did, a gap its critics noted and its successor schools, the segmentation theorists and the social-structure-of-accumulation writers among them, moved to fill from within the tradition's own premises. A profile records the gap with the achievement: the founders built the instrument and the problem; the working out of wages, the labour process, and the state fell to the heirs, and the heirs acknowledged the inheritance in print.

## A journal without advertisements

The institution embodied its doctrine in one detail too perfect to be accidental, though the founders treated it as housekeeping. Monthly Review carried no advertising. A journal whose economic theory named the sales effort among the great wastes of the age could hardly finance itself by selling its readers to the sales effort, and it never did: the enterprise lived on subscriptions, on the press's book sales, and on the founders' readiness to subsidise it from their own resources in lean years. The detail is the school's materialism applied to itself. The tradition taught that a publication's economics decides its freedoms more surely than its editors' intentions, that an advertiser-supported left press would be absorbed by its revenue exactly as the surplus is absorbed by the institutions it creates, and it organised its own survival on the contrary principle, poverty with independence preferred to comfort with a patron. The profession's journals, financed by institutions, outlasted lean decades on endowments the school never had; that the school outlasted them in continuous voice is the standing experimental result. Historians of the press treat the no-advertising rule as the material base of the editorial line this profile has described, the independence, the freedom to revise the canon, the willingness to print the unprintable, and the rule therefore belongs in the economics of the school and not merely in its charm.

## Sweezy's last heresy: the post-revolutionary society

The founder's independence of mind completed its circuit in his last theoretical work, and the profile would be incomplete without it. In essays of his final decades, collected under the title Post-Revolutionary Society, Sweezy argued that the Soviet order was neither the socialism of the doctrine nor a capitalism of the ordinary kind, but a new social form, a post-revolutionary society in which a ruling stratum appropriated the surplus through its control of the state, and which had to be analysed in its own categories rather than absolved or condemned by analogy. The judgement estranged him from the Trotskyist tradition that had classified the Soviet Union as a degenerated workers' state and from the Communist tradition that classified it as socialism achieved, leaving the school's surviving founder, once more, in a camp of one journal. The surplus category did the work in the analysis, which is why the episode belongs here: value magnitudes could not be computed for an economy without markets in labour and capital goods, but the questions the school had always asked, who appropriates the excess, by what institutional right, and to what ends, could be asked of the planned economy in exactly the founders' vocabulary, and were. The measurability trade's deepest justification, on the school's own best account of itself, is that one: its category travels where the value categories cannot be unpacked, into economies of administered prices of every political colour, and asks its question of each without asking permission of their ideologies.

## Baran in the Indian debate

The growth book's first foreign reception was its most consequential, and it fixes the geography of Baran's influence. India in the nineteen fifties was conducting the century's most ambitious experiment in planned development within a mixed economy, and its economists, divided between the planners' optimism and the sceptics' doubts, seized on Baran's framework as the radical pole of their argument: his potential surplus gave a name and a method to the suspicion that the economy's poverty was an arrangement rather than a fate, and his inventory of waste and drain supplied the debate with its accounting. Indian scholars of the next generation, including several who carried development economics into its radical phase, acknowledged the book as a formative text, and the acknowledgement travelled onward into the dependency literature, where Baran's name is cited by Latin American and African writers who met him through the Indian and the journal's pages alike. The episode returns the profile to its opening finding. The school's influence ran through readers and debates rather than through departments, and it ran furthest where the founders' category met a live political question, the mobilisation of a poor country's surplus under a watching world. Baran, the exile who never held a development post, became development theory's radical founder by the same route as everything else in this school's history: the printed word, addressed to whoever would argue with it.

## The record of error, stated plainly

Neutrality requires the founders' misses to be itemised with the same care as their hits, and the school's archive, characteristically, supplies most of the items itself. The tradition expected stagnation where the postwar boom delivered a generation of growth, and although the editorials could name the boom's absorbers, the arms budget, the suburbanisation wave, the automobile complex, the fact remains that the school's central tendency spent twenty-five years being outrun by its countertendencies, a record its critics cite as the tendency form's congenital excuse. The school underestimated the absorptive power of innovation, treating the epoch-making industries as rarities of the heroic age when the century kept producing them. Its editorials of the Maoist years credited the Chinese experiment with achievements and intentions that the subsequent record dissolved, and its treatment of the Soviet economy, Sweezy's late heresy aside, long gave the planned systems a benefit of the doubt their own archives have withdrawn. Its category, finally, proved easier to wield than to discipline: the school's lesser writers classified by reflex what the founders had classified by argument, and the surplus in weak hands became a label for whatever expenditure the writer already disliked. The profile sets these items beside the achievements without cancelling either column. A school whose questions returned in 2008 may still have been wrong about 1968, and the series' standard, applied to friendlier subjects than this one, is that the record be printed whole.

## The planner's instrument: the school on the calculation problem

The oldest objection to the socialism the founders defended was not moral but computational: without market prices formed by private exchange, the critics of planning argued from Mises onward, a socialist economy cannot calculate, cannot know what anything costs, and must therefore misallocate on a civilisational scale. The school's engagement with that objection is the final place its category shows its versatility. The surplus framework is, at bottom, a planner's arithmetic: it asks what an economy produces beyond its costs, what its surplus might build if consciously directed, and what its existing order does with the excess instead. Baran's potential surplus is unintelligible except as a planning magnitude, the distance between an economy's performance and its possibility stated so that a deliberating society could close it, and the growth book's entire polemic assumes that the computation can be made and argued over in public. Sweezy met the calculation debate head-on in the treatise's treatment of planning, conceding the problem's reality while denying its decisiveness, and the school's later exchange with Bettelheim worried the same ground from inside socialism: what magnitudes a plan should target, and whether the categories of value survive into the order that abolishes the market. The founders never produced the worked theory of socialist accounting their position implied, one more promissory note the orthodoxy's bookkeepers recorded. But the category's double life, as the indictment of one economy and the instrument panel of its successor, explains the school's confidence that it had not left science for rhetoric: the surplus was always meant to be computed twice, once against capitalism's record and once against socialism's plans.

## Prose as method: the founders' register

A last feature of the school is literary, and belongs in the profile because it did argumentative work. The founders wrote in a register the profession of their time had abandoned: long, periodic sentences, a vocabulary drawn from the classical economists and from the newspaper rather than from the new mathematics, and a tone of address to an educated public that assumed the reader's patience and repaid it. The register was a methodological claim in disguise. Economics written for initiates can hide its classifications inside its technique; economics written for the public must defend every classification in words the public can refuse, and the surplus category, born classified in the open, is the doctrinal precipitate of that discipline. The school's prose also performed its independence: no house style was imposed on the journal's contributors beyond clarity, and the founders' own voices, Baran's sardonic compression, Sweezy's patient exposition, Huberman's plain warmth, remain distinguishable across sixty years of mastheads, a fact any reader can verify and no committee could have designed. When the revival's young readers praise the joint book's readability with surprise, they are registering the survival of a contract between writer and reader that the profession had torn up, and the school's whole history suggests the contract was the load-bearing wall: remove the public, and the category, the institution, and the founders' authority dissolve together, leaving three economists whose books a library happens to hold.

## An American Marxism

The school is the most American of the century's Marxist traditions, and the adjective explains several of its peculiarities at once. Its materials were American before they were doctrinal: the giant corporation as the New Deal's investigators had documented it, the Tennessee Valley and the war mobilisation as proofs that planning worked when a society willed it, the national income accounts of the Commerce Department as the category's raw stuff. Its institutional form was American too, the independent little magazine of opinion, descendant of the muckrakers and the Partisan Review circle, a form that assumes a reading public exists and can be convened by print. Even its heresies were national in style: the willingness to revise the founder in the founder's name is a recognisably American relation to scripture, repeated across the country's religious and political history, and the founders' European educations did not prevent their school from reasoning like the country that had refused them tenure. The contrast with the European Marxisms of the same decades is instructive. The Frankfurt tradition, treated in the previous cluster, theorised from exile and returned to a university; the French tradition argued inside a mass party's discipline; the Italian tradition worked through a party's cultural apparatus. The Monthly Review school worked through a subscription list, and its doctrine bears the mark throughout, optimistic about readers, impatient with apparatus, and convinced that an argument which cannot find its public has only itself to blame. The measurability trade itself can be read as the most American decision in the school's record: confronted with a choice between a rigorous category and a usable one, the founders chose the tool that could go to work in the open, and trusted the argument to survive the exposure.

## The treatise's footnotes: a map of a vanished republic

A final apparatus note belongs to the young Sweezy's scholarship, because it preserves something the school itself superseded. The Theory of Capitalist Development was written when the Marxist economics of the German and Russian classics could still be surveyed whole, and its chapters and footnotes assemble the republic of that literature entire: the Austro-Marxist debates, the Russian controversy over the schemas, the Polish and German crisis theorists, the English-language expositors, each assigned a place and a grade in the young interpreter's calm handwriting. Later generations of Marxist economists, formed after the wars and the purges had broken that republic's continuity, learned its geography substantially from this book, which is one reason the school's later heresies carried such weight: they came from the tradition's own mapmaker, who could not be accused of revising what he had not read. The treatise's survey function also clarifies the joint book by contrast. The work of 1942 is a map of other men's country; the work of 1966 is a settlement in new territory, and the quarter century between them is the distance between a scholar's first duty, to state the inheritance exactly, and his second, to spend it where the times require. Readers who wish to grade the measurability trade at its fairest are advised to begin with the map, since the price of the settlement can only be reckoned by someone who knows what the inheritance contained, and the map's author, alone among the century's revisers of Marx, had drawn the inheritance himself before deciding what it could not buy.

## Two arsenals: the arms race as shared absorption

The school's reading of the cold war's central institution shows its framework at its most characteristic, and its most contestable. Both superpowers, the journal argued across the decades, maintained military establishments on a scale that peacetime politics alone could not explain, and the surplus framework supplied the explanation: each arsenal absorbed the output its economy could not otherwise employ, and the rivalry's terrible symmetry, each side's buildup licensing the other's, made the arrangement self-renewing. The reading spared neither bloc's ideology. The Soviet arsenal, in the school's analysis, absorbed the planned economy's surplus through the same institutional demand that the American arsenal served the monopoly economy, and the founders' refusal to exempt the socialist states from their categories, the refusal completed in Sweezy's post-revolutionary heresy, began in these pages. Military Keynesianism was the school's name for the American half of the symmetry, and its critics, then and since, have objected that the argument flatters the arms budget with a rationality its politics never possessed, that budgets are set by fear, prestige, and bureaucratic appetite rather than by the economy's need. The school's answer was its doctrine of tendencies: no ministry computes the surplus, but budgets that rise are retained and budgets that fall are restored at the first recession, and the filtration, over forty years, prints the tendency's signature on the ledger. The reader grades the exchange by the standard this article has used throughout: mechanisms named in advance, offsets honestly entered, and a conclusion held at exactly the strength the evidence, rather than the school's hopes, will carry.

## The structuralist parallel: Baran and the Latin American school

Baran's growth book had a twin born in another hemisphere, and the kinship shaped what followed. In the same postwar years, Raúl Prebisch and the economists of the United Nations Economic Commission for Latin America were building the structuralist account of underdevelopment: the world economy divided into a centre that keeps its technical progress as incomes and a periphery that surrenders its progress in falling prices for its primary exports, so that development requires deliberate industrialisation behind protection. The two frameworks were independent in formation and different in allegiance, structuralism being a reformist programme for national governments where Baran's was a revolutionary sociology, yet they share the founding gesture that defines both against the orthodoxy of their day: the poor economy's condition is produced by its place in the whole, not by its own deficiencies, and the remedy must change the place rather than exhort the deficient. The dependency generation of the nineteen sixties was the child of the crossing, taking its centre and periphery from the structuralists and its surplus, its comprador classes, and its verdict on the local bourgeoisies from Baran, which is why the lineage article in this series treats the growth book as the dependency tradition's founding text in English while the structuralists' own historians claim the same generation for Prebisch. For this profile the parallel completes the founders' geography. Sweezy's school faced north, toward the metropolis whose surplus it theorised; Baran's faced south and east, toward the economies whose surplus was being drained; and the joint book, for all its American frame, was written by a partnership one of whose members had already founded the economics of the other half of the world. A school so composed was never going to remain a national affair, and it never did.

## Why the joint book is not a textbook

The school's central work refuses the form of the books that made its founders' reputations, and the refusal completes the institutional portrait. A textbook organises a settled doctrine for transmission, exercises at the chapter ends, the master's voice throughout. The joint book organises an argument for use: it withholds the drills, multiplies the applications, and leaves its instrument in the reader's hands in working order. The difference descends from the school's material circumstances. Textbooks are commissioned by a profession that controls the courses where they will be read, and the founders controlled no courses; what they controlled was a press and a subscription list, so their book had to recruit its readers rather than inherit them, persuading a public rather than instructing a cohort. Sweezy's treatise, the school's one near-textbook, proves the rule by its route to standard status, adopted by the readers the faculties had not assigned it to. The tradition's teachers have lived with the consequence ever since: the school's ideas enter classrooms carried by the persuaded, which is slower than adoption by syllabus and harder to reverse, since a reader recruited by an argument keeps the argument when the syllabus changes. The measurability trade was, among its other natures, a publishing decision, and the joint book's form is where the decision is most visible on the page.

## For students and exam candidates

Hold the school in three separations and most examination questions on it will open. Separate the category from the value category it replaced, using the substitution table's rows, and be able to state the measurability trade in one sentence each way: what the surplus can count, and what it cannot derive. Separate the two founders, their formations, and their books, since questions that credit the pair jointly are invitations to display the difference. Separate the generation of the surplus from its absorption, and send the absorption argument, the rising tendency's outlets and the stagnation conclusion, to the companion treatment of [the school's crisis theory](/2014/02/01/monopoly-capital-stagnation-theory/), where the examinable machinery lives. Keep the definitions as working cards, the distinction between actual and potential surplus on one side and the trade on the other, in a [VaultBook card on the category substitution](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html), and rehearse the objection at full strength before the defence, since the school's questions are set by examiners who reward the candidate that states the value criticism fairly before answering it.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The substitution table is the finest classroom instrument in this cluster for teaching what a basic category does, because the whole dispute can be staged from its rows. Set the table before the seminar with the column headings concealed and have the room reconstruct, from the definitions alone, what each category must be able and unable to do; then reveal the school's version and let the derivation row carry the argument. The measurability trade generalises the lesson beyond Marxist economics: every field has its version of the bargain between a concept that explains and a concept that counts, and students who have priced this instance can be trusted to spot the next one. For the founders, assign the treatise's chapter on crisis theories beside the joint book's opening chapters and ask what changed in the author between them; the exercise teaches more about how economists revise than any methodology lecture. Assessment should credit the answer that presents the value criticism at full strength, since a student who can state the strongest case against the school has understood the school, and should penalise the conflation of surplus with surplus value wherever it appears, as the one error that dissolves the topic.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier on this school is archival and bibliometric as much as theoretical. The journal's complete run permits the tradition's positions to be dated month by month, and the editorials constitute an underexploited source for the history of the postwar left's economic imagination. Baran's papers and the record of his Stanford years document the profession's cold war from inside its most exposed outpost, while the New Hampshire case file, from subpoena to Supreme Court reversal, is a primary source for the constitutional history of academic freedom. For the lineage questions, begin from the growth book's reception in the development debates and follow the descent traced in the article on [the dependency tradition's formation](/2016/07/01/dependency-theory-explained/), where Baran's surplus meets its Latin American successors. Journalists should observe the school's own discipline in reverse: attribute every surplus magnitude to the classifications that produced it, name the school's claims as claims, and treat the founders' biographies as evidence about the profession of their time rather than as decoration. The tradition rewards researchers who read its institution and its category as one object, which is how its members understood themselves.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who were Baran and Sweezy?

Paul Baran was a Russian-born economist, educated in Moscow and Germany, who held a chair at Stanford from 1949 until his death in 1964 and was described as the only tenured Marxist economist at a major American university. Paul Sweezy was a Harvard-trained economist who resigned his assistant professorship after the war, co-founded Monthly Review in 1949, and edited it for the rest of his long life. Baran wrote the founding development study of the school; Sweezy wrote its standard exposition of Marxist economics; together they wrote Monopoly Capital.

### Q: What is the economic surplus?

It is the school's central category: the excess of a society's output over the cost of producing that output, reckoned in money from the accounts an economy keeps. Baran distinguished the actual surplus, the excess a society in fact generates over its consumption, from the potential surplus, the larger excess it could generate if its resources were fully and rationally employed. The category is definitional and procedural rather than value-theoretic: it is computed by classifying expenditures as costs of production or as uses of the excess, and the classification embodies a judgement about which activities a rational society requires.

### Q: How does economic surplus differ from surplus value?

Surplus value is a magnitude of the labour process, defined by the gap between the value labour power creates and the value of its own upkeep, and it exists to explain where profit originates. Economic surplus is a magnitude of the national accounts, defined by output over cost, and it exists to be measured and followed through its uses. The two differ in their units, in their evidence, and in their purpose: the first derives the excess from exploitation, the second sizes the excess for a whole economy. This article's substitution table arrays the contrast in full; the FAQ answer stands alone as the short form: a theory of origin exchanged for a programme of measurement, knowingly and at a price both sides can state.

### Q: What is The Political Economy of Growth about?

Baran's 1957 book argues that underdevelopment is produced, not original. Working with his distinction between actual and potential surplus, it shows how poor economies generate far less surplus than they could, and how the surplus they do generate is wasted, consumed in luxury, or drained abroad by the alliance between domestic propertied classes and foreign capital, which blocks every road to development that would dissolve their position. The remedy implied is political: only a social order that mobilises the potential surplus for accumulation can develop. The book founded the dependency tradition in English and remains the school's most influential single-author work.

### Q: Did Baran and Sweezy abandon the labour theory of value?

Yes, deliberately, and the word abandon needs their gloss. They held that the labour theory was the price theory of the competitive capitalism Marx analysed, in which prices gravitate around values, and that the administered pricing of the giant corporation had superseded that formation, leaving the value magnitudes without an observable anchor. Sweezy had expounded the theory to English readers more clearly than anyone, and had carried its chief difficulty, the transformation problem, in print since 1942, so the decision was a considered judgement by the theory's own interpreter. Critics inside Marxism judged the same act a defection; the founders judged it fidelity to Marx's method over his conclusions.

### Q: What is the Monthly Review school?

It is the tradition of Marxist economics and social criticism formed around Monthly Review, the independent journal founded in 1949 by Leo Huberman and Paul Sweezy, and around the press of the same name founded three years later. Its marks are the surplus category, the analysis of monopoly and stagnation, the attention to imperialism and underdevelopment, and an institutional independence from universities and parties maintained across three quarters of a century and three editorships. The phrase names both a doctrine and an address: a school whose membership was its readership and whose continuity was its subscription list.

### Q: Was Sweezy's textbook the standard introduction?

The question needs care, because two different books are meant by textbook talk. The Theory of Capitalist Development of 1942 was never a set text of the profession, but within Marxist economics it served for decades as the standard exposition in English, the book through which students in many countries met Marx's categories in systematic form, and its chapter on crisis theories remained the assigned map of that terrain for a generation. No rival Marxist treatise in English displaced it during the founders' lifetimes. The school's own teaching instrument was the journal rather than any textbook, and the treatise's standard status belongs to the readership that adopted it, not to the faculties that ignored it.

### Q: What is monopoly finance capital?

In this school's own usage, the phrase names the stage at which the monopoly corporation and the great financial institutions cease to be separate powers and operate as one structure, and the later Monthly Review tradition made it the label for monopoly capital's most recent phase. Sweezy's late essays and his work with Magdoff argued that finance, once the surplus's servant, had become an autonomous centre of accumulation, so that the system's centre of gravity shifted from the giant industrial corporation to the fused bloc of monopoly and finance. John Bellamy Foster's formulation of monopoly-finance capital, built directly on those essays, carried the concept into the tradition's account of stagnation and crisis in the new century: the same absorption problem, with the financial sector now both the surplus's chief destination and its chief claimant. The phrase has other lives in other traditions; in this article it is used strictly in the school's sense, as the name its own writers gave to the phase their founders lived to see begin.

### Q: Why did the school decline and then revive?

The decline had institutional and intellectual causes together. The founders died or aged, the cold war conditions that had isolated the school gave way to a long boom that seemed to refute its stagnationist expectations, the profession's centre moved to formal modelling its writers refused on principle, and the broader left whose readership sustained the journal contracted sharply in the nineteen eighties. The revival followed the evidence and the generations. The crisis of 2008 restaged the school's questions about finance and stagnation before a mass audience; scholars formed in the neighbouring disciplines carried its books back into print and syllabi; and the journal's own continuity meant the tradition was still at its address, with its archive unbroken, when the readers returned. Decline and revival are the subscription graph of an independent institution, and the school lived and died by the readership it had chosen over the profession.

### Q: What did critics say about the surplus category?

Critics of several kinds converged on the origin question. Value theorists held that the surplus, lacking any derivation from the labour process, could not explain why an excess exists or whose claim on it is legitimate, and read the category as national product accounting with a radical vocabulary. Defenders of the classical wage fund tradition in new forms objected that the necessary and the wasteful could not be separated without a prior theory of value, so the category smuggled in what it claimed to replace. Mainstream reviewers treated the whole construction as unscientific for its dependence on contested classifications. The school's replies are given in this article at full length; the criticism at its strongest begins from the concession that the measurement works, and denies that measurement is enough.

### Q: How does the measurability trade grade the surplus category?

The trade grades the category well on reach and badly on depth, and the grading is the verdict this article defends. On reach: the surplus can be computed from published accounts, taught without a theory of value, and carried into development, stagnation, and finance arguments in public, which is why the school, alone among Marxist traditions, kept a monthly public economics alive for three quarters of a century. On depth: the category cannot say where the excess originates, cannot ground the claim that it belongs to anyone in particular, and leans on judgements of necessity it cannot supply from within itself. A grader who prizes reach ranks the category among the century's successful concepts; a grader who prizes depth ranks it a renunciation. The trade's lesson is that the two rankings measure different things, and that confusing them is the commonest error in the school's reception.

### Q: What should a student separate between necessary and wasteful expenditure in the argument?

Separate the two judgements the argument runs together and the confusion dissolves. The first judgement is technical: given the output a society actually produces, which expenditures were required to produce it? Materials, energy, transport, and the upkeep of the producers belong here, and the classification is an accountant's work. The second judgement is social and prospective: given the output a rationally ordered society could produce, which expenditures serve ends worth serving? The arms budget, the duplicated sales apparatus, and the luxury consumption of the possessing classes are condemned at this bar, and the condemnation is a political philosophy's work, not an accountant's. Baran's potential surplus is the measure of the distance between the two judgements. The student who keeps the bars distinct can state exactly where any surplus estimate is arithmetic and where it is argument, and can defend or attack each part at the right level.

### Q: Why did the giant corporation replace the competitive firm at the centre?

Because the founders judged that the economy's typical unit had changed, and theory must follow its object. The competitive firm of the classical texts takes prices from a market, earns a normal return, and accumulates at the market's pace; the giant corporation sets its prices, generates its own investment funds internally, plans across years and continents, and bends the market around its requirements. A theory whose centre is the first unit will systematically misdescribe an economy populated by the second: its price theory will look for a competition that administration has replaced, and its account of saving will look for a capital market the corporation's treasurer has internalised. The substitution at the level of the unit is the deep reason for the substitution at the level of the category, since the surplus is the concept the giant corporation's own accounts suggest, and the school's originality begins with its willingness to let the modern firm's bookkeeping instruct the theory.

The table tracks them by holding the two categories still and rotating the questions. ### Q: What separates absorption of the surplus from its generation?

Generation concerns how much excess an economy produces; absorption concerns where that excess goes, and the school's whole architecture depends on not confusing the ledgers. An economy may generate an enormous surplus and absorb it without strain, in a war mobilisation or a building boom, or generate a modest one and choke on it, if consumption is compressed and investment distrusted. Marx's reproduction schemes, treated elsewhere in this cluster, track the balance between the two sides inside a value framework; the school's contribution was to make the absorption side a theory of institutions, the sales effort, the state's budgets, the epoch-making innovation, rather than a residual. The separation also disciplines criticism: an objection to the school's stagnation conclusion belongs to the absorption ledger and leaves the surplus's generation untouched, while an objection to the category itself strikes the generation side and must be argued there.
