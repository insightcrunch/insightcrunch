Consumer law in India is not one law, and the reader who treats it as one will knock on the wrong door. A rejected insurance claim, a failed payment, a short weighed packet of flour, an inflated electricity bill and a defective telephone handset all feel like the same kind of grievance, and all of them are, in the loose language of daily life, consumer complaints. In law they are not the same complaint at all. Each belongs to a different layer of a system that was built in pieces across four decades: a general consumer statute with its own regulator and its own three tier of commissions, a set of sectoral ombudsmen and forums created by financial and utility regulators for their own industries, and a standards and safety apparatus that polices what may be sold at all. The same grievance taken to the wrong layer is not answered wrongly; it is often not answered at all, because the forum lacks the power the grievance needs, or because a shorter and free route existed that the complainant never knew about.

![Consumer law in India mapped as a layered field, showing the general statute, the sectoral ombudsmen and the standards bodies that divide grievances between them - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-38.webp)

The organising fact of the field is a sentence most consumer pages never print: the general statute is the residual route, not the first one. For insurance, banking, electricity, telecommunications and securities, a dedicated ombudsman or forum exists which costs the complainant nothing, works faster than a commission, and binds the provider while leaving the complainant free. The consumer commissions were designed for everything else: the defective good, the deficient private service, the trader, the builder, the professional, and the residue of grievances no sectoral scheme reaches. This article maps the whole field on that design. It sets out the three layers and the statutes that compose them, explains who counts as a consumer and what counts as a legal wrong, walks through each sectoral door and what it can award, states the concurrency rules that let a complainant move between doors without losing the second by trying the first, and gives the defence and the criticism of the layering itself in the terms their own proponents use. Procedure at the level of forms and filings is owned by a companion article; the subject here is the map.

## Consumer law in India: the field in three layers

The first layer is the general statute, the Consumer Protection Act, in its 2019 form the successor of the Act of 1986. It defines the consumer, defines the wrongs (a defect in goods, a deficiency in service, an unfair trade practice, a restrictive trade practice, a misleading advertisement and, since 2019, harm from a defective product), creates a regulator called the Central Consumer Protection Authority with power to investigate, recall and penalise, and maintains a three tier of adjudicatory commissions at district, State and national level, with a mediation chapter beside them. Its remedies are expressly additional to every other law, which is the textual hinge on which the whole layered system swings.

The second layer is sectoral grievance machinery, created not by Parliament in the consumer statute but by the regulators of individual industries, each acting under its own statute. The Reserve Bank runs an integrated ombudsman scheme for banks, non banking finance companies and payment system participants. The insurance regulator's system rests on an Insurance Ombudsman constituted under rules made by the Central Government under the insurance statute. Every electricity distribution licensee must maintain a consumer grievance forum, with an Electricity Ombudsman appointed by the State regulatory commission above it. Telecom subscribers move through a two tier mechanism inside their service provider under regulations of the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India, a sector in which, conspicuously, no ombudsman was ever created. Securities investors complain through the market regulator's platform and then through an online dispute resolution portal the regulator caused to be built in 2023. None of these bodies owes its existence to the consumer statute, and each has its own grounds of complaint, its own time limits and its own ceiling on what it can award.

The third layer is the standards and safety layer, which answers a different question. It does not compensate an individual buyer; it decides what may be sold and on what declarations, and it punishes the seller who breaks the rules whether or not any particular buyer complains. The Bureau of Indian Standards sets standards and administers conformity assessment under the Bureau of Indian Standards Act, 2016, and specified goods can be brought under compulsory certification so that they may not be sold without the standard mark. The Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006 governs food through a dedicated authority, its licensing system and its own adjudication of penalties. The Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 governs drugs and cosmetics through licensing and inspection, and the Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954 prohibits advertisements claiming cures by magic or for scheduled diseases. The Legal Metrology Act, 2009 governs the units, the declarations on a package and the price printed on it. A grievance that is really about a product being unlawful to sell belongs to this layer; a grievance about a product that failed one buyer belongs to the first or the second.

The layers are easiest to hold in mind along the life of a single purchase. A packet of packaged food is licensed and labelled under the food statute, weighed and priced under the metrology statute, manufactured to a standard the Bureau or the food authority has set, advertised under the discipline of the misleading advertisement provisions, sold under the contract and sale of goods floor, paid for through a payment system the Reserve Bank supervises, and complained about, if the quantity is short, the label false, the payment duplicated or the food unsafe, to four different desks of the same State. Nothing in that itinerary is redundant: each desk answers the failure proper to it, and the citizen's difficulty is not that the desks exist but that the itinerary is nowhere signposted, which is the gap a field guide exists to close.

Beneath all three sits a fourth body of law that is not a layer of machinery at all but the doctrinal floor: the Indian Contract Act, 1872 and the Sale of Goods Act, 1930. The implied conditions as to title, correspondence with description, fitness for purpose and merchantable quality that the Sale of Goods Act writes into a sale are the ancestors of the consumer statute's definition of defect, and the civil suit on the contract remains the route of last resort where no statutory forum fits, for parties who are not consumers within the definition, or for claims too large or too factually tangled for the summary machinery.

One correction of vocabulary belongs at the map's head, because it misroutes real complainants. The commissions are universally called consumer courts, and the phrase is wrong in a way that matters: they are statutory commissions exercising judicial power for a defined class of disputes, not courts of the judiciary, their presidents are appointed under the consumer statute rather than through the judicial service, their procedure is the statute's summary procedure rather than the civil procedure code, and their orders appeal within their own ladder to the Supreme Court rather than through the High Courts. The misnomer matters because complainants arrive expecting a court's powers in full, including execution against any asset and contempt for disobedience of every kind, and because respondents occasionally argue the opposite error, that a body not called a court cannot bind them. Both errors dissolve on the same text: the commissions are what their Act says, a forum with a court's relief, a summary procedure and a decree's executability, created precisely because the courts could not be all of those things for a five hundred rupee claim.

The layers arose in sequence rather than by design, and the sequence explains the shape. A general consumer statute came first, in 1986, among the earliest of its kind in a developing economy, enacted in the years after the United Nations General Assembly adopted its Guidelines for Consumer Protection in 1985. Sectoral ombudsmen followed from the middle nineteen nineties, as the financial system widened and the regulators discovered that individual grievances were arriving at their doors in volumes the consumer commissions could not have absorbed and their own supervisory staff could not decide one by one. The banking ombudsman scheme dates to 1995 and the insurance ombudsman to rules of 1998. The consumer statute acquired a regulator with investigative and recall powers only in 2019, thirty three years after the commissions were created. The result is a field in which the oldest institution is the general one and the most used institutions, measured by the grievances of ordinary households, are the sectoral ones built afterwards around it.

## How the layers were built: the sequence from 1986 to 2023

The layering is unintelligible without its dates, because each layer was a response to the failure, or the success, of the layer before it. The Act of 1986 was a product of the first global wave of consumer legislation. The United Nations Guidelines of 1985 had given governments a template: legitimate consumer needs, safety, information, choice, representation and redress, and the Indian statute of the following year, introduced as a measure of social welfare rather than of market regulation, translated the template into councils and commissions. It was, for its time and setting, a radical procedural offer: no court fee of consequence, no lawyer required, a summary procedure, and commissions at the district level, in a country where the civil courts were already slow. What it did not have, because the template of the nineteen eighties did not supply it, was any machinery for harm that had not yet crystallised into an individual complaint, and any answer to the question of who polices a market practice between complaints.

The nineteen nineties supplied the second layer from an unexpected direction. Financial liberalisation multiplied the number of households holding bank accounts, insurance policies and securities, and multiplied correspondingly the grievances those products generate: a wrongly debited account, a repudiated claim, a lost certificate. The regulators watched individual grievances arrive at their own offices, because the citizen writes to the most powerful address she knows, and discovered that neither supervision nor the consumer commissions could process them at the volume arriving. The ombudsman was the imported solution, a Scandinavian institution naturalised in Britain and already familiar in Indian banking debate: the Reserve Bank adopted it in 1995, the insurance system in 1998, and the design detail that travelled with it was the one sided binding award, an acknowledgment from the start that a forum created by the regulator of an industry carries an appearance problem that only asymmetry can cure. The electricity reforms at the turn of the century wrote the same insight into a statute, because the Electricity Act, 2003 was drafted when the ombudsman idea was in circulation, and its grievance forum and ombudsman are the sectoral pattern in statutory rather than scheme form.

The third phase was consolidation in the financial sector and silence elsewhere. The Reserve Bank unified its three schemes in 2021, the insurance ombudsman rules were recast in 2017 and their award ceiling raised in 2023, and the securities regulator added a genuine adjudicatory tier in 2023, while telecom, the sector with the largest subscriber base of all, kept the two tier internal mechanism its regulator had prescribed in 2012 and saw the ombudsman recommendation of 2017 declined. The fourth phase belongs to the general statute alone: the replacement of 2019, attempted first in a Bill of 2018 that passed the Lok Sabha and lapsed on the dissolution of that House, and carried in a fresh Bill the following year, which gave the field its regulator, its product liability chapter, its mediation chapter and its e-commerce rules in a single enactment. Behind the legislative sequence stands a civic one that the record should not omit, because the machinery was demanded before it was designed. Organised consumer advocacy in India predates the statute by two decades: the Consumer Guidance Society of India was founded in Bombay in 1966 to test products and publish the results to subscribers, the Consumer Education and Research Centre in Ahmedabad and the Voluntary Organisation in Interest of Consumer Education in Delhi followed in the following two decades, and the associations these bodies built were the constituency in whose name the Act of 1986 was moved and to whom its councils and its complaint rights for recognised associations were addressed. The consumer movement's own instruments, comparative testing, published price and quality surveys, and complaint assistance to individual buyers, survive beside the statutory machinery as a private layer of the field, and its organisations remain the most persistent critics of the statutory design quoted in this guide, a continuity explained by their history: they asked for a system, received three layers built by different hands across forty years, and have audited the result ever since.

The sequence explains the field's oddity, which a purely structural account conceals: the parts of consumer law that work at the largest volume are not in the consumer statute at all, because they were built by regulators solving their own problem, and the consumer statute's own additions of 2019 are aimed at the problems the regulators never had, the unregulated trader, the class wide practice and the unsafe product.

## Layer one: the general statute

The Consumer Protection Act, 1986 (Act 68 of 1986) created the architecture that Indian consumer law still uses: consumer protection councils for policy, and a three tier of commissions, district forums at the base, State commissions above them and a National Commission in Delhi, deciding complaints about defects in goods and deficiencies in services on a summary procedure. It was amended substantially in 1993 and again in 2002, when the pecuniary tiers were recast and the commissions acquired stronger enforcement powers. The 1993 amendment widened who could complain, admitting complaints by recognised consumer associations and by governments, and by one or more consumers for numerous consumers having the same interest, and added the restrictive trade practice to the catalogue of wrongs. The 2002 amendment recast the tiers on the basis the system kept until 2019, the District Forum taking complaints where the value of the goods or services and the compensation claimed together did not exceed twenty lakh rupees, the State Commission up to one crore rupees and the National Commission above, and armed the commissions with interim orders and stronger attachment powers. The basis of those tiers, value plus compensation claimed, contained the defect the 2019 Act removed: a complainant could choose a higher forum by the simple device of claiming more, and the commissions spent real time on objections that the claim had been inflated for forum, a pathology that measuring the consideration actually paid abolished at a stroke. For three decades it was the field. Its limits were structural rather than accidental: it had no regulator, only adjudicators, so nobody could investigate a trade practice that harmed consumers as a class, order a product recall, or act against a misleading advertisement that had not yet produced an individual complainant. It had no product liability chapter, so harm caused by a defective product travelled by the law of torts and contract, with their costs. And it was written before electronic commerce, so the online transaction sat inside it awkwardly if at all.

The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 (Act 35 of 2019), which received assent on 9 August 2019 and whose principal provisions were brought into force in two tranches on 20 July 2020 and 24 July 2020, repealed and replaced the Act of 1986 rather than amending it, and the detail of what it changed is owned by [the article on the Consumer Protection Act 2019 and the replacement it effected](/2020/12/01/consumer-protection-act-2019-explained/). The guide records only the load bearing additions. It created the Central Consumer Protection Authority, a regulator empowered to inquire and investigate on a complaint, on a reference or of its own motion, to order recall of goods and discontinuation of practices, and to penalise misleading advertisements, including the endorsers and publishers of them: the penalty for a misleading advertisement may extend to ten lakh rupees for a first contravention and to fifty lakh rupees for every subsequent one, and an endorser may be prohibited from endorsing any product or service for up to one year, and up to three years for a subsequent contravention. It added a product liability chapter that distributes liability among manufacturer, service provider and seller on stated conditions. It added a mediation chapter under which commissions may refer a dispute to a mediation cell. It brought electronic commerce expressly inside the definition of consumer and authorised the rules governing marketplaces. And it recast the commissions' pecuniary jurisdiction on a new basis: the value of the goods or services paid as consideration, rather than the value together with the compensation claimed, which removed the old incentive to inflate a claim in order to reach a higher forum.

As enacted, the 2019 Act placed complaints where the consideration paid did not exceed one crore rupees before the District Commission, complaints between one crore and ten crore rupees before the State Commission, and complaints above ten crore rupees before the National Commission. The Central Government revised those figures by the Consumer Protection (Jurisdiction of the District Commission, the State Commission and the National Commission) Rules, 2021, notified on 30 December 2021: the District Commission takes complaints where the consideration paid does not exceed fifty lakh rupees, the State Commission where it exceeds fifty lakh rupees but not two crore rupees, and the National Commission where it exceeds two crore rupees. Under the Consumer Protection (Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions) Rules, 2020, no fee is charged on a complaint where the consideration paid does not exceed five lakh rupees; above that figure a graded fee applies. The limitation period for a complaint is two years from the date the cause of action arose, and a commission may condone delay for sufficient cause recorded in writing. Complaints may be filed electronically through the e-Daakhil portal, and a complainant may file where the complainant resides or personally works for gain, a territorial choice the Act of 1986 did not give.

### Why did Parliament add a regulator to consumer law in 2019?

Because the commissions could only answer the complainant in front of them. An adjudicatory system leaves class wide harm, misleading advertising and unsafe products unaddressed until individual victims file and win, so the Act of 2019 created the Central Consumer Protection Authority to investigate, order recalls and penalise misleading advertisements without waiting for a victim.

What a commission can do, once seized of a complaint, remains the core relief set the field runs on: direct the opposite party to remove the defect, replace the goods, refund the price, discontinue the unfair or restrictive practice, withdraw the hazardous goods, and pay compensation for loss or injury, including for harassment and mental agony. In Lucknow Development Authority v. M.K. Gupta, (1994) 1 SCC 243, the Supreme Court held that housing construction is a service within the Act of 1986, that statutory authorities are amenable to the commissions like any other provider, and that compensation under the Act extends to the harassment and mental agony a defective service inflicts, a holding that took the commissions deep into the territory of public housing authorities and municipal bodies. The holding pattern of the decades since is that the commissions are courts of a summary kind, armed with the relief of courts and bound by their own statute's definitions rather than by the technicalities of civil procedure, and the 2019 Act kept that settlement intact while building a regulator and a product liability chapter on top of it.

The commissions sit in an appellate ladder that the general statute also owns. An appeal from the District Commission lies to the State Commission within forty five days; from the State Commission to the National Commission within thirty days; and from the National Commission to the Supreme Court within thirty days, in each case with power in the appellate body to condone delay for sufficient cause. A party directed to pay money who wishes to appeal must first deposit fifty per cent of the amount awarded, a pre deposit rule that runs through the ladder and disciplines speculative appeals. An order of a commission is executable as a decree of a civil court, and failure to comply with it is itself an offence punishable with imprisonment for a term that may extend to three years, or with fine, or with both. The ladder matters to the map because it is the only appellate spine in the field: the sectoral schemes have their own internal appeals, to the Reserve Bank's Appellate Authority or to the regulator, but the authoritative statements of what consumer, defect and deficiency mean are made on appeals inside the general statute's system, and the sectoral forums borrow those meanings.

The last feature of the general layer is the one that organises the rest of this article. Section 3 of the Act of 1986 provided that its provisions were in addition to and not in derogation of any other law, and Section 100 of the Act of 2019 repeats the formula. The sentence does quiet but decisive work. It means the consumer statute never closes the sectoral doors: a complainant may use an ombudsman and a commission in sequence, may sue on the contract, and may invoke a sectoral penalty regime, subject to one discipline, that the same relief is not recovered twice. Every concurrency question in this field is an argument about the edges of that sentence.

## Who counts as a consumer, and what counts as a wrong

The definition of consumer decides which door a grievance may use, because every statutory forum in the field is built for consumers and turns away those outside the definition. Under Section 2(7) of the Act of 2019, a consumer is a person who buys goods or hires or avails services for a consideration, including a user of the goods or a beneficiary of the service with the buyer's approval, and the definition expressly covers transactions conducted offline or online, through electronic means, teleshopping, direct selling or multi level marketing. Two exclusions do the excluding work of the field. A person who obtains goods for resale or for a commercial purpose is not a consumer, and a person who avails services for a commercial purpose is likewise outside. An explanation attached to the definition then cuts the exclusion back: use of goods bought by a person exclusively for earning a livelihood by means of self employment is not a commercial purpose. In Laxmi Engineering Works v. P.S.G. Industrial Institute, (1995) 3 SCC 583, the Supreme Court read the corresponding words of the Act of 1986 to ask whether the purchase was for a commercial purpose or for the buyer's own livelihood through self employment, holding that the two inquiries are different and that the livelihood case stays inside the statute. The taxi bought by the person who will drive it, the photocopier bought by the person who will run a one person copying shop, are consumer purchases; the same goods bought for a fleet or a chain are not.

The two working wrongs of the statute are defect and deficiency, and the difference between them decides how a complaint is framed. A defect, under Section 2(10), is a fault, imperfection or shortcoming in the quality, quantity, potency, purity or standard of goods, judged against the standard required by law, by contract or by the trader's own claim. A deficiency, under Section 2(11), is a fault, imperfection, shortcoming or inadequacy in the quality, nature or manner of performance of a service, judged against the standard required by law or undertaken to be performed. Goods have defects; services have deficiencies. A washing machine that does not wash raises a defect; the repair service that returns it unwashing raises a deficiency, and a single transaction can produce both, pleaded in the alternative. Around the two working wrongs sit the trade practice wrongs: an unfair trade practice under Section 2(47), a long enumerated list running from false representations and misleading price claims to hoarding and the refusal to issue a cash memo; a restrictive trade practice, which manipulates price or supply to the consumer's detriment; and, since 2019, the misleading advertisement as a defined wrong in itself, defined by what it falsely describes, conceals or claims.

Two further boundaries of the definition matter because grievances cross them weekly. Services rendered free of charge are outside the Act, and services under a contract of personal service are outside it. The first exclusion generated the medical question: whether a patient is a consumer of the doctor. In Indian Medical Association v. V.P. Shantha, (1995) 6 SCC 651, the Supreme Court held that services of medical practitioners are within the Act where rendered for consideration, that a patient who pays is a consumer, and that services rendered free of charge to every patient, and services under a contract of personal service, remain outside. The second boundary, resale and commercial purpose, is where small businesses live, and the livelihood explanation is the hinge of their access to the commissions. Education marks the definition's most contested edge. In Maharshi Dayanand University v. Surjeet Kaur, (2010) 11 SCC 159, the Supreme Court held that a university discharging a statutory function in conducting examinations was not rendering a service the Act reaches, while coaching and training sold commercially are routinely treated as services by the commissions, and the status of the fee paying student of a university or college has been argued both ways in the commissions without a single settling word for every case; the practical position is that the statutory and examination functions of universities stand on one side of the line and the market in instruction on the other, with grievances about fees, refunds and promised facilities after enrolment decided as services where the institution sells them as such. The brief against these boundaries is usually framed by the excluded party and answered by the text; the commissions read the definition daily and the case law is a working map of its edges rather than a debate about its existence.

The beneficiary limb of the definition does more work than its brevity suggests. Because a user of goods and a beneficiary of services count as consumers with the buyer's approval, the person who pays and the person who complains need not be the same. In Spring Meadows Hospital v. Harjol Ahluwalia, (1998) 4 SCC 39, the Supreme Court treated the parents of a child treated at a hospital as consumers, both as the persons who hired the service and as beneficiaries of it, so that compensation for deficiency could run to the family and not merely to the infant patient. The same limb carries the employee covered by a group insurance policy the employer bought, the family member using the appliance another member purchased, and the passenger travelling on a ticket booked by someone else. A large share of real complaints are filed by beneficiaries, and the sectoral schemes reproduce the pattern, since the insurance ombudsman entertains the nominee's and the beneficiary's complaint as readily as the policyholder's.

The word service carries its own map. The Act of 2019 defines it broadly, naming banking, financing, insurance, transport, processing, supply of electrical or other energy, board and lodging, housing construction, entertainment, amusement and the purveying of news or other information, while excluding service rendered free of charge and service under a contract of personal service. The enumeration means the sectoral grievances of this article are also, in the general statute's eyes, services, which is the textual basis of the concurrency the next sections describe: the bank's customer, the policyholder, the electricity consumer and the subscriber are consumers of the general statute even while they are complainants of a sectoral scheme, and the commissions measure the deficiency they allege against the standards the sector itself has set, the bank's commitments, the policy's terms, the supply code. The generalist forum judging by the specialist's rulebook is a recurring feature of the field, and it is why sectoral standards matter even to complainants who never approach a sectoral forum.

## The contract floor beneath the layers

The consumer statute did not abolish the older law; it built a summary storey on top of it, and the older law still carries the cases the statute cannot take. The Indian Contract Act, 1872 supplies the general law of agreement, breach and damages. The Sale of Goods Act, 1930 supplies, in Sections 14 to 17, the implied conditions and warranties of a sale: the seller's right to sell, correspondence with description, fitness for a particular purpose made known to the seller, merchantable quality, and correspondence with sample. A buyer complaining that goods were not of the quality promised is often, without knowing it, pleading the Sale of Goods Act's condition as to quality and fitness in the consumer statute's vocabulary of defect, and where the buyer is not a consumer (goods bought for resale, or for a commercial purpose with no livelihood answer), the civil suit on those implied conditions is the remedy that remains, with the ordinary courts' costs and timelines.

Beside contract stands the tort of negligence, which the consumer statute absorbed rather than replaced. A professional's service is deficient, in the commissions' working law, when it falls below the standard of the reasonably competent practitioner of that calling, the test the English courts framed in Bolam and Indian courts adopted for medical services in the line running through Indian Medical Association v. V.P. Shantha to Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab, (2005) 6 SCC 1, which addressed the criminal side of medical negligence and restated the civil standard with it. The tort layer shows through the statute wherever the claim is for harm rather than for price: the largest compensations of the consumer era have been medical negligence awards, culminating in the Supreme Court's judgment in Balram Prasad v. Kunal Saha, (2014) 1 SCC 384, which recalculated compensation for a death caused by negligent treatment on principles drawn from tort, and which demonstrated that the summary forum could carry a claim of a size and complexity its founders would not have predicted. The commissions did not cease to be summary by carrying such claims, but the case stands as the outer marker of what the storey can bear before the floor's ordinary trial is the honest forum.

The relationship between the floor and the storey matters for a reason this article returns to under concurrency. The commissions' summary procedure was designed for claims that would be uneconomic in a civil court; the civil court remains for claims that are too disputed on fact, too entangled with accounts, or too large for summary decision, and the commissions have on occasion declined complaints raising genuinely complex questions of fact that need a full trial. The floor also explains the statute's continued use of contract ideas inside its own definitions: consideration, the undertaking to perform a service, the standard the trader claimed for the goods. Consumer law in India is best understood as contract law with a courthouse attached and a definition gate at the door, overlaid by regulators who police the markets in which the contracts are made.

## A complaint's journey through the general layer

The procedure of the commissions is owned in its filing detail by [the companion guide to filing a consumer complaint in India](/2021/01/01/file-consumer-complaint-india-guide/), and this guide states only the architecture, because the architecture is itself a routing fact. A complaint is instituted before the commission of the right tier for the consideration paid, sitting where the statute permits, which since 2019 includes the district where the complainant resides or personally works for gain, a change whose importance to a claimant suing a distant seller is difficult to overstate. The commission first decides admission, ordinarily within twenty one days, and may reject a complaint at that threshold if it is frivolous or outside the Act. On admission, the opposite party is called upon to give its version of the case within thirty days, a period the commission may extend by not more than fifteen days, a tighter discipline than the civil courts know, and one that structures the whole proceeding: the consumer complaint is decided, in the ordinary run, on the complaint, the version, and evidence by affidavit, with oral hearing for argument and cross examination only where the case genuinely requires it. Where the goods need analysis or testing, the commission sends a sample to an appropriate laboratory and works to its report. The Act directs that every complaint be disposed of as expeditiously as possible, with an endeavour to decide within three months of notice to the opposite party where no analysis or testing is required, and within five months where it is, timelines whose status as endeavours rather than limits the delay section of this guide has already recorded. The order that results is a reasoned direction from the relief set described above, signed and executable as a decree, and carrying the appeal rights and the fifty per cent pre deposit described in the appellate ladder. Mediation may interrupt the journey at the admission stage or later by consent, and a settlement recorded there ends the complaint as an order of the commission.

Filing has itself been re-routed by the electronic layer. The e-Daakhil portal allows a complaint to be filed, fees paid and the case tracked online before any commission in the country, and the sectoral schemes are digital by design, the Reserve Bank's centralised intake and the securities portal receiving complaints in any form and language. The change is more than convenience: a system whose first forums were district offices with paper files allocated grievances by geography, while a digital intake allocates them by subject, which is the administrative form of this article's thesis, and the commissions' own adoption of electronic filing is the general layer learning from the sectoral one.

Two features of the journey explain its continued centrality despite the queue. The first is that the commission is the only forum in the field obliged to take the complaint as the complainant frames it: an ombudsman works to the grounds its scheme enumerates and may return a grievance that falls between them, while the commission's grounds, defect, deficiency and unfair practice, are open textured enough to receive almost any consumer wrong that can be stated. The second is that the commission's order is the only small forum outcome that is both binding and precedential within its system, feeding the appellate ladder whose definitions the entire field uses. A system that routed every grievance to processing lines would lose the case law that tells the processing lines what deficiency means.

## The class dimension: councils, representative complaints and the Authority

Individual compensation is not the only shape a consumer grievance takes, and the general statute carries two further devices for grievances shared by many buyers at once. The first is the representative complaint: the Act of 2019 permits a complaint to be filed by one or more consumers on behalf of, and for the benefit of, numerous consumers having the same interest, with the commission's permission, applying the representative suit principle of civil procedure to the summary forum. The second is the consumer protection council at the Centre, in the States and in the districts, advisory bodies the statute creates to promote and protect consumer rights, without adjudicatory power, whose function is to keep consumer questions on the administrative agenda of governments. Neither device carries the field's traffic, and their honest weight is modest; but the representative complaint matters doctrinally, because it shows the commissions were conceived as more than a small claims court, and the councils matter institutionally, because they are the one place in the statute where consumers appear as a constituency rather than as complainants.

The Central Authority is the third and operative form of the class dimension, and its working methods complete the picture of the general layer. A complaint to the Authority may be made by a consumer, a voluntary consumer association, a government, or the Authority's own machinery on information received; its investigation wing, headed by a Director General, may search, seize and summon in aid of an inquiry; and its outcomes are market facing directions, recall, discontinuation, reimbursement of the prices of goods recalled, and penalties, rather than compensation between parties. In the misleading advertisement field the Authority has an additional and distinctive target set: the manufacturer who commissions the advertisement, the endorser whose face and voice carry it, and the publisher who runs it are all within the penalty provision, an allocation of responsibility that recognises how advertising is actually produced. The Authority and the commissions are therefore not two sizes of the same instrument but two instruments pointed in different directions, one at practices, the other at parties, and a field guide that merges them misleads the reader about both.

## The commissions as working courts, and the delay problem

A map of the general layer that omitted its working condition would flatter it. The commissions decide by a summary procedure built on affidavit evidence, and their design promise was disposal in months. Their working reality, across long periods and most States, has been arrears, and the causes are structural rather than mysterious: posts of presidents and members left vacant by the appointing governments, benches unable to sit for want of a quorum, infrastructure and staff supplied by the same State administrations whose departments are frequent respondents, and a volume of complaints that the sanctioned strength was never sized to meet. The Supreme Court has repeatedly been moved to intervene in the administration of the system itself, directing governments in proceedings concerning the consumer commissions to fill vacancies, frame appointment processes and provide infrastructure, an unusual posture in which the apex court acts as the system's administrator of last resort. The Act of 2019 answered with design changes rather than with establishments: it empowered the Central Government to prescribe appointment and service conditions by rules, it allowed filing where the complainant resides, and it introduced electronic filing, measures that ease access without adding a single bench.

The delay problem is the strongest practical argument for the sectoral layer, and it is usually left unstated in the layering debate, so this guide states it. A complainant routed to an ombudsman is not merely routed to a cheaper and faster forum; the complainant is routed away from a queue. The ombudsman schemes publish disposal measured in weeks and months because they are staffed as processing systems with timelines written into their instruments, while the commissions are staffed as courts and inherit the courts' disease. The honest defence of the commissions is that their work is different in kind: they decide disputed facts, award compensation against contest, create precedent and sit in appeal over their own tiers, tasks for which speed is a virtue but not the only one. The honest criticism is that a forum designed for the small claimant that behaves like a slow court betrays its founding bargain, and that the sectoral layer grew as large as it did partly because the general layer left the space empty.

## What each forum can actually award

Guides to this field usually stop at jurisdiction, which forum may hear the complaint, and omit the question the complainant actually asks, which is what the forum can give. The relief sets differ as much as the doors do, and the differences are design, not accident.

The consumer commission holds the fullest relief set in the field, because it is a court in summary dress. On a complaint proved, it may direct the opposite party to remove the defect from the goods or the deficiency from the service, to replace the goods, to return the price with interest, to discontinue the unfair or restrictive trade practice and not repeat it, to withdraw hazardous goods from sale, to cease manufacture of such goods, and to pay compensation for loss or injury suffered, including compensation for mental agony and harassment, together with costs; and in a product liability action it applies the liability chapter's distribution among manufacturer, service provider and seller. Its orders bind both sides, travel up the appellate ladder described above, and execute as decrees. What it cannot do is regulate: it decides the case before it, and a practice affecting a million buyers who have not complained is beyond its reach, which is the gap the Central Authority fills from the other side.

The Central Authority's relief runs the other way, to the market rather than to the complainant. It can order recall of goods and withdrawal of services, reimbursement of the prices of goods and services recalled, discontinuation of practices that are unfair or prejudicial, and penalties for misleading advertisements, and it can direct the machinery of district administration through the district collectors it may ask to inquire. It awards no compensation to an individual; a complainant who wants money must still go to a commission, and the two proceedings answer different questions, whether the practice may continue, and whether this buyer recovers.

The ombudsman's award is deliberately narrower, and the narrowness is the price of its speed. It is confined to the loss that flows directly from the deficiency, subject to the ceilings already stated, plus, in the Reserve Bank's scheme, the additional lakh for time, expense and harassment; it can direct the entity to perform what it failed to perform, reverse the charge, restore the position, and pay, but it does not punish, does not set precedent that binds another case, and does not entertain the class complaint. The electricity forum and ombudsman work closer to the service itself: their characteristic orders correct the bill, restore the connection, direct a meter test or replacement, and award the compensation the standards of performance prescribe for the failure, which is relief shaped like the grievance rather than like a damages claim. The securities portal's conciliation produces a settlement the parties sign; its arbitration produces an award enforceable as an arbitral award, final in the arbitral way, which is why invoking it is a graver step than invoking an ombudsman. The standards layer, finally, gives the complainant nothing directly at all: its outcomes are seizure, licence action, prosecution and penalty payable to the State, and its benefit to the individual buyer is evidentiary, since a product condemned by the standards machinery rarely needs its defect proved again before a commission.

Interest and costs complete the relief picture and differ by door in ways complainants feel. A commission moulds interest into its award, commonly awarding interest on the sum found due for the period of the default, and may award costs of the proceeding. The Insurance Ombudsman's award carries interest at the rate the insurance regulations specify, running from the date the claim ought to have been settled, a provision that disciplines delay by pricing it. The Reserve Bank's scheme folds the time and trouble of the complainant into its additional lakh rather than into interest, reflecting its processing character. The standards layer's penalties are not relief at all and carry nothing for the complainant. A complainant choosing between an ombudsman and a commission on a large, old claim is therefore also choosing between the scheme's capped and quickly paid award and the commission's uncapped relief with interest for the years of contest, and the arithmetic of that choice has decided many real elections.

Read as a table of powers rather than of institutions, the field resolves into a simple grammar. Compensation for a personal loss comes from a commission or, within ceilings, from an ombudsman. Correction of a running service comes from the sectoral machinery that supervises the service. Suppression of a practice comes from the Central Authority or the sector regulator. Punishment comes from the criminal courts on the standards and penal statutes. A grievance that needs more than one of these must be taken to more than one door, in the sequence the concurrency rules allow, and a great deal of practical failure in this field consists of asking one door for another door's relief.

## Layer two, first part: the financial ombudsmen

The banking ombudsman is the oldest of the sectoral mechanisms and the one whose design the others copied. The Reserve Bank of India introduced a Banking Ombudsman Scheme in 1995 under its power in Section 35A of the Banking Regulation Act, 1949, appointing ombudsmen to decide customer complaints against banks outside the courts. Separate schemes for non banking financial companies and for digital transactions followed in 2018 and 2019. On 12 November 2021 the Reserve Bank replaced the three schemes with one, the Reserve Bank Integrated Ombudsman Scheme, 2021, under the slogan of One Nation One Ombudsman: a single portal, a single address and a centralised receipt and processing centre, with territorial jurisdiction of the old kind abolished. The scheme covers banks, non banking financial companies, payment system participants and, for specified matters, credit information companies. A complaint lies for a deficiency in service across an enumerated list of grounds, from non payment and delay to failure to follow the Reserve Bank's directions on charges, lending and customer protection, and including a residual ground for other deficiencies the scheme specifies.

The scheme's grounds and its refusals mark the boundary between a regulator's office and a court as clearly as its awards do. The grounds run to deficiencies in the services the regulated entities provide: accounts and deposits, loans and advances, cards and digital payments, charges levied without authority or notice, failures of the commitments the entity has made under the codes of conduct the Reserve Bank recognises, mis-selling of products of the entity or through it, and failures in the services of the entities the scheme covers, including pension accounts administered through banks and the Locker and remittance services of ordinary banking life. The refusals are equally instructive. The ombudsman will not entertain a complaint that is in the nature of a commercial judgment of the entity, the refusal of a loan being the standard example, since the scheme reviews service and not credit decisions; nor a matter already decided by or pending before a court or another forum on the same cause; nor a complaint that is frivolous, anonymous, or outside the one year window; nor a dispute between regulated entities themselves. The pattern of grounds and refusals is the pattern of a supervisory instrument: it polices how the regulated treat their customers, and it declines everything that would make it a parallel judiciary.

The mechanics reward stating precisely, because they are the pattern of the sectoral layer. The customer must first complain in writing to the regulated entity. The ombudsman may be approached when the entity has rejected the complaint, has not replied within thirty days, or has replied in a way the customer finds unsatisfactory, and the approach must be made within one year of the entity's reply or of the expiry of the period for reply. The proceeding costs the complainant nothing. The ombudsman attempts facilitation and conciliation first, and failing settlement passes an award. The award may direct payment of the loss suffered as a direct consequence of the deficiency, subject to a ceiling of twenty lakh rupees, and may add compensation of up to one lakh rupees for the loss of time, the expenses incurred and the mental agony and harassment suffered. The design feature that matters most is the binding pattern. The award binds the regulated entity once the complainant accepts it in full and final settlement within the time the scheme allows; if the complainant does not accept, the award lapses and the complainant's other remedies survive intact. Either side aggrieved by an award may appeal to the Appellate Authority designated within the Reserve Bank within thirty days, save that an entity that failed to furnish information the ombudsman required loses its right of appeal. There is no ceiling on the amount in dispute that the ombudsman may entertain; the ceilings bind the compensation, not the complaint.

Below the ombudsman sits an internal layer that the schemes presuppose and that a complainant experiences as the true first door. Regulated entities must run their own grievance machinery: a bank must publish its grievance procedure and answer a written complaint within the period the scheme works to, and the Reserve Bank additionally requires banks to maintain an internal ombudsman, an officer outside the line of the complaint to whom complaints the bank proposes to reject wholly or partly must be referred before the rejection is final, so that a refusal the customer receives has already survived one internal appeal. Insurers maintain grievance officers under the insurance regulations, and the regulator's own complaint portal, Bima Bharosa, registers and tracks a policyholder's grievance against the insurer before the Insurance Ombudsman is approached. Electricity licensees publish their complaint handling procedure as part of the supply code. The internal layer disposes of the largest number of grievances in the field and produces the record, the complaint, the reply, the dates, on which every later forum works; a complainant who skips it has usually not saved time but forfeited jurisdiction, because the ombudsman's door opens on the entity's rejection, silence or unsatisfactory reply, and not before.

### Is complaining to a sectoral ombudsman free of cost?

Yes. The Reserve Bank's Integrated Ombudsman Scheme, the Insurance Ombudsman Rules and the electricity grievance machinery charge the complainant no fee at any stage, and none requires a lawyer. The costlessness is deliberate design: a forum that charged a fee would reproduce the barrier the consumer commissions exist to remove.

### Must a bank customer complain to the bank before approaching the ombudsman?

Yes. Every sectoral scheme in this part of the field is an escalation mechanism, not a first port of call. The customer must first put the complaint in writing to the regulated entity and allow it the period the scheme prescribes, thirty days under the Reserve Bank's scheme, and the ombudsman's jurisdiction opens on rejection, silence or an unsatisfactory reply.

The insurance ombudsman is a statutory cousin rather than a sibling. The institution was created by the Redressal of Public Grievances Rules, 1998 and reconstituted by the Insurance Ombudsman Rules, 2017, made by the Central Government under the Insurance Act, 1938 and the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority Act, 1999, and administered through a governing body of the insurance council under the supervision of the regulator whose own statute is the subject of [the article on the IRDAI Act 1999 and the insurance regulator](/2021/02/01/irdai-act-1999-explained/). There are Insurance Ombudsman offices across the country with defined territorial jurisdiction. A complaint lies in personal lines insurance, life, general and health, on grounds that include delay in settlement of claims, partial or total repudiation of claims, disputes over premium paid or payable, misrepresentation of policy terms by the insurer or its intermediary, disputes about the legal construction of a policy as it relates to a claim, and non issue of a policy after receipt of premium. The preconditions mirror the banking design: a written representation to the insurer first; then complaint within one year of the insurer's rejection, of its decision, or of the expiry of one month without a reply; and no complaint lies where the same subject matter is or was before a court, a consumer forum or an arbitrator, an election rule stricter than the banking scheme's. Where mediation by consent fails, the ombudsman passes a reasoned award, which the Rules direct be made within three months of the receipt of all requirements from the complainant. The award cannot exceed the loss suffered as a direct consequence of the cause of action, and it carries a monetary ceiling that stood at thirty lakh rupees under the Rules of 2017 and was raised to fifty lakh rupees by an amendment notified in November 2023. The award is binding on the insurer, which must comply within thirty days; the complainant who accepts it accepts in full and final settlement, and the complainant who does not accept it keeps the consumer commission and the courts, which is the one sided binding effect in its purest statutory form.

The insurance caseload has a centre of gravity that explains the office's working character. Health insurance claim disputes, repudiation for non disclosure, short settlement on room rent limits and deductions, and delay, form the mass of the complaints, with motor own damage and life insurance maturity and death claims behind them, and the office's jurisprudence, if a documents based office can be said to have one, is a working gloss on the policy wordings and on the regulator's policyholder protection regulations, which prescribe the insurer's claim handling duties, the timelines for deciding a claim after the last necessary document is received, and interest for delay beyond them. Third party administrators, who process health claims for insurers without being insurers, sit inside this machinery at one remove: the complaint lies against the insurer whose policy it is, and the administrator's conduct is the insurer's deficiency for the purposes of every forum in this article, a small attribution rule that decides many real complaints.

The two financial schemes share a philosophy that distinguishes the sectoral layer from the general one. They are supervisory instruments as much as adjudicatory ones. The ombudsman's data flows back to the regulator and identifies the entities and products generating grievances, and both regulators publish the pattern; the award in the individual case is, from the regulator's chair, also evidence about the regulated entity's conduct. That double character explains the features a pure adjudicator would not have: the insistence that the entity be given the first chance to repair, the regulators' power to name deficient entities, and the absence of any bar on the complainant's parallel statutory remedies, because the scheme's purpose is not to replace the courts but to discipline the market and dispose of the mass of small grievances the courts would drown in.

## The State as respondent: government services in the commissions

A map of forums is incomplete until it says what happens when the provider is the State itself, because a large share of Indian services, railways, posts, public hospitals, municipal water, government housing, are supplied by public bodies, and the consumer statute reaches them on the same terms as private providers wherever what they supply is a service for consideration. Lucknow Development Authority v. M.K. Gupta established the point for housing authorities, and the principle runs through the field: the railway passenger is a consumer of the transport service, and complaints of the service kind, a berth not provided against a confirmed reservation, catering failures, a refund wrongly refused, lie before the commissions as deficiency in service. The boundary is drawn by subject matter rather than by the respondent's status: compensation for death and injury in a railway accident belongs to the Railway Claims Tribunal under its own statute, and the commissions do not entertain it, so the same railway is answerable in two systems for two kinds of failure, and the complainant's task is again classification. Government hospitals divide on the payment line the definition draws: treatment rendered free of charge to all is outside the Act, treatment in a paying ward or on a charged service is inside it, and the mixed hospital has generated the expected litigation on which side of the line a partly charged treatment falls. Municipal and utility services divide on a similar line between the tax funded civic function, which is not a service hired for consideration, and the metered, billed supply, which is, so that the water bill and the electricity bill are consumer transactions while the unpaved street is a grievance for a different law. The sector's own machinery often absorbs these cases first, the electricity ladder being the standing example, but the commissions' jurisdiction over the State as provider is the background fact that disciplines every public counter in the field, and it is a feature of the Indian design that surprises readers from systems where the State's services stand outside consumer law altogether.

## Mis-selling: the grievance that crosses the sectors

One grievance deserves a passage of its own because it reveals the seams of the layered design better than any other: mis-selling, the sale of a financial product unsuited to the buyer on representations that were false or incomplete. A bank customer sold an insurance policy as a deposit product holds a grievance that is simultaneously a banking complaint, an insurance complaint and an unfair trade practice. The sectoral map answers it by respondent: the ombudsman's jurisdiction follows the regulated entity complained against, the Reserve Bank's scheme expressly includes mis-selling within its grounds where the entity sold a product in breach of the applicable directions and codes, and the insurance ombudsman entertains misrepresentation of policy terms as its own ground, so the same facts may be carried to either office depending on whose conduct is at the centre of the complaint. The general statute answers it by wrong: the sale is an unfair trade practice under the Act of 2019, the misrepresentation is within the enumerated practices, and the Central Authority may act against the practice at market level where the pattern is systemic. Pension and securities products sold with false assurances trace the same triangle through their own regulators' machinery. The lesson the example teaches is the field's working rule of attribution: identify the conduct, identify the entity whose conduct it was, and the map assigns the door; complainants who begin with the product name rather than with the conduct regularly choose the wrong one, because a single product may be manufactured by one regulated entity, distributed by another and advised upon by a third, each answerable to a different scheme for a different part of the wrong.

## The ombudsman model examined

Because the sectoral layer rests on one institutional invention, the invention repays examination as a whole. The ombudsman came to India as a financial institution: an officer appointed by the regulator, deciding complaints against the industry on documents, free to the complainant, attempting settlement first and awarding second, reporting patterns upward to the regulator, and financed by a levy on the regulated industry rather than by the complainant or the Consolidated Fund. The model's five design decisions are constant across the Indian schemes. The intake is gated by the entity's own process, so the scheme is an appeal and not an alternative front door. The grounds are enumerated, so the office can decline what is outside its list, unlike a commission's open textured grounds. The first stage is settlement, since a regulator's office disposing of grievances by agreement has done supervision's work in the individual case. The award is capped, keeping the office out of the territory of courts. And the binding effect is asymmetric wherever the scheme is mature, the feature this guide has treated as the model's signature.

The model's origin explains its location. An ombudsman inside the regulator was chosen, in banking in 1995 and insurance in 1998, because the grievances were already arriving at the regulator and because the alternative, an independent consumer ombudsman for finance on the pattern some countries built, would have required legislation nobody tabled. The Financial Sector Legislative Reforms Commission, chaired by Justice B.N. Srikrishna, which reported in 2013, examined the resulting patchwork across banking, insurance, securities and pensions and recommended a unified Financial Redress Agency, a single consumer redress body for the whole of finance that would have replaced the sectoral schemes with one institution on a common design. The recommendation was not enacted, and the field instead converged piecemeal: the Reserve Bank unified its own three schemes in 2021, the securities regulator built its portal in 2023, the insurance rules were recast and their ceiling raised, and the pension regulator operates a centralised grievance system for the National Pension System on the same escalation pattern. The direction of travel is toward the Commission's destination by separate roads: common intake discipline, common escalation, common one sided or settlement based design, without the single agency. Whether the remaining differences among the schemes, the ceilings, the windows, the appeal routes and the binding effects, justify the citizen's need to know which scheme she is in, is the standing question of the financial layer, and the honest answer is that they reflect the industries' different products more than any principle of redress; a failed payment, a repudiated claim and a mis-sold pension are different wrongs, but not so different as the five rulebooks written for them.

Access design inside the model has converged even where the rulebooks have not. Complaints to the Reserve Bank's scheme may be made online, by post or by email, and the centralised receipt centre accepts and processes physical complaints in the complainant's own language, translating the file into the system's working record; the Insurance Ombudsman sits in regional offices across the country so that the complainant's hearing, where one is held, is held near home; the securities portal is online by construction. Each scheme publishes its intake and disposal in annual reporting, and each requires the regulated entities to display the scheme's existence at the point of service, on statements, websites and branch notices, the publicity duty being the schemes' own answer to the knowledge criticism this guide records below. The convergence is incomplete, the windows and ceilings still differ, but the direction, toward a single manner of knocking whatever the door, is the financial layer's clearest trend.

The comparison with the one sector that refused the model completes the examination. Telecom has the subscriber volumes of finance and grievances of the same small repetitive kind, and it has no ombudsman because the recommendation to create one was declined, leaving its complainants to the provider's own tiers. The difference in outcomes between a sector with the model and a sector without it is the field's natural experiment on the institution itself, and on the evidence available to any reader of the two systems' published accounts, the model's presence is the difference between a grievance system and a grievance suggestion box with a court behind it.

## Layer two, second part: electricity, telecom and securities

The electricity machinery is inside the sector statute itself. Under Sections 42(5) to 42(8) of the Electricity Act, 2003, every distribution licensee must establish a forum for the redressal of consumer grievances in accordance with the guidelines of the State Electricity Regulatory Commission; a consumer aggrieved by the forum's non redressal or by its decision may make a representation to the Electricity Ombudsman appointed by the State Commission; and the ombudsman settles the grievance within the time and in the manner the Commission specifies. Section 57 of the same Act obliges the Commission to specify standards of performance for licensees and the compensation payable to a person affected when a licensee fails to meet them, compensation payable in addition to any penalty. The position of the electricity consumer under that Act, its supply code, its standards of performance and its grievance ladder, is the subject of the article on the Electricity Act 2003 and is consistent with the account here: the Act expressly preserves the consumer's remedies under other laws, the consumer protection framework included. Billing disputes, defective meters, delayed connections and failures of restoration are the daily matter of this ladder, and the complainant pays nothing to climb it. The ombudsman's orders bind the licensee; the consumer dissatisfied with the outcome retains the consumer commission, where the claim will be reframed as a deficiency in service measured against the licensee's own statutory duties and the supply code.

Telecom is the exception that proves the layering was never planned. The Telecom Regulatory Authority of India Act, 1997 gives the Authority standard setting, tariff and recommendatory functions, but it does not empower the Authority to redress individual complaints, and the Authority itself has always said so. What exists is the Telecom Consumers Complaint Redressal Regulations, 2012, under which every service provider must run a two tier mechanism: a complaint centre where a service complaint is registered and docketed, and an appellate authority appointed by the provider to which an appeal lies when the complaint centre's redressal is unsatisfactory or delayed. Beyond the two tiers, a complainant may take the grievance to the public grievance machinery of the Department of Telecommunications, and the consumer commissions remain open throughout for a deficiency in service. What does not exist is the third figure the other sectors have: an independent ombudsman with power to award compensation binding on the provider. The Telecom Regulatory Authority recommended the creation of an office of telecom ombudsman, on the banking and insurance pattern, in recommendations issued on 10 March 2017; the recommendation was not accepted, and the office was never created. The State dimension of the electricity machinery deserves a sentence of its own, because it is where the uniform statute dissolves into State practice. The forum's composition, the ombudsman's office and the timelines are prescribed by each State Commission's regulations, the standards of performance and their compensation amounts are set State by State, and the supply codes differ in their detail, so two identical billing grievances on opposite sides of a State border are governed by the same sections of the same central Act and by materially different rules beneath them. The pattern is the field's federal signature: Parliament and the central regulators build the frame, State commissions and State legal metrology departments operate most of its counters.

The consequence is structural. The telecom complainant's escalation beyond the provider's own appellate tier runs either to a government grievance portal that monitors rather than adjudicates, or to the general statute, which is why telecom billing and service complaints form a steady stream in the consumer commissions out of proportion to the sums involved. Nor does the sector's tribunal fill the gap: the Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal adjudicates disputes between licensor and licensee, between licensees, and between a licensee and a group of consumers, a jurisdiction framed for collectives and competitors that does not receive the individual subscriber's billing complaint, so the individual is returned to the provider's tiers, the department's portal and the general statute, in that order of practical use.

The telecom regulator's consumer work is not exhausted by complaints. The Authority prescribes quality of service standards for the networks and publishes performance against them, regulates the tariff framework within which providers price, and maintains the regime against unsolicited commercial communication, under which telemarketing messages and calls are governed by subscriber preferences registered on a distributed ledger platform that commercial senders must use, the Telecom Commercial Communications Customer Preference Regulations, 2018. A subscriber's grievance about spam therefore travels a regulatory route with its own complaint discipline inside the provider's system, while a grievance about being charged for the spam's consequences is a billing complaint for the two tier mechanism. The sector shows, more clearly than any other, the difference between a regulator that protects consumers as a class by standards and enforcement, which the Authority does, and machinery that redresses the individual consumer, which in telecom remains the provider's own.

### Why is there no ombudsman for telecom complaints?

Because none was ever created. The Telecom Regulatory Authority of India has no statutory power to decide individual complaints, its 2012 regulations route subscribers through the service provider's own complaint centre and appellate authority, and the Authority's recommendation of March 2017 for an ombudsman on the banking pattern was not accepted by the government.

The securities market built its machinery later and in two stages. An investor's first complaint lies to the market participant itself, the broker, depository participant, registrar or listed company. Escalation runs to the Securities and Exchange Board of India's complaints platform, SCORES, on which the regulator takes up the complaint with the entity and monitors its disposal, a supervisory escalation of the same family as the Reserve Bank's. In 2023 the Securities and Exchange Board added an adjudicatory stage: by a circular of 31 July 2023 it caused the market infrastructure institutions to establish a common online dispute resolution portal, the SMART ODR portal, through which disputes between investors and listed companies or specified intermediaries may be resolved by online conciliation and, failing settlement, online arbitration whose award binds as an arbitral award. Registration of a dispute on the portal carries no fee, the conciliation and arbitration stages carry fees allocated between the parties under the framework, and the route cannot be used for a dispute already pending before a court, tribunal or consumer forum. The securities pattern is therefore a hybrid: supervisory escalation like the banking scheme, then a binding adjudication that, unlike an ombudsman's award, binds the investor too once the arbitration is invoked and concluded. Alongside it, the exchanges run their own investor grievance and arbitration mechanisms under the regulator's direction, a further sub layer the map records without unrolling. The pension sector completes the financial map: subscribers to the National Pension System complain through the centralised grievance management system maintained under the Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority, escalating from the intermediary to the pension regulator on the same pattern as the banking scheme, so that the whole of retail finance, bank, insurer, broker and pension, is covered by an escalation ladder of the same family, and the financial consumer's true position is that the general statute is her second system, kept for the claims the ladders cannot pay.

Read together, the five sectoral doors teach one lesson about design. Where the sectoral body is the regulator's own instrument, constituted outside the industry and armed with a binding award, as in banking and insurance, the complainant holds the advantage of one sided binding: accept and the provider pays, reject and the courts remain. Where the machinery is the provider's own, as in telecom, or a settlement forum inside the licensee with an ombudsman who is the sector regulator's appointee, as in electricity, the advantage is speed and specialism rather than asymmetry. And where the sector has built a true adjudication, as in securities since 2023, the complainant trades the one sided advantage for an award that closes the matter. The choice among doors, where more than one stands open, is a choice among these properties, and the routing table below is organised by them.

## Layer three: standards, safety and the package

The third layer begins from a different premise. The first two layers ask whether this buyer was wronged; the third asks whether this product should be on the shelf at all, and it answers through standards, licences and inspectors rather than through complaints. Its institutions are correspondingly different: they act on their own information, they bind the trade generally, and their sanctions run to seizure, prosecution and penalty rather than to an individual complainant's compensation.

The Bureau of Indian Standards, working under the Bureau of Indian Standards Act, 2016 (Act 11 of 2016), is the national standards body. It formulates Indian Standards, runs conformity assessment schemes and hallmarking, and licenses the use of the standard mark. Most standards are voluntary in themselves; the coercive edge arrives when the Central Government, by notification, brings a product under compulsory certification, after which the product may not be manufactured, imported or sold without certification and the mark. The list of products so brought in, from cement and steel to specified electrical appliances, toys and footwear, is the working boundary of lawful sale for those goods. A buyer who has been sold a sub standard product has a complaint for the first layer; a market flooded with uncertified goods of a notified kind is a matter for this layer, and the Bureau and the legal metrology and customs machinery are its enforcement hands.

### What does the Bureau of Indian Standards have to do with a consumer complaint?

Indirectly, a great deal. The Bureau's standards supply the benchmark against which a defect in goods is judged, its certification decides which notified products may lawfully be sold at all, and a trader who sells an uncertified notified product hands the complainant a defect claim measured against a published standard.

Two further instruments of this layer touch the buyer directly enough to name. Hallmarking applies the standards idea to precious metal: gold jewellery and artefacts were brought under compulsory hallmarking in phases beginning in June 2021, so that an article offered for sale must bear the Bureau's mark and the jeweller's identification, and the purity the buyer pays for becomes a certified fact rather than a representation, with the buyer's complaint for short purity measured against the hallmark standard. The grading and marking of agricultural produce under the Agricultural Produce (Grading and Marking) Act, 1937, the AGMARK system, performs the same function voluntarily for food commodities, a standard the seller may adopt and the buyer may then hold him to. Both illustrate the layer's method, which is to convert a quality claim into a verifiable mark before any dispute arises.

Food has its own statute and authority. The Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006 (Act 34 of 2006) consolidated a shelf of food laws into one, created the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India, and built a system of licensing and registration of food businesses, food safety officers, sampling and laboratory analysis, and adjudication of penalties by officers the Act designates, with an appellate tribunal above them. The consumer's position inside it is mostly that of a beneficiary: the standards for contaminants, additives, labelling and claims are made here, the licences are granted here, and the unsafe product is recalled or prosecuted here, while the individual buyer's claim for the price of a spoilt article or for harm suffered runs in the general statute, where the food standard supplies the measure of the defect. The food statute carries one refinement the pattern elsewhere lacks: it provides, within its own adjudication, for compensation to a consumer injured, or to the family of a consumer killed, by an article of food, assessed by the adjudicating officer of the food system rather than by a consumer commission, alongside the penalties for the offence itself. The buyer may still elect the general statute, and for harm from food the two routes overlap in the manner this article's concurrency discussion describes, but the food system is the one standards regime that kept a compensatory instrument of its own, a survival from the consolidated food laws it replaced, which had treated the poisoned or injured buyer as a person to be compensated by the same machinery that punished the seller.

Drugs and cosmetics stand on the same pattern under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 (Act 23 of 1940), a licensing and inspection statute administered by drug controllers at the Centre and in the States, under which manufacture and sale of spurious, adulterated and misbranded drugs and cosmetics are offences; and the advertising edge of the drug trade is governed by the Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954 (Act 21 of 1954), which prohibits advertisements of drugs and remedies claiming to cure or treat the diseases and conditions it schedules, and advertisements attributing magical qualities to an article. The pattern of this layer is uniform: Parliament writes the standard, an authority licenses the trade, inspectors police the market, and the individual's money claim is sent back to the general statute.

The enforcement temper of this layer has itself been changing, and the change belongs on the map because it alters what a complaint to an inspector sets in motion. The metrology offences were historically criminal, punishable with fines and, for repeat offences, imprisonment, prosecuted before magistrates on the department's complaint. The Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act, 2023, which amended the Legal Metrology Act among a schedule of business facing statutes, replaced imprisonment with enhanced monetary penalties for the offences it covers, continuing a policy of decriminalising regulatory default while raising its price. The standards layer is thereby converging, in its sanctioning style, on the rest of the field: money penalties administered by the enforcing authority, with prosecution reserved for the gravest conduct, and the complainant's compensation claim still travelling, as always, to the general statute, which no amendment of the standards layer has ever touched.

Quantity and price live in a statute of their own. The Legal Metrology Act, 2009 (Act 1 of 2010), which the article on [the Legal Metrology Act 2009 and the law of the package](/2021/03/15/legal-metrology-act-2009-explained/) owns in full, establishes the units of weights and measures, requires verification and stamping of instruments, and, through Section 18 and the Legal Metrology (Packaged Commodities) Rules made under the Act, prescribes the declarations every pre packaged commodity must carry: the name and address of the manufacturer or packer, the common name of the commodity, the net quantity, the month and year of manufacture or packing, and the maximum retail price inclusive of all taxes. Selling above the printed maximum retail price, selling a package short of its declared quantity, and selling without the prescribed declarations are offences under the metrology statute, policed by the State controllers of legal metrology and their inspectors. The same facts also make a consumer complaint, since overcharging sits inside the definition of an unfair trade practice and a shortfall in quantity inside the definition of a defect, and the complainant chooses between complaining to the metrology inspector, whose remedy is a penalty to the State, and suing in the commission, whose remedy is compensation to the complainant. A grievance about an advertisement makes the same double journey in mirror image: the competition statute's machinery does not entertain the individual buyer's complaint, because the Competition Act, 2002 addresses agreements and abuse of dominance in markets rather than the advertisement that misled one buyer, and the misleading advertisement as a wrong to consumers belongs to the Central Authority and the commissions of the general statute, to which this article returns below.

## Advertising as a consumer wrong

Advertising runs across all three layers and deserves its map. The oldest control is self regulation: the Advertising Standards Council of India, a body of the advertising and media industries formed in 1985, maintains a code and decides complaints from the public and from competitors, quickly and without fee, but with no statutory force behind its directions beyond the willingness of media owners to honour them. Statutory control sat for decades in fragments, in the Drugs and Magic Remedies Act for cures, in the food and metrology statutes for claims on labels, and in broadcasting codes for television content. The Act of 2019 made the misleading advertisement a defined wrong of the general statute: Section 2(28) defines it by its content, an advertisement that falsely describes a product or service, gives a false guarantee, conveys an express or implied representation that would constitute an unfair trade practice, or deliberately conceals important information. The Central Authority is its principal enforcer, empowered to order discontinuation or modification and to penalise the manufacturer, the endorser and the publisher on the scale stated earlier in this guide, and the Authority's Guidelines for Prevention of Misleading Advertisements and Endorsements, issued in 2022, specify the discipline in detail, covering bait advertisements, surrogate advertising, free claims and the disclaimers' prominence. The individual buyer's claim travels separately to a commission, where the advertisement is evidence of the unfair trade practice and of the standard the trader claimed for the goods, and the same advertisement may therefore be answered in three places at once, by the industry's council, by the regulator and by a commission, each with a different remedy and none barring the others. The pattern is the concurrency of the field in its most everyday form, and it explains why the misleading advertisement, a wrong that rarely justifies one buyer's litigation, is still among the most acted upon: it is the one wrong for which the law built a plaintiff that does not need to have bought anything.

## The e-commerce layer within the general statute

Electronic commerce needed no ombudsman because it was absorbed into the general statute. The Act of 2019 defines buying through electronic means into the definition of consumer, reaches the unfair trade practice and misleading advertisement wherever published, and extends its product liability chapter to the online seller. The working detail sits in the Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020, made under the Act and brought into force with its principal provisions. The Rules address both models of the trade, the marketplace entity that provides the platform and the inventory entity that owns the goods and sells them, and they impose a common floor of duties. Every e-commerce entity must display its legal name and address, its grievance officer's name and contact details, and the information a buyer needs before purchase, including the terms of return, refund, exchange, warranty and delivery, the payment methods and their security, and the country of origin of the goods. A grievance officer must acknowledge a consumer complaint within forty eight hours and redress it within one month of receipt. No e-commerce entity may impose a cancellation charge on a consumer unless the entity bears a similar charge when it cancels, may record consent to a purchase by any means other than an explicit and affirmative act of the consumer, or manipulate price to gain unreasonable profit. Sellers on a marketplace must give the importer details for imported goods, must not refuse to take back goods that are defective, deficient, delivered late or different from their description, and the marketplace must require and display the sellers' undertakings and details the Rules specify.

The advertising frontier of the online layer is the dark pattern. In 2023 the Central Authority issued the Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns, enumerating interface practices designed to mislead a user into an action not intended, among them false urgency, basket sneaking of additional items into a checkout, confirm shaming, forced action, subscription traps, interface interference, bait and switch, drip pricing, disguised advertisements and nagging, and treating their use as an unfair trade practice under the Act. The guidelines are the general statute's method applied to interface design: the wrong is defined in the vocabulary of defect and deception the statute already has, and the enforcement runs through the Authority against the practice and through the commissions for the individual buyer.

Product liability distributes itself across the online models in a way buyers rarely see and sellers litigate. The Act's chapter fastens liability on the manufacturer for the product, on the service provider for the service, and on a seller who is not the manufacturer on the narrower grounds the chapter states, among them exercising substantial control over the design, testing, manufacture, packaging or labelling of the product, altering the product, making an express warranty of its own, or selling the product of a manufacturer who cannot be identified or is not subject to the law in force in India. A marketplace entity that merely hosts is treated by the scheme of the Act and the Rules as an intermediary with its own duties of diligence and grievance handling, while an inventory entity that owns and sells the goods stands in the seller's place with the seller's exposure, and the Rules separately make a marketplace answerable where it fails the duties they impose on it. The practical consequence is that the buyer's claim follows the control: the more the platform selected, stored, priced, packed and delivered the goods, the more of the seller's liability it carries, and the grievance officer's answer is only the first, cheapest form of that accountability.

Cross border selling produces the layer's characteristic gap. The Rules apply to e-commerce entities offering goods or services to consumers in India, including entities not established in India, and the general statute's definitions reach the transaction; but enforcement against a seller with no presence, assets or officer in India depends on the platform's accountability and on payment intermediaries, and the complainant's practical lever is usually the marketplace entity and the chargeback discipline of the payment system rather than the foreign seller. The honest statement of the position is that the law asserts jurisdiction it cannot always execute, and that the graded answer available to the buyer is the platform grievance mechanism, the Central Authority's power over the entity's Indian facing operation, and the criminal and civil process where the entity submits to it.

## Concurrency: using the doors in sequence

Overlapping remedies work only because the law disciplines them, and the discipline has three rules. The first is the additionality rule of Section 100 of the Act of 2019, carried from Section 3 of the Act of 1986: the consumer statute adds to other laws and does not displace them, so the existence of a sectoral scheme does not close the commission, and the existence of the commission does not close the scheme. The second is the single recovery rule: the same loss cannot be compensated twice, so a complainant who has accepted an ombudsman's award in full and final settlement cannot reopen the same claim before a commission, and a complainant who recovers in one forum must disclose and account for it in another. The third is the election rule that particular statutes write into the concurrency: some schemes bar their own doors once another is opened, the insurance ombudsman being the clear case, since no complaint lies there on a subject matter that is or was before a court, a consumer forum or an arbitrator, while the banking scheme states no such bar and instead lapses its own award if the complainant does not accept it.

The arbitration clause is where concurrency was fought hardest. Builders and service providers argued that a consumer who had signed an arbitration agreement had elected the arbitral forum. In Emaar MGF Land Limited v. Aftab Singh, (2019) 12 SCC 751, the Supreme Court held that the existence of an arbitration clause does not bar a consumer complaint, because the remedies of the consumer statute are in addition to and not in derogation of other laws, and a party cannot by contract withdraw a dispute from a statutory public forum the legislature created for consumers as a class. The practical sequence the law therefore blesses is the one the brief of this guide is built on: use the free sectoral door first where one exists, because it costs nothing and, where the award is one sided in binding effect, preserves everything; and keep the commission for the grievance the sector cannot reach, the claim above the sectoral ceiling, and the award the complainant declines.

A worked sequence shows the rules in motion. A policyholder whose mediclaim is repudiated complains in writing to the insurer, as the Rules require; the insurer rejects the representation; within the year she complains to the Insurance Ombudsman, who fails to settle the matter by mediation and passes an award below the loss she claims. She does not accept the award, and it therefore does not bind her; she files before the District Commission within the two years the general statute allows, pleading repudiation as deficiency in service, and the commission decides the claim on the policy's terms, uninfluenced in law by the award she declined, though the insurer remains bound by the award only if she had accepted it. Had she accepted and taken the money in full and final settlement, the commission door would have been closed by the single recovery rule. Her neighbour, whose identical claim exceeded the ombudsman's ceiling from the start, went directly to the commission, and took longer; the difference in their journeys is the design working exactly as intended, the small claim processed by the regulator's instrument and the large one by the court.

Evidence decides these small cases more often than doctrine does, and the layering has a paperwork consequence worth stating. Each forum works on the complainant's file: the policy and the repudiation letter, the bank statement and the failed transaction record, the bill and the meter reading, the advertisement as published and the invoice as issued, and the party that kept the file usually holds the case. A complainant assembling that file, invoices, screenshots, correspondence and recordings, is doing in miniature what investigators and reporters do at scale, and the discipline of keeping original material intact and organised is the same discipline described in [a practical account of how journalists use a vault to protect their sources and records](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/), which transfers directly: keep the original, keep the date, keep the chain. The consumer commissions' electronic filing portal and the sectoral schemes' online intake have quietly made the well kept digital file the standard instrument of the field.

Concurrency has limits that are not election rules at all, and two deserve record because complainants meet them as surprises. The first is subject matter: a complaint that is in substance a dispute the sector statute reserves elsewhere may be turned away from the commission on the ground that a specialised regime governs it exhaustively, and the boundary is fought case by case, in electricity between the assessment proceedings of the Act for unauthorised use and theft, which carry their own appellate route, and the ordinary billing grievance, which does not. The second is that parallel proceedings of a different kind do not bar a complaint: a prosecution under a standards or penal statute, a regulator's supervisory action, and a consumer complaint for compensation arising from the same facts travel independently, because they answer different questions, and the concurrency the statute protects is a concurrency of remedies for the consumer, not a queue in which every proceeding must wait for the first to finish.

## The housing fork

Housing is the grievance where the choice of door carries the largest consequences, and it is treated here at the level the cluster assigns to this guide, the dedicated comparison being owned by [the article on RERA and consumer protection for homebuyers](/2019/02/15/rera-vs-consumer-protection-homebuyers/). The Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016 (Act 23 of 2016) created State real estate regulatory authorities with power over registered projects, including power to order refund with interest and compensation for delay and defects, and it originally appeared to channel homebuyers away from the consumer commissions. Two holdings settled the fork. In Pioneer Urban Land and Infrastructure Limited v. Govindan Raghavan, (2019) 5 SCC 725, the Supreme Court held that one sided clauses in a builder buyer agreement, of the kind that bind the buyer to the builder's delay while freeing the builder from his own, amount to an unfair trade practice under the consumer statute. In Imperia Structures Limited v. Anil Patni, (2020) 10 SCC 783, the Court held that the bar in the real estate statute against civil courts does not bar the consumer commissions, that the remedies under the two statutes are concurrent, and that a homebuyer may elect either forum. The election is real and consequential: the real estate authority offers a specialist regulator with continuing supervision of the project and its accounts, the commission offers the general statute's relief set and its own execution machinery, and the buyer who has pursued one to a conclusion cannot pursue the other for the same relief. The real estate machinery itself divides the work in a way homebuyers must notice: complaints to the authority about the promoter's obligations are decided by the authority, while claims to compensation for specified defaults, of the kind arising on false statements, failure to hand over possession and structural defects, are decided by an adjudicating officer appointed under that Act, with an appellate tribunal above both. Housing is therefore the one field with two complete statutory systems standing side by side, each with its own appellate route, and the choice between them is the most consequential forum election in Indian consumer law. A third, softer door has grown inside the real estate system and belongs on the map for completeness: several State authorities, the Maharashtra authority most prominently, operate conciliation forums in which panels drawn from the industry and from consumer bodies attempt a settlement between buyer and promoter before or alongside a formal complaint, and a settlement recorded there disposes of the grievance by agreement in the manner of the general statute's mediation. The conciliation door charges the field's usual price for speed, which is the surrender of a reasoned order, and its popularity with promoters and buyers alike is evidence for the proposition this guide has argued throughout, that most grievances in this field want a supervised negotiation more than they want a judgment.

## The defence and the criticism of the layering

The defence of the layered design is made most strongly by the regulators who operate it, and it runs on volume and specialism. The Reserve Bank, in presenting the integrated scheme of 2021, rested it on the proposition that a single, free, jurisdiction neutral intake disposes of the mass of banking grievances faster and more cheaply than any court structured body could, and on the supervisory dividend that a regulator learns where its regulated entities fail by reading their complaints. The specialism argument is the same claim one level down: an ombudsman who reads insurance repudiation letters all year applies the policy wordings, the regulator's claim settlement norms and the industry's own practices to the file, where a generalist commission applies the open textured standard of deficiency; and a specialist forum that charges nothing, sits in the sector's regions and works on documents is, for a small claim, simply a better instrument than a court. The electricity design adds a third argument, that the grievance ladder is part of the regulatory compact itself: the standards of performance the commission sets are enforced, for the individual consumer, by the forum and the ombudsman the same Act creates, so standard, compensation and forum arrive in one statute.

The volume evidence gives the defence its weight. The Reserve Bank's integrated scheme receives complaints in the several lakhs each year on its published returns, and the insurance ombudsman system recorded close to fifty thousand complaints disposed of in the year 2023 to 2024 on the figures of the Council for Insurance Ombudsmen, volumes that no conceivable expansion of the commissions could have carried at the same speed. Set against them, the commissions' own intake across the country is also large but is measured in a queue rather than in a flow, and the comparison of the two systems' published numbers is the closest the field comes to an experiment: same citizen, same grievance classes, one system built as a processing line and the other as a court.

The criticism is made most persistently by consumer organisations, and it was put on record in the consultation that produced the telecom regulations of 2012, where consumer organisations argued that a mechanism located inside the service provider, of the nodal officer and appellate authority pattern, had not been effective and sought a body with consumer representation outside the provider. The general form of the criticism has three limbs. The first is constitutional in the small sense: the sectoral ombudsmen are constituted and funded within the regulatory apparatus of the industries they sit against, their offices are paid for through levies on those industries, and a complainant is entitled to wonder whether an adjudicator fed by the respondent's regulator will lean; the answer given is the one sided binding design, which is itself an admission that the complainant needs protection from the forum's possible tilt, since a forum trusted completely would not need to leave its award open to rejection. The second limb is the ceiling: the sectoral awards are capped, at twenty lakh rupees of loss in the banking scheme and fifty lakh rupees in the insurance scheme, so the largest grievances, which are often the gravest, are excluded from the fast track precisely when the complainant most needs it, and must travel the commissions' slower road with everyone else. The third limb is knowledge: a remedy that must be discovered is a remedy unevenly distributed, and the sectoral machinery is used most by the complainants who least need its protection, the urban, the literate and the banked, while the grievances of the rest continue to arrive at the commissions or nowhere.

The capture limb of the criticism also has an answer on the record, and fairness requires it beside the charge. The funding of the ombudsman offices by levy on the regulated industry is the funding of the regulators themselves, whose budgets are not voted as ordinary departmental expenditure, and no one has shown a pattern of awards bending toward the industry that pays; the published returns show awards and settlements running overwhelmingly in complainants' favour on entertained complaints, which is the pattern the one sided design predicts, since an office that decided for the industry as a habit would simply be bypassed for the commissions and wither. The reform position with the strongest pedigree is not abolition of the sectoral offices but their unification, the Financial Redress Agency recommended by the Financial Sector Legislative Reforms Commission under Justice B.N. Srikrishna in 2013, which accepted the ombudsman model and attacked only its fragmentation. The criticism that survives scrutiny is therefore narrower than the one usually printed: not that the offices are captured, but that a citizen must learn five rulebooks to use them, and that the sector most in need of one, telecom, has none.

Between the defence and the criticism, the record supports one verdict and refuses another. It supports the verdict on architecture: the one sided binding effect is a genuinely well designed compromise, because it lets a regulator run a fast, free, specialist forum without asking the citizen to surrender the courts as the price of using it, and the thirty years since the banking scheme of 1995 have produced no coherent proposal for replacing it in the sectors where it operates. It refuses a verdict on performance, because the published figures of the schemes are figures of disposal, counting complaints closed, and disposal is not the same as satisfaction; a complaint closed by an unaccepted award counts as disposed in the scheme's return and as unresolved in the complainant's household. The honest conclusion is that the layering works as designed and the design's weakest joint is not capture or ceilings but the first step, the citizen's knowledge that the door exists, which is a failure of publicity rather than of law and is therefore the one failure the law's own institutions could repair without legislating.

## The digital services frontier

The field's growth area is the service sold as software and subscription, and its treatment shows the layered design absorbing a new subject without new statutes. A streaming service that charges for a catalogue it then thins, an application that sells a premium tier whose features migrate behind further payment, a cloud service that loses the customer's files, a food delivery platform whose charges differ from the menu it displayed: each is a service hired for consideration, each failure a deficiency measured against what was undertaken, and each complaint lies to a commission on the general statute's ordinary terms, with the e-commerce rules supplying the disclosure discipline where the sale was made on a platform. The frontier difficulties are evidentiary and jurisdictional rather than conceptual. The terms of the service live in documents the provider can amend, the performance data sits on the provider's servers, and the provider may be established abroad with an Indian subsidiary that disclaims the contract, so the commissions' affidavit procedure and the Authority's information powers do work the founders of the summary forum did not imagine, and the complainant's kept file, the screenshots and invoices whose keeping this guide commended in the concurrency discussion, becomes the case. Sectoral machinery reaches into the frontier only where the service is a regulated one: the failed wallet or payment transaction is a Reserve Bank scheme complaint about a payment system participant, the broadband failure is a telecom grievance for the two tiers, the demat failure is a SCORES complaint, while the unregulated subscription stands on the general statute alone. The pattern confirms the article's organising claim from a new direction: the layers were assigned by industry long before the products merged, and the digital service is routed by asking which regulated industry, if any, its provider belongs to, a question whose answer is increasingly a matter of corporate structure rather than of the service the citizen bought.

## The distinctions that organise the field

For the reader who must retain the field entire, in study, in advice or in examination, it resolves into seven distinctions, and every section above is an elaboration of one of them. The first is between a defect and a deficiency, the division of wrongs by subject matter, goods against services. The second is between adjudication and regulation, the commission that decides between parties against the Authority and the sector regulators that police practices and markets. The third is between binding both sides and binding one, the design feature that separates a court from an ombudsman and that makes the sequence of remedies possible. The fourth is between compensation and correction, the money a commission or ombudsman awards against the bill corrected, the connection restored and the practice discontinued, which no single forum supplies together. The fifth is between the value of what was paid and the value of what is claimed, the basis on which the 2019 Act assigns a complaint to its tier, having discarded the older Act's invitation to value the claim. The sixth is between the standard and the sale, the layer that decides what may be marketed at all against the layers that decide what follows when a lawful sale goes wrong for one buyer. The seventh is between election and accumulation, the points at which the law makes a complainant choose a road, acceptance of an award, pursuit of one housing forum to conclusion, against the general rule that remedies accumulate and only recovery may not be doubled. A reader who can state these seven, and give the statute or scheme that governs each, holds the field in a form that survives the amendment of any particular limit, ceiling or rule, because limits change by notification while the distinctions are written into the architecture.

## The machinery around the machinery: helplines, funds and State counters

Around the forums stands a penumbra of machinery that carries a large share of the field's actual grievances and belongs on any honest map. The Department of Consumer Affairs maintains the National Consumer Helpline, a public intake reachable on the short code 1915 and through an integrated grievance portal, which registers complaints against companies, forwards them to the companies' nodal officers for response within a stated period, and advises callers on the statutory route where the company's answer fails; it decides nothing and awards nothing, and its value is as the field's front desk, converting grievances that would otherwise be letters into tracked files. State consumer helplines perform the same function at the State counter. Behind the commissions sits the Consumer Welfare Fund, the repository into which sums that cannot be traced to identifiable consumers are credited under the statute, applied to consumer welfare and education purposes, and drawn on among other things for the costs of mediation cells, a small fiscal loop in which unclaimed consumer money funds consumer machinery. District collectors assist the Central Authority's inquiries and enforcement within their districts, and the State and district consumer protection councils advise the governments that appoint them. None of this machinery appears in the statute's chapter headings as a remedy, and all of it decides, in practice, which statutory remedy a grievance reaches, because the helpline's advice is the moment at which most complainants first learn that an ombudsman exists.

## What the general statute still owns

Strip away every sectoral door and a residue remains that only the general statute can answer, and the residue is large. It owns the defective good sold by the unregulated trader and the deficient service of every provider no regulator supervises: the builder outside a registered project, the coaching institute, the courier, the clinic, the repair shop, the professional. It owns the misleading advertisement as a class wrong, through the Central Authority's power to act without an individual victim, and the compensation claim of the individual buyer misled, through the commissions. It owns harm from a defective product as a structured cause of action since 2019, where the sectoral schemes own only the complaint against their own regulated entity. It owns the mediation chapter, the one place in the field where a dispute may be settled by agreement facilitated by a cell attached to the very commission that would otherwise decide it. And it owns the appellate spine of the system, State Commission to National Commission to the Supreme Court, through which the definitions of consumer, defect and deficiency acquire the settled meanings every other forum borrows. The reader who began with a rejected claim or a failed payment and was routed away from the commissions should therefore not mistake the routing for a hierarchy: the ombudsman is the faster door for the grievances it fits, and the general statute is the house. What remains unbuilt is equally part of the map. Telecom still lacks its ombudsman, decades into mass subscription. The financial schemes still differ in ceiling, window and appeal for reasons of institutional history rather than of principle, and the unified redress agency recommended in 2013 remains a report. The commissions still wait on their appointing governments for the benches their statute promises. And no single intake exists at which a citizen may state a grievance once and be routed by the State to the right desk; the routing remains the citizen's own work, assisted by helplines and, in the end, by guides of the kind this article attempts. The practical method of the field, stated as one sentence, is to ask first whether a sectoral door fits the grievance and its ceiling, to use it if it does because it costs nothing and preserves the alternative, and to take everything else, and everything the sectoral door could not satisfy, to the commissions, which is where consumer law in India, in the end, lives.

For the business and compliance reader, the same map reads in reverse, as a table of exposures. A seller of goods stands under the standards layer for what it may market, the metrology layer for what its packages must declare, the advertising discipline for what it may claim, the general statute for every defect and deficiency its customers plead, and, where it sells through platforms, the e-commerce rules for the disclosures and grievance clocks of the channel. A regulated financial or utility business stands under all of that and under its sectoral ladder besides, with the added exposure that its complaint record is read by its regulator as supervision evidence, so that a grievance mishandled is not merely a claim lost but a finding invited. The compliance conclusion the map supports is that consumer law is not a department's subject but the operating condition of every consumer facing business in the country, priced into its documentation, its complaint staffing and its advertising sign off, and the businesses that treat the layers as one system, routing each grievance to its right desk within their own walls before the State routes it for them, are the ones the machinery touches least.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which laws protect consumers in India?

The central law is the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, which replaced the Act of 1986 and supplies the definition of a consumer, the wrongs of defect, deficiency, unfair trade practice and misleading advertisement, a regulator in the Central Consumer Protection Authority, and the three tier of district, State and national commissions. Around it stand sectoral mechanisms the consumer statute does not create: the Reserve Bank's Integrated Ombudsman Scheme for banking and payments, the Insurance Ombudsman under rules of 2017, the electricity grievance forums and ombudsman under the Electricity Act, 2003, and the securities market's SCORES platform and SMART ODR portal. Beneath all of them sit the Indian Contract Act, 1872 and the Sale of Goods Act, 1930, and beside them the standards statutes, the Bureau of Indian Standards Act, 2016, the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006 and the Legal Metrology Act, 2009.

### Q: Who counts as a consumer in law?

Under Section 2(7) of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, a consumer is a person who buys goods or hires or avails services for a consideration, including a user of the goods or a beneficiary of the service with the buyer's approval, and including purchases made online. A person who obtains goods for resale or for a commercial purpose is excluded, as is a person who avails a service for a commercial purpose. The exclusion is cut back by an explanation: goods bought and used exclusively for earning a livelihood by self employment are not bought for a commercial purpose, the position the Supreme Court explained in Laxmi Engineering Works v. P.S.G. Industrial Institute. Services rendered free of charge and services under a contract of personal service are also outside the definition.

### Q: Which sectors have their own consumer grievance machinery?

Five sectors have machinery of their own. Banking, non banking finance and payment systems are covered by the Reserve Bank's Integrated Ombudsman Scheme, 2021. Insurance is covered by the Insurance Ombudsman under the Insurance Ombudsman Rules, 2017. Electricity distribution carries a grievance forum in every licensee and an Electricity Ombudsman appointed by the State Commission under the Electricity Act, 2003. Telecommunications has a two tier mechanism inside the service provider under the Telecom Consumers Complaint Redressal Regulations, 2012, but no ombudsman. Securities investors use the Securities and Exchange Board's SCORES platform and the SMART ODR portal for online conciliation and arbitration. Housing has the real estate regulatory authorities under the Act of 2016 beside the commissions, and every other sector falls to the general statute alone.

### Q: When should a consumer use a sectoral ombudsman instead?

Whenever the grievance fits a sectoral scheme and the amount fits its ceiling, because the ombudsman route costs nothing, requires no lawyer, works on documents, and in banking and insurance binds the provider without binding the complainant. A rejected insurance claim, a failed or wrongly charged banking transaction and an electricity billing dispute are the standard cases. The ombudsman is the wrong door when the claim exceeds the scheme's award ceiling, when the grievance is about a product's defect rather than a regulated entity's service, when findings on disputed facts and cross examination are genuinely needed, or when the complainant wants precedent or punishment rather than compensation. The deciding question is not which forum is stronger in the abstract but which one's powers match the grievance.

### Q: Which authorities regulate product safety and set standards?

The Bureau of Indian Standards, under the Bureau of Indian Standards Act, 2016, is the national standards body: it formulates standards, runs conformity assessment and hallmarking, and licenses the standard mark, and the Central Government may bring specified products under compulsory certification so they cannot lawfully be sold without it. Food safety sits with the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India under the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006. Drugs and cosmetics are licensed and inspected under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940, and drug advertising is restrained by the Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954. The Central Consumer Protection Authority adds a market wide power to order recall of unsafe goods under the Act of 2019.

### Q: Can a consumer pursue more than one remedy?

In sequence, yes; for double recovery, no. Section 100 of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 provides that the Act is in addition to and not in derogation of any other law, so the consumer statute does not close the sectoral schemes, the civil courts or the contract remedies, and the Supreme Court confirmed in Emaar MGF Land Limited v. Aftab Singh that even an arbitration clause does not bar a consumer complaint. The discipline is that the same loss cannot be recovered twice, and acceptance of an ombudsman's award in full and final settlement closes the claim it settles. Some doors close others: the Insurance Ombudsman cannot be approached on a matter that is or was before a court, a consumer forum or an arbitrator.

### Q: What consumer protection applies to e-commerce?

The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 covers online purchases within the definition of consumer and reaches unfair trade practices and misleading advertisements wherever published. The working duties sit in the Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020, which apply to marketplace and inventory models alike. An e-commerce entity must display its legal name, address and grievance officer, and disclose return, refund, warranty, delivery and payment terms and the country of origin before purchase. The grievance officer must acknowledge a complaint within forty eight hours and redress it within one month. Cancellation charges may not be imposed unless the entity bears a similar charge for its own cancellations, consent to purchase must be an explicit act, and sellers must take back goods that are defective, deficient, late or unlike their description.

### Q: Which consumer statutes has India replaced and when?

The general statute itself is the main case: the Consumer Protection Act, 1986 was repealed and replaced by the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, whose principal provisions were brought into force on 20 July 2020 and 24 July 2020. The standards layer was rebuilt the same way: the Bureau of Indian Standards Act, 1986 gave way to the Act of 2016, and the Legal Metrology Act, 2009 replaced the Standards of Weights and Measures Act, 1976 and the Standards of Weights and Measures (Enforcement) Act, 1985. The Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006 consolidated and replaced a shelf of food orders and Acts. In the sectoral layer, the Reserve Bank replaced its three separate ombudsman schemes with the integrated scheme of 2021.

### Q: What is the difference between a defect in goods and a deficiency in service?

The two words divide the subject matter of a consumer complaint. A defect, defined in Section 2(10) of the Act of 2019, is a fault, imperfection or shortcoming in the quality, quantity, potency, purity or standard of goods, measured against what the law requires, the contract provides or the trader claims. A deficiency, defined in Section 2(11), is a fault, imperfection, shortcoming or inadequacy in the quality, nature or manner of performance of a service, measured against the standard the law requires or the provider undertook. Goods therefore have defects and services have deficiencies, the framing decides what the complainant must prove and against which benchmark, and one transaction can yield both, as where a defective appliance is followed by a repair service that fails to repair it.

### Q: How much compensation can the Reserve Bank ombudsman award?

Under the Reserve Bank Integrated Ombudsman Scheme, 2021, the ombudsman may pass an award directing the regulated entity to pay the loss suffered by the complainant as a direct consequence of the deficiency in service, subject to a ceiling of twenty lakh rupees. Separately, the ombudsman may award compensation of up to one lakh rupees for the loss of the complainant's time, the expenses incurred, and the mental agony and harassment suffered, so the practical maximum combines the two heads. There is no ceiling on the value of the dispute the ombudsman may entertain; the ceilings limit the award, not the complaint. The award binds the bank, non banking financial company or payment participant once the complainant accepts it in full and final settlement, and an unaccepted award lapses, leaving the complainant's other remedies intact.

### Q: How much can the Insurance Ombudsman award on a complaint?

The Insurance Ombudsman cannot award compensation exceeding the loss suffered by the complainant as a direct consequence of the cause of action, and the award carries a monetary ceiling fixed by the Insurance Ombudsman Rules, 2017. That ceiling stood at thirty lakh rupees, inclusive of relevant expenses, and was raised to fifty lakh rupees by an amendment to the Rules notified in November 2023. Complaints whose substance exceeds the ceiling belong before the consumer commissions, whose jurisdiction runs by the consideration paid and has no equivalent cap on the compensation they may award within their tiers. The ombudsman must pass the award within three months of receiving all requirements from the complainant, and the insurer must comply within thirty days of an award the complainant has accepted.

### Q: Who may file a consumer complaint?

Section 35 of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 lists the complainants, and the list is wider than the buyer alone. A complaint may be filed by the consumer to whom the goods were sold or the services provided; by a recognised voluntary consumer association, whether or not the consumer is its member; by one or more consumers on behalf of numerous consumers having the same interest, with the commission's permission; by the Central Government, a State Government or the Central Consumer Protection Authority; and, where the consumer is a minor, by a parent or legal guardian, and where the consumer has died, by the legal heir or representative. The association and government routes matter because they let a grievance with a small value and a large principle be carried by a body with the resources to carry it.

### Q: What does it cost to file a complaint before a consumer commission?

Under the Consumer Protection (Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions) Rules, 2020, no fee is charged on a complaint where the value of the goods or services paid as consideration does not exceed five lakh rupees. Above that figure a graded fee applies, rising with the consideration through the District, State and National tiers, and remaining modest by the standards of civil court fees at every level. The sectoral routes cost nothing at all: neither the Reserve Bank's Integrated Ombudsman Scheme nor the Insurance Ombudsman charges a fee, and the electricity grievance forum and ombudsman are likewise free to the consumer. Cost is therefore a reason to choose the sectoral door where one fits, but it is rarely a barrier at the commission either, since the largest class of complaints by value pays no fee.

### Q: How is the consumer commission chosen by the value of the claim?

Since the Act of 2019, the tier is chosen by the value of the goods or services paid as consideration, not by the compensation claimed, which reverses the basis the Act of 1986 used. As enacted, the District Commission took complaints up to one crore rupees of consideration, the State Commission from one crore to ten crore rupees, and the National Commission above ten crore rupees. The Consumer Protection (Jurisdiction of the District Commission, the State Commission and the National Commission) Rules, 2021, notified on 30 December 2021, reset the tiers: the District Commission where the consideration paid does not exceed fifty lakh rupees, the State Commission where it exceeds fifty lakh rupees but not two crore rupees, and the National Commission above two crore rupees.

### Q: What can the Central Consumer Protection Authority do that a consumer commission cannot?

A commission answers the complainant before it; the Central Consumer Protection Authority, created by the Act of 2019, acts for consumers as a class and needs no individual victim to begin. It may inquire and investigate violations of consumer rights on a complaint, on a reference from the Central Government or of its own motion, through an investigation wing headed by a Director General. It may order recall of goods and withdrawal of services found dangerous, order discontinuation of unfair trade practices, direct reimbursement, and impose penalties for misleading advertisements on the manufacturer, the endorser and the publisher, none of which a commission deciding a single complaint is built to do. Its orders are enforceable across the market rather than between two parties, which is the difference between a regulator and a court.

### Q: Where does an investor take a complaint against a broker or a listed company?

The first complaint lies to the market participant itself. If that does not resolve the grievance, the investor escalates it on SCORES, the Securities and Exchange Board of India's complaints platform, on which the regulator takes the complaint up with the broker, depository participant, registrar or listed company and monitors disposal. If the investor remains dissatisfied, a circular of the Board dated 31 July 2023 added a dispute resolution stage: the SMART ODR portal, a common online portal run by the market infrastructure institutions, through which the dispute is attempted to be settled by online conciliation and, failing settlement, decided by online arbitration. Registration on the portal is free, and the route is unavailable for a dispute already pending before a court, tribunal or consumer forum.

### Q: How does a telecom subscriber get a billing complaint decided?

Not by an ombudsman, because telecom has none. Under the Telecom Consumers Complaint Redressal Regulations, 2012, the subscriber first registers the complaint at the service provider's complaint centre, which must docket it and state the time within which it will be resolved. A subscriber dissatisfied with that redressal, or whose complaint is not addressed in the stated time, may appeal to the appellate authority the provider appoints under the same regulations. The Telecom Regulatory Authority of India frames the regulations and monitors the system but has no statutory power to decide an individual complaint, which is why its recommendation of March 2017 proposed an ombudsman the government did not create. Beyond the two tiers the subscriber may use the Department of Telecommunications grievance machinery and may file before a consumer commission for deficiency in service.

### Q: Can a person complain about a misleading advertisement without having bought the product?

Yes. A misleading advertisement is actionable in two ways, and only one of them needs a purchase. An individual complaint before a consumer commission is built on the complainant's own grievance, but the Central Consumer Protection Authority under the Act of 2019 may inquire into a misleading advertisement on a complaint by any person, on a reference, or of its own motion, and may order its discontinuation and penalise the manufacturer, endorser and publisher. The Authority's jurisdiction is over the practice, not over a transaction, so the absence of a purchase is no obstacle. The advertisement regulator of the advertising industry, the Advertising Standards Council of India, also receives complaints from any member of the public under its self regulatory code, though its directions carry no statutory force of their own.

### Q: What must a claimant prove in a product liability action?

The product liability chapter of the Act of 2019, Sections 82 to 87, requires the claimant to show harm caused by a defective product, and distributes the burden by defendant. A product manufacturer is liable where the product contains a manufacturing defect, is defective in design, deviates from its manufacturing specifications, does not conform to an express warranty, or fails to carry adequate instructions for correct use or warnings of improper use. A product service provider is liable for faulty, imperfect or deficient service, for acts of omission or negligence, for withholding information and for failure to honour an express warranty. A product seller who is not the manufacturer is liable on narrower grounds, including exercising substantial control over the product, altering it, giving an independent express warranty, or selling for a manufacturer who cannot be identified or reached.

### Q: What happens if a consumer dispute is sent to mediation?

The Act of 2019 gives every commission a mediation cell and the power, at the stage of admission or later with the parties' consent, to refer a dispute to mediation where the commission sees an element of settlement acceptable to the parties. The mediator conducts the process within the time the commission fixes, and the proceeding is confidential. If the parties settle, the settlement is recorded, signed by the parties and the mediator, and the commission passes an order in its terms, which gives the settlement the enforceability of a commission's order without a contested hearing. If mediation fails or the mediator reports non settlement, the complaint returns to the commission and is decided on its merits, so a failed mediation costs time but not rights. Disputes involving serious fraud, criminal allegations and medical negligence causing death or grievous injury are kept out of the reference power.
