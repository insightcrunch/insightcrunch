## Eleven Minutes at the Corra Lock

The cut still held its night cold when Anse Vetter came down the towpath, and the water in the chamber lay black and flat under a skin of mist. Wet stone met his boots at the steps, stone darkened to the knee by years of rise and fall, and the gate timbers above it stood beaded with dew that had not yet begun to run. The air smelled of green weed softening in the shallows, of iron kept wet, of tar from the paddle gear, and under all of it the faint sour breath of the east basin, where the bonded warehouses waited with their doors shut. He carried his lamp unlit in his left hand, because his right was the one he trusted least before it had worked.

Thirty years on the Carrowmere locks had taught his hands the order of a morning before his mind had finished waking to it. He set the lamp on the coping, where the stone had been worn to a shallow dish by other lamps and other mornings, and went first to the upper gate, as he always went, because the reach pressed hardest there and a fault showed earliest. The balance beam gave under his shoulder with its familiar complaint. The paddle gear turned stiff and then eased, as it eased every morning once the night cold was worked out of it, and the water stirred against the gates and settled again. He noted the level against the marked post, as he noted it whether anyone read the book or not. Then he took the lamp down the iron rungs into the gate recess to look at the frame and the sill.

The charge was set against the frame of the upper gate, low where the masonry shouldered out to meet the timber, and he saw it first as a wrongness in a place he knew by touch better than by sight. He did not reach for it. His hand stopped in the air, and the poor light showed him a packed shape bedded hard against the wrong face of the frame, set where stone and water stood between it and the work it pretended to threaten. Even a keeper, no engineer and no artificer, could read the error. The masonry would take the force and spend it into the chamber wall. And the line that ran from it, led toward the travelling gear of the gate, had been fixed where the gate in its ordinary work would draw it slack rather than taut, easing away from the pull it would need if it was ever to do anything at all. It was sabotage-shaped. The longer he looked, crouched on wet rungs with his knees complaining, the more surely he understood that it could not perform the work its shape promised.

He climbed back to the chamber top with care, because his hands had gone clumsy with something that was not the cold, and stood looking at the bell rope, the signal line, and the path toward the basin office. The order of reporting stood up in his mind as plain as the marks on the level post. If he pulled the bell first, it would empty the lock houses and bring men running with boots and poles and questions, and the first story told on the basin that morning would not be about a device in a chamber. The lock would close on suspicion alone, because no officer who heard a bell at dawn would dare leave it open. Once closed it would be drained by men in a hurry, and the chamber that held the only honest account of what had been done there would be emptied and trampled by ladders and boots, until whatever the placement itself could have told was mud underfoot. What would not survive was the order in which it had been placed, and the marks around it, and whether it had been set once or twice. He knew all of this in the first minute, with his hand not yet on the rope, and knowing it was why his hand stayed where it was.

The second minute he spent looking down the reach, where the water was beginning to take the light. A laden barge showed above the bend, low in the water, moving at the pace of a master who wanted the first locking of the day, and behind it there would be another. If he closed the Corra on a bell and a shout, the reach would stack up by midmorning, and by evening every warehouse holder on the basin would know the keeper had found something, and would shape it to fit his own fears. A quiet word carried in the right order would do none of that. It would send one qualified reader to the chamber before the water was touched. It would put his name on the paper as the man who found it, in the hour he found it, before anyone could ask when he had first seen it and why he had waited. Except that the hour he was about to write was already wrong, and getting wronger with every breath, and he knew that too, and took the third minute anyway, and then the fourth, with his eyes on the packed shape below and his mind going where he did not want it to go.

It went, as it had gone every morning for some weeks, to the upper service gate, and to two nights when it had stood unlatched because he had left it unlatched, and to the short round he had walked on those nights, turning back at the second marker instead of going on to the frame he was meant to touch with his own hand. There had been a payment. It had not been large, which was somehow the worst of it, as if a larger sum would have made it a transaction a man could name, instead of a smallness agreed to for a smallness. He had not spent it. It sat in the hut in a tin that had once held grease for the paddle gear, under a rag, where he saw it whenever he reached for the grease and had to move his hand around it. The telling had worn thin with use. Standing above a charge set against his frame, in a chamber someone had needed quiet access to, by someone who had needed to know when the keeper walked and when he did not, the thin telling tore. He did not know the two things were bound together. He knew only that if they were, his eleven minutes would one day be read as something worse than fear, and if they were not, his shame was still sitting in a grease tin in his hut.

In the sixth minute he did the round he should have finished already, and he did it properly, because if the chamber was to be read by someone qualified he would not have it said the keeper had left the rest of his lock unexamined. He put his shoulder to the lower beam and felt the gear run true. Nothing else was wrong. The wrongness was all in one place, placed by hands that had wanted it found on a morning round, or had not cared, or had counted on something he did not understand about how it would be found. In the seventh minute he stood in the hut doorway with the report book open on the shelf that served as his desk, and wrote the level figure, the state of the lower gate, and the weed on the sill, in his hand, and left the next line empty. 

He used the eighth minute to walk the approach path from the service gate to the chamber top, slowly, the way a stranger would have come in the dark. He found nothing he could swear to, only his knowledge of how quiet the stones were before the basin woke, and how a man who knew the rhythm of the place would know the keeper's round turned at the second marker on a short night and came all the way to the frame on a true one. If someone had learned it lately, they had learned it by watching, which anyone might do from the far bank, or by being told the two nights when the round would be short and the service gate unlatched. In the ninth minute he stood again at the chamber top, looking down at the thing against the frame, and let himself think past reporting to what reporting would set moving. Someone would come, the Ninth Office or the basin engineer, Reeve, precise and unsentimental, who would read the masonry like a page. Someone would ask him to sign his name to the hour. Someone would ask about the service gate, because a device in a locked chamber asked that question by itself, and then he would have to decide in front of witnesses what kind of man he was going to be for the rest of his life. He did not decide that in the ninth minute, or the tenth. He knew only that a bell pulled in panic would ask it of him in a yard full of running men, and a paper carried in order would ask it at a desk, which was the only mercy left, and he had no right to it, and he took it anyway.

In the tenth minute the mist lifted off the reach in strips, the barge showed its whole length, and the master raised a hand from the stern, an ordinary greeting between men who had passed each other through locks for years. Vetter raised his own hand back and hated the ordinariness of the gesture, because below them both the lock held a packed shape that could be made to look like the end of the basin if you did not look closely, and looking closely was the whole of the case. He thought of the four bonded warehouses around the lower basin, full of other people's goods held under seal, and of what the water would do if an upper gate ever did fail at high level, going down the lower cut in one body and standing deepest where the sills were lowest. This thing against his frame could not make that sound. Whoever had set it had either not known, which made them dangerous in a new way, or had known and set it badly on purpose, which made the badness itself a message he could not read.

The eleventh minute he spent with his hand on the cold iron of the signal line housing, watching the light come up over the warehouse roofs and the first gulls move off the basin water, and he let the minute run out without reaching for more reasons. Eleven minutes was already a fact. It would sit in the report whether he wrote it honestly or left it to be found, and facts found by other people were heavier than facts carried in by the man they belonged to. What he had bought with the minutes was a chamber untrampled, a lock not closed on a shout, a basin not alarmed into inventing its own story, and a report that would go first to the duty desk on paper, in sequence, as a found thing should go, and only then to whoever the duty desk judged fit to read the chamber. That order was right. He would stand on the order even if no one ever stood on anything else about him. He took his hand off the iron, picked up the report line where it waited on its hook in the hut doorway, and began, eleven minutes late, to send word.

## Two Masters in the Room

The room Harrand used on the upper floor of the Office had been a counting room when the building belonged to the canal interest, and it still held the shape of that work in its bones.  The air smelled of paper kept too long in drawers, of the oil used on the door hinges, and faintly, through the window that never quite shut, of the cut below the building, weed and wet stone carried up on the warming air. Mira stood just inside the door with her back not quite touching the wall, because the wall was cold and the cold would go through her coat and find her shoulder, and she kept her hands loose at her sides where both of the people at the table could see them, which was a courtesy she had learned in rooms where rank was uncertain. Standing was the first cost of the meeting, and she paid it the way she paid most costs by habit, in small adjustments no one was meant to notice: weight shifted from one foot to the other before the ache in her legs could climb, a hand set flat for a moment on the back of the empty chair beside her, as if she had merely chosen to rest it there.

Commissioner Ilse Harrand sat at the head of the table with the papers squared in front of her, two sets, one in the canal authority's binding and one in the plain cover the Office used for a keeper's report, and she did not invite Mira to sit. Arvo Leyden sat at her right hand, which was his usual place in such rooms and not his place at all in this one, because he had not called the meeting, and his displeasure at that was in the room before anyone spoke. He looked at Mira once, briefly, the look a handler gives an officer who has been summoned over his head, and then he looked at Harrand and said, in front of Mira, with no softening of it, that tasking his officer directly was not the Commissioner's prerogative, that the Office had a chain for good reasons, and that if the chain was to be broken he wanted the breaking said aloud and minuted, so that no one later could pretend it had been ordinary. Harrand received this without heat. She said that it would be minuted, that she was breaking it, and that he was present precisely so that the breaking could not later be called a private arrangement between her and a field officer.  Leyden inclined his head by the smallest degree that counted as acknowledgment and folded his hands on the table, and 

Harrand opened the keeper's report first, and laid the case out from its pages, in the order the paper gave it, which Mira noted and was quietly grateful for. Anse Vetter, keeper of the Corra Lock on the north cut above the east basin, thirty years on the Carrowmere locks, had on his morning inspection found a charge set into the gate chamber against the frame of the upper gate. He had reported it in sequence, on paper, to the duty desk, eleven minutes after the hour his round should have produced the report, a delay he had entered in his own hand on the face of the report, without explanation. The canal authority paper, which Harrand opened next, was the work of the basin office surveyor pending the engineer's own examination, and it described the same object in a surveyor's flatter language. The charge lay packed against the wrong face of the gate frame, where the masonry shoulder and the water of the chamber stood between it and any useful effect. Its line had been led to the travelling gear of the gate and fixed where the gate's ordinary movement would pull it slack rather than taut. On the surveyor's reading, which Harrand gave without embroidery, the thing was shaped like sabotage and could not do what sabotage at that gate would need to do. If the upper gate had failed at high water the consequence was not in doubt, and Harrand stated it once and plainly: the basin reach would go down the lower cut in one body, and the lower basin, with its four bonded warehouses standing around it, would take the water in sequence. 

She tasked Mira then, directly, by name and by callsign, and the directness of it altered the air in the room. Not through Leyden, not by a paper passed down the chain in the ordinary way, but here, in front of the handler whose prerogative it properly was, the Commissioner of the oversight that did not believe the Ninth Office should exist gave the Office's youngest field officer a case at the seat of the Accord and set the terms of it herself. Leyden said, again in Mira's hearing, that he objected to the precedent and not to the officer, and that he wanted that distinction on the minute as well, because he would not have it read later that he had doubted the officer he supplied.  Mira was to go to the Corra Lock. She was to read the chamber and the keeper and the keeper's hut, in that order of care if not of sequence, and she was to establish three things before anything else was decided: whether the device had ever been meant to fire, whether it had been placed once or more than once, and what, if anything, the place itself held about the hands that had done the work. No drain was authorised. No closure was authorised. The chamber was to be read as it stood, live, with the reach above it in use, because a drained chamber was a chamber destroyed for her purposes, and Harrand, who did not love the practice and did not pretend to, understood its terms well enough to say so in plain words: what the water and the stone held would not survive mud and boots, and she would not order its destruction to make an administrative difficulty easier.

Then Harrand named the cost, openly, in the same level tone she had used for the surveyor's findings, and Mira respected her for naming it rather than leaving it to be discovered. The lock would stay open to traffic while the work was done. The cut would carry its ordinary burden of barges, bonded cargo would move through the Corra on its ordinary rhythm, the basin would wake and work and lock its gates up and down past a chamber that held a device no one had yet made safe, and the city above the east basin would go about its morning exposed to whatever the thing in the chamber turned out, in the end, to be. Harrand said that closing the north cut on an unproven device was not caution but a different kind of act, and she laid out the shape of it. A closed cut stranded bonded cargo across three members of the Accord, cargo held under seal and bond, with penalties and claims that would begin before the day was out and arguments about liability that would outlast everyone in the room.  If someone had set the thing to be found, closing the cut might be the very effect they had bought with it. So the lock stayed open, and the exposure stayed with it, and that was the price of the case, and she wanted it understood in the room that the price had been chosen by her, at her level, and not left on the shoulders of the officer who would stand in the chamber while the traffic passed overhead. Leyden, when she had finished, said that the minute should show the cost had been named before the officer accepted the tasking, and Harrand said that it should, and the clerk's pen moved on.

Mira accepted in few words, because few were needed and because the meeting had already cost her more standing than the words would show. By its last third she had taken her weight once on the edge of the table itself, a flat palm laid down as if to steady a paper that did not need steadying, and she had felt the effort of keeping her head still and her attention level in the particular way that tiredness taxed her now, a tax she had been paying since the Barrow detail and the two nights that had gone out of her life there and not been repaid. She carried other things into the room besides the tiredness. She carried the last minutes of Edran Voss, which surfaced at unchosen moments and had surfaced twice that morning before the summons came, and she carried the knowledge of Reni's nineteen minutes, which she kept the way a person keeps a hand off a sore tooth, by knowing exactly where not to press. None of it made her less able to read a chamber. It made the reading dearer, and standing dearer first, and she resented the room slightly for charging her the standing before the work had even begun. Since [the sealed store room at the Teshkar depot](/2026/11/03/nightjar-bonded-cargo-004/), she had kept a private count of the places that held nothing, and the count stood at two.  She said that she would need the basin engineer assigned to her and not to the authority's own timetable, that she would need the keeper kept at his post and not relieved, because the keeper at his own lock was either a witness in place or a question in place and either way he was worth more standing at his gates than sitting in a room being asked about them, and that she would need Vetter's report and the surveyor's paper to take down with her, copies, not the originals, because the originals should stay where the clerk's minute could protect them. Harrand agreed to each point as she made it, without referral to Leyden, and Leyden let the agreement pass, and the clerk wrote it all down, and the tasking was complete.

Leyden walked her to the door, which was either courtesy or a reclaiming of some portion of his role, and let her go without another word. She went down the Office stairs at the pace the stairs required, one hand finding the rail at the turn where the stone was worn, not from weakness she would have named but from the simple arithmetic of a body spending carefully what it had. The copies were brought after her in a folder by the clerk's runner. She took them in the entry hall, under the board where the nine names stood, hers last and youngest by nine years, and she put the folder under her arm and went out into the morning, down toward the north cut and the Corra Lock, with the shape of the work set: a live chamber to be read before it was touched, a device that wore the shape of sabotage and might be nothing but a message, a keeper who had written his own delay on his own report, a lock that would stay open over all of it, and a city working its ordinary morning on the far side of the gate.

## Four Warehouses and a Bad Piece of Work

The Corra Lock announced itself before Mira saw the gates, by smell and by sound carried along the north cut. Wet stone came first, cold and mineral, then coal smoke from the lock houses, then the green smell of canal water held too long against masonry, algae and iron and the faint rot of timber that never dried. A barge bell sounded somewhere above the reach, a single flat note that rolled off the cutting walls and died against the basin, and under it she could hear water working steadily through the paddle gear, a patient hiss that never quite stopped. The towpath narrowed where the cut bent, its setts polished by boots and rope and the hooves of generations of patient horses, each stone dipped in the middle and shining with damp, and the handrail on the chamber side was worn bright where keepers had steadied themselves in all weathers. When the lock came into view it sat in its stone throat exactly as a lock should, upper gates closed against the higher reach, lower gates below, the chamber between them a deep rectangular well of dressed blocks darkened to the waterline and pale and crusted above it, and beyond the lower gates the cut opened and fell away toward the east basin where four bonded warehouses stood around still water like clerks around a ledger, their high windows shuttered, their hoists idle, their roofs shedding the morning in thin bright runnels. Gulls from the basin quarter turned above the warehouses and cried without urgency. A thin rain had passed earlier and left every rope beaded and every iron fitting beaded, and the light, when it came through the cloud, made the wet coping stones shine as if they had been oiled.

Sanna Reeve waited on the chamber top beside the upper gate balance beam, a canvas coat over her working clothes, her survey staff grounded beside one boot and a leather case of levels and tapes at her feet. She was a compact woman with a precise way of standing, as if she disliked wasting ground, and she looked at the lock the way Mira had seen customs examiners look at a manifest, with attention that had no curiosity left in it because curiosity had been replaced by method. Anse Vetter stood a little apart near the gate recess, near enough to be useful and far enough to show that he understood his position had changed since his morning inspection, and his cap was in his hands rather than on his head. His hands drew the eye despite any wish to be polite about them. Thirty years of gate work had thickened them at the knuckles and split the skin across the backs in old white lines, and the nails were short and dark edged from grease that no washing shifted, and when he turned the cap between his fingers the movement was careful in the way of a man who had learned to spare what hurt without making a display of it.

Reeve wasted no time on greeting beyond a nod. She had kept traffic moving on Harrand's order and she had a basin to hold. She walked Mira along the chamber top from the upper gate heel to the lower gate mitre and made her look at the place in the order a working engineer would look at it, from water to stone to iron to warehouse, and Mira, who had spent six years on bonded cargo at Valtessa docks before the Office took her, recognised the discipline and was grateful for it. A lock was a machine for moving weight in the other sense, the ordinary sense, tons of water and cargo lifted and lowered by patience and by gearing, and anyone who interfered with it interfered with levels that did not forgive guessing.

Vetter's report, which Reeve had taken down in a clerk's hand on a single sheet and weighted against a stone to keep it from the wind, said what he had found on the morning round in plain language that did not reach for effect. On opening the upper gate chamber for inspection before the first passage of the day, he had seen a mass packed hard against the frame of the upper gate on its chamber side, low where the frame met the masonry, with a line led away from it along the frame toward the travelling gear of the gate. He had not touched it. He had not worked the gate. He had closed nothing and opened nothing beyond the inspection hatch he had already opened, and he had sent the basin boy running for the canal authority and stood on the chamber top where he could see the device and the reach at once until Reeve arrived. Whatever else would be said about Vetter before the case closed, Mira marked that part and kept it. A frightened man who wanted distance from a thing usually ran from it or pushed it away or called so loudly that other men trampled the ground. Vetter had stood still with it and kept his boots in one place.

From the chamber top, with Reeve beside her and the gate held, Mira looked down at the thing itself for the first time and saw at once why Reeve's message to the Office had used the word wrong twice. The chamber fell away below the coping in a sheer stone well, its walls weeping in dark vertical bands, weed bearding the joints below the waterline, the upper gate rising out of black water with its timbers swollen and its iron straps furred with rust at the edges and bright where the gear worked. Against the frame, on the face that turned toward the masonry rather than toward the open chamber, a charge had been packed close and hard, shaped to the angle of the frame as if whoever placed it had believed that closeness was the same as effect. Even from the top, without descending, without touching, the consequence read plainly to a woman who had watched bonded stores handled and mis-handled for years. The masonry shoulder stood immediately behind that face. The mass of the gate stood in front of it. The water of the chamber stood below and around it. If the thing had ever acted, most of what it spent would have gone into stone and timber and water that had nowhere particular to go, and the gate, the one object it pretended to threaten, was the object best placed to survive it. Reeve said nothing while Mira looked, which Mira also marked in her favour. Engineers who trusted their own reading did not crowd another reader with conclusions.

The line was the second wrongness, and in some ways the plainer. It left the packed mass and ran, kept close to the frame, toward a moving part of the gate gear, a part whose travel Mira could see marked in a bright arc on the iron where years of working had polished a crescent. Reeve, without working the gate itself, moved the disconnected demonstration arm on the chamber top by two notches, the small teaching model the keepers used for training boys, and the part travelled. It travelled away. That was the whole of it. When the gate worked in its proper direction, the part to which the line had been led moved away from the charge and let the line fall slack rather than drawing it taut. A line that needed a pull would get less pull the better the gate behaved. A line that needed tension would hang looser with every honest operation of the lock. Mira looked from the model to the chamber and back, and felt the dissatisfaction that came when a piece of work was bad in a way that required knowledge to achieve. Ignorance packed a charge badly by accident all the time, in the histories the Office kept of harbour sabotage and warehouse fires and men who had mistaken noise for effect. Ignorance also led lines to whatever iron stood nearest. But this badness had an address. The charge sat against the wrong face. The line ran to a part that travelled the wrong way. Each error, taken alone, could be a fool's error. Together they faced the same direction, away from effect, and Mira distrusted work whose errors all agreed with each other.

Reeve took her down from the chamber top by the stone stair to the basin level, because she said a flood could not be understood from above, and Mira understood that a flood was also a kind of reading, done by water, without pity and without imagination. The lower cut left the lock in a straight reach walled with stone, then opened into the basin, a broad rectangular harbour of stiller water where barges waited under tarpaulins and where the four warehouses stood on a shelving shore that was not obvious to a visitor and was everything to the people who stored goods there. Reeve set her staff against the basin coping and showed Mira the marks cut into the stone at the stair foot, a column of notches and numbers that recorded levels the way a family recorded heights on a door frame, except that these heights could ruin men. The chamber, emptied suddenly through a failed upper gate, would deliver its water and the reach behind it down the lower cut in one body, not as a rise that gave clerks time to lift stock, but as a surge that arrived standing up, struck the basin, and then settled to find its level warehouse by warehouse according to the height of each sill and the height of each floor above its sill. The basin shelved. That was Reeve's word and she used it without apology. The shore under the warehouses rose in a long shallow ramp from the water toward the higher ground at the back of the basin quarter, and the builders had set each warehouse on that ramp at the height its ground gave it. One stood low and took the basin weather in its cellars in any hard season. Three stood a full course of masonry higher and relied on raised floors. Two stood highest of all, on the crown of the shelving, with a sill that lesser floods never reached and a floor above that sill that gave its stock a second defence. Stock, Reeve added, did not sit on floors in a bonded warehouse worthy of the name. It sat on racking, and the height of the lowest rack mattered as much as the height of the sill, because water that stood a hand's breadth deep spoiled only what it touched, while water that stood to the first rack took the bottom tier of everything and wrote the rest off through damp and contamination.

She unrolled her survey on the flat top of a bollard, weighting its corners with a tape, a plumb bob, a short iron rule and her own gloved hand, and she talked Mira through the four buildings in basin order, east to west. Warehouse one stood lowest, its sill barely above ordinary basin level, its stock racked close above its floor because its holder traded in bulk goods that turned over fast and could not afford height. Warehouse three stood next in height and in exposure, its floor raised but its racking low and dense. Warehouse four stood higher again, set back where the ramp began to climb in earnest, with a sill that had kept it dry in the flood year the basin clerks still used as their measure. Warehouse two stood on the highest sill on the basin, at the crown, and its stock sat on racking a full span above the line the other three would drown under, a fact Reeve stated flatly and then underlined once with her pencil, because men who read surveys quickly tended to read heights as opinions. Aske's clerks could watch a basin flood from their loading doors with dry boots long after their neighbours were baling. Reeve set out the reckoning in the form the canal authority used for its own flood book, so that the figures would stand in one place and could be argued with honestly. The table settled, before any motive was allowed to speak, exactly which doors the water would enter first, which stock it would reach, and which holder it would leave standing dry longest while his neighbours lost their goods.

Mira read the table twice, once for the heights and once for the names, and committed both to the part of her memory that did not depend on paper. Paper could be taken, burned, misfiled by a clerk with cold fingers. Heights stayed where builders had put them. She noted Aske's position at the crown of the basin and noted also, with the fairness she owed a case before it hardened, that a wet floor was not nothing to a bonded holder. A flood that entered even shallowly closed a warehouse while authority men inspected it, spoiled seals, and broke the chain of custody that made bonded goods valuable. Insurance clerks could argue a closed warehouse and a broken custody chain into a substantial claim without a single bale drowned. She put the table in the place where inconvenient facts waited, neither dismissed nor enthroned, and asked Reeve what the water would do to the lock itself if the gate had failed at high water. Reeve answered that the lock would survive its own failure better than the basin would. The chamber walls were built to be struck. The warehouses were built to be dry. A device that meant to threaten Carrowmere would have threatened the buildings that held the Accord's bonded wealth, and the device in the chamber threatened principally the idea of those buildings, which was a different kind of weapon and one Mira intended to take seriously on its own terms.

The lock-side office stood at the chamber top on the basin side, a stone room no larger than a good cupboard, with a window that commanded the upper reach, a stove that commanded nothing at all until Vetter coaxed it, and a wall of record that commanded Mira's attention the moment she stepped inside. Logbooks stood in a row on a shelf bowed by their weight, their spines labelled by session in Vetter's hand, which had been a strong clear hand thirty years earlier and had coarsened with his knuckles without ever becoming careless. Below the shelf, inspection boards hung on nails, one for each gate, each board ruled into columns for the round, the weather, the state of the gear, the state of the water and the keeper's mark, and the boards for session after session showed the rounds entered at their proper hours with a regularity that was not mechanical, because a mechanical regularity would have looked smoother, but human and kept, the mark of a man who had walked the same stones at the same hours in rain and in heat and in the dark of early morning when the basin was only a smell of water and a creak of moored barges. The gate gear book lay open on the desk, its last entry in that same hand, the morning inspection recorded up to the moment it stopped being a morning inspection. Reeve stayed in the doorway, saying nothing, understanding perhaps that the office was a place Mira needed to read in the other sense before she read it in hers.

Mira set her hand flat on the desk edge, then on the iron handle of the gate gear demonstrator, then on the worn wooden grip of the inspection lamp, and she read the office the way eleven years of reading had settled in her, not reaching, not forcing, letting what the room held in its metal and its stone come up through her palm in its own order. Places held what had been felt in them with intensity and with repetition, and this room held repetition the way a stair holds the shape of feet. Round upon round settled under her hand, years of them, the same circuit taken at the same hours, an attention almost entirely without drama, which was why it read so deep. Any single inspection left nearly nothing. Ten thousand inspections, each carrying the small sober intent of a man who believed the lock depended on his looking, had laid down a stratum in the iron and the stone as steady as geology. Under it she felt the texture of the man's care, not love, which was too large a word for gate gear, but a keeper's proprietary watchfulness, the satisfaction of a wheel that turned true, the irritation of a boy who left a rope uncoiled, the deep unshowy pride of a lock that had never failed on his watch. The cost came as it always came, quietly and in kind. She took on a portion of what she read, and what she read was steadiness, so the cost was bearable and strange at once, a heaviness settling into her own hands, an ache ghosting along her knuckles as if her fingers had spent years around cold iron. She lifted her hand and let the room go, and the ache stayed while she flexed her fingers, a reminder in her own joints that the rhythm of this place was not a background detail. The rhythm was the lock's true defence. Gates could be watched and gear could be guarded, but the safety of the Corra Lock rested on a particular man walking a particular circuit at particular hours and looking at particular iron with eyes that knew what that iron looked like on every other morning of thirty years. Anyone who wanted time unobserved in that chamber did not need to defeat the gates first. He needed to buy minutes out of that rhythm, or learn it so exactly that he could move inside its gaps, and either way the rhythm itself was the thing worth paying for. She wrote that thought in the margin of Vetter's report sheet, in pencil, in words plain enough to survive being read back to her by Harrand, and Reeve, reading over her shoulder, gave a single short nod of recognition.

The keeper's hut stood twenty paces from the office along the towpath, a smaller stone building with a chimney smoking thinly and a garden no wider than a barge. Mira looked at it as they passed, at the door with its iron latch polished by one hand, at the window where a cloth had been hung with care, at the boots set sole up under the eave to dry, and she noted it and deliberately did not read it. A man's lodging held more than his work held, and it held it less guardedly, and she had a procedural interview to conduct before she had any right to the inside of Anse Vetter's private weather. There would be a proper time for the hut, with the case further along and her questions sharper, and reading it early would fill her with his life in no useful order and spend her strength to no purpose. She was already carrying more than she should have been. The Barrow detail sat behind her eyes on two lost nights of sleep, and older carries, Voss in his last minutes and the long echo of other rooms, made her capacity a budget she had to spend like coin. So she let the hut be a hut, a door and a window and a wisp of stove smoke, and she turned back to the chamber top where Vetter waited with his cap still in his ruined hands.

She interviewed him there, procedurally, with Reeve present as witness and the report sheet between them on the balance beam, and she kept her voice to the register she used for customs examinations, factual and unhurried, a register that told a man exactly which questions were record and which were conversation. She asked him to walk the morning round in order, from the moment he left the hut, and he did, naming each check as a man names the stops on a road he could walk blind. Lamp lit. Upper reach observed. Debris boom inspected. Service gate on the upper side checked by hand, as always, its latch drawn and tested. Paddle gear examined. Chamber looked over from the top, both gates, both recesses. Gate gear room entered, book opened, first entries made. Then the return along the chamber wall to the frame of the upper gate, the part of the round he always left for last because the light reached it last, and there the morning had stopped being a morning he recognised. She asked what he had touched and he told her again, nothing beyond the hatch, and his account matched the sheet without the smoothness of rehearsal, because he stumbled once over the order of the boom and the boom's chain and corrected himself with irritation at his own tongue. She asked about the traffic held above the lock and he named the barges and their cargoes from memory, bonded crates for warehouse three, sealed casks for warehouse one, a mixed load for the basin general store that did not concern the Office. She asked who held keys besides himself and he listed the authority's duty engineer, the relief keeper who came up from the lower locks on his rest days, and Reeve, and he described where each key lived with the precision of a man for whom keys were a moral subject. Through all of it his hands turned the cap, and twice she watched him glance toward the chamber, not toward the device, which he could not see from where he stood, but toward the gate itself, as a man glances toward a horse he has ridden for years that has thrown a shoe in public. His care for the lock was not a performance she needed her gift to detect. It was in the order of his answers, in the fact that his distress expressed itself first as anger at the insult to the chamber, to the gear, to the round, and only second as fear for himself. She closed the interview without pressing him on money, on evenings, on any gap in any round, because a procedural interview had edges and she intended to keep this one inside them. He was careful. He was not yet opened, and she did not yet know which part of him would open, or whether what lay behind the care was only an old man's pride and an old man's shame at having been the keeper on whose watch a bad piece of work had been planted in a clean chamber.

The messenger came up the towpath in the early afternoon with Dunmore's paper in a sealed wallet, and he waited while Mira broke the seal on the chamber top with the wind worrying the pages. Petra Dunmore did not waste paper. Her covering note said that she had sent what existed rather than what would have been convenient, a sentence Mira read twice because it was exactly the sentence on which cases turned. The first enclosure was a schedule drawn from the canal authority's own gate log and from the relief keeper's counter entries, and it established a fact that changed the temperature of the whole case. The upper service gate, the small pedestrian gate that gave keepers and engineers access to the gate gear path without working the main towpath gate, had stood unlatched through the night on two occasions some weeks apart, each occasion falling inside Vetter's span of duty and each recorded obliquely, not as a fault but as a note in another hand about a gate found open at dawn and closed without comment. Two nights. Weeks apart. On Vetter's rounds. Mira set that page by itself on the balance beam and put a stone on it, not because the wind required the stone but because the fact required a place of its own. An unlatched gate once was a tired man's oversight at the end of a long watch. An unlatched gate twice, weeks apart, with the same keeper on duty, in the same season in which a bad device came to rest in the chamber below that gate's path, was either a coincidence of a size that insulted arithmetic or it was access, bought or begged or stolen, and access was the one ingredient the chamber had seemed to lack. She looked across at Vetter, who was helping Reeve's boy coil a rope at the far end of the chamber top, and she kept her face still. The fact belonged to the paper for the moment. It would belong to a room and a formal question soon enough, and a man deserved to meet such a fact across a table rather than across a lock top with the wind taking the edges.

The second enclosure was a financial summary assembled from filings, court notices and the kind of quiet enquiries the Office could make through the Accord registries when a commissioner asked personally. Its subject was Corin Aske, holder of Aske Bonded Stores, warehouse two on the basin, the building on the highest sill with the high racking and the dry floor in Reeve's table. Aske owed money in three directions, and Dunmore's paper laid the directions out with a clerk's pitiless clarity. He owed the basin merchants' association on a loan taken to refit his loading gear, a loan whose payments he had met late twice in the season. He owed a Valtessa factoring house on advances against cargo that had moved slower than his projections, which in the language of factors meant cargo that had barely moved at all. And he owed, privately and at worse terms, a sum to a lender in the lower city whose name appeared in Dunmore's summary without comment, which was itself a comment. Against those three debts the paper set one asset of a particular kind. Aske had insured his stock, comprehensively and at a valuation described as full replacement with consequential loss, under a policy taken out in the same season as the second of his late payments, for a sum that exceeded the honest market value of everything a visitor could see through his warehouse doors by a margin that made Mira read the figure twice and then check the unit. The policy was large. The policy was new in the life of the business. And the policy covered flood by name, among a schedule of perils that read, to a woman who had spent six years among bonded cargoes, like a list written by a man who had stood in his own warehouse and imagined, item by item, the ways his stock could die. Mira had examined cargo at Valtessa long enough to know the smell of an insurance arrangement before the paperwork confirmed it, and the smell here was strong enough to carry across a basin. A holder in debt in three directions, insured above value, against flood by name, in the one warehouse quarter where a device had appeared in a lock chamber above his basin. If the case had been a cargo fraud on her old docks, she would have had her suspect under examination before the light changed.

Reeve read the enclosures standing at Mira's shoulder, and when she finished she was quiet for the length of time it took a barge above the lock to sound its bell once for passage and receive no answer, because the lock stayed open to traffic by Harrand's order and also stayed a crime scene by Mira's. Then Reeve said that the Office would want the possibilities stated, and Mira stated them aloud, on the chamber top, with the device below them and the basin beyond, because a possibility spoken in the place it concerned could be tested against stone and water while it was still warm.

The first possibility was a warning. A device meant to be found, planted where the morning round would discover it, built badly because its badness was beside the point, since a warning did not need to work, it needed to be seen and understood. Warnings of that kind were an old language in the basin quarters of canal cities, spoken by men who wanted a holder to sell, or to pay, or to remember that his warehouse stood where other men's water could reach it. A warning explained the wrong face and the slack line without strain, because a man building a message did not trouble himself with effect. It explained the discovery on a routine round. It even explained the unlatched service gate, because a warning party would need access as much as any other party. What a warning did not yet explain was its address. No demand had reached any holder that the Office knew of. No holder had reported a threat, and Aske, if Aske was the address, had reported nothing at all, which could mean innocence, or fear, or that a warning addressed to a man in debt in three directions would be received as one more creditor and filed with the rest.

The second possibility was the insurance arrangement, and once Dunmore's paper lay open on the balance beam it argued for itself in columns. A holder arranged, or paid to have arranged, a flood that would take his stock and convert it, through a generous policy, from slow cargo and mounting debt into clean money. The device in the chamber failed to be a good device, and an insurance reading could absorb that failure in two ways, each plausible, each ugly. Perhaps the holder had hired cheap work, and the cheap work had packed its charge against the wrong face and led its line to a part that travelled the wrong way because cheap work did exactly that. Or perhaps the failure was the refinement of the arrangement, a device intended to fire badly and make a smaller flood than its shape promised, enough to wet a warehouse and break its custody chain and start a claim, not enough to drown a basin quarter and bring investigators down on every policy in Carrowmere. Mira distrusted how well the second version fitted, because a reading that could absorb any fact was a reading that had stopped being tested. And yet the debts stood. The policy stood. The flood clause stood. She had seen arrangements on the Valtessa docks built on less, prosecuted on less, confessed to on less. Of the men around the basin, exactly one held a financial reason to wish his warehouse wet, and his name was already in the table Reeve had drawn, in the row with the highest sill, which Mira noted again and filed again in the place where inconvenient facts waited, because a high sill reduced a loss without abolishing a claim, and because men arranging floods were not famous for reading surveys before they acted. Aske might not know his own advantage. Men in debt rarely studied the geography of their salvation with an engineer's care. They studied the policy.

The third possibility was the one Mira liked least, which was a reason, she told herself with the wind coming off the reach, to hold it as seriously as the others. The device was a rehearsal. Somewhere behind it stood people who intended, at another lock or another basin or another kind of gate entirely, to place a device that would work, and who had needed first to learn the things that could not be learned from drawings. How a working chamber sounded at night. How the traffic moved and where the barges held. How long the inspection round took, where the keeper looked first and where he looked last, how the light fell on the frame of the upper gate at the hour when the basin slept. A rehearsal explained the badness of the work differently from either rival, not as message and not as incompetence, but as sequence. The first attempt at any hard thing, done in a live place under live risk, would be tentative where a later attempt would be exact. A rehearsal also explained the two nights of the unlatched service gate with a completeness the other readings could not match, because access for a single planting needed a single night, while learning a chamber, measuring it, fitting a device to it and returning, was work that spread itself across occasions. And a rehearsal explained the device being found, because a device built to be carried away again after its lessons were taken, and instead left behind by some failure of nerve or timing, would sit in the chamber exactly as this one sat, sabotage shaped and effectless, a draft of a thing rather than the thing itself. What a rehearsal did not give her was a person. It gave her an intention pointed elsewhere, a somewhere else she could not see, and she resented the tidiness of that absence even while she respected the discipline it implied.

Reeve, who had listened to all three readings with her arms folded, asked which of them the evidence would look like if a person came to the basin fresh, with no gift and no headquarters theories, and Mira answered honestly that it would look like the basin itself, four warehouses around still water, one of them insured above its worth by a man sinking in three directions, above them a lock with a device in it that could not do what it pretended to do, and a gate that had stood open on two nights under a keeper with thirty years of clean rounds behind him. The warning had a language but no address. The rehearsal had a shape but no person. The insurance arrangement had a name, a motive, a policy, a flood clause and a debtor's arithmetic that a clerk could follow from the first late payment to the chamber wall without once needing to guess. Mira did not close the case on the chamber top. She was too tired for certainty and too well trained to mistake a strong reading for a proved one, and the table with its high sill and its dry floor waited in her memory like a stone in a road she would have to walk again. But as the light thinned over the basin and Vetter, at the far end of the chamber top, straightened from the rope he had coiled and looked once toward his gate, the three possibilities stood in the order the evidence had dealt them, and the insurance reading stood first, strongest, with a man's name on it, waiting for the arithmetic of water and sill and stock to be asked, properly and without mercy, whether it could survive.

## The Water Would Have Spared Him

The chamber top was wet stone and iron under thin morning light, and the air off the reach carried cold water, tarred rope and the iron tang of the gate gear. Mira stood where the upper gate met its frame and looked down into the chamber, her hands resting lightly on the coping, not gripping, because gripping made its own noise in the skin and she wanted the place quiet before she touched it in earnest. Below her the water lay dark and still between the gates, its skin of reflected sky breaking whenever a mooring line tightened out of sight. Reeve stood a pace behind with a survey chain over one shoulder and her levels held flat against her chest against the damp. Dunmore's paper, come by hand that morning under seal, lay weighted under a stone on the coping. Vetter, by Mira's choice, waited by his hut, the gate keys in his pocket, his ruined hands in his coat.

Reeve set the levels out first, because numbers should be numbers before they were anything else. The basin shelves in steps, she said, and put her finger on the first column of figures. At high level the water that leaves the lock does not spread evenly across the basin, the way a man drawing a flood on a map with a wet thumb might imagine. It runs. It takes the lower cut in one body and stands where the shelving tells it to stand. She read the sills in order, one, two, three, four, flat and precise, and Mira listened with her eyes on the water below, the only honest witness to what the figures meant.

Warehouse one stood lowest, its sill a hand below the survey datum, its stock floor only a little above that, so any serious body of water down the cut would find it first, fill it first and stand in it longest. Warehouse three stood next, low enough for its stock floor to drown in any event that held the basin above working level for an hour. Warehouse four stood higher than three and lower than two, clear of an ordinary rise and not clear of the rise a failed upper gate at high water would deliver. Warehouse two stood highest of the four. Aske's warehouse. Its sill was the highest sill on the basin by a margin Reeve did not round and did not soften, and its stock sat on racking a full span above the flood line that would drown the floors of the other three.

Dunmore's paper said the rest in the dry language of policy schedules, which Mira had learned at Valtessa to read as other people read letters, for what was included and what sat just outside the edge of the page. Aske's policy was recent. It was large. It covered stock at a valuation assuming the stock present in the quantities and at the heights declared, and the heights declared matched Reeve's survey almost to the line. The policy paid on water damage to stock, with exclusions Dunmore had marked in a small, tired hand. It did not pay on a near miss. It did not pay on fear. It paid when water took goods shown to have been there and ruined, and in proportion to the ruin.

Mira held the two papers side by side on the coping, the stone cold coming up through the paper into her palms, and did the arithmetic as she had been taught checking manifests against holds, slowly, refusing to skip the step that felt obvious. If the upper gate failed at high water, the reach would go down the lower cut in one body. The water would stand deepest against warehouses one, three and four. It would fill one. It would stand in three. It would take the lower stock in four and climb. Against warehouse two it would lap at a high sill and find racking a full span above its line, leaving Aske's declared stock dry while the holders either side of him counted sodden bales and ruined crates. Reeve called it the flood pattern, and by that plain name, the flood pattern, Mira fixed it as she would need to report it: a pattern that paid the neighbours and spared the insured man. A flood arranged for Aske's policy would have been a gift to three holders who had not asked for it and a dry disappointment to the one man whose debts made the arrangement worth arranging. The insurance reading, which that morning had fit Corin Aske the way a coat fits a man measured for it, died on the coping between two columns of figures, the way false sums die, by refusing to add up when both sides of the account were finally on the same stone.

That left the warning, and Mira turned to it while Reeve coiled her chain. A warning had been the kindest of the three possibilities when Harrand laid them out, and kind possibilities deserve the hardest testing, because kindness in an investigator is only laziness wearing a better coat. A warning says, we can reach your lock, we can reach your basin, mend your behaviour or your debts before the next one works. A warning wants to be found. On that reading the badness of the device was not a flaw but the message itself, a charge packed against the wrong face of the frame, its line led to gear that would pull it slack rather than taut, a piece of work announcing capability while withholding consequence. Mira had carried that reading through the groundwork without dismissing it, because dismissing it early would have been a failure of seriousness. Now she set it down beside the levels and looked at it without favour.

It thinned as she looked. A warning needs to be found once, clearly, by the right eyes, and then it needs to stop, because a warning that continues becomes a different kind of statement. The chamber, even from the coping, was already telling her the work below had not happened once. She could see it without weight, with ordinary eyes, as her customs years had taught her to see disturbance in a place meant to be undisturbed. Marks on the frame belonged to two separate visitations of hands and tools, overlapping as later work overlaps earlier work when the later worker knows it is there and steps around it rather than over it. A fixing point showed bright metal inside a ring of older darkening, as if something had been seated, lifted, and seated again with a small correction of angle. Reeve saw her looking, followed her gaze, and said nothing, which from Reeve was agreement more reliable than speech. A warning does not need two placements. A warning does not need a removal and a return, a first attempt lifted clear and a second set down in the corrected knowledge of the first. A warning does not practise. The possibility did not die as the insurance reading had died, on arithmetic any clerk could check. It thinned, as mist thins over the cut, until what had looked solid an hour earlier was a shape she could see through to the harder shape behind it. Something had been learned in that chamber. Learning leaves exactly the kind of trace that warning never needs to leave.

She would read the chamber now, from the top and the gate, before she asked Vetter anything, because questions put to a man before you know what his room will say are questions wasted, and because the chamber's evidence lay held in stone and iron, which hold weight best and forgive least. She told Reeve the terms they had settled on the walk up. Reeve would watch the reach. If traffic called, Reeve would answer. No one would work the gate gear while her hands were on the frame. Reeve repeated the terms back once, exactly, and stepped to the wheel housing, where she could see both the upper reach and Mira's shoulders. Mira took off her gloves, because gloves were for weather and this was not weather, and set her bare palms on the cold coping, and then, deliberately, on the iron of the gate frame where the device had been seated against the wrong face.

The cold came first, as it always came when the anchor was stone and iron and the occupation had been intent. It was not weather cold, which sits on the skin and can be shaken off. It came up through the frame into the bones of her hands, a deep, patient cold belonging to hours spent in a wet chamber by whatever muffled light a man carries when he does not want his light seen from the chamber top. She let it come without bracing, because bracing distorts a read as squinting distorts a manifest, and let her attention go where the weight wanted to go: not to the device alone, but to the occupation that had surrounded it. Weight records intensity and affect left in places and objects, and a chamber worked in for hours by someone who knew he was forbidden there holds the shape of that working as a hold holds the shape of cargo after the cargo is gone.

There were two occupations. She knew it in her hands before she could have defended it in words, by a difference in texture eleven years of reading had taught her to trust and to verify in the same breath. The first occupation was longer. It lay deeper in the frame and the stone beside it, settled and distributed, the residue of sustained intent held over hours, of a worker who had time and knew it and used it as careful workers use time: testing, seating, lifting, considering, seating again. Its concentration lay so steady it had soaked into the iron in an even field, without the ragged signature of haste. Whoever worked it had been afraid in the general way forbidden work makes a person afraid, but fear had not governed their hands. Attention had. She could feel that attention still in her own fingers as she read, her hands growing precise and unhurried in imitation of hands no longer there, and she had to set the imitation aside as evidence about them rather than instruction to herself.

The second occupation lay over the first like a second coat of paint over a first, thinner, shorter, and everywhere aware of what lay beneath it. Its weight concentrated in fewer places, at the fixing points and along the path of the line, and it carried a quicker, more decisive intent, a worker returning to known ground with a correction already formed before he descended. Where the first occupation had spread its attention across the whole problem of the chamber, the second narrowed to the placement itself, and it worked around the first occupation's marks with a care Mira read in her palms as clearly as she had read the bright metal inside the darkened ring with her eyes. The second worker had seen where the first worker's tools had bitten and chosen fresh purchase beside the bite rather than on it. He had seen where the first seating had sat and lifted it, or received it lifted, and set it down again a small distance corrected. Placed twice. Removed once. Replaced. The sequence sat in the frame in that order and no other, and the interval read as days, not hours, because the first occupation's weight had begun the slow settling weight undergoes when nothing renews it, days clearing its surface, while the second lay fresh and sharp over the settled field.

She stayed with her hands on the frame until the read stopped giving anything new, a discipline younger readers fail, because pressing a read past its yield produces invention dressed as evidence. Then she lifted her hands and stood with them hanging at her sides, feeling the occupation's concentration still resident in her fingers, a borrowed carefulness that made her want to touch everything precisely and set it down exactly, and beneath it the chamber cold, which had climbed into her wrists and settled there like water finding its level in her own body. That was the cost and she paid it without commentary, flexing her fingers against the borrowed intent until they were her own again, cold and stiff and hers. Reeve, who had watched without interrupting, asked the only useful question. How many times. Twice, Mira said. Placed, lifted, placed again, days apart. The first time longer. The second time working around the first. Reeve nodded once and wrote it on the back of her levels in pencil.

What the frame would not give her was the handler. She had known before she touched the iron that it would not, because weight has walls and she has spent eleven years learning where they stand, but the case required her to state the wall aloud so no one above her could later pretend it had not been reported. The device told the same story as its frame. Whoever had handled it had worn gloves, working gloves worn as discipline, so nothing of the handler would pass into the work. Weight needs an anchor and contact, and gloved contact leaves the work's own affect, the concentration, the patience, the haste, all of which she had read in full, but nothing of the body that did the work: no fear specific enough to be a man's own, no grief, no elation, nothing that could be laid beside a suspect and compared. She said it to Reeve on the chamber top with her cold hands tucked under her arms, and without apology, since no reader, however practised, gets a body or a face out of weight. It does not identify who. It never has. If the Office wanted a face, it would have to earn it by ordinary means, by paper and testimony and the slow arithmetic of who had the chamber on which night. [Rask had said on the record in the Barrow hearing](/2026/11/04/nightjar-standing-detail-005/) that his own flatness had a source and a slope, and that the empty places had neither. She thought of that sentence with her hands aching on the coping, because the chamber had given her sources and slopes in abundance, two of them, layered, and the handler behind them remained exactly as faceless as an empty place, present in effect and absent in person, and the honesty of the case required her to hold both facts at once.

The hut was next, a different kind of reading, because a hut is lived in and a chamber is only worked in, and lived-in places hold their weight in the furniture of ordinary days: the kettle, the ledger, the chair whose legs have worn shallow cups into the floorboards over thirty years. Vetter's hut smelled of lamp oil, damp wool, tea kept too long on a small stove, and the green mineral smell of canal water that gets into a keeper's clothes and never entirely leaves them. A narrow bed stood against the far wall, its blanket folded to a neatness that spoke of a man who makes his bed correctly even when, especially when, the rest of his conduct has slipped. The inspection ledger lay open beside a cold cup, its entries steady for page after page of years, and steady still in the recent pages, which was its own testimony, because a man who falsifies a ledger usually falsifies the hand as well without knowing it. Mira did not open with the ledger. She stood in the doorway until Vetter understood she wanted him inside his own hut for what came next, and came in, and shut the door against the wind off the reach with the care of a man shutting his own door in his own house, which it was, whatever the canal authority's papers said.

She read the room before she spoke, and she asked his leave first in the form they used, which was not courtesy but boundary. She would touch his table, his chair, the hook by the door where the service gate key hung on its ring. Nothing else. Nothing of his bed, nothing of his person, because no such practice exists and she would not pretend otherwise to frighten a confession out of a frightened man. He nodded with his eyes on the key hook, as if the hook were the part of the room he had already confessed to in his own mind a hundred times. She set her hand on the table edge, and the room gave her its burden without resistance, because shame held long and daily in a small room sinks into its wood as occupation sinks into iron, only warmer, only worse.

Vetter had been paid. The fact sat in the hut plainly, and the metaphor arrived unbidden and exact: the payment sitting in the room like a stone in a shoe, a small hard foreign thing a man carries with every step and cannot forget between steps, until the whole man is organised around a smallness he is too ashamed to sit down and remove. It was not a large payment. Weight does not deal in sums, but it deals faithfully in proportion, and the proportion here was petty against the risk, which was part of the shame: he had sold two nights of his own lock for a sum that would not keep him a season. The shame had two layers. Beneath lay the taking, weeks ago, a transaction conducted quickly, with the haste of a man who wants the money already in his pocket and the conversation already over. Above it lay the longer shame of the weeks since, renewed at every inspection round, compounded on the morning he found the device and understood, with the completeness only the man who sold the access can understand, what his two unlatched nights might have bought. That second shame was the heavier deposit. It had soaked the chair, the table edge, the key hook above all, where his hand went twice a night to a key he had twice not used to lock what he was paid in honour and in wages to lock.

And the shape of what had been bought stood clear beside the payment. What had been bought was the unlatched service gate, on two nights, and with the gate the inspection rhythm, which was the true commodity. A gate unlatched for an hour that coincides with a keeper's round is merely a risk. A gate unlatched for an hour whose timing is known, because the round has been walked short and the pattern sold with the key, is an appointment. Whoever paid Vetter had bought knowledge of when entry would go unseen, which is the difference between trespass and rehearsal.

She took her hand off the table and told him what she had, in the shape only, as she had undertaken to do. She did not accuse him of the device. She did not use the word device in the hut at all. The room held a payment taken and regretted, shame renewed nightly, and the shape of two nights, a gate left unlatched and a round walked short, bought together. She said it in the order the room had given it, without emphasis, the way Reeve read levels. Then she stopped, because what happened next had to be his, or it would be worth nothing to Harrand, to the Office, or to the lock.

Vetter looked at the key hook for a long time. When he spoke, he spoke procedurally, which Mira understood was not evasion but the only grammar a man of thirty years of rounds and ledgers possesses for a confession. He named the payment by its handling rather than its amount: a folded packet, left hand to right hand, on the towpath by the upper gate, on an evening he named by its traffic, the last upbound barge of that day's working, because a keeper dates his life by barges as other men date theirs by bells. He named the two nights the same way, by traffic and weather, one with rain that had kept the towpath empty, one clear, with a moon he called sufficient, a keeper's word, meaning sufficient to see a gate by and sufficient to be seen at one. The gate had stood unlatched, he said, for the span of his shortened round and no longer, because longer had not been asked, and he had clung to that precision since as a man clings to the one plank of a wreck that still floats: it was only this much, it was never more than this much. The round had been walked short by the eleven minutes it takes to go down to the lower paddle and back, eleven minutes standing by the lower gate with his hands in his coat, listening to his own lock work without him. He had found the thing in the chamber on his full round, restored to its proper length, and he had reported it in the wrong order, and he knew why, and the reason shamed him more than the money, because the money had been weakness and the order had been calculation: a report made in the wrong order would make the keeper the story, close the lock on suspicion alone, and bury the chamber evidence under a drained and trampled lock before anyone qualified read it, and whatever else he had sold, he had not been willing to sell the chamber's evidence with the gate. There was shame in every sentence and self pity in none. He did not ask what would happen to him. He asked whether the lock could stay open while the case was worked, and when she said that was Harrand's decision and for the present yes, he nodded at the key hook as if the hook had answered him.

Mira believed the shape of what he said because it matched the shape the hut had given her before he spoke, and she was careful about the limits of that belief. Weight does not compel truth in speech and does not detect it. She had not read his truth. She had read his shame, then listened to his words, and found words and shame describing the same two nights from opposite sides of the same gate. That is corroboration of the ordinary kind, and she would report it as exactly that and no more. The admission settled that the payment bought access and rhythm, not, on his evidence, knowledge of what the access was for. It settled that the two nights existed and could be dated by traffic, and that the keeper had reported himself to a Weigher before any other office could report him first, which would matter to Harrand in ways Mira would not presume to decide. What it did not settle was the handler. Vetter had seen no face that mattered, only a man muffled against canal weather on a wet towpath, and weight gave her nothing more of him from the hut than from the frame. Gloves on the device. A packet hand to hand. No body, no face. She said so again, to Vetter this time, because a witness deserves to know the shape of the wall his testimony runs against, and he received it with the bleak relief of a man learning that his shame, at least, would not be asked to identify a man it had never seen.

Reeve waited on the chamber top with her chain reslung and Dunmore's paper secured against the wind. She read Mira's face, and Vetter's behind her, and asked no question either of them would have had to answer in open air. Mira gave her the hut in summary: the payment, the two nights, the rhythm sold with the latch, the keeper's statement to be taken formally when Harrand required it. Reeve received each item with a single nod, filing them as she filed levels, in order, without decoration. The question that remained was the chamber itself, forming since her hands were on the frame, since the two occupations resolved under her palms into a sequence a coping read could outline but could not finish. She had the order of the placements from the top. She did not have the placement itself at close hand, the corrected angle of the second seating, the path of the line where it met the travelling gear, the full shape of the work at the distance at which work is understood, which is the distance of the hands that did it. Nor did she have the chamber's other burden, the place in the chamber air where the weight she expected simply was not, a suggestion of edge and interruption a read from above could register and could not map. A map needs a body inside the territory. Hers.

The alternative stood ready and respectable and ruinous. Drain the lock. A day's work, the cut closed, the reach emptied, the chamber laid open for engineers and clerks, and at the end of that day the chamber's weight would lie destroyed under mud and work boots, trampled by the very thoroughness claiming to preserve it, the two occupations smeared into one general disturbance, the edges of the empty place gone like a footprint in a flooded towpath. She would get her drained chamber and lose her read, and the case would proceed on everything except the one kind of evidence that could distinguish a rehearsal from an attempt. She stood on the wet coping with the chamber cold still in her wrists and Vetter's shame settling behind her ribs, lower and slower than chamber cold and harder to flex away, and felt the decision finish forming with the inevitability of a sum completing itself. The chamber must be read from inside, at the gate, by her, while the reach was live and the weight intact, before any drain destroyed it. She looked at Reeve, and Reeve, who had already begun to check the gate ladder's fixings with her boot and her hand, met her eyes and waited to be told what she had plainly already understood.

## The Chamber

Mira opened the upper gate herself, because the decision had to be visible in her own hands before it was real anywhere else, and because a day spent waiting for a drain would have cost the chamber everything she had come down to save. She set her shoulder to the wheel with Reeve beside her, feeling the gear take the weight of the reach through the iron and into her arms, and she worked the gate open the measure they had agreed, no more, the width a woman needs to reach the ladder and not a hand beyond it, while below her the chamber breathed out its held smell of wet stone, stagnant water, lamp oil carried down on other nights and the green mineral reek of the cut. The reach stayed live beyond the gate, dark and pressing, and the water in the chamber stirred against the walls as the levels argued with each other and settled. Then she went over the coping and down the gate ladder into the chamber, rung by rung, with the stone close at her back and the water waiting below, and Reeve's voice counted nothing and said nothing, which was the agreement, and the last of the morning light narrowed above her to a bright slot between the gate and the frame as she descended into the cold shadow the gate had kept all night.

The chamber was smaller from inside than any plan of it had suggested, a stone box built to hold water and gates and nothing else, its walls weeping in seams, its floor lost under black water that came to her ankles where she stood on the submerged ledge at the ladder's foot and then to her shins as she moved, careful, toward the gate frame. The cold of the water was a different cold from the frame cold she had taken through her palms on the coping. It took her feet first as simple fact, then her ankles as argument, then her legs as a slow negotiation she refused to conduct, because negotiation would have spent attention she owed to the work. Her breath shortened in the first minute and she disciplined it the way she disciplined a read, by giving it a count and holding the count. Above her the slot of light lay across the gate timbers. Behind her the lower gate held its own water at its own level, and between the two gates the chamber kept its working silence, broken only by drips finding their seams and by the faint, irregular tick of cooling iron somewhere in the gate gear.

She read the placement at close hand, the distance of the hands that did it, and the coping read resolved under her fingers into the fuller shape it had only outlined from above. The charge sat against the wrong face of the gate frame, packed to the masonry in the manner of a thing meant to be looked at by lamplight and admired for its seriousness, and she let her hands travel the seating without haste, learning what her eyes in the poor light could only partly confirm. Here was the first seating, its ghost still legible in the frame's older darkening and in the shallow bites where tools had worried the iron on a long night of testing and patience. Here, beside those bites and never on them, was the second seating, corrected by the small distance the coping read had promised her, set down by hands that had plainly studied the first attempt before they touched anything, that had chosen fresh purchase with a deliberation she could feel cooling her fingertips even through the chamber's general cold. The line ran from the placement toward the travelling gear along the path the second occupation had narrowed to, and she followed it with two fingers the way she had once followed stitching on a suspect bale, reading the slack in it by touch. The slack was real and it was architectural. When the gate worked, the gear travelled the way that fed slack into the line instead of drawing it out. The geometry of the thing was the geometry of failure, built in from the first seating and preserved, untouched and uncorrected, by the second. A worker capable of the second placement's care was capable of seeing that geometry. Whoever set the corrected seating had seen it and left it, and that leaving was the loudest fact in the chamber, louder than the drips, louder than her own disciplined breathing.

Beyond the frame, in the open air of the chamber, her attention found the other burden, and she moved toward it the way she would have moved toward a draught in a sealed hold at Valtessa, by feel, one slow step at a time through the black water, with her hands raised and open at the height of her waist. The chamber's weight lay around her as she expected it to lie, layered and legible: the gate's long working life in its iron, the occupations soaked into frame and stone, the faint residue of inspections and oilings and routine hands, decades of keepers and engineers passing through a working place and leaving the ordinary sediment of work. Then, at a step she could have marked on the floor if the floor had been visible, the weight stopped. It did not thin. It did not fade with distance from some centre, the way honest suppression thins and the way all natural fading thins. It ended, at an edge her leading hand found the way a hand finds the edge of a table in a dark room, by the sudden completeness of the change. Inside that edge there was nothing for her hands to read at all. No occupation. No sediment. No fear, no patience, no work. A block of nothing, standing in the chamber air where the work had been done.

She mapped it standing inside it, which was the strangest discipline of her eleven years of reading, to stand where reading was impossible and to map the impossibility by its boundaries, the way a surveyor maps a lake by walking its shore. The nothing had a floor, at the level of the water and the ledge and the chamber's working floor beneath, where the ordinary weight of the place resumed at her ankles like a step down. It had sides her hands could find, two of them within her reach as she turned with care in the water, planes where nothing became something at the width of a fingertip, where the chamber's sediment and occupation began again beyond the edge as if nothing had interrupted them, the way a road resumes on the far side of a bridge. And it had a ceiling. She found it by raising one hand slowly above her head, palm up, fingers spread, reading the air the way she had read the frame, and a span above her own height the nothing ended and the chamber read normally again, the old iron life of the gate gear and the faint high residue of lamps and hands at the wheel housing, all of it present, all of it ordinary, lying over the empty block like water lying over a stone. A surface would have had no ceiling. A stain on the world would have had no height to measure. This had height, and breadth, and a floor, and it stood in the chamber with its edges exact, a volume of absence occupying the very air where two occupations had laboured, as if the work itself had been done inside a room the world could not enter. She stood within it a moment longer than evidence required, committing its dimensions to the memory she trusted, her body the only instrument that had ever been inside it, and the cold climbed her legs and settled in her knees and made the committing expensive, which she noted as cost and paid without bargaining.

She was turning back toward the frame to close the read with a last pass along the line when the gate gear above her took a strain that was not hers and not Reeve's. She knew it first by sound, the deep wooden complaint travelling down through the gate timbers into the water at her shins, and then by the change in the chamber's air, a pressure shift against her face as the upper gate shouldered against its frame under a load it had not been carrying a moment before. Somewhere above the lock, out on the live reach where traffic had been held at a distance all morning, a laden barge was holding against the current and the bank and the wind, and its holding threw its strain down the reach against the gate, the way a held horse throws its weight against a gate in a fence line, patient and enormous and indifferent. The gate took the strain the way old gates take everything, by giving a little and complaining a lot, and in the giving, the travelling gear moved through the small arc of its travel, and the slack in the firing line ran out the wrong way along its path beside her hand, feeding away from the placement instead of toward it, exactly as the geometry had promised her it would, so that even in the chamber's sudden emergency the device kept its built-in failure like a promise it had been constructed to keep.

Then the gate shifted on its frame. It was not a large movement. Gates in their frames do not make large movements until the moment they fail entirely, and this gate did not fail. It shifted by inches, timber against iron, iron against stone, with a sound that came through the water and the walls and the soles of her feet at once, and the seal the gate had kept against the reach broke along its lower edge, and the chamber began to take water fast over the sill. The black water at her shins became water at her knees with a speed that abolished the idea of wading, and the chamber's working silence drowned under a new sound, a pouring, battering rush that filled the stone box and doubled itself off the walls until it seemed to come from inside her own ears. The temperature of the rising water was the temperature of the reach in the cold season, and it took her legs the way the frame cold had taken her hands, except that her hands had been given a choice about touching and her legs had not, and the cost registered in her body as a narrowing, a pulling-in of everything she was to the single task of remaining upright on a ledge she could no longer see, in water that pushed at her knees and then at her thighs with the steady malice of a current that had found its door.

The water took the rungs. Her world shrank to work. Sound filled the stone box. Cold climbed past her knees. Her hand found the ladder. The iron was wet iron. Her grip closed a fraction late. The lag frightened her more than water. Her shoulder answered half a second behind. Everything cost more than it should. Reeve was shouting her name now. Vetter was shouting something else. She did not sort the words. She climbed by count instead. One rung. Then the next rung. Water pushed at her back. Water dragged at her legs. The gate complained above her. Stone scraped her knuckles raw of feeling. Her field had narrowed to three things and a sound: the rung under her hand, the rung above her hand, the iron edge at the chamber top where the light was, and beneath all of it the pouring sound that had eaten the world and would, she understood with the clarity depletion lends to arithmetic, keep rising whether she climbed or not. So she climbed. Her grip went late again. She waited for her shoulder. The shoulder came, half a second late. She climbed through the wait. Pain was a price list now. Standing cost. Gripping cost. Turning her head cost. She paid and kept climbing. Nothing in the chamber fired. Nothing in the chamber would fire. The slack had run the wrong way. The gate ground against its frame. Inches. Only inches. Inches were enough for water. Water needed no more than inches.

Above her, through the narrowing slot of light and noise, the gate wheel began to move against the strain, and she knew the hands on it without seeing them, because Reeve worked a wheel the way Reeve read levels, without waste and without panic, feeding the gate back against the barge's held weight by the same inches it had surrendered. Below and behind her, at the lower end of the chamber she could no longer see, the lower paddles took up their own labour, and she knew those hands too, ruined hands on paddle gear, Vetter working his own lock the way thirty years had taught him, against the pain his hands charged him for every turn, buying her a level the way he had once sold a rhythm, in minutes and in effort nobody would ever itemise. The water at her thighs stopped climbing. It held. It battered her where she stood on the ladder and it held, and holding was enough, because the rungs above her were clear of it and the light slot widened as Reeve's wheel work eased the gate back toward its seat, and Mira climbed the last of the ladder the way she had climbed all of it, by count, by patience, by paying the listed prices without arguing with the list, her grip late and her shoulder later and her will, which depletion had not yet found a way to tax, exactly on time.

She came out at the top of the ladder onto the chamber top with Reeve's hands leaving the wheel to take her arm at the elbow, steadying rather than lifting, because Reeve had judged correctly that she would refuse to be lifted out of her own decision. The morning air hit her wet clothes and her wet skin and she stood shaking on the coping in a wind that had been ordinary an hour earlier and was now an instrument, and the shaking was cost, and the cold standing in her legs and her hands was cost, and the effort of turning her head to look down into the chamber as the water settled back toward its working level was cost, itemised by her body with bureaucratic thoroughness. Vetter came up from the lower paddles with his hands held slightly away from his body, the way men hold hands that have spent themselves, and he looked at her and then at the gate and then at the water calming in the chamber below, and said nothing, because the lock had spoken for all of them. Nothing had fired. The device sat in its corrected seating against the wrong face of the frame, inert in fact before it was ever inert in law, its line slack in the wrong direction, exactly as it had sat through both its placements. Mira stood on the chamber top, soaked and shaking and complete in the one way the case required, with the read whole in her memory: the placement at close hand, the slack that could only feed away, the block of nothing standing in the chamber air with its floor and its sides and its ceiling a span above her height, and the finding the whole descent had been bought to prove, plain now as a level read aloud. The second placement had worked around the first. It had studied the first, corrected its seating by a hand's breadth, and preserved its failure entire. Whatever had happened twice in that chamber, it had never once been an attempt.

## A Volume, Not a Surface

The chamber top was wet stone and iron under her knees, and wind off the upper reach found every gap in the blanket Reeve had put around her shoulders. Below, water settled in the lock with a slow slap against the gates, and cold mud and rope tar rose from the ladders. Her hands would not warm. The shake had gone deep, into her forearms and ribs, keeping a rhythm her breathing could not alter.

She did not speak. Harrand waited on the towpath and Leyden waited closer, both patient in the way of people who knew a read had to settle before it could be carried. She let the case take its true order, which was not the order she would use aloud.

First, the water. The flood pattern had done its clean work, level by level and sill by sill. Water leaving the chamber at high level stood deepest against warehouses one, three and four, where stock sat low enough to drown. Warehouse two stood apart from that arithmetic. Its sill was the highest on the basin, its racking a full span above the line that would ruin the others. A flood arranged by its holder would have paid his neighbours and spared him. That sparing unmade the neatest story the case had offered.

Then the keeper. Eleven minutes, held as a shape rather than a number, the round walked short where habit should have carried a tired man the full length of his own lock. Under the eleven minutes, the payment. She had felt it in the hut as a heaviness that belonged neither to work nor age. It had bought access and rhythm, two nights of an unlatched gate and an early ending. She would say the minutes, the gate, the payment as a fact of access. She would not make his regret into evidence for people who would file it in colder language.

Then what had not been in the chamber. She had found its edges palm by palm in rising water, where reading stopped and ordinary stone and air began. The nothing had a floor where the chamber floor was, sides that turned her read aside like a wall, and a ceiling a span above her head, beyond which the chamber read normally, damp and worked and layered with old fear. It was not [the line she had walked on a floor in Melora](/2026/11/01/nightjar-edge-of-nothing-002/), where the nothing ended at her boot and the room went on being a room on either side of it. A line could be an accident of a room. A volume with a ceiling could not. Whatever emptied a place did so in three dimensions, a block taken out of the world with its top closed over. She set that in her private count.

The rest assembled quietly. Two placements, the first longer and more careful, the second shorter and working around the first, a fitting and a learning. A device built to be found failing. The word rehearsal, carrying only what the evidence carried. No target she did not have. No guess at gloved hands that had left nothing to hold. No spending Vetter to buy Harrand a simpler story.

The blanket slipped. Her fingers fumbled its edge twice before they closed, and that small failure told her her state plainly. Reduced going in, lower coming out, still exact where exactness mattered. When she stood, she would speak in the order that kept the keeper from becoming the story before the chamber had been heard. Until then she knelt on wet stone, holding flood and payment and ceilinged nothing together in silence, while the lock water settled and her hands kept their borrowed cold.

## Made Inert

Night came down on the Corra Lock with a damp that sat on the coping stones and beaded on the ironwork, and the cut above and below the chamber lay unnaturally still. For four hours by agreement the north cut was closed, the first closure of the case, and the silence of held traffic had a texture of its own. No hull knocked at the gates. No wash moved under the footbridge. Lanterns burned at both ends of the chamber in hooded glass, throwing a low amber light that made the wet masonry shine and left the water black between the walls. Mira stood on the chamber top where she had knelt that afternoon, dry clothes under an oilskin coat that was not hers, and felt the stone cold come up through the soles of her boots while her hands, deep in the coat pockets, kept a cold that had nothing to do with the night air.

The partial drain had been Reeve's condition and Harrand had accepted it without argument once the reading was on the record. Not the full emptying that a day of closure would have bought, with its mud and its trampled weight and its stranded cargo listed in ledgers across the basin, but a lowering, controlled and measured, enough to bring the gate frame and the work set against it within reach of hands and eyes, and no further than the work required. Water went out by the paddles in a slow, governed fall, and the chamber gave up its levels reluctantly, sighing against the gates, leaving tide lines of weed and silt on stone that had not seen air since the last full inspection. Mira watched the upper gate settle as the pressure changed and thought of the afternoon, when that same gate had shifted by inches over her head and the reach had come in fast over the sill.

Reeve went down when the level reached the mark she had set, and she went down as she did everything, without hurry and without waste. The basin engineer wore her Binder training in the way she treated the chamber, as a structure with obligations rather than a scene with a story. She had Mira's read written in her own hand on a board that would not run in the damp, the two placements laid out in sequence, the marks of the first occupation and the corrections of the second, and she worked to that read and to nothing else. Mira called down only when she was asked. From the top she could see the crown of Reeve's head and the set of her shoulders as she reached the frame, and she kept her own reading open at the lowest level she could hold without cost she could not afford, feeling for the familiar texture of the chamber now that the water had dropped, the worked stone, the old layered apprehension of the place, and, still, that block where nothing answered her at all.

The firing line was taken off first. That order was the heart of the whole procedure and Reeve stated it aloud before she touched anything, for the record and for the two canal authority men waiting on the lower gate with the sealed case between them. A line led to a moving part that travelled the wrong way when the gate worked was a line that had to cease to connect anything to anything before any other part of the work was disturbed, whatever its slack, whatever its pretence. Mira watched the shape of that separation happen at the level of consequence only, a connection ended, a path closed, the device made less than it had been by the removal of the one link that gave its parts a single intention.

With the line off, the charge itself was lifted clear of the gate frame. Mira had described it in the afternoon with the words she would use again in the finding, a charge packed against the wrong face of the frame, where masonry and water would take most of its force, sabotage shaped and unable to perform as sabotage. Seeing it come away from the stone changed none of that judgement and sharpened all of it. The thing had presence without competence. It occupied the frame as a threat occupies a room, by being seen, and its removal had the odd flatness of a prop being struck from a stage after the audience had gone home. Reeve brought it up clear of the water and clear of the ironwork, steady between her hands, and the authority men received it on the gate top and closed it into the sealed case and made their marks on the seal in turn, each man watching the other write. No one spoke over the work. The case went away up the towpath under seal by the canal authority, between two lanterns, toward a store where it would be kept as an object in a proceeding rather than a danger in a lock, and the sound of their boots faded into the damp dark beyond the upper bridge.

Only then did Mira let the reading go fully. She had held it longer than she needed, out of a habit formed in customs sheds at Valtessa where a consignment was not cleared until the last seal had been seen to close, and the release of it brought the evening down on her all at once. The chamber cold stayed in her hands as she flexed them in her pockets, a deep, stupid cold that the oilskin could not reach, as if the water had left something of its temperature in the bones and the small muscles and meant to keep it there until morning.

The statement was taken in the keeper's hut, procedurally, with the door shut against the night and the stove lit for the first time since the morning, and that was when the other cost presented itself for payment. Vetter sat at his own table with his ruined hands flat on the wood where everyone could see them, and the shame Mira had read in that room in the morning came off him in the lamplight as steadily as heat off the stove iron. She took on a portion of it simply by being in the room and listening, the way she always took on a portion, and it sat in her differently from the cold. The cold was in her fingers. The shame was lower and older in feeling, a wish to be elsewhere in one's own life, to have the two nights back and the gate latched and the round walked full, and it would sit in her longer than the cold would because it resembled things she already carried. Edran Voss had gone out of the world with minutes she still held. Reni had waited nineteen minutes without support in a place Mira had not reached in time. Vetter's regret found the company it kept in her without her inviting it, and she let it sit, and she did not mistake it for her own, and she did not offer him the comfort of saying so.

Leyden conducted the statement in the order they had agreed on the chamber top, the order Vetter himself had feared would be reversed by a system that punished the man who reported. The discovery came first, in the keeper's own words, the morning inspection, the thing seen against the frame, the report made. Then the chamber, and what the chamber had yielded to a qualified read before any drain could trample it. Only then the eleven minutes, the unlatched service gate on two nights, the round walked short, and the payment, named as a sum taken and regretted, for access and for rhythm and for nothing else that the evidence supported. Vetter answered without embellishment.

While the paper was being finished, the cut prepared to reopen. The partial drain reversed itself with the same slowness it had fallen, water returning to the chamber by governed degrees until the levels stood as traffic required them, and the gates took up their working weight again and settled into it with the deep wooden sounds that keepers read as a lockman reads weather. Reeve walked the frame once more at full level, hands trailing where her eyes could not reach in the dark, and declared the chamber clear of the work and clear of its remnants, and her declaration was entered beside Mira's read in the case paper. The first barge held above the lock since the afternoon came down in the last of the four hours, its master silent at the tiller as the gates admitted it, as if the whole crew understood that the cut had been returned to them on time and that questions would only delay the water. The cut reopened on schedule.

The finding itself was written before midnight, in Leyden's office above the basin, with the basin lamps pricking the dark outside the window and the four warehouses standing in their order around the water. The rehearsal reading was filed as the finding, entire and without a softer alternative beside it. The placement pattern was set down first, two occupations of the work, days apart, the first longer and more careful, the second shorter and working around the first, the signature of a fitting and a learning rather than an attempt. The payment was set down next, for access and for rhythm, the gate unlatched and the round walked short, the keeper's knowledge of his own lock sold cheap and regretted at full price. The device was described last, built to be found failing, its force arranged to spend itself against stone and water, its line led where work would undo it. Against all of that, the flood pattern was entered once more as the arithmetic that closed the other door, the sills and shelves that would have paid three holders and spared the one whose policy had drawn their eyes. The conclusion followed the evidence and stopped where the evidence stopped. The rehearsal was for somewhere else, and the somewhere else was not in this case. No target was named, because none had been found. No hands were described, because gloves had left nothing for weight to hold and weight had never been able to give a body or a face in any case. The absence in the chamber was recorded as a measured fact of the read, a volume with edges and a ceiling, and was assigned no cause in the finding, because the case had produced none.

Harrand accepted a closed case. Her acceptance came back on the paper before the lamps burned low, brief and complete, with one instruction about Vetter that Leyden read aloud without emphasis: the keeper's statement stood as taken, the access offence stood on his record, and the lock remained his under supervision until the canal authority ruled, because a service that discarded the man who reported would teach every keeper on the cut the lesson Vetter had already learned too well. Mira listened with her cold hands around a cup she had to think about holding, and felt Vetter's shame shift once in her, like a stone settling in a shoe, as the case closed around its absent centre. Somewhere else, the lesson of the chamber was already learned by whoever had paid to learn it. In Carrowmere the water moved again, the gates worked, and the device was a sealed object in a store, inert and filed and finished.

## What the Lock Houses Say

The lock houses of Carrowmere stood along the lower cut in a row of low stone buildings with slate roofs beaded with night damp, their windows lit one by one as keepers came off the water and stoves were coaxed back to life. Mira walked the towpath below them, with woodsmoke and hot iron and canal water in the cold air, and through a door left ajar she heard the case already changing its shape in the telling. She did not stop.

In the lock houses they had begun to say that NIGHTJAR swam the chamber. By the third house the story had grown as stories in lock houses grow. She opened the gate and walked in, they said. She went down into the chamber as a swimmer goes into a reach, and the water had made way for her, or borne her, or given up its secret because she had asked in the right language. None of them said ladder. None of them said a gate shifting by inches, or Reeve's hands on the wheel, or Vetter at the lower paddles working against the ruin of his own hands. None of them said cold, as she still carried it. The woman in their telling entered the water as an element she commanded. The woman on the towpath had entered it as a place she barely afforded, at low endurance, with two lost nights behind her and the shake still in her forearms under her coat.

Mira let the difference stand without resentment, and recorded it privately and exactly. The legend had moved again. Each telling put the story told about NIGHTJAR further from what any witness on the chamber top had watched, and this one had travelled far in a single evening, from a read performed under pressure in a rising chamber to a swim performed as if the chamber were a bath and she its familiar spirit. A legend that kept strangers looking for a swimmer kept them from looking for a tired officer assembling a case in silence. Let the lock houses have their version.

Her private count received its entry on the towpath, between the third lock house and the footbridge, where the lamps thinned and the water went dark under the arch. Six. The absence is always a volume, never a surface. In Melora it had shown her an edge on a floor and she had taken it, carefully, for the whole of the thing. In the Corra chamber she had mapped it standing, in rising water, with her hands, and found its sides turn her reading aside and its top closed over a span above her head, with ordinary weight resuming beyond and above it like weather beyond a wall. A surface has no ceiling. The nothing in the chamber had one. From now forward she would reach for the top of an absence before she trusted its floor, because the count was hers and the cost of adding to it had been hers each time.

The file closed above the basin in lamplight while she walked. She knew its shape without seeing the paper, because she had assembled it on the chamber top before she spoke. Device made inert and taken away under seal. Keeper's statement taken. Cut reopened on schedule. Rehearsal filed as the finding, on a placement pattern and a payment for access and rhythm and a piece of work built to be found failing. Commissioner satisfied. Case closed, in the full administrative sense, with seals and signatures and traffic moving again on the north cut as if the chamber had never held anything but water and gates.

What the file could not close walked with her under the footbridge and out along the basin, past the four warehouses standing in their order in the dark. Somewhere a real device was coming. It was arithmetic of the same plain kind that had killed the insurance reading, applied to effort instead of water. People did not pay for access to a working lock, and learn its chamber with their hands across two careful occupations, and build a failure convincing enough to be found and filed, in order to stop. The rehearsal had taught its makers what the Corra Lock had to teach, and the lesson would be spent where the frame was right and the line ran true. The Office had filed the case closed, because the case in Carrowmere was closed, and the accuracy of that would be used, somewhere, as cover for what came next. The cold would leave her hands by morning. The shame she had taken in the hut would last longer. The carried question would last longest of all, unanswered in any ledger she was permitted to write, while behind her the lock houses lit their windows and improved their story and the water went on moving through the chamber she had mapped.

Behind her, in the keeper's hut, on paper already cooling beside a stove that ticked as its iron contracted, Anse Vetter signed his statement left handed because his right hand will not stop shaking.

## The Case File

### Q: How does the Carrowmere lock system move traffic between levels?
The Carrowmere system lifts and lowers barges between reaches that stand at different heights, using enclosed chambers fitted with gates at each end. A vessel enters, the gates close behind it, and water is admitted or released through paddles until the chamber level matches the reach ahead. Only then do the forward gates open. The Corra Lock on the north cut follows the same pattern as the other city locks, with a single chamber serving traffic in both directions under keeper control. Passage is sequenced by the keeper, who holds or releases vessels according to water available, load, and priority recorded for bonded cargo. The system depends on stored water in the upper reach, so movements are rationed in dry periods and grouped where loads allow.

### Q: How is insurance arranged for stock held in a bonded warehouse?
Stock held under bond remains the property of its holder, and insurance is placed by the holder with an underwriter accepted for Accord purposes. The policy must describe the goods by manifest reference, state the warehouse and bay where they lie, and record the sill height and racking level at which they are stored. Cover is written against named perils only, and flood cover, where taken, is priced from the surveyed flood line for that building rather than from a general city rate. The holder files a copy of the policy with the bond record, and any change in stock, position, or value requires an endorsement before the change is recognised. A policy does not alter the duties owed under the bond, and a claim does not release goods from bond until the Accord share has been settled and recorded.

### Q: What does the Office mean by a rehearsal device?
A rehearsal device is a charge or fitting placed so that its makers can learn a site, a routine, or a response, without the placement itself completing the harm it appears to threaten. The term is a finding, not a description of construction, and it is applied only where the evidence shows learning as the purpose. Indicators recorded in past files include a fitting that cannot perform as placed, a first occupation of the work longer than a second, and marks showing that a later placement was worked around an earlier one. The classification matters because it changes the inquiry. Attention moves from the building that was threatened to the knowledge that was taken away, and the file remains open in substance even where the case at the site is closed and the site itself has been made safe.

### Q: What jurisdiction does the Accord hold inside Carrowmere as its seat city?
Carrowmere hosts the seat of the Accord, and Accord jurisdiction inside the city is defined by function rather than by territory alone. The Accord governs its own premises, its bonded infrastructure, and matters that pass under bond, including the locks and warehouses that serve bonded traffic. Ordinary streets, dwellings, and local trade remain under city authority, and city officers retain their usual powers there. Where a matter touches both spheres, the file records which authority acts first and on what ground, and disputes over that order are referred upward rather than settled on the spot. Seat status gives the Accord no general power over residents. It gives the Accord direct responsibility for the systems its members share, and direct answerability when those systems fail or are interfered with.

### Q: Why does the Ninth Office answer to two authorities?
The Ninth Office is tasked through the Accord administration and is answerable in addition to Commissioner oversight, and the two lines are kept separate by design. The administration directs work, assigns officers, and holds the Office budget and establishment. Oversight holds no tasking power over daily operations but may call for files, require explanations of method, and record dissent where it judges that a power has been used beyond its proper scope. The arrangement reflects the character of the Office itself. Its officers work inside member systems under Accord authority, and a single line of answerability was judged insufficient for work of that sensitivity. In practice an officer may receive a task from one line and be required to account for its execution to the other, and both records are preserved on the same file.

### Q: What is a gate chamber?
A gate chamber is the enclosed section of a lock in which a vessel is raised or lowered, bounded by the upper and lower gates and by masonry walls on either side. The term also covers the gate recesses, the paddle openings, and the working space at the gate frame when those features are treated as part of a single inspection unit. In Office and canal authority papers the chamber is the unit of record for condition, access, and interference. A report that names the chamber fixes the place more narrowly than a report that names the lock as a whole. Ladders, gear mountings, and drainage points inside the walls are recorded as chamber fittings. Anything found within the chamber is therefore logged by position against the gates and the frame, so that a later reader can reconstruct where it stood in relation to moving parts and to the water line.

### Q: What does a lock keeper's inspection round consist of?
A keeper's round is a fixed walk of the lock and its immediate fittings, made at set points in the working day and recorded on completion. The keeper examines both gates and their gear, checks paddles and their mountings, reads the chamber for obstruction or damage, and confirms that service gates and access points stand as they should. Water levels above and below the lock are noted, together with any change in leakage, movement, or sound in the gear. The round is made in a prescribed order so that an omission shows as a break in the record. Findings are entered in the keeper's own hand in the lock record, minor defects are marked for the canal authority work list, and anything touching safety or interference is reported at once through the lock house line rather than left for the next round.

### Q: How is canal traffic held or released on the north cut?
Traffic on the north cut is controlled from the lock, where the keeper holds vessels in the reach or in designated holding places and releases them in an order that the water and the chamber can support. A hold may be imposed for level, for weather on the upper reach, for work in the chamber, or on instruction recorded from the canal authority or the Accord where bonded traffic is affected. Held vessels are told the reason in general terms and their place in the order is preserved unless a priority load is directed over them. Release follows the same order, with laden bonded traffic ordinarily taken before empty hulls where levels are falling. Every hold and release is timed in the lock record, because delay on a cut carrying bonded cargo creates claims, and the record is the means by which those claims are tested.

### Q: What is a partial drain, and who may order one?
A partial drain lowers the water in a lock chamber or a short reach to a stated level below the full working level, without emptying the cut or closing it for a full drain period. It is used to expose a gate frame, a sill, or a fitting for inspection or removal while the structure of the lock remains waterborne and the surrounding reaches stay in use. The order must state the level to be reached, the hours during which it will be held, and the officer or engineer responsible for the work done inside it. Authority rests with the canal authority engineer for the basin, acting with Accord agreement where Accord infrastructure or a live inquiry is involved. Keepers execute a partial drain but may not order one on their own authority, and the levels before and after are entered in the lock record.

### Q: How is a charge found on Accord infrastructure removed under seal?
Removal begins only after the position of the charge has been recorded in place, with its relation to the frame, the gear, and the water line fixed in writing and by measure. The basin engineer directs the making safe of the item, working to a sequence agreed before anyone enters the drained or partly drained chamber. The firing connection, where one exists, is separated first, and the body of the charge is then lifted clear and placed in a sealed carrier provided for the purpose. The carrier is sealed at the chamber side in the presence of two witnesses, and its seal number is entered in both the canal authority record and the Office file. Custody passes by signature at each handover until the carrier reaches the designated store, and no part of the item is examined, opened, or described beyond external appearance before that store accepts it.

### Q: What is the canal authority, and how does it differ from the Ninth Office?
The canal authority is the body charged with the water itself. It maintains locks, gates, gear, and banks, employs keepers and basin engineers, sets traffic order on the cuts, and answers for levels and for the physical safety of navigation. The Ninth Office holds no maintenance function and gives no orders on ordinary navigation. Its concern begins where interference, concealment, or misuse of Accord systems is suspected, and its officers work on canal ground by Accord authority rather than by any standing in the authority chain of command. The distinction is preserved in the records. A defect is a canal authority matter. A defect that has been arranged, exploited, or hidden becomes an Office matter as well, and both files run in parallel until each body closes its own part of the case.

### Q: How are warehouse sills and racking heights surveyed?
Each bonded warehouse on a basin is surveyed as a building in relation to water, not as floor space alone. The surveyor records the height of the sill at each entrance above a fixed basin datum, the fall of the floor inside, and the height of each racking tier above that floor. The figures are taken by level and chain from the datum mark and are checked against the previous survey for the same building. A flood line for the building is then stated as the level that water standing in the basin would reach against each tier. Holders receive a copy of the survey for their own building, and the Accord copy is kept with the bond record. Resurvey follows any alteration to sills, floors, or racking, and an unrecorded alteration suspends the flood figures until new figures have been taken and filed.

### Q: What does a bonded warehouse holder owe the Accord?
A holder owes the Accord the duties set out in the bond under which stock is received, and those duties continue for as long as any bonded stock remains in the building. They include keeping the premises and their fittings in the surveyed condition, admitting authorised inspection without delay, maintaining a true stock record that matches the manifests, and reporting loss, damage, or interference at once. The holder also owes the Accord share on any goods released from bond, and may not move, divide, or repackage bonded stock except as the bond permits and the record shows. Debt, dispute with an underwriter, or pressure from a creditor does not suspend any of these duties. Breach is dealt with first against the bond itself, by restriction or removal of stock, before any wider question of fault is addressed on the file.

### Q: How are statements taken from canal staff after an incident?
Statements from keepers, engineers, and other canal staff are taken by an officer or by a canal authority official authorised for the purpose, and each statement is taken separately. The witness is asked for a continuous account in order of events, with times fixed against the lock record and the traffic log where those records exist. Questions follow the account rather than preceding it, and any correction the witness wishes to make is entered as a correction, in the witness's presence, rather than written over the first wording. The finished statement is read back, signed on each page, and a copy is offered to the witness. Staff remain employees of the canal authority throughout, and a statement taken for an Office file does not transfer them to Office discipline. Refusal, delay, and inconsistency are all recorded in the same neutral form.

### Q: What happens to bonded cargo when a cut is closed?
Closure of a cut stops movement, not liability. Cargo already afloat remains under bond in the vessel that carries it, and cargo in warehouses at either end remains under bond in store. Holders and carriers are notified of the closure and of its expected length where that can be stated, and perishable or time bound loads may be rerouted only under a fresh transit record that preserves the chain of seals. No cargo may be unloaded to an unbonded place merely because the cut is closed, and a closure gives no holder a right to break bond or to claim release. Delay claims are tested against the closure record, which states the hour of closing, the ground for it, and the hour of reopening. Where a closure is ordered for inquiry purposes, that fact is recorded separately from closures ordered for water or repair.

### Q: How do the lock houses pass news along the cut?
Lock houses communicate by a dedicated line and by written message carried with traffic, and the two methods are used for different classes of news. Operational matters, including holds, releases, levels, and defects, pass on the line in a fixed form that names the lock, the hour, and the instruction or report in brief. Matters that must be preserved word for word, including statements of interference or orders closing a cut, are written, signed, and sent by the next reliable movement or by a runner where no movement is available. Each lock house keeps a message book in which incoming and outgoing items are entered in sequence, so a gap in numbering shows a missing message. Informal report also travels with crews and keepers changing shift, but no action is founded on informal report alone until it has been entered in a book or confirmed on the line.

### Q: What is a service gate?
A service gate is a smaller access gate set into or beside the main gate structure, giving authorised persons entry to the chamber side, the gate recess, or the gear spaces without working the main gates. Service gates serve inspection, maintenance, and the passage of tools and materials, and they are fitted with their own latching and sealing arrangements. Because a service gate can give quiet access to the working parts of a lock, its state is part of the keeper's round and its seal, where one is fitted, is checked with the other seals of the lock. An unlatched or unsealed service gate is recorded as a defect in its own right, whether or not any interference follows from it. Keys and latching gear for service gates are held under the same discipline as other lock keys, and lending or leaving them is treated as a breach of that discipline.

### Q: How is gate gear inspected?
Gate gear, including wheels, linkages, paddle gear, and their mountings, is inspected on two cycles. The keeper's daily contact with the gear is a working check for stiffness, slack, noise, and visible damage, and its results belong to the lock record. The formal inspection is made by the basin engineer or a fitter acting under the engineer, at intervals set for each lock, and with the gate worked through its full travel under observation. Measurements of travel, play, and seating are compared with the figures from the previous formal inspection, and any change beyond the stated tolerance is entered as a defect with a date for remedy. Gear that moves a firing risk, a gate that will not seat, or a linkage that travels against its proper direction stops traffic through that lock until the engineer passes it in writing.

### Q: What may Accord oversight demand of a lock keeper?
Oversight may require a keeper to produce the lock record, the message book, and any seal or key account for the lock, and to give an account of a round, a hold, or a report in the order in which events occurred. It may direct that a keeper's statement be taken promptly and that the original record, unaltered, be copied for the file. Oversight may not direct the keeper's daily work, alter a traffic decision, or impose discipline, which remains with the canal authority as employer. Where oversight judges that a keeper's conduct raises a wider question, its remedy is a recorded requirement addressed to the canal authority and copied to the Office file. The keeper answers questions put through that process with the same protection as any other witness. Nothing in the process permits an oversight officer to enter a chamber or work any gear at the lock.

### Q: How are basin levels read and recorded?
Basin levels are read from fixed gauges set at the basin head, at each lock, and at marked points on the lower reach, all referred to a single datum for the city system. The keeper reads the gauge at the lock at set hours and at every change of traffic state, and the basin engineer collates the lock readings into the basin figure for the day. Readings are entered as figures, not as descriptions, and a reading that cannot be taken is entered as missed with the reason, never estimated from the previous hour. Flood and drain calculations for the basin are made from these figures alone. In a dispute over what the water would have done, the gauge record outranks recollection, holder estimates, and underwriter figures, because it is the only record made at the time by a person with no interest in the outcome.
