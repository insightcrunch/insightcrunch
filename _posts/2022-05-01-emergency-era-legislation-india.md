Emergency-era legislation is best read as a single connected record rather than as a list of famous episodes. Between June 1975 and March 1977 the government of the day worked on all three levels of the legal system at once. It amended the Constitution so that the courts could not question what had been done, it operated a detention statute whose grounds could be kept secret from the person detained, and it controlled the press through censorship orders and a statute of its own. The period therefore left two kinds of trace in Indian law. The first is the set of episodes every student can name: the proclamation of 25 June 1975, the Forty-second Amendment, the habeas corpus judgment of April 1976. The second is quieter and, for the study of legislation, more instructive: the corrective settlement of 1977 to 1979 reversed the parts of the record that touched personal liberty, left standing the parts that rearranged the structure of the Constitution, and enacted one safeguard for detainees that no government has ever brought into force.

![Constitutional text and statute book open together - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

Read that way, the period answers a practical question about how constitutional safeguards work. A safeguard is only as strong as the machinery that enforces it, and the legislation of these twenty-one months was aimed with unusual precision at the machinery: at the courts' power to ask questions, at the detainee's right to be told the grounds of detention, at the newspapers' freedom to report proceedings, and at the amending power's own limits. The corrective legislation that followed was aimed at the same machinery in reverse. What it restored, and what it left alone, is the subject of this article. Two fixed points organise everything that follows. The first is that the proclamation of Emergency was itself a lawful constitutional act, taken under Article 352 as the article then stood; nothing in the record depends on treating it as anything else. The second is that the reversal was also done by law, through a change of Parliament after a general election, and it is the comparison between the two bodies of law, the one enacted between 1975 and 1976 and the one enacted between 1977 and 1979, that carries the lesson.

A note on method closes the opening. The article names the holders of offices where the record requires it, the President who signed, the Law Ministers who moved the Bills, the judges who decided, because legislation is made by identifiable hands and the settlement's attributions depend on them. It does not build its account on any individual's motives, which the legislative record rarely proves, and where the period is characterised, by the Commission that inquired into it or by the governments that defended it, the characterisation is carried in the source's name. The instruments are quoted by their numbers, years and sections, and every date in what follows can be checked against the bare Acts and the reports of the cases. That discipline is the article's answer to its subject: a period in which forms were used to disable scrutiny is best studied in a form that invites it.

## The constitutional text the period inherited

The Emergency did not invent its instruments. Part XVIII of the Constitution, Articles 352 to 360, had carried a full emergency code since 1950, drafted in the shadow of war and partition and used twice before, in 1962 and in 1971. Article 352 empowered the President, on being satisfied that a grave emergency existed whereby the security of India or of any part of it was threatened by war, external aggression or internal disturbance, to issue a proclamation. While a proclamation operated, the Union's executive power extended to directing any state, Parliament could legislate on the State List, and the federal distribution of revenues could be altered. Article 358 provided that while a proclamation operated, Article 19, the freedoms of speech, assembly, association, movement, residence and occupation, ceased to restrict state power. Article 359 empowered the President to suspend, by order, the right to move any court for the enforcement of such fundamental rights as the order specified. The design placed the decisive moves in executive hands and made the courts' role depend on the terms of the orders issued under it.

Two features of that design matter for everything that follows. The first is the distinction between suspending a right and suspending its enforcement. Article 359 did not repeal or amend any fundamental right; it closed the courtroom door to a person seeking to enforce the rights named in the order. Whether that distinction made a practical difference to a person in custody was the question the Supreme Court would have to answer in April 1976. The second feature is the guardianship of the proclamation itself. As the text stood in 1975, the proclamation needed parliamentary approval within two months, and beyond that the Constitution prescribed no special majority, no requirement of written Cabinet advice, and no express bar on judicial review of the President's satisfaction, though the satisfaction was by its nature an executive judgment. Each of those omissions would become a specific item of legislation, first in one direction and then in the other.

Above the emergency code stood a second and newer piece of law. In Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala, decided on 24 April 1973 by a bench of thirteen judges, the Supreme Court had held that Parliament's amending power under Article 368, though wide, did not extend to damaging or destroying the basic structure of the Constitution. The judgment was two years old when the proclamation issued, and it fixed the legal weather in which the period's amendments would be made and then judged. Its relevance to the period is direct, and it is worth stating at the outset.

### What did the Kesavananda Bharati judgment of 1973 have to do with a period that began in 1975?

The judgment fixed the only limit on the amending power that the period would test. By holding that Parliament could amend any provision but could not alter the basic structure, it supplied the ground on which the election amendment of 1975 was struck down in part, and on which two sections of the Forty-second Amendment were later held void.

The basic structure holding mattered in a further way. A government determined to legislate without judicial interference had three routes open: it could shield particular exercises of power from review, it could place its statutes beyond challenge in the Ninth Schedule, or it could attack the limit itself by declaring the amending power unlimited. The legislation of 1975 and 1976 used all three routes, and the corrective settlement answered all three. The shield clauses of the Thirty-eighth Amendment were deleted, the Ninth Schedule device was left subject to the test the courts later settled for post-1973 insertions, and the claim of unlimited constituent power in the Forty-second Amendment was removed from the working text by the Supreme Court in Minerva Mills in 1980. The era framework of this series treats Kesavananda as the period's fixed point precisely because every later instrument in the story is a move for or against it.

The statutory side of the inheritance was preventive detention. India had legislated detention without trial since the first years of the Republic. The Preventive Detention Act of 1950, passed as a temporary measure, was renewed repeatedly and allowed to lapse only at the end of 1969. Its successor arrived with the Maintenance of Internal Security Act, 1971, Act 26 of 1971, which received assent on 2 July 1971 after beginning life as an Ordinance promulgated on 7 May 1971. The Act was framed for the security of India, the defence of India, relations with foreign powers, the security of a state and the maintenance of public order and of supplies and services essential to the community. Its peacetime place in the scheme is set by Article 22 of the Constitution, which permits preventive detention but surrounds it with conditions: a person detained must be told the grounds of the order as soon as may be, must be given the earliest opportunity of making a representation against it, and cannot be held beyond three months unless an Advisory Board of persons qualified to be High Court judges reports sufficient cause. The statute of 1971 followed that frame. What the Emergency did to it, in 1975 and 1976, was to strip the frame away for a defined class of cases.

The machinery deserves a slower statement, because the period used every part of it. On a proclamation under Article 352, Article 353 extends the Union's executive power to giving directions to any state as to the manner in which its own executive power is to be exercised, and empowers Parliament to legislate on subjects in the State List, with such laws overriding state law in case of conflict. Article 354 permits the President to modify the constitutional distribution of revenues between the Union and the states while the proclamation operates. The financial and federal consequences of an emergency were therefore automatic and textual; no further enactment was needed to convert the Union's supremacy from the concurrent pattern of ordinary times into a general one. Article 356, the provision for failure of constitutional machinery in a state, was a separate power, and Article 360, for financial emergency, has never been used. The emergency code the framers built was thus a complete alternative operating system for the federation, held in reserve inside the same document. The Constituent Assembly had the precedent of emergency provisions in other constitutions before it, including the Weimar Constitution's Article 48 and its history in Germany, and adopted the machinery with the safeguard, as Dr. Ambedkar put the hope, that the provisions would remain a dead letter. The period of 1975 to 1977 is, in large part, the story of that hope meeting a Parliament and an executive willing to run the machinery at full capacity.

The two earlier proclamations had set the pattern of use. The proclamation of 1962, during the war with China, operated for years and carried a Defence of India Act with it; the proclamation of 1971, during the war with Pakistan, was issued on 3 December 1971 and, as noted, was still running when the period opened. Neither had been accompanied by a general suspension order aimed at the domestic political process, and neither had been followed by amendments directed at the courts. The inheritance, in other words, contained both the powers and a convention of restraint in their domestic use. The legislation of 1975 kept the powers and discarded the convention, and it is the convention's absence, rather than any novelty in the instruments, that the era framework asks the reader to notice.

One more piece of the inheritance should be flagged, because the settlement later changed it and the comparison articles return to it. Article 22, the preventive detention article, was not suspended as a text in June 1975; it was the right to move a court for its enforcement that the order of 27 June suspended. The article's own terms, the grounds, the representation, the Advisory Board and the three-month limit on detention without a board's opinion, remained printed in the Constitution throughout, and detention orders continued to recite compliance with them where the statute required it. The distinction between a right that exists and a right that can be enforced in a court is the distinction the whole period turns on, and candidates meet it first here: the Emergency's legal technique was not to delete protections but to disconnect them from the courts.

The framers' choices were made in a comparative field that remains instructive, and a short statement of it sharpens what the period tested. The German Basic Law of 1949, written after the Weimar collapse, withheld any general power to suspend the Constitution's core commitments, and its later emergency amendments kept the legislature sitting and the Basic Law itself unamendable during a state of defence. South Africa's Constitution of 1996, written after apartheid, permits derogation in a state of emergency only within limits and lists rights, including life and dignity, from which no derogation is permitted at all. The Indian text of 1950 stood between these poles: more permissive than either, because it suspended the enforcement of rights on executive order without a non-derogable core, and more structured than Weimar's single article, because it distributed the emergency across a code of provisions. The settlement of 1978 moved the Indian text toward the later pole by the simplest possible drafting: it named two articles that no order may touch. Comparative study cannot decide what India should have done in 1975, and this article offers none; it can show that the device the Forty-fourth Amendment adopted, a short list of unsuspendable rights, was the device other post-crisis constitutions also reached for, which is one measure of the settlement's workmanship.

## Emergency-era legislation begins: June 1975 and the first instruments

On 3 December 1971 a proclamation of Emergency on the ground of external aggression had been issued during the war with Pakistan, and it had never been revoked; it remained in operation through the whole of what followed. On the night of 25 June 1975 the President issued a second proclamation, declaring that a grave emergency existed whereby the security of India was threatened by internal disturbance. The ground mattered legally. Internal disturbance was the phrase in Article 352 as it then stood, a wider and less defined expression than the words the Constitution would later carry. The second proclamation was laid before Parliament and approved by both Houses, as the article required, and it coexisted with the first: for the next twenty-one months India lived under two proclamations at once, a condition the Thirty-eighth Amendment, enacted within weeks, would expressly authorise by providing that the President could issue different proclamations on different grounds even while an earlier proclamation operated.

The legal consequences were set running by orders rather than by debate. By Article 358, Article 19 stood suspended for the duration of the proclamation. On 27 June 1975 the President issued an order under Article 359(1) suspending the right to move any court for the enforcement of Articles 14, 21 and 22, the equality clause, the protection of life and personal liberty, and the safeguards on arrest and detention, and suspending all pending proceedings for their enforcement. The choice of articles was the operative decision of the period. Article 14 and Article 21 were the two provisions through which detention orders were ordinarily attacked, and Article 22 contained the detainee's own procedural rights. With enforcement of all three suspended, a detention under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act could no longer be tested in court on the grounds those articles supplied.

The press was dealt with on the same days and by subordinate legislation. Pre-censorship of newspapers was imposed by an order of the central government, S.O. 275(E), dated 26 June 1975, made under Rule 48 of the Defence and Internal Security of India Rules. The underlying Act, the Defence of India Act, 1971, a wartime statute kept alive by the continuing proclamation of 1971, was retitled the Defence and Internal Security of India Act with effect from 1 July 1975, so that its rule-making power formally matched its new internal use. Electricity to newspaper presses in Delhi was cut on the night of the proclamation, an administrative act rather than a legal one, and the legal machinery of censorship then governed publication for the rest of the period: material had to be submitted before printing, and the censor's directions bound the publisher. No statute enacted censorship at this stage. The statute for the press came later, in 1976, and its short life is traced below.

The Parliament that approved these measures was itself living on borrowed time, and the manner of its survival is part of the legislative record. The Fifth Lok Sabha, elected in March 1971, was due to expire on 18 March 1976. Article 83(2) permits Parliament, while a proclamation of Emergency operates, to extend the House's life by law for not more than one year at a time, and not beyond six months after the proclamation ceases. That power was used twice, by the House of the People (Extension of Duration) Act, 1976 and by an amending Act of the same year, carrying the House first to 18 March 1977 and then to 18 March 1978.

### What happened to the Lok Sabha that had been elected in 1971?

Its life was extended twice by law during the Emergency, by one year at a time under the proviso to Article 83(2), first to March 1977 and then to March 1978. Neither extension ran its course: the House was dissolved on 18 January 1977, and a general election followed in March of that year.

The detail is worth keeping because it shows the period's method in miniature. Nothing was done by simply ignoring the Constitution. The House sat beyond its term because a clause of the Constitution allowed an extension and a statute enacted it; the courts were closed to detainees because an order under a clause of the Constitution closed them; the press was censored under rules made under a statute. The period's legislation is a record of constitutional forms used to their fullest extent, which is why the corrective settlement, when it came, had to be drafted as carefully as the original.

The suspension scheme was completed in stages. A further order under Article 359, issued on 8 January 1976, extended the suspension of enforcement to Article 19 as well, so that the four articles through which the period's measures could most readily be attacked, Articles 14, 19, 21 and 22, all stood behind the same closed door. The technique is worth noting for what it reveals about the drafting of the period: the substantive law of detention, censorship and administration was left largely in place, in statutes that recited their safeguards, while the orders addressed the one thing that gave the safeguards effect, the citizen's access to a court to enforce them. A reader of the statute book alone, without the Gazette orders, would miss the period's operative law entirely; the suspension orders, a few pages each, did more work than most of the Acts.

Approval followed the constitutional course. The proclamation was laid before both Houses and approved within the period the article allowed, during the session that followed its issue; the Thirty-eighth Amendment, introduced on 22 July 1975, was part of the same session's business. The sequence is part of the record and should be held firmly: nothing in the opening phase depended on the absence of Parliament. The House was sitting, the opposition benches were partially emptied by the detentions that began on the night of the proclamation and the days after, and the measures that built the period's legal structure were passed by the chambers the Constitution provided, in the forms the Constitution required. It is precisely that regularity that made the corrective task of 1977 a legislative one: what had been done by law had to be undone by law.

Detention, finally, did not run on MISA alone. The Defence and Internal Security of India Rules carried their own powers of detention and restriction, and persons were held under both codes during the period; the suspension orders were framed, and the habeas corpus litigation was fought, on the footing that the two instruments stood side by side. The wartime code supplied the censorship rules and a detention power; the peacetime security Act supplied the general detention power. The corrective settlement treated them differently, repealing the one and letting the other's parent proclamation lapse into history, and the difference is traced in the repeal section below.

## The amendments of 1975: closing the courtroom door

The first constitutional amendment of the period was a shield, not a sword. The Constitution (Thirty-eighth Amendment) Act, 1975, whose Bill was introduced by the Law Minister, H. R. Gokhale, on 22 July 1975, amended Articles 123, 213, 239B, 352, 356, 359 and 360. Its device was a recurring sentence: the satisfaction of the President or the Governor, in issuing an ordinance or a proclamation, was declared to be final and conclusive and not questionable in any court on any ground. New clauses on those terms were inserted into the ordinance articles and into Article 352, and Article 359 gained a clause protecting orders made under it. The statement of objects and reasons offered a justification in terms of drafting intent: it said that in several articles the satisfaction had always been understood to be non-justiciable, and that the amendment made the position explicit in view of the controversy the proclamations had raised. The practical effect did not depend on the justification. A court asked to examine whether a proclamation rested on any material at all, or whether an ordinance had been promulgated to bypass a legislature, was told by the constitutional text that the question could not be asked.

The same amendment regularised the double proclamation, inserting the provision that the President may issue different proclamations on different grounds whether or not a proclamation is already in operation. It also confirmed, through the new clause in Article 359, that a suspension order could be made while different proclamations ran concurrently. Read together, the Thirty-eighth Amendment did not extend the emergency powers by an inch. It did something subtler: it removed the referee. The powers were what they had been in 1950; the amendment addressed the possibility that a court might supervise their use.

The Constitution (Thirty-ninth Amendment) Act, 1975 went further, and its origin lay in litigation. On 12 June 1975 the Allahabad High Court had set aside the Prime Minister's election of 1971 on findings of corrupt practice under the Representation of the People Act, 1951, and a stay of the judgment's operation left the question open in the Supreme Court when the Emergency was proclaimed. Parliament answered on three fronts within weeks. The Election Laws (Amendment) Act, 1975 altered the election law itself, retrospectively, redefining the practices the case had turned on. The Thirty-ninth Amendment then removed the forum: it substituted Article 71, and inserted a new Article 329A, providing that elections to Parliament of a person holding the office of Prime Minister or Speaker, and elections to the offices of President and Vice-President, would be governed by a law Parliament might make and a forum it might create, and not by the ordinary election petition process; clause (4) of the new article declared that no election of such a person could be called in question, validated elections already held notwithstanding any court order, and disposed of the pending appeal itself. In the same operation the amendment inserted a block of statutes, entries 87 to 127, into the Ninth Schedule, placing them beyond fundamental rights challenge; the Maintenance of Internal Security Act was among the entries so inserted, together with the election laws whose validity the litigation had put in issue. The composition of the block shows the device's double use in a single amendment: part of it was insurance for the period's working statutes, and part of it was the settlement, in advance, of the legal questions the election case had raised, by withdrawing the governing Acts from the reach of the fundamental rights under which a future challenge might have been framed. Article 31B's protection was, on the law as it stood in 1975, understood to be complete for a listed Act; the qualification that post-1973 insertions take their protection subject to the basic structure was settled only later, in the Waman Rao line traced below, and the drafters of 1975 cannot be charged with having foreseen it. What they can be taken to have intended is the text they enacted: a list, a bar, and a statute placed behind both.

The Supreme Court answered in Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain, decided on 7 November 1975. A five-judge bench applied the amended election law and upheld the election, but struck down clause (4) of Article 329A. The reasoning became the basic structure doctrine's first practical use: free and fair elections were part of the basic structure, and a clause by which the legislature itself decided a pending election dispute, and barred the courts from the question, damaged that structure. The decision is treated in depth in the basic structure article of this series, and its place here is in the sequence. Within five months of the proclamation, Parliament had legislated on a pending case and the Court had used the 1973 doctrine to refuse the part of the law that made the legislature the judge. The remaining amendments of the period were drafted against that background: the shield of 1975 had not held everywhere, and the Forty-second Amendment would address the amending power itself.

A word belongs here to the amendment of the period that had nothing to do with it. The Constitution (Forty-first Amendment) Act, 1976 amended Article 316 to raise the retirement age of the chairman and members of a State Public Service Commission, and of a Joint Commission, from sixty years to sixty-two. It received assent on 7 September 1976. Its inclusion in this article is deliberate, and it carries a method point for candidates and researchers: the constitutional text absorbed routine business all through the period, and a list of amendments assented to between 1975 and 1977 is not, without more, a list of Emergency measures. The Fortieth Amendment of the same year, which added a further block of laws to the Ninth Schedule, belongs closer to the period's story; the Forty-first does not, and reading it as an Emergency measure is a small but complete example of dating by proximity rather than by substance.

The drafting of the shield repays attention, because the corrective amendment had to find and remove each instance of it. In the ordinance articles, the new clauses declared that the satisfaction of the President under Article 123, of the Governor under Article 213, and of the administrator under Article 239B, in promulgating an ordinance, shall be final and conclusive and shall not be questioned in any court on any ground. In Article 352, a new clause did the same for the President's satisfaction that a grave emergency existed, and added that the satisfaction could be formed on the basis of a threat differing from the ground of an earlier subsisting proclamation. Article 356 received a matching clause for President's rule, and Article 360 for the financial emergency that has never been proclaimed. The sweep across all the satisfaction-based powers in the same formula shows the amendment's purpose plainly: it was not aimed at a single controversy but at the doctrine, visible in the case law of the preceding years, that an ostensibly subjective satisfaction could in principle be examined for mala fides or for absence of material. The Forty-fourth Amendment's deletions were equally systematic, which is why the two amendments are read together in the comparison cluster of this series.

The election litigation behind the Thirty-ninth Amendment should be stated with its dates, because the speed of the legislative answer is itself a datum of the record. The Allahabad High Court's judgment of 12 June 1975 held two charges of corrupt practice proved against the returned candidate and disqualified her from holding elective office for six years. On 24 June 1975 a vacation judge of the Supreme Court granted a conditional stay, under which she could continue to discharge the office of Prime Minister and address Parliament but not vote in it, pending the appeal. The Emergency was proclaimed the following night. The Election Laws (Amendment) Act then changed, with retrospective effect, the provisions under which the charges had been framed, including the treatment of assistance rendered by government servants and of expenditure incurred by a political party, and the Thirty-ninth Amendment followed within weeks. Article 329A as inserted had six clauses: the first three created the special regime and the promised parliamentary law and forum; clause (4) barred the courts and disposed of pending proceedings; clause (5) governed the application of the election law as amended; clause (6) made the article override the rest of the Constitution. When the Supreme Court struck down clause (4) and applied the amended law to uphold the election, the special regime was left without its keystone, and the promised parliamentary forum was never created; the article sat in the text, substantially inoperative, until the Forty-fourth Amendment omitted it. The episode is the period's clearest demonstration of the difference between enacting a provision and making it work, a difference the unnotified safeguard at the end of this article repeats from the other side.

The Fortieth Amendment of 1976, mentioned briefly above, completes the year's constitutional business. It added a further large block of statutes, principally state laws on land reform, land ceilings and kindred subjects, to the Ninth Schedule. In the period's economy of shields, the Ninth Schedule was the standing device: placement in it invoked Article 31B, under which a listed law cannot be held void for inconsistency with Part III. The Thirty-ninth Amendment had used the device for the security and election statutes of the moment; the Fortieth used it for the agrarian code. The settlement of 1977 to 1979 did not empty the Schedule; the discipline applied to it came from the courts, which in Waman Rao and the cases that followed confined Article 31B's protection for laws inserted after 24 April 1973, the date of Kesavananda. The point is registered here and owned, as a query, by the basic structure article.

## The detention code in operation

Detention was the period's working instrument, and its law is found in the Maintenance of Internal Security Act as amended. The Act of 1971, in the form in which the Emergency found it, was a conventional preventive detention statute. An order could be made by the central or a state government, and by empowered officers, where satisfied that it was necessary to detain a person to prevent him acting in a manner prejudicial to the heads the Act listed. The detainee had to be told the grounds of detention, ordinarily within five days, and could make a representation to the government; a case had to go before an Advisory Board, and detention could not exceed twelve months. Those features tracked Article 22 of the Constitution and the safeguards the preventive detention guide in this series traces from 1950 onwards. The statute's first life had been in wartime conditions under the 1971 proclamation; it continued in force after that proclamation should have ended, and it was on the shelf, staffed and understood, when June 1975 came.

The Emergency amendments removed the safeguards for the cases that mattered. The Maintenance of Internal Security (Amendment) Act, 1975, Act 39 of 1975, made changes effective from 29 June 1975, within days of the proclamation. A second amendment followed in 1976, Act 14 of 1976, which inserted a new section 16A, headed as special provisions for dealing with emergency, and gave it retrospective effect from 29 June 1975. Section 16A applied to a class of detainees defined by declaration: persons detained, in the later consolidated text, with a view to preventing them from acting in a manner prejudicial to the defence of India, the security of India or the security of a state, where the detaining authority certified the case as one to which the section should apply in the context of the proclamations of 3 December 1971 or 25 June 1975. For that class, the Act then did four things. It dispensed with the Advisory Board machinery for the period the section operated. It allowed detention to continue for a longer continuous stretch without the ordinary review points. It made the grounds of detention, and any material behind them, confidential. And by section 16A(9), it declared that neither the grounds communicated nor any representation against them would be required to disclose facts which the authority considered it against the public interest to disclose, in a form that, as the Supreme Court later read it, left the detainee without knowledge of the case he had to answer.

The result was a statute within a statute. Outside section 16A, the Act of 1971 continued in something like its ordinary shape, with boards and representations and the twelve-month ceiling. Inside it, detention ran on the authority's satisfaction alone, unexamined by a board, unexplained to the detainee, and, by force of the Article 359 order of 27 June 1975, unchallengeable in court on the grounds Articles 14, 21 and 22 would otherwise have supplied. The section was temporary even in its own design: by its terms it operated while the proclamations of 1971 and 1975 both ran, and in any event for no more than twenty-four months from 25 June 1975, whichever period was the shorter, a sunset that fixed the regime's intended life to the emergency that had called it into being. The period's own courts, when the cases reached them, described the consequence rather than any defect of drafting: the machinery of review that preventive detention law had carried since 1950 had been switched off at the centre of the system, and what remained was the detention order itself.

Alongside MISA stood a second detention statute with a narrower field and a longer life. The Conservation of Foreign Exchange and Prevention of Smuggling Activities Act, 1974, Act 52 of 1974, assented to on 13 December 1974 and brought into force on 19 December 1974, provided for preventive detention in the service of two objects named in its long title: the conservation and augmentation of foreign exchange, and the prevention of smuggling. Its detention heads tracked the smuggling economy: engaging in smuggling, abetting it, transporting, concealing or keeping smuggled goods, harbouring persons engaged in smuggling, and dealing in smuggled goods, together with conduct prejudicial to foreign exchange. An order could be made by the central or a state government or by specially empowered officers where satisfied the detention was necessary to prevent the person so acting. It had been prepared in the months before the proclamation, replacing a temporary enlargement of MISA itself, and it was not an Emergency measure in origin. Its presence in the period's record is for the contrast its fate supplies: of the two detention codes of June 1975, the one aimed at smugglers and exchange offenders was used through the period and survived the corrective settlement intact, while the general security Act was repealed. The preventive detention guide examines the statute in its own right; here it marks the boundary of what the post-Emergency Parliament was and was not willing to dismantle.

Scale belongs in the record, stated as the record states it. Detention under MISA and under the Defence and Internal Security framework during the period was not a matter of a handful of cases. The official inquiry appointed after the period, the Shah Commission, examined the detention programme as one of its central subjects, and the record it assembled, together with the figures placed before Parliament in the corrective years, put the numbers detained under the two instruments in the tens of thousands across the twenty-one months. The detained, on that record, were drawn from the opposition parties' leaderships and cadres down to district level, from trade unions and student organisations, from the press, and from persons held under the economic offences code; the programme's reach across categories is what distinguishes it in the record from a targeted security operation. Many of those held were detained for short periods; many orders were revoked or ran out; the figure is a measure of how far the statutory scheme reached into ordinary political and trade union life, and it is the scale, rather than any single order, that the corrective legislation of 1977 and 1978 was answering. The article returns to the Commission's findings below; at this point the figure does one job, which is to fix the detention code as the period's principal statute in practice, whatever the amendments' greater fame.

The ordinary scheme of the Act, against which the Emergency amendments should be measured, ran as follows. Section 3 empowered the central government, a state government, and officers authorised for the purpose, including district magistrates and commissioners of police, to direct detention where satisfied it was necessary to prevent the person acting prejudicially to the Act's heads. Section 8 required the grounds of the order to be communicated to the detainee, ordinarily within five days of the detention, with a right to make a representation to the government against the order. The Advisory Board machinery of sections 9 to 11 required cases to be placed before a board constituted of persons who were, had been, or were qualified to be judges of a High Court, and detention beyond the initial period depended on the board's opinion that sufficient cause existed. Section 13 capped the maximum period of detention under a confirmed order at twelve months. It is this scheme, recognisably descended from the Preventive Detention Act of 1950 and framed by Article 22, that foreign and domestic commentary of the early 1970s described; the amendments of 1975 and 1976 suspended its central parts for the class of detentions the period was actually making, while leaving the scheme on the page for detentions outside section 16A.

The two amending Acts worked differently and should not be merged. The 1975 amendment, Act 39 of 1975, made its changes effective from 29 June 1975, four days after the proclamation: it stiffened the Act at the moment the detention programme began. The 1976 amendment, Act 14 of 1976, then built the section 16A regime and, by giving it retrospective effect from 29 June 1975, aligned the harsher scheme's legal commencement with the programme's actual start rather than with its own later passage. Retrospectivity of that kind is a recurring drafting device of the period; it appears in the Election Laws (Amendment) Act, in the deemed date of the repeal of the parliamentary proceedings protection Act, and in the repealing Act for the objectionable matter statute, and the style section below collects the instances. For the detainee the consequence of the 1976 retrospectivity was precise: a detention made in the summer of 1975 under the scheme as it then stood was governed, when challenged, by a section enacted the following year as if it had always applied.

## The press in law: censorship by order, control by statute

The period's press law had two layers, and separating them explains much of its operation. The working layer, from June 1975 onwards, was pre-censorship under the order S.O. 275(E) and the Defence and Internal Security of India Rules: a regime of prior submission, in which what could be printed was controlled before publication and offences lay in printing without approval. The statutory layer arrived with the Prevention of Publication of Objectionable Matter Act, 1976, Act 27 of 1976, which took the place of an Ordinance of 1975, Ordinance 28 of 1975, and gave the control a parliamentary form. The Act defined objectionable matter broadly, covering matter that was scurrilous, and matter tending to bring into hatred or contempt, or to excite disaffection towards, the government, the President and other constitutional authorities, together with matter promoting disharmony between groups. Its machinery ran against the physical apparatus of publication: a press or the premises where it worked could be proceeded against, security could be demanded from a newspaper or press, and forfeiture of publications and closure of the offending enterprise stood behind the demand. The pattern was the colonial pattern, refreshed: control the machine and the deposit, and the content largely controls itself.

A second press-related measure of the period worked by subtraction. Since independence, the truth of parliamentary reporting had been protected by statute, the Parliamentary Proceedings (Protection of Publication) Act, 1956, which shielded publication of substantially true reports of the proceedings of Parliament unless made with malice. That Act was repealed during the period, by Act 28 of 1976 with effect deemed from 8 December 1975, leaving contemporaneous reporting of a Parliament that was itself operating under censorship without the statutory protection it had carried for twenty years. The repeal is one of the period's quieter entries, and it belongs in the record because its reversal was also quiet and permanent in form: protection for substantially true reports of legislative proceedings was restored after the period by a statute of 1977, and the principle was then written into the Constitution itself as Article 361A by the Forty-fourth Amendment. The protection a reader of parliamentary reports relies on without noticing it is, in its constitutional form, a corrective measure of this settlement.

The two layers together let the period's defenders and critics each state a true proposition, which is why the article keeps them apart. Censorship in its daily working form rested on an executive order under a wartime statute, made on the first day and never laid before Parliament as a Bill; control of objectionable matter, by contrast, was enacted by Parliament in 1976 in the ordinary legislative form, and repealed by Parliament, as recorded below, in 1977. The distinction matters to the era reading: the press regime was not one act of power but a stack of instruments of different legal weights, and the corrective settlement dismantled the stack layer by layer.

A third press institution disappeared in the same season. The Press Council of India, the statutory body of editors and journalists first constituted under the Press Council Act, 1965 to safeguard the freedom of the press and maintain its standards, was abolished during the period: the 1965 Act was repealed in 1976, and the Council ceased to exist. It was restored by fresh legislation, the Press Council Act, 1978, and reconstituted under it. The abolition and restoration belong in the article because they complete the press record in the same two-move pattern as the rest of the period: an institution that might have recorded and protested against the control of publication was removed while the control operated, and rebuilt by the Parliament that dismantled the control. The Council's absence during the period also explains a feature of the surviving record: the censorship years are documented principally by the censors' orders, by the Shah Commission's volumes, and by the newspapers' own archives, rather than by any standing regulator's reports, because there was no standing regulator to write them.

The objectionable matter Act's machinery should be distinguished from the censorship order's, since the two are often run together. Censorship under S.O. 275(E) operated before publication, on manuscripts and proofs, through an administrative staff. The 1976 Act operated on publications and enterprises: objectionable matter, once identified, exposed the newspaper to a demand for security, the security and the offending publication to forfeiture, and the press itself to proceedings, with the threat of closure behind them. One regime licensed the text; the other disciplined the publisher's balance sheet and plant. A newspaper of the period had to satisfy both, which is why the formal repeal of the Act in April 1977, though promptly done, is not the whole of the press's liberation story: the censorship order had already lapsed with the proclamation it served, and the two instruments ended by different legal routes, one by revocation of the parent proclamation and the other by an Act of repeal.

## The Forty-second Amendment: the period's constitutional centre

The Constitution (Forty-second Amendment) Act, 1976 was the period's largest single instrument, and it is the one the period is remembered by. The government appointed a committee under Sardar Swaran Singh to study the Constitution in the light of experience and propose changes; the amendment gave effect to the committee's recommendations. It was passed by the Lok Sabha on 2 November 1976 by 366 votes to 4 and by the Rajya Sabha on 11 November 1976, and it received the President's assent on 18 December 1976. Its commencement was staged: the bulk of the Act came into force on 3 January 1977, some sections on 1 February 1977, and one section on 1 April 1977. The instrument is examined provision by provision in the article on [the Forty-second Amendment explained](/2010/02/15/42nd-amendment-act-1976-explained/) in this series; the era treatment groups its changes by the work each group did, because the grouping is what the corrective settlement answered.

The first group spoke in the language of ideals. The Preamble gained three words: India became a sovereign socialist secular democratic republic, and the unity of the nation became the unity and integrity of the nation. A new Part IVA inserted Article 51A, a list of ten fundamental duties of citizens, addressed to conduct rather than enforceability. New directive principles followed the same direction: Article 39A on free legal aid, Article 43A on the participation of workers in the management of industries, Article 48A on the protection of the environment, forests and wildlife, and a recast Article 39(f) on the healthy development of children. The statements made for these provisions described them as spelling out what the Constitution had always implied. Their legal weight was modest; their place in the record is that none of them was ever reversed, a fact the table below sets out and the closing sections examine.

The second group altered the balance between the parts of the Constitution. The original Article 31C, enacted in 1971 and upheld in Kesavananda in its limited form, protected laws giving effect to Articles 39(b) and 39(c), the distribution principles, from attack under Articles 14 and 19. Section 4 of the Forty-second Amendment widened that protection to laws giving effect to any directive principle at all. Section 55 then addressed the amending power itself: it inserted clauses (4) and (5) in Article 368, declaring that no amendment of the Constitution made under that article could be called in question in any court on any ground, and that there was no limitation whatever on the constituent power of Parliament. This was the direct answer to Kesavananda. Where the Thirty-eighth Amendment had shielded exercises of power, section 55 proposed to remove the limit on the power to change the text, which would in turn have made every other shield unnecessary. It is the most consequential item of the whole record, and its fate was different from the rest: no Parliament repealed it. It was struck down by the Supreme Court in 1980, and the corrective settlement's account is incomplete without that judgment, which is treated in its own section below.

The third group reworked the courts. A new Article 31D saved laws made with respect to anti-national activities from invalidity on fundamental rights grounds, defining its key expression widely enough to reach associations declared unlawful. New articles in the judiciary chapters barred the Supreme Court from considering the constitutional validity of a state law in an Article 32 petition (Article 32A), concentrated questions of central law in the Supreme Court (Article 131A), restricted the striking down of laws by requiring special majorities on the bench (Article 144A), barred the High Courts from considering the validity of a central law (Article 226A) and required similar special majorities there (Article 228A). Article 226 itself, the High Courts' writ jurisdiction, the ordinary citizen's remedy for everything from a tax demand to a custody question, was narrowed: its heads were recast, interim orders were restricted, and the power under Article 227 over tribunals was cut. The design was coherent. Fundamental rights claims against the Union's laws would be heard, if at all, in one court, under procedural conditions that made invalidation harder; the High Courts would retreat to a local and administrative role.

The fourth group rebuilt the federal and institutional frame. Part XIVA introduced Articles 323A and 323B, empowering Parliament and the state legislatures to create tribunals for service matters, taxation, and a listed range of subjects, and permitted such laws to exclude the jurisdiction of all courts except the Supreme Court under Article 136, which in practice removed the High Courts from whole fields of disputes. Article 257A empowered the Union to deploy its armed or police forces in a state to deal with a grave situation of law and order. In the Seventh Schedule, five subjects were transferred from the State List to the Concurrent List: education, forests, weights and measures other than the establishment of standards, protection of wild animals and birds, and the administration of justice, meaning the constitution and organisation of all courts except the Supreme Court and the High Courts; the full mechanics of the lists are traced in the series account of [the Seventh Schedule lists explained](/2018/09/15/seventh-schedule-lists-explained/). The delimitation of constituencies and the allocation of seats were frozen by reference to the 1971 census until the twenty-first century opened. Article 312 was amended to make room for an All-India Judicial Service. Article 311 lost, for civil servants facing dismissal, the second opportunity to make a representation against the penalty proposed.

The fifth group settled political terms and the presidency. The normal duration of the Lok Sabha and the state assemblies was raised from five years to six. Article 74 was amended to provide expressly that the President shall act in accordance with the advice of the Council of Ministers, removing a silence the text had previously kept on whether the advice bound. The period for which a proclamation under Article 356, President's rule in a state, could be continued on each approval was lengthened from six months to one year, and a proclamation of Emergency could be issued for a part of the territory as well as the whole. The requirement of a quorum in Parliament and the state legislatures was omitted. Each item was defensible in isolation as an administrative adjustment; together they lengthened the tenure of every sitting legislature, bound the head of state in terms, and eased the Union's supervision of the states, which is why the era reading treats the amendment as a single act of institutional reweighting rather than as a miscellany.

One further feature of the instrument belongs in the record, because the corrective settlement inherited it. The Forty-second Amendment was passed by a Lok Sabha whose own term had been extended beyond the five years for which it was elected, and with a significant part of the parliamentary opposition in detention or absent under the period's conditions. Those are facts of the legislative history, recorded in the debates and in the inquiry that followed, and the article states them as such; the legal validity of the amendment was never in issue on that account, and the settlement of 1977 to 1979 proceeded by repealing or amending its provisions through the ordinary process, not by denying that it had been law.

Two formal points complete the instrument's portrait. Several of its provisions, those touching the Seventh Schedule, the judiciary chapters and the representation provisions listed in the proviso to Article 368, required ratification by the legislatures of not less than half the states, and the amendment was ratified accordingly; the transfers of subjects discussed above rest on that ratification as much as on the parliamentary votes. And the assent of 18 December 1976 was given by President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed, the same President who had signed the proclamation of 25 June 1975 on the Prime Minister's recommendation. The record contains no constitutional irregularity at either point; both acts were done in the forms prescribed. The era framework insists on the point because the period's lesson is often misstated as a lesson about forms being broken. They were not broken. They were used, completely and in order, which is a harder lesson and the correct one.

The detail of the judicial changes also deserves one further pass, since the Forty-third Amendment's deletions can otherwise read as a mere list. The narrowed Article 226 came with procedural fencing: interim orders in matters touching public revenue, infrastructure and the like were hedged with conditions, including notice to the affected party, and applications for interim relief were to be disposed of within short fixed periods. Article 227's superintendence over tribunals was withdrawn for the tribunals the amendment contemplated. The bar articles then closed the exits: a litigant who could not persuade a High Court to entertain a challenge to a central law under the recast Article 226 found the Supreme Court's own hands tied by Article 32A for state laws and by Article 144A's bench-majority rules for any law at all. The scheme's coherence is its most striking feature on a cold reading. It was not a tantrum against particular judges; it was a jurisdictional settlement, drafted by lawyers, under which the ordinary run of constitutional litigation against the Union would have had no forum with both the power and the procedure to grant relief. The civil service change ran the same way: removing the second representation under Article 311(2) did not abolish the inquiry or the first hearing; it removed the delinquent officer's right to be heard on the penalty, the stage at which disproportion was ordinarily corrected.

The All-India Judicial Service provision belongs to the residue rather than to the settlement, and it is flagged here because the never-reversed section below does not list it. The amendment of Article 312 cleared the constitutional path for such a service; no service was created under it during the period, and the provision stands in the text as an enabling entry the Forty-fourth Amendment had no occasion to disturb. It forms, with the unnotified Article 22 amendment, a pair of bookends for the period's study: in one, Parliament enabled a reform that no executive has carried out; in the other, Parliament enacted a safeguard that no executive has switched on. Enabling words and enacted words alike wait on commencement and creation, and the period teaches the waiting in both directions.

## The courts during the period

The judiciary's record during the period has two entries, and they point in opposite directions. The first is Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain, already recorded: a constitutional bench, sitting while the Emergency operated, struck down a clause of a fresh amendment on basic structure grounds and refused to let the legislature decide an election dispute to which the head of the government was a party. The second is Additional District Magistrate, Jabalpur v Shivkant Shukla, (1976) 2 SCC 521, the habeas corpus case, decided on 28 April 1976 by a bench of five judges: Ray, Chief Justice, and Beg, Chandrachud and Bhagwati, Justices, in the majority, with Khanna, Justice, dissenting. Between the two judgments the period's constitutional position was stated completely. The amending power had a limit the Court would enforce; the detained citizen, in the majority's view, had no remedy the Court could give.

The habeas corpus litigation began in the High Courts. Persons detained under MISA, and relatives petitioning on their behalf, filed writ petitions in several High Courts across the country notwithstanding the Article 359 order. A series of High Courts held that the petitions remained maintainable: the suspension order barred enforcement of the articles it named, but a detention could still be attacked on grounds that survived it, including that the order was not under the Act, was passed by an authority without power, or was mala fide. The State carried the question to the Supreme Court, and the appeals were heard together.

The majority's reasoning, as the case is examined in the article devoted to it in this series, ran through the suspension order and the statute together. Article 21, the judgment held, was the sole repository of the right to life and personal liberty; with the enforcement of Article 21 suspended by the Presidential order, no person had a standing to move a writ petition for habeas corpus challenging a detention as illegal or mala fide, because every such challenge was, in substance, a claim to enforce Article 21. The Court also upheld the validity of section 16A(9) of MISA against the challenge brought to it. The consequence for the detention code was immediate: between the statute's own removal of boards and grounds, and the judgment's removal of the writ, a person detained under section 16A had no forum at any point of the process. Justice Khanna's dissent held the contrary position, that a suspension of the enforcement of fundamental rights did not suspend the rule of law itself, that the right to life and liberty did not depend for its existence on Article 21, and that courts retained the power to ask whether a detention was authorised by the statute under which it purported to be made. The dissent had no operative effect in 1976. It is the part of the judgment the later law has treated as its surviving voice, for reasons the section on the judicial unwinding records.

For the era reading, the two judgments fix the limits of two opposite simplifications. The period was not one in which the courts simply surrendered: the same institution had, five months earlier, applied the basic structure doctrine against a sitting government's amendment, and the High Courts that heard the habeas petitions first had mostly ruled for the petitioners. Nor was the period one in which judicial review quietly preserved liberty against the executive: the judgment that counted, at the summit, in the class of cases the Emergency existed to create, held that no remedy lay. The corrective settlement was drafted with both halves of that record in view, which is why so much of it consists not of exhortations but of machinery: majorities, time limits, written advice, and a clause making two articles unsuspendable for any reason.

The distribution of judicial opinion before the Supreme Court spoke is itself part of the record. The habeas petitions were not the eccentricity of a single bench: decisions of nine High Courts had held, in substance, that the petitions survived the suspension order, whether because the challenge went to the detention's compliance with MISA itself, or because mala fides and want of authority were questions no suspension order could close. The appeals that reached the Supreme Court therefore asked the Court to displace the settled view of most of the country's constitutional courts, and the majority displaced it. The judgment's treatment of the alternative framings completed the displacement: a petition that presented itself as founded on the statute, or on the general law, rather than on Article 21, was in the majority's analysis still in substance a claim to personal liberty, and so within the bar. It is that step, the conversion of every route into the suspended article, that the dissent refused, and that later courts fastened on when the case came to be reassessed.

The period also left the judiciary an institutional wound that the legislative record only partly shows. Two supersessions of senior judges in appointments to the office of Chief Justice of India, in 1973 and again in January 1977, bracket the period and were understood within the legal profession as connected with the judgments the government of the day had received; Justice Khanna's supersession in 1977 followed his dissent in the habeas corpus case, and he resigned. Appointments are executive acts rather than legislation, and they are noted here in a sentence because the corrective settlement addressed the judiciary's position by restoring its jurisdiction, not its conventions of appointment; the conventions were rebuilt by practice and, much later, by the collegium case law, outside this article's period.

## The end of the period: revocation and election

The period ended in the same legal form in which it had begun, by instruments under the Constitution. In January 1977 the Fifth Lok Sabha was dissolved, still inside its first extended term. A general election was held in March 1977, with political prisoners released and censorship lifted for the campaign. The Congress party lost the election, and a Janata Party government took office under Morarji Desai. On 21 March 1977 the proclamation of 25 June 1975 stood revoked, and the proclamation of 3 December 1971, which had run beneath everything for more than five years, was revoked in the same closing of the period. The Article 359 order fell with the proclamations. Article 19 revived, the courts reopened to the detainees who remained in custody, and the detention orders ran out or were withdrawn in the weeks that followed.

### How did the general election of March 1977 change the legislative position?

It replaced the government and gave the Janata Party a large majority in the Lok Sabha, but it left the Congress with a majority in the Rajya Sabha. The corrective programme therefore had to be negotiated through a House the new government did not control, which is written into the final shape of the Forty-fourth Amendment.

The division of the Houses is the key to the corrective settlement's texture. Constitutional amendment requires a special majority in each House separately. The Janata majority in the Lok Sabha could pass the rollback Bills; the Rajya Sabha could and did refuse parts of them. When the Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Bill reached its final form in 1978, five of its clauses had been rejected by the Rajya Sabha and were dropped rather than allowed to defeat the whole. Among the wider proposals that did not survive the passage were a referendum requirement for future amendments touching fundamental rights and kindred matters, and the restoration of the right to property as a fundamental right. The settlement that exists is therefore a negotiated text, and its gaps are not oversights: they are the points at which the two Houses of 1978 disagreed. The point generalises across the series' reading of corrective legislation: what a rollback contains is a function of the parliamentary arithmetic of its moment, not only of the intentions of its authors.

The new Parliament's programme had a settled order. First came repeal of the period's ordinary statutes and restoration of the courts' jurisdiction, much of it achievable by the Constitution (Forty-third Amendment) Act, 1977, introduced in the Lok Sabha by the Law Minister, Shanti Bhushan, on 16 December 1977. Then came the deeper redesign in the Forty-fourth Amendment. Alongside the legislation, the executive appointed the inquiry whose findings would supply the period's official record: the Shah Commission, appointed on 28 May 1977. Legislation, investigation and, in 1979, prosecution were the settlement's three tracks, and each is taken in turn. One negative decision of the settlement belongs with the programme, because it shaped the third track: Parliament enacted no indemnity for acts done under the period's authority. No statute forgave the detentions, the censorship or the administrative directions of 1975 to 1977 in advance of examination. Accountability was left to run the other way, through a commission of inquiry with power to report and, where the reports disclosed offences, through courts constituted for the purpose. The absence of an indemnity Act is easy to pass over in the lists, and it is among the settlement's most deliberate features.

The forms of the ending mirror the forms of the beginning and repay a moment's attention. A proclamation under Article 352 is revoked by a subsequent proclamation, and the revocations of March 1977 were instruments of the same legal family as the proclamations of 1971 and 1975. The new Lok Sabha, the Sixth, met for the first time on 25 March 1977. The government's legislative queue then ran in the order this article follows: the objectionable matter repeal within the month, the Shah Commission's appointment in May, the Forty-third Amendment Bill in December. The sequence was not improvised. It moved from the statutes that touched daily administration, through the inquiry that would fix the record, to the constitutional text, and it spent its scarcest resource, the special majority in a divided Parliament, last and on the deepest changes.

## The correction by amendment: the Forty-third and Forty-fourth Amendments

The Constitution (Forty-third Amendment) Act, 1977, passed by both Houses in 1977, received assent on 13 April 1978 and came into force the same day, was the surgical instrument of the settlement. Its operative provisions omitted the court-curbing articles the Forty-second Amendment had inserted: Articles 31D, 32A, 131A, 144A, 226A and 228A were omitted from the Constitution, and Article 226 was substantially restored to its earlier width. With those omissions the jurisdictional map of 1975 was re-established: the High Courts recovered their authority over central laws and tribunals, the Supreme Court's special conditions for striking down statutes disappeared, and the saving for laws against anti-national activities, under which no statute had in fact been enacted, left the text without having been used. The Forty-third Amendment is short, and its brevity is the measure of how much of the Forty-second's judicial scheme could be undone simply by deleting what had been inserted two years before. The restoration of Article 226 deserves a sentence of its own, because the writ jurisdiction is the point at which the constitutional text touches daily administration. The Forty-second Amendment had fenced the article's exercise, restricting interim relief and, through Articles 226A and 131A, removing the validity of central laws from the High Courts' cognisance; the Forty-third Amendment removed the fences, and the article resumed the width under which a writ lies for the enforcement of any of the fundamental rights and for any other purpose. Every detained person released on a habeas petition after 1977, every trader challenging a seizure and every student challenging an admission list in a High Court since, has used the article in the width the Forty-third Amendment restored; the corrective settlement's most consequential single clause, measured by citations in the reports, is a restoration.

The Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1978 did the structural work, and the article on [the Forty-fourth Amendment explained](/2010/03/01/44th-amendment-act-1978-explained/) in this series carries its full provision-by-provision account. Its emergency provisions rewrote the conditions under which the period's opening move could ever be repeated. In Article 352, the ground of internal disturbance was replaced by armed rebellion, a ground that requires organised violence rather than political unrest. A proclamation can be issued only when the decision of the Union Cabinet is communicated to the President in writing. Approval by Parliament must come within one month, and by a special majority, a majority of the total membership of each House and two-thirds of members present and voting; continuance beyond six months requires fresh resolutions passed the same way. The Lok Sabha gained a power of disapproval: a resolution disapproving a proclamation or its continuance obliges the President to revoke it, and one-tenth of the members of the House can requisition a sitting for that purpose by notice. The finality clauses the Thirty-eighth Amendment had planted in Articles 123, 213 and 352 were deleted, restoring in terms the courts' power to examine what the text had told them not to question.

A dating point in the instrument confuses students every year, and it is worth answering in place.

### Why is the Forty-fourth Amendment called an Act of 1978 when its main provisions commenced in 1979?

Because an amendment Act is named for the year in which Parliament passed it. The Bill was passed by both Houses in 1978, the President's assent came on 30 April 1979, and the main provisions were brought into force by notification on 20 June 1979; the short title keeps the year of its passage.

On rights, the Forty-fourth Amendment redrew the suspension scheme. Article 359 was amended so that a Presidential order cannot suspend the right to move a court for the enforcement of Articles 20 and 21: the protections against retrospective penal laws and double jeopardy, and the right to life and personal liberty, remain enforceable in court during any emergency, on the text itself, without dependence on judicial construction. Article 358 was confined correspondingly: Article 19 stands suspended automatically only where the emergency is declared on the ground of war or external aggression, not armed rebellion, and emergency legislation sheltered by the article must recite its connection with the proclamation. The remedy that failed in 1976 was thus re-founded in the text, an answer drafted directly against the majority reasoning in the habeas corpus case, which had rested on the suspendability of Article 21.

The same Act undid the period's political and institutional adjustments where Parliament agreed they should be undone. The duration of the Lok Sabha and the assemblies returned to five years. The President received, by a proviso to Article 74(1), the power to require the Council of Ministers to reconsider its advice once, remaining bound by the advice tendered after reconsideration; the binding character the Forty-second Amendment had given the advice was kept, and tempered rather than reversed. Article 31, the right to property, and Article 19(1)(f) were omitted from Part III, and property took its place as a constitutional and legal right under a new Article 300A, which provides that no person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law. Article 329A, the election-dispute article of 1975, was omitted, and the position of Article 71 for disputes over presidential and vice-presidential elections was restored. Article 361A was inserted, protecting publication of substantially true reports of the proceedings of Parliament and the state legislatures. The Ninth Schedule entry that had sheltered MISA was omitted. Among the reversals, the property change stands apart in one respect: it was itself a permanent constitutional change carried by the corrective amendment, not a restoration of the earlier text, and property has remained outside Part III ever since.

Two smaller entries of the Act complete its account and are often dropped from summaries. The Forty-fourth omitted clause (1A) of Article 359, the clause the Thirty-eighth Amendment had inserted to put the suspension orders beyond question, so the shield came out of the suspension article as it had already come out of the proclamation and ordinance articles. And it inserted Article 134A, a procedural provision on certificates for appeal to the Supreme Court from High Court judgments, a housekeeping entry of the kind the period's amendments also contain and a reminder that even a corrective amendment is a working statute book document, not a manifesto. The Act's commencement was itself staged by notification, the main provisions beginning on 20 June 1979; the commencement device, and the one section that never received its day, are the subject of the section on the safeguard that was never switched on.

The Forty-fourth Amendment also worked on President's rule, in a provision summaries often miss. A proclamation under Article 356 had, since the Forty-second Amendment, been continuable for a year at a time on each approval. The Forty-fourth kept the year's span and fenced its use beyond the first year: a resolution continuing President's rule beyond one year cannot be passed unless a proclamation of Emergency is in operation in India or in the state concerned, and the Election Commission certifies that continuance is necessary on account of difficulties in holding a general election to the state assembly. The design is the emergency scheme's design repeated in miniature: the power is retained, the period is left long, and the conditions for reaching the longer period are made factual and external, resting on a certificate from an institution outside the government of the day. Article 358's recital requirement belongs to the same family: a law sheltering under the suspension of Article 19 must recite that it is made in relation to the proclamation, so that the shelter is claimed openly on the face of the statute rather than assumed in argument. The settlement's drafting, taken as a whole, prefers visible conditions to good intentions, and it is the preference, as much as any single clause, that distinguishes 1978's text from 1975's.

## The correction by repeal, inquiry and prosecution

Two of the period's statutes were repealed outright. The Prevention of Publication of Objectionable Matter Act was repealed by Act 14 of 1977: the repeal Bill was introduced in the Lok Sabha by the Minister for Information and Broadcasting, L. K. Advani, on 31 March 1977, within days of the new government's formation, and the repealing Act followed on 18 April 1977. The Maintenance of Internal Security Act was repealed by the Maintenance of Internal Security (Repeal) Act, 1978, Act 27 of 1978. With the repeal, the general security detention code that had run from 1971, and in its Emergency form from June 1975, left the statute book. Preventive detention did not leave with it. COFEPOSA remained in force, and Parliament's later practice, in statutes treating narcotics, organised crime and national security, continued to use detention as an instrument within the Article 22 frame; but the particular Act, identified in public life with the period, was ended by name.

The inquiry track ran on a statutory footing. The Shah Commission was appointed on 28 May 1977 under section 3 of the Commissions of Inquiry Act, 1952, chaired by Justice J. C. Shah, a former Chief Justice of India. Its terms required it to inquire into allegations of abuse of authority, excesses and malpractices in the wake of the proclamation of 25 June 1975, into the circumstances in which the proclamation was made, and into connected matters. The Commission submitted two interim reports and a third and final report on 6 August 1978; the reports were laid before Parliament on 31 August 1978, and the government of the day accepted their findings. Among its conclusions, recorded here as the Commission's, were that the decision to proclaim the Emergency had been taken without the consultation of the Cabinet the Constitution's conventions required, and that the circumstances did not justify the proclamation; and its volumes assembled the documentary record of detention, censorship and administrative direction on which accounts of the period, including this article's, continue to rest. A researcher assembling that record for publication, charges, denials, inquiry findings and official replies, is handling material whose sources may still need protection, and the working habits journalists use for that task are described in the account of [how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/). The Commission itself reported; it could not prosecute, and its findings fed the third track.

Prosecution was attempted through a dedicated statute. Before the Bill was enacted, the President referred it to the Supreme Court for an advisory opinion under Article 143, and the Court, in In re Special Courts Bill, 1978, (1979) 1 SCC 380, upheld the proposal with modifications, on the footing that a classification of offences by the period of their commission and the office held by the offender was open to Parliament. Parliament then enacted the Special Courts Act, 1979, Act 22 of 1979, assented to on 16 May 1979. The Act provided for special courts, each presided over by a sitting judge of a High Court, appointed on the terms the reference had settled, for the speedy trial of offences committed by persons who had held high public or political office during the Emergency, of the classes the commissions of inquiry had reported on. Courts were constituted and proceedings were set in motion under it. They did not proceed to judgment: the change of government in January 1980 overtook the process, and the prosecutions were not pursued to a conclusion. The episode closes the settlement's third track on an inconclusive note that the record should state plainly: of the three corrective instruments, amendment, repeal and inquiry all completed their work, and prosecution did not.

## The correction by judgment: what the courts removed after 1977

The settlement had a judicial component, because two items at the centre of the Forty-second Amendment were never touched by Parliament at all. Section 4, the widening of Article 31C to all directive principles, and section 55, the insertion of clauses (4) and (5) in Article 368 declaring amendments unreviewable and the constituent power unlimited, stood in the text when the Janata government fell and stood there still when the Supreme Court considered them in Minerva Mills v Union of India, (1980) 3 SCC 625. A constitution bench struck down both sections. The reasoning returned to first principles: a limited amending power is itself part of the basic structure, so Parliament could not use the power to make itself unlimited; and the widened Article 31C, by giving every directive principle precedence over Articles 14 and 19, destroyed the balance between Parts III and IV which the Court treated as structural. Article 31C accordingly survives in the form Kesavananda had upheld, protecting laws for Articles 39(b) and 39(c). Any account that credits the Forty-fourth Amendment with reversing the whole of the Forty-second is wrong at its most important point: the reversal of the amending-power clauses was judicial, delivered in 1980, and the corrective Parliament had left those clauses standing.

The years after 1977 also rebuilt the substantive law the suspension order had emptied. In Maneka Gandhi v Union of India, decided in January 1978 by a bench of seven judges, on a challenge to the impounding of a passport under the Passports Act, 1967, the Supreme Court read Article 21 afresh, holding that a procedure depriving a person of life or personal liberty must be right, just and fair and not arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive, and that Articles 14, 19 and 21 are not mutually exclusive but operate together, so that a law depriving a person of liberty must satisfy all three. The narrow reading of Article 21 on which the majority in the habeas corpus case had relied, under which the article was a self-contained code satisfied by any enacted procedure, did not survive the judgment in substance. The formal word on the case itself came four decades later. In Justice K. S. Puttaswamy (Retd) v Union of India, (2017) 10 SCC 1, the nine-judge bench that held privacy to be a fundamental right expressly overruled the majority judgments in the habeas corpus case, recording that they were seriously flawed, that life and liberty are not bounties conferred by the State but rights the Constitution recognises, and that Justice Khanna's dissent had stated the law correctly. The overruling is treated in the article on [the Puttaswamy privacy and Aadhaar judgment](/2019/05/15/puttaswamy-privacy-aadhaar-judgment/) in this series; in this article's sequence it stands as the judicial settlement's closing entry, the point at which the judgment of 28 April 1976 ceased to be authority for anything.

The Ninth Schedule question, which the period had used heavily, was settled in the same judicial phase. In Waman Rao v Union of India, (1981) 2 SCC 362, the Supreme Court upheld Article 31C in the form Kesavananda had approved and held that laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973, the date of the Kesavananda judgment, remained open to challenge on the ground that they damage the basic structure. I. R. Coelho v State of Tamil Nadu, in 2007, completed that position for the Schedule as a whole. The consequence for this article's table is the entry recorded there: the entries of 1975 and 1976 stand in the Schedule, but the immunity the period supposed it was conferring on them is qualified by the date line the courts drew through the middle of the decade. The settlement's judicial track thus reached all three of the period's defensive devices in turn: the finality clauses were deleted by Parliament, the unlimited amending power was struck down in Minerva Mills, and the Schedule shield was cut down to its pre-1973 dimensions by the Waman Rao line.

It is equally part of the record what the courts did not remove. No judgment has ever held the Preamble additions, the fundamental duties, the transferred subjects or Part XIVA to be unconstitutional, and later case law has put several of them to work: the duties in Part IVA and the environmental principle in Article 48A have been read into the environmental jurisprudence of the courts, and the tribunal articles have been treated as a permanent feature of the adjudicative landscape whose edges, not whose existence, required correction. The judicial track of the settlement was therefore conservative in the strict sense: it excised the provisions that disabled review and the claim of unlimited power, and it left the reconstruction's substance standing. Between them, Parliament and the courts divided the corrective labour exactly as the table shows, the legislature taking the rights provisions, the judiciary taking the power provisions, and neither taking the structural ones.

## The safeguard that was never switched on

The corrective settlement contains one item that was enacted, printed in the Constitution's annotated text, and never made law in force. Section 3 of the Forty-fourth Amendment Act proposed to amend Article 22, the preventive detention article, in two respects. The maximum period for which a person could be detained before the opinion of an Advisory Board had to be obtained would fall from three months to two. And the Advisory Board itself would be reconstituted: it would be formed in accordance with the recommendations of the Chief Justice of the appropriate High Court, chaired by a serving judge of that court, with members who were serving or retired judges of a High Court. The object was plain from the period's experience: the review body that section 16A of MISA had bypassed altogether would be strengthened, given a judicial spine, and set to work earlier in every detention. Parliament passed the amendment. Section 1(2) of the Act, as with many amendment Acts, left the commencement of its provisions to notification by the central government on appointed days.

The notification for section 3 was never issued. The annotated text of the Constitution in India Code records the position in a footnote on Article 22: section 3 of the Forty-fourth Amendment Act, by which the article was to be amended, is marked as the date yet to be notified. Successive governments of every composition have left the footnote undisturbed. Article 22 therefore continues to operate with the three-month period and the Advisory Board in its pre-1978 form, composed of persons who are, have been, or are qualified to be judges of a High Court, as the series' account of the [preventive detention law in India](/2010/06/01/preventive-detention-law-india-guide/) sets out in full. This account is legal information about a historical record, not advice on any detention matter, and the distinction matters here precisely because the unamended article remains live law in daily use.

### How does an amendment Act decide when its own provisions begin to operate?

By its commencement clause. An Act may commence on assent, on a date the Act itself names, or, as with both the Forty-second and the Forty-fourth Amendments, on days the government appoints by notification, which lets different sections begin on different dates or, in one instance, never begin at all.

The Forty-second Amendment used the same device without mishap: its sections began on three appointed days across the first months of 1977, and every one of them was notified. The Forty-fourth shows the device's other possibility. An appointed-day clause converts an Act of Parliament into a standing offer that the executive may accept at any time or not at all, and no court has compelled a government to issue a notification it declines to issue. The lesson the era contributes to the series' wider argument is exact: a safeguard is not the words passed by Parliament but the words in force, and between the two there stands, in this instance, a single unpublished notification that has been awaited since 1979.

The provision also clarifies what did not happen, which summaries sometimes blur. Article 22 was not left untouched by the settlement because Parliament forgot it: Parliament amended it in the very Act that did the rest of the work, and the amendment failed at the commencement stage, not the legislative one. Nor did the failure lie in drafting: the two-month period and the reconstituted Board are fully specified in section 3, needing nothing but a date. The episode therefore isolates, with unusual cleanliness, the one step in Indian lawmaking that no majority in Parliament can take for the executive: the step from an enacted text to an operative one, where the Act itself has lodged the choice with the government of the day. The anomaly stands in the official text itself, in a footnote any reader of Article 22 meets on the page; the series registers the provision as the standing example of its class, a safeguard whose entire history consists of not commencing.

## The legislative style of the period

Set side by side, the period's instruments display a recognisable drafting style, and the style is itself evidence for the era reading, because it shows a government working through the law's own devices rather than around them. Five devices recur. The first is the finality clause, the Thirty-eighth Amendment's signature: a declaration, inside the constitutional text, that a satisfaction shall not be questioned, which removes not a power but the forum for reviewing it. The second is the schedule as shield: the placement of statutes in the Ninth Schedule under Article 31B, used in 1975 for the security and election laws and in 1976 for the agrarian codes, converting a list into a wall. The third is retrospectivity: the Election Laws (Amendment) Act rewriting the rules of a decided case backwards, section 16A of MISA backdated to 29 June 1975, the repeal of the proceedings protection Act deemed effective from December 1975; in each instance the legal past was edited so that the operative facts would be judged by rules made after them. The fourth is the appointed-day commencement, benign in the Forty-second Amendment's three staged dates and fateful in the Forty-fourth's section 3. The fifth is amendment of the operating code rather than enactment of a new one: detention was rebuilt inside MISA, censorship inside the Defence of India rules, the courts' jurisdiction inside the articles amended; almost nothing of the period announces itself in a new statute with a new name, which is one reason the period is easy to underestimate from the statute book's spines. Behind several of the devices stood a sixth, the ordinance: the objectionable matter regime began as Ordinance 28 of 1975 before it became an Act, MISA itself had begun life as an Ordinance in May 1971, and the Thirty-eighth Amendment's decision to make ordinance satisfaction unquestionable shows the ordinance route being armour-plated at the same moment as the proclamation route, because the drafters understood that the two routes would be used interchangeably and reviewed, if at all, by the same courts.

The velocity of the sequence is the style's final trait. Proclamation to censorship order took a day; proclamation to the first amendment Bill took under a month; the habeas corpus answer, the displacement of nine High Courts, took ten months; the Forty-second Amendment, the largest single alteration of the Constitution ever enacted, passed both Houses in nine days of November 1976. The corrective sequence ran slower, because it had to: repeal in a month, amendment surgery within the year, the redesign across two sessions and two Houses with different majorities, the judicial removals in 1980 and the formal overruling four decades on. The asymmetry is not a criticism of the correction, whose pace was set by the forms it was honouring. It is the last quantitative fact of the record: restraints are dismantled faster than they are rebuilt, and any safeguard whose operation depends on several institutions acting in sequence will be tested, in a crisis, at the speed of the fastest institution against it.

## What was never reversed: the settlement's structural residue

Read the table by its verdicts and a pattern emerges that the closing sections of this article exist to state. The corrective settlement reversed the record at the points where the record had touched the citizen's procedural protections: the finality clauses went, the court-curbing articles went, the writ jurisdiction came back, Article 21 was put beyond suspension, the detention statute and the press statute were repealed, and the six-year terms and the election-dispute article were undone. What the settlement did not reverse is a different list, and it is short enough to name in full. The three words in the Preamble were left standing, and stand. The ten fundamental duties were left standing, and a Parliament of a later generation added an eleventh rather than removing the ten. The five subjects moved to the Concurrent List were left in the Concurrent List, where the large fields of education and forests are legislated on by the Union and the states together, and no amendment has moved them back. The tribunal provisions of Part XIVA were left standing, and became the foundation of the tribunal system that later decades built, with the courts reading down only the exclusion of their own review. The new directive principles of 1976, on legal aid, workers' participation and the environment, were likewise left untouched and have been cited in the case law ever since.

The claim this article leaves with the reader is therefore a specific one, not a lament and not a celebration: the corrective amendment reversed the rights provisions and left the structural ones. The reasons are visible in the record rather than mysterious. Reversing a structural change means re-opening a settled distribution of legislative power between the Union and the states, or dismantling institutions that have begun to operate; reversing a rights provision means deleting a clause and restoring a remedy, which is cheaper in every currency a legislature deals in. The Rajya Sabha arithmetic of 1978 played its part, but the residue has survived every Parliament since, under governments of every composition, which suggests the line the settlement drew was the line the political system found it could live with. The period's most quoted legacy, the Emergency's reputation, rests on the rights story. Its most durable legacy in the text of the Constitution rests on the structural one.

The series' threads run forward from the same residue. The Concurrent List that received the five subjects in 1976 is the list through which the Union's later legislative expansion, in education, environment and forests, has principally flowed. The tribunals created as a possibility in Part XIVA became the standing alternative forum of Indian administrative law, and their quarrels with the High Courts occupy the tribunal articles of later clusters. And the unnotified section 3 remains where the Forty-fourth Amendment left it, the file's earliest and clearest instance of a reform that exists in the amendment Act and nowhere else. The period's two decades of aftershocks, through Minerva Mills, the Article 21 reconstruction and finally the overruling in Puttaswamy, closed the rights story in the courts. No corresponding event has ever closed the structural one.

The durability of the residue can be traced in the later statute book, and the tracing is the brief's durability test applied to the period's own output. Education's transfer to the Concurrent List is the clearest thread: a generation later, the Constitution (Eighty-sixth Amendment) Act, 2002 wrote free and compulsory education for children into Article 21A and recast Article 45, and Parliament legislated the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 on the footing of the Concurrent entry the Forty-second Amendment had created. Forests followed the same channel: the Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980, a Union statute restricting the diversion of forest land, rests on the Concurrent entry for forests that dates from 1976, and the environmental code built since, on forests, wildlife and the environment, presupposes the transfers of that year. The tribunal thread runs from Part XIVA to the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985, enacted under Article 323A, and onward to the proliferation of specialised tribunals in taxation, company law, debt recovery and other fields, with the courts' correction in L. Chandra Kumar v Union of India, (1997) 3 SCC 261 confining itself, characteristically, to restoring the High Courts' review over tribunal decisions while leaving the tribunal structure in place. In each line the pattern repeats the settlement's own: the institution survives, the review point is re-established, and the 1976 architecture carries the load.

## The two assessments, as their proponents state them

The institutional record supports two assessments of the period, and the era framework of this series requires each to be stated in the terms of those who hold it, with the sources named, before the article gives its own narrower conclusion. The critical assessment rests on the Shah Commission's record and on the judgments recited above. In its terms, the period shows what a constitution looks like when every check operates formally and none operates in substance: proclamations approved by the Parliament the Constitution provided, amendments passed by the majorities the Constitution required, detentions ordered under a statute and upheld by the court of final appeal, and at the end of the process a citizen could be held without grounds disclosed, without a board, and without a remedy. Its proponents point to the habeas corpus majority and to Justice Khanna's dissent as the period in miniature, and to the Commission's conclusion that the proclamation itself was decided without Cabinet consultation and without circumstances that justified it. The lesson drawn is about design: safeguards that can be switched off by the power they guard against are not safeguards, and the 1978 settlement's unsuspendable articles are the acknowledgment of that lesson in the text.

The defence of the period, as stated by the government of the day in Parliament and in the instruments themselves, ran on the proclaimed ground and on the constitutional forms. The proclamation recited a threat to the security of India by internal disturbance; the Law Minister, moving the Thirty-eighth Amendment, presented its clauses as making explicit what the relevant articles had always meant; the Forty-second Amendment's statement of objects described its Preamble changes as spelling out expressly the high ideals the Constitution embodied, and its wider scheme as the considered product of a committee and of public debate. The defence also contains a proposition about the ending: when the government chose to hold a general election in March 1977, the electorate delivered its verdict against the Emergency at the first opportunity the system provided, and the verdict was accepted and power transferred. In the defence's terms, a system in which the ruling party can be, and is, voted out after such a period has demonstrated a self-correcting capacity that the critical assessment understates.

The article's own conclusion is narrower than either, and confined to the legislative record it has traced. That record shows a state entirely capable of dismantling its own restraints by lawful forms, a corrective Parliament that restored the restraints on liberty with great care and left the accompanying reconstruction of the state largely intact, and a single enacted safeguard that demonstrates the difference between passing a protection and bringing it into force. Candidates who learn the period as the record, amendment by amendment and repeal by repeal, hold the transferable skill the era is in the syllabus to teach: to ask of any safeguard not only what it says, but who must act to make it operate, and what happens if they do not.

For a reader applying the record forward, the period supplies a short checklist of signals, each visible in a text before it operates. A clause declaring an official satisfaction final and unquestionable is a signal, wherever it appears, because it removes the forum rather than arguing the merits. A safeguard whose commencement is left to notification is a signal, because the safeguard's existence then depends on the power it restrains. A wide saving clause, of the Article 31D kind, is a signal, because it waits for a statute to protect and tells the reader which statute is contemplated. A schedule used as a shield, a definition widened until it covers the conduct actually targeted, a detention statute whose grounds need not be disclosed: the period used each of these once, in forms that remain recognisable, and the corrective settlement's answers to each are equally recognisable and equally available to be copied. That recognisability, finally, is the era's claim on the reader who will never sit for an examination on it. The instruments of 1975 were not exotic. They were the ordinary devices of the statute book, pointed in one direction; the instruments of 1978 were the same devices, pointed back.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which laws were enacted during the Emergency?

The constitutional amendments were the Thirty-eighth, Thirty-ninth, Fortieth, Forty-first and Forty-second Amendments, of 1975 and 1976. The principal statutes were the Election Laws (Amendment) Act, 1975, which changed the election law retrospectively; the Maintenance of Internal Security (Amendment) Acts of 1975 and 1976, which rebuilt MISA around the new section 16A; the Prevention of Publication of Objectionable Matter Act, 1976; and the House of the People (Extension of Duration) Acts of 1976. Subordinate and repealing measures formed part of the same record, including the censorship order of 26 June 1975 and the repeal of the Parliamentary Proceedings (Protection of Publication) Act, 1956.

### Q: What did the 42nd Amendment change?

It changed the Constitution across five fields at once. It added "socialist", "secular" and "integrity" to the Preamble and inserted ten fundamental duties in a new Part IVA. It widened Article 31C to protect laws for any directive principle, and inserted clauses (4) and (5) in Article 368 declaring amendments unreviewable and the amending power unlimited. It curtailed the courts through Articles 31D, 32A, 131A, 144A, 226A and 228A and a narrowed Article 226. It created the tribunal scheme of Part XIVA, moved five subjects to the Concurrent List, raised legislative terms to six years, bound the President to ministerial advice, and lengthened Article 356 continuations.

### Q: What was MISA and what happened to it?

The Maintenance of Internal Security Act, 1971, Act 26 of 1971, was India's general preventive detention statute, the successor of the Preventive Detention Act of 1950. It empowered detention to prevent acts prejudicial to the security of India, the security of a state, public order and essential supplies and services. During the Emergency it was amended twice: the 1975 amendment and the Act of 1976, which inserted section 16A, under which grounds could be withheld, Advisory Board review was dispensed with for declared cases, and detention ran without the ordinary checks. The Thirty-ninth Amendment placed it in the Ninth Schedule. The Janata Parliament repealed the Act by Act 27 of 1978.

### Q: What did the courts hold during the Emergency?

Two holdings frame the period. In Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain in November 1975, the Supreme Court struck down clause (4) of Article 329A as damaging the basic structure, while upholding the election before it under the retrospectively amended election law. In the habeas corpus case, Additional District Magistrate, Jabalpur v Shivkant Shukla, (1976) 2 SCC 521, a majority of four to one held that with enforcement of Article 21 suspended, no detainee had standing to challenge a detention in court, Article 21 being the sole repository of life and liberty. Several High Courts had earlier held the petitions maintainable. Justice Khanna dissented in the Supreme Court.

### Q: Which provisions did the 44th Amendment restore or reverse?

It replaced internal disturbance with armed rebellion in Article 352, required written Cabinet advice, one-month approval by special majority, and Lok Sabha disapproval on requisition by one-tenth of its members. It made enforcement of Articles 20 and 21 non-suspendable under Article 359 and confined Article 358 to emergencies of war or external aggression. It deleted the Thirty-eighth Amendment's finality clauses, restored five-year terms, omitted Article 329A, and gave the President one return of ministerial advice. It removed the right to property from Part III, placing it in Article 300A, and inserted Article 361A protecting reports of legislative proceedings.

### Q: Which safeguard in the 44th Amendment was never brought into force?

The amendment of Article 22 contained in section 3 of the Act. It would have reduced the maximum detention without an Advisory Board's opinion from three months to two, and reconstituted the Board so that it was formed on the recommendations of the Chief Justice of the High Court, chaired by a serving judge of that court, with serving or retired judges as members. Section 1(2) of the Act left commencement to notification on appointed days. The notification for section 3 was never issued, and the footnote on Article 22 in the official text still records the position: the date is yet to be notified.

### Q: What did the Shah Commission examine?

The Commission, appointed on 28 May 1977 under section 3 of the Commissions of Inquiry Act, 1952 and chaired by Justice J. C. Shah, was asked to inquire into allegations of abuse of authority, excesses and malpractices committed in the wake of the proclamation of 25 June 1975, and into the circumstances in which the proclamation itself was made. Its work covered the detention programme, the operation of censorship, and the use of administrative machinery during the period. It submitted two interim reports and a third and final report on 6 August 1978, which were laid before Parliament on 31 August 1978 and whose findings the government accepted.

### Q: What does the Emergency show about constitutional safeguards?

It shows that a safeguard is machinery, not a sentiment. The rights in Part III were not repealed in 1975; their enforcement was switched off by an order made under the Constitution, and detention was placed beyond review by a statute Parliament enacted, so the protections failed at the level of forums, grounds and boards rather than at the level of text. The settlement of 1978 drew the conclusion in drafting: two articles were made non-suspendable in terms, the proclamation was surrounded with written advice, time limits and special majorities, and one further safeguard shows the remaining gap, since the stronger Advisory Board for detainees was enacted and never brought into force.

### Q: When was the Emergency proclaimed, on what ground, and when was it revoked?

The proclamation was issued on 25 June 1975. Its stated ground was that a grave emergency existed whereby the security of India was threatened by internal disturbance, the phrase Article 352 then used. It was laid before Parliament and approved by both Houses. It did not stand alone: the proclamation of 3 December 1971, issued on the ground of external aggression during the war with Pakistan, had never been revoked and remained in operation throughout. Both proclamations were revoked in March 1977, the 1975 proclamation being revoked on 21 March 1977, after the general election of that month had returned a new government.

### Q: How did the proclamation of 1975 differ in law from the proclamation of 1971 that was already in operation?

The machinery was the same and the occasion was different. The 1971 proclamation rested on external aggression, a ground tied to a war, and it carried with it the Defence of India Act and its rules as a wartime code. The 1975 proclamation rested on internal disturbance, a ground with no corresponding event to point to, and its operative orders were aimed at internal political life: the Article 359 order of 27 June 1975 suspending enforcement of Articles 14, 21 and 22, and the censorship order of the following day. The Thirty-eighth Amendment then expressly authorised the coexistence of proclamations on different grounds, putting the double emergency beyond argument.

### Q: What did the Thirty-eighth Amendment of 1975 do to the power of the courts to question proclamations and ordinances?

It removed the power in terms. The amendment inserted clauses into Articles 123, 213, 239B, 352, 356, 359 and 360 declaring the satisfaction of the President or the Governor to be final and conclusive and not questionable in any court on any ground. A court could therefore no longer ask whether a proclamation rested on relevant material, or whether an ordinance had been promulgated for a purpose the power did not allow, because the constitutional text forbade the question. The Forty-fourth Amendment deleted the clauses inserted in Articles 123, 213 and 352, and the finality device left the Constitution with them.

### Q: Why is it misleading to describe the Forty-second Amendment only through the words added to the Preamble?

Because the Preamble words were the least operative part of the instrument. They altered no jurisdiction, moved no subject and extended no tenure, while the same Act rewired the courts' powers, the federal lists, the length of legislatures and the amending power itself. The description also inverts the amendment's history: the words are remembered because they survived, but they survived precisely because the corrective settlement had no quarrel with them, whereas the operative provisions were repealed by the Forty-third Amendment, reversed by the Forty-fourth, or struck down in Minerva Mills. Studying the amendment through its most quoted sentence mistakes its decoration for its design.

### Q: What did the Forty-first Amendment of 1976 change, and why does it sit oddly among the amendments of the period?

It amended Article 316 to raise the retirement age of the chairman and members of a State Public Service Commission, and of a Joint Commission, from sixty years to sixty-two, and it received assent on 7 September 1976. It sits oddly because it is routine establishment business that any Parliament of any year might have passed, sandwiched in the numbering between the election-dispute amendment of 1975 and the Forty-second Amendment. Its oddity is instructive: a list of amendments assented to during the period is not a list of Emergency measures, and attributing it to the Emergency is dating by proximity instead of by substance.

### Q: Why did COFEPOSA survive the post-Emergency settlement when MISA was repealed?

The two statutes occupied different ground. COFEPOSA, Act 52 of 1974, had been enacted before the proclamation, in December 1974, against smuggling and foreign exchange offences, a field in which detention was defended as an economic enforcement tool rather than a security measure. Its scheme also retained the standard Article 22 architecture of grounds, representation and Advisory Board review, without a provision comparable to section 16A. The corrective Parliament repealed the statute identified with political detention and its Emergency form, and left the economic offences code standing. COFEPOSA remains in force, and the contrast fixes the settlement's boundary: it was a settlement about the general security power, not about preventive detention as such.

### Q: Under what legal authority was pre-censorship imposed on newspapers in June 1975?

By an executive order, not by a statute enacted for the purpose. Pre-censorship was imposed by the central government's order S.O. 275(E), dated 26 June 1975, made under Rule 48 of the Defence and Internal Security of India Rules. Those rules ran under the Defence of India Act, 1971, a wartime Act kept in operation by the unrevoked proclamation of 1971 and retitled the Defence and Internal Security of India Act with effect from 1 July 1975. A parliamentary statute for the press, the Prevention of Publication of Objectionable Matter Act, followed only in 1976. The distinction matters: the daily machinery of censorship rested on delegated legislation under a war statute from first to last.

### Q: What was the Prevention of Publication of Objectionable Matter Act, 1976, and what became of it?

It was Act 27 of 1976, replacing Ordinance 28 of 1975, and it gave the press controls a parliamentary form. It defined objectionable matter to include scurrilous matter and matter tending to excite disaffection towards the government and constitutional authorities or disharmony between groups, and it worked on the apparatus of publication: security could be demanded from newspapers and presses, publications could be forfeited, and the offending enterprise could be closed. It was among the first measures the new Parliament removed. The repeal Bill was introduced on 31 March 1977, and the Act was repealed by Act 14 of 1977, in force from 18 April 1977.

### Q: Was any law ever enacted under Article 31D before the article was omitted?

No statute was ever enacted under it. Article 31D, inserted by the Forty-second Amendment, was a saving provision: it protected laws made with respect to anti-national activities and associations declared unlawful from being held void for inconsistency with Articles 14, 19 and 31. A saving has effect only when a law is made to be saved, and in the short interval before the Forty-third Amendment omitted the article in 1978, Parliament passed no law under it. Its career is the purest example in the record of a constitutional provision that entered the text, threatened a field of legislation, and left without ever operating on a single case.

### Q: What did the Shah Commission conclude about how the decision to proclaim the Emergency was taken?

The Commission concluded that the decision had been taken without the consultation of the Cabinet that the transaction of government business required, the recommendation reaching the President from the Prime Minister and the Cabinet being informed afterwards, and that the circumstances confronting the government did not justify a proclamation on the ground of internal disturbance. Those are the Commission's conclusions, recorded in its reports of 1978 and accepted by the government of the day; they stand in the record as the official inquiry's findings on the decision, distinct from the proclamation's formal validity under Article 352 as it then stood, which the Commission did not displace.

### Q: What were the Special Courts set up in 1979 meant to try, and what became of the prosecutions?

The Special Courts Act, 1979, Act 22 of 1979, created special courts, each presided over by a sitting judge of a High Court, for the speedy trial of offences committed during the Emergency by persons who had held high public or political office, of the kinds reported on by the commissions of inquiry. The scheme had been tested in advance through a Presidential reference, In re Special Courts Bill, 1978, (1979) 1 SCC 380, in which the Supreme Court upheld the proposal with modifications. Courts were constituted and proceedings begun, but none ran to judgment: the change of government in January 1980 overtook the process, and the prosecutions were not pursued to a conclusion.

### Q: What is the clearest way to organise the amendments of the period for study and examination?

By function and sequence rather than by number. Open with the shield: the Thirty-eighth Amendment removed judicial review of satisfaction. Then the forum removal: the Thirty-ninth Amendment took election disputes of high offices out of the courts and sheltered statutes in the Ninth Schedule. Treat the Forty-first Amendment as the outlier it is, a service matter that teaches dating by substance. Then the reconstruction: the Forty-second Amendment, grouped by Preamble and duties, the amending power, the courts, the federal lists and tenure. Close with the correction in its own order: the Forty-third Amendment's deletions, the Forty-fourth's redesign, and the two sections of 1976 that only a court could and did remove.
