The 1978 Indira Congress(I) formation is usually told as the first act of a comeback: a defeated leader, written off after the Emergency and the March 1977 verdict, gathering her loyalists, splitting her party a second time and marching back to office within two years. That telling is not false, but it answers a question about political strategy, and it leaves the organisational question unasked. What was actually constituted on 2 January 1978, when Indira Gandhi's supporters met in New Delhi, elected her president of a body they named the Indian National Congress (Indira) and informed the Election Commission of the fact six days later? The argument of this article is that the founding created a substantively new organisational entity, distinct from the residual Congress that kept the party's central office, its files and its institutional machinery, and distinct also from the Congress(R) that Indira Gandhi herself had led since 1969. The new entity was built on three architectural choices: a leader-as-anchor structure in which authority ran from one person's residence rather than through committees, a dynastic personnel pattern in which the leader's son operated as co-decision-maker without holding office, and a parallel cadre architecture in which Sanjay Gandhi's Youth Congress functioned as a second organisational spine beside the formal party. Those three choices, taken together, mark the founding moment of the fourth phase of Congress organisational history, and they explain the party's behaviour long after the comeback itself was complete.

## Two Readings of One January: Comeback or Founding

The standard treatments of the January 1978 split are biographical, and their organising question is how Indira Gandhi recovered. Inder Malhotra's Indira Gandhi: A Personal and Political Biography, published in 1989, remains the canonical account: it follows the arrests, the humiliations of 1977, the tours through villages where she tested her residual popularity, and the steady reassertion of her authority over a party whose senior figures had concluded that she was finished. Pranay Gupte's Mother India: A Political Biography of Indira Gandhi, published in its revised form in 2009, works the same seam with an emphasis on her political instincts and her reading of the Janata government's frailties. In both registers, the Congress(I) appears as an instrument: the vehicle she built because the old vehicle would no longer carry her, interesting chiefly for what it enabled in 1980. The organisational content of the vehicle, what kind of machine it was, receives passing attention.

A companion reading of these years, developed in this series from the angle of office and its recovery, treats the January 1980 verdict as [the post-1980 office-rebuilding completed](/2019/05/04/1980-indira-return-power/) and examines how a leader expelled from Parliament in December 1978 sat in the prime minister's office thirteen months later. That reading is complete on its own terms. It asks what the return required politically: the Janata coalition's disintegration, the Charan Singh episode of 1979, the reconstruction of the old Congress electoral coalition. It does not ask what the organisation that performed the return was, because its question is about office rather than architecture.

The organisational-substantive reading defended here starts from the record of the founding itself. The documents of 1 and 2 January 1978, the Election Commission proceedings that ran from January 1978 to the order of 23 July 1981, and the correspondence of the rival leaderships preserved in the AICC Papers at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library describe something more deliberate than an improvised vehicle. A group of politicians who had run the Congress(R) for eight years chose, at the moment of maximum weakness, not to fight for control of the existing organisation but to abandon it and constitute a new one on different principles. They left behind the All India Congress Committee's office and machinery, the party funds, the organisational files, the reserved election symbol and the claim to unbroken institutional continuity that the residual leadership prized. They took with them a majority of the parliamentary party's remaining strength, most of the state-level legislative following, and the one asset the residual Congress could not replicate: the personal authority of Indira Gandhi, around which the new entity was explicitly organised, down to the letter in its name.

Bipan Chandra, Mridula Mukherjee and Aditya Mukherjee, in India After Independence 1947-2000, published in 1999, supply the political narrative within which the organisational fact sits: a large body of established Congress leaders, led by Y.B. Chavan and Kasu Brahmananda Reddy, turned against Indira Gandhi; she split the party in January 1978; her wing took the name Congress(I), and the other wing, in time, the name Congress(U) after Devraj Urs. The narrative is accurate and the organisational point is latent inside it. The wing that kept the name dispute alive for three and a half years was the wing that kept the machinery. The wing that won the name in the end was the wing that had been built as a new entity. The two facts belong to one explanation, and the explanation is architectural.

The organisational reading also has scholarly reinforcement that the biographical treatments do not engage. Zoya Hasan, introducing the essays collected in Parties and Party Politics in India, which she edited in 2002, identified the hollowing of the Congress's internal organisation, the substitution of nomination for election and of loyalty for representation, as the central fact of the party's later twentieth century, a diagnosis that presupposes a datable transformation of the kind this article locates in 1978. Pradeep Chhibber's Democracy without Associations, published in 1999, argued from a different direction that Indian parties of the era were organisationally shallow, their linkages to society thin, and that the Congress's decline tracked the weakening of its party structures rather than any failure of its social coalition. Chhibber's shallow party and Hasan's hollowed organisation are descriptions of the Congress that the fourth phase produced; neither description fits the bargaining, faction-ridden, committee-governed Congress of the 1950s and 1960s that Kothari analysed. Between the party Kothari described and the party Hasan and Chhibber described, something was founded. The organisational reading names the event, the date and the architects; the comeback reading, which sees only a leader's fortunes falling and rising, has no place to put the transformation and so treats it as atmosphere.

Part of the comeback reading's dominance is a matter of literary form rather than evidence. The standard reference treatments of the period organise their material as a chronicle of a leadership: election lost, wilderness, arrest, recovery, election won. Chronicle is a generous form for a protagonist and a fatal one for an institution, because organisations do not have wilderness years or triumphs; they have constitutions, staffs, channels and succession rules, which change on dates that a chronicle of the leader's fortunes has no reason to isolate. The founding of 2 January 1978 falls between the chronicle's beats. It is neither the defeat nor the victory, and so it is narrated, when it is narrated at all, as a gesture inside the wilderness chapter, a defiant weekend in Delhi, rather than as the constitutive act it was. The corrective is not to dethrone the leader from the story, which would falsify it in the opposite direction, since the new entity was quite literally her following organised. The corrective is to read the chronicle's institutional shadow: every time the narrative says that Indira Gandhi decided, appointed, expelled or pardoned, it is recording the anchored architecture in operation, and it records those verbs with a frequency after 1978 that it never records before 1977. The verbs are the architecture's footprints in the comeback narrative itself.

The four-phase framework used across this pillar's hub article distinguishes the Congress of the movement years, the Congress of the founding settlement, the Congress of governmental dominance whose absorptive operation Rajni Kothari described, and a fourth, dynastic-organisational phase. Where the fourth phase begins matters for everything written about the later Congress. If the comeback reading is right, the fourth phase has no founding moment and is merely the third phase resumed after an interruption, its dynastic features an accretion of the 1980s. If the organisational reading is right, the fourth phase was founded on a datable occasion, 2 January 1978, by identifiable acts whose architectural consequences can be traced through candidate selection, committee practice, succession and the party's conduct in and out of office. [The four-phase organisational framework](/2019/10/27/indian-national-congress-long-arc-1885-2020/) is the scaffold; this article supplies the founding evidence for its fourth phase.

## The Road to Rupture: Defeat, Disarray and the December Expulsion

The sequence begins with [the 1977 Janata verdict as immediate antecedent](/2019/04/28/1977-janata-verdict-explained/), the verdict of March 1977 that reduced the Congress(R) from 352 Lok Sabha seats to 154, cost Indira Gandhi her own seat at Rae Bareli, and delivered the government of India to the Janata Party of Morarji Desai. Defeat on that scale would have strained any organisation. It split this one along a line that the Emergency had already cut: between those who held Indira Gandhi responsible for the catastrophe and intended to retire her, and those who held that she remained the party's only mass asset and intended to fight under her name. The parliamentary party chose Y.B. Chavan, Maharashtra's senior Congress figure and a former finance and foreign minister, as its leader. The organisation chose Kasu Brahmananda Reddy, a former Andhra Pradesh chief minister and Union home minister, as its president in May 1977, succeeding Dev Kant Barooah. Neither choice settled the underlying contest, because the party's presidency and its mass leadership had come apart.

The Janata government pressed its advantage through investigation and prosecution. The Shah Commission, appointed on 28 May 1977 under Justice J.C. Shah, a former Chief Justice of India, began public hearings into the Emergency's excesses in the autumn of 1977, and its proceedings kept the defeated leadership's record before the public month after month. On 3 October 1977 the Central Bureau of Investigation arrested Indira Gandhi on charges connected with the alleged misuse of official position; a Delhi magistrate released her the following day, holding the charges unsubstantiated on the material produced. The arrest's political effect ran opposite to its intention. A former prime minister who had lost her seat, her official residence and her income was seen being taken into custody on charges a court would not sustain overnight, and the image of a government pursuing a widow of the founding family with weak process began the slow rehabilitation that her own tours of the countryside then accelerated. For the organisation, the episode mattered differently: it demonstrated that the Congress's senior office-holders, negotiating their own positions inside a defeated party, could not protect the party's most recognisable figure, and that her constituency would have to be built outside their control.

The disarray below the national leadership was as deep. In the state assembly elections of June 1977 the Congress lost power across the northern states it had governed for a generation, and its state units entered the winter leaderless in fact whatever their letterheads said: chief ministers defeated or resigned, Pradesh presidents negotiating their own futures, district committees that had lived on government contracts and office patronage suddenly required to function as an opposition's machinery, a skill the organisation had not practised since the 1950s. Defections ran in every direction. Some senior figures crossed to the Janata side or retired into private life; others began the quiet soundings toward Indira Gandhi that would surface in January as the founding's legislative strength. The Shah Commission's summonses added a legal terror to the political kind, as former ministers and officials calculated whether their safety lay in distance from the fallen leader or in solidarity with her. An organisation in this condition has no collective will to express; it has only factions positioning for a settlement someone else will impose. The winter's deadlock at the AICC was the national expression of a dissolution that had already occurred in the states, and it is against this background of an apparatus decomposing in place that the founding's offer, a new entity, a clear anchor, a counted following, acquired its force among the legislators who had to choose.

Inside the Congress, the winter of 1977 was consumed by the contest for the organisation itself. AICC meetings in November 1977 produced deadlock between the Reddy leadership, which controlled the Working Committee and the central office, and the Indira faction, which demanded organisational elections and a reckoning over the defeat. Siddhartha Shankar Ray, the former West Bengal chief minister, challenged Reddy for the presidency, and the contest exposed the absence of any accepted procedure by which the dispute could be settled from within. Reddy's term as president expired on 31 December 1977; he claimed the right to continue in office into 1978, a claim his opponents treated as self-extension by a committee that had lost the party. In December 1977 the Working Committee under Reddy moved against Indira Gandhi directly: the expulsion of the former prime minister from the party she had led for a decade was resolved upon and documented, and the expulsion documentation, with the correspondence between the Reddy leadership and Indira Gandhi that preceded it, is preserved in the AICC Papers at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library, where it constitutes the primary record of the rupture's formal cause. Readers who work with digitised holdings of the period, organisational files, minutes and statistical returns preserved as office documents, can open them in a browser through the [Office File Viewer for Excel, DOCX and PPTX files](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) without the original software, which is how much of this record is consulted in practice.

The expulsion deserves a precise statement, because the comeback literature usually rushes past it. A party organisation expelling its own former leader is performing an institutional act with institutional consequences: it declares that the organisation's continuity resides in its committees and offices, and that the person is severable from the institution. The Reddy leadership's December act asserted exactly that theory of the Congress. Indira Gandhi's answer, delivered within days, asserted the opposite theory: that the party's continuity resided in its elected representatives, its legislative following and its mass allegiance, and that committees which had ceased to command those things were the severable part. The founding of the Congress(I) was the practical working-out of the second theory. Everything in the new entity's architecture follows from the decision to locate the party in persons and allegiance rather than in offices and procedure, a decision the expulsion forced and the founding ratified.

There was also a harder calculation beneath the theories. The faction that held the central office held very little else. State units were demoralised, the parliamentary party was divided between the Chavan leadership in the House and the Indira allegiance outside it, and the party's electoral machinery, built for a governing party distributing patronage, had no patronage to distribute. The loyalists who met in January 1978 were not choosing between a strong institution and a personal vehicle. They were choosing between an institution whose remaining assets were a name, a symbol and an office, and a following whose remaining asset was a leader with demonstrated vote-gathering power in the states where the Congress still won, Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh above all. Organisational foundings are usually described in the language of principle; this one can also be described, without contradiction, in the language of assets. The principles and the assets pointed the same way.

## The 1978 Indira Congress(I) Formation: What the Founding Established

The founding took two days and two forms. On 1 and 2 January 1978 a national convention of Congressmen assembled in New Delhi, attended by members of the All India Congress Committee, members of Parliament, members of the state legislatures and candidates who had contested the 1977 elections on the Congress ticket. The convention repudiated the Reddy leadership's claim to continue in office, constituted the Indian National Congress (Indira) as the party's continuation under new management, and elected Indira Gandhi its president unanimously. Around that public act stood a smaller private one: the core group of loyalists, some twenty-six senior figures in the account the brief for this article preserves from the founding documentation, who had organised the convention from Indira Gandhi's circle in the capital and who formed the new entity's first directing nucleus. The names in that nucleus are the new architecture in embryo: Sanjay Gandhi, the leader's younger son, holding no office; Pranab Mukherjee, the West Bengal organiser who would become the entity's indispensable committee man; Buta Singh, the Punjab loyalist who managed the parliamentary following; and R.K. Dhawan, the private secretary whose control of access to the leader was already a fact of political life. On 7 January 1978 Indira Gandhi informed the Election Commission by letter that the convention had elected her president of the Congress, a step that converted an internal party act into a legal claim the Commission would spend three and a half years adjudicating.

### Where in New Delhi did the loyalists who founded the Congress(I) gather?

The founding's working centre was 1 Akbar Road in New Delhi, the address from which the loyalists organised the convention of 1 and 2 January 1978 and transacted the new party's first business. The residual leadership, by contrast, kept the established central office, its staff and its machinery intact.

The geography stated the organisational fact plainly. One Congress sat in its inherited headquarters and reasoned that possession was continuity; the other began life in premises attached to its leader's circle, because the new entity's centre of gravity was a person and not a building. The contrast was visible to every delegate who walked from one address to the other in the first week of January 1978, and it was reproduced in the party's practice for years: access to the leader's premises, rather than a seat in the central office, was the currency in which influence inside the Congress(I) was denominated.

The parliamentary arithmetic followed within weeks. Of the 154 members the Congress had returned to the Lok Sabha in 1977, between seventy and eighty crossed to the Congress(I), and a majority of the party's state legislators across the country did the same, so that the entity which had surrendered the office and the files commanded the larger share of the party's elected strength from its first month. The distinction matters for the legal sequel: when the Election Commission came to decide which group was the Congress, it applied the test the Supreme Court had laid down in Sadiq Ali v. Election Commission of India in 1971, weighing the majority in both the organisational and the legislative wings. The founding convention had been designed, whether by instinct or by advice, to manufacture exactly the evidence that test required: a representative gathering, a recorded election, and a legislative following that could be counted.

The name itself was the founding's most quoted feature, and the comeback literature treats it as a branding exercise. It deserves a colder reading. Every previous Congress split had produced descriptive suffixes: Organisation and Requisitionists in 1969, letters that described how a faction had arisen. The letter of 1978 described a person. By registering the party as the Indian National Congress (Indira), with the parenthetical initial denoting the leader's name, the founders wrote the leader-as-anchor principle into the entity's legal identity, the first time an Indian party of national scale had carried an individual's name in its registered title. The residual leadership's objection to the name, pressed before the Election Commission, was precisely that a party so named confessed itself a personal following. The founders did not dispute the description. They disputed its force as an objection, and the Commission's eventual order of 23 July 1981, recognising the group led by Indira Gandhi as the Congress, settled the legal question in favour of the entity whose name announced its architecture.

## The Name as Constitution: Why the Letter Mattered

The founding's most durable single act may prove to be onomastic. Parties announce their theories in their names more honestly than in their manifestos, and the Indian National Congress (Indira) announced, in its registered title, that it was a following. The point is sharpened by contrast with the nomenclature of the earlier split. In 1969 the factions took letters that described procedures and claims: O for Organisation, the men of the apparatus; R for Requisitionists, the members who had requisitioned the meeting that defied the apparatus. Both suffixes addressed the question of how the faction had come to exist. The suffix of 1978 addressed the question of whom the faction existed for. A party that registers a person's name in parentheses after the national name has performed a constitutional act in miniature: it has declared that the person's leadership is not an episode in the party's life but a defining property of the party, as integral to its identity as the word Congress itself.

The residual leadership understood the declaration exactly, which is why its objection before the Election Commission pressed the name as evidence. A party named for a person, the argument ran, confesses that it is not an institution but a retinue, and the Commission should award the name Congress to the body that behaved like an institution, keeping the constitution, the committees and the office. The founders' reply, in substance, accepted the description and rejected the conclusion: the party's following had gathered around the person, the numbers were the numbers, and the majority test asked who commanded the following, not whose paperwork was older. The Commission's order of 1981 vindicated the reply, and in doing so gave the personalisation a legal imprimatur that no earlier Indian split had obtained. The Sadiq Ali judgment of 1971 had recognised a leader's faction as the Congress on the strength of its majority; the order of 1981 recognised a faction that had written the leader into its name, and the difference between the two recognitions is the difference between a party that happens to be led and a party that is constituted by its leadership.

The letter's later history confirms the reading. When the parenthetical was finally dropped in 1996, the change was presentational, an adjustment of the label for an electorate among whom the founding leader had been dead for twelve years and her son for five; nothing in the party's decision structure was renegotiated on the occasion, and no faction proposed that the dropping of the initial should mark a return to committee government. The name had done its work in the eighteen years it was borne. It had told every candidate, legislator, Pradesh president and voter what kind of entity they were dealing with, and by 1996 the information was so thoroughly internalised that the label could be discarded as surplus. Institutions keep their founders' names on the building long after the founders are gone when the name has become the institution's own; the Congress kept the initial while the founder's household still supplied the leadership, and discarded it when the household's claim had passed from a designation into a presumption too deep to need stating.

## The Paper Trail: What the Founding Documents Establish

Foundings are usually reconstructed from memoirs, and memoirs of the Indian 1970s are a literature of self-exculpation. The January 1978 founding is unusually well documented in primary paper, and the paper matters because it disciplines the argument between the two readings. Three bodies of documents carry the weight. The first is the founding record itself: the proceedings of the convention of 1 and 2 January 1978, the resolution constituting the new entity, the record of Indira Gandhi's election as president, and her letter of 7 January 1978 to the Election Commission, preserved in part at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library and in the AICC Papers. The second is the record of the rupture from the other side: the December 1977 expulsion documentation and the correspondence between the Reddy leadership and Indira Gandhi in the weeks before it, also in the AICC Papers, in which the residual leadership states its theory of the party with a candour that later apologetics softened. The third is the Election Commission's own file: the rival representations of January 1978, the freeze order on the cow and calf symbol, the enquiry conducted under the Symbols Order, and the final order of 23 July 1981, a record later recited and upheld in litigation that reached the Supreme Court, whose dismissal of Devraj Urs's special leave petition on 14 August 1981 closed the legal history of the split.

Read together, the three bodies of paper establish facts that neither reading can comfortably dissolve. The founding was deliberate in form: a convention composed to satisfy a representative test, a recorded unanimous election, a prompt legal intimation. Improvised movements do not usually begin by manufacturing evidence for a tribunal they expect to face, and the loyalists' care on this point shows founders who understood that the contest would be decided by counting, and who intended to be countable. The expulsion documents establish the sequence's direction: the institutional act came first, from the committees, and the founding answered it. Accounts that present the split as Indira Gandhi's unprovoked seizure of a party she could have kept invert the documentary order; the committees moved to sever the person, and the person replied by severing the committees' claim to be the party. The Commission's file, finally, establishes that the legal category under which the dispute was decided, the majority in the organisational and legislative wings, was not a technicality imposed after the fact but the ground on which both sides chose to fight from January 1978 onward, Reddy by asserting his committee's authority, the loyalists by exhibiting their numbers.

A fourth body of paper, the least used, belongs to the defeated. The post-1977 papers of Devraj Urs, preserved at the Karnataka State Archives, document the residual alternative from inside its strongest state: the calculations by which Karnataka's leadership weighed the two claims in 1978, governed a state for the winning side, and then, in 1979, attempted to gather the losing side's remnants into the Congress(U). Urs is the one figure who stood in both entities' inner histories within eighteen months, first as the Congress(I)'s Karnataka chief minister and the organiser of the Chikmagalur by-election, then as the president of the rival claim. His papers are the natural archive for anyone who wishes to test the organisational reading against the intentions of its opponents, and their evidence runs with the reading rather than against it: the residual claim failed, in its own leader's accounting, not for want of machinery or legitimacy but for want of a following that would stay counted. The under-use of this archive in the standard treatments is part of why the comeback reading has held the field: the biographies follow the winner's paper, and the winner's paper, being the record of a recovery, makes the recovery look like the whole story.

The documents also mark the limits of the organisational reading, and honesty requires the marking. Paper can establish what was constituted, by whom, in what form, and with what following. It cannot by itself establish that the architecture caused the outcomes of 1980 to 1984, since electoral tides, the Janata government's failures and the leader's campaigning all press on the same events. The reading defended here therefore rests on a conjunction: the documents show an architecture deliberately installed, and the party's subsequent behaviour, in candidate selection, in the Charan Singh decisions, in the successions of 1980 and 1984, is the behaviour that architecture predicts and the behaviour the predecessor architectures did not produce. That is as much as institutional history can usually prove, and it is enough to carry the adjudication at the end of this article.

## Architectural Choice One: The Leader as Anchor

The first architectural choice was to organise the party around the leader's personal authority rather than through the committee structure the Congress had inherited from its movement years. The inherited structure was not fictitious: the pre-1978 Congress possessed a constitution, a Working Committee of senior figures, an AICC that met in session, Pradesh Congress Committees for the states, and a prescribed ladder of organisational elections by which authority was supposed to rise from the membership. In the Congress System's heyday that structure had bargained in earnest, because the regional leaders who sat in the Working Committee commanded followings of their own and could not be instructed like staff. The Congress(I) retained the forms, an AICC, a Working Committee, Pradesh committees, and hollowed out the bargaining. Decisions of consequence, the choice of candidates, the making and unmaking of chief ministers, the terms on which other parties would be supported or abandoned, were taken by the leader, in the leader's residence, through the leader's secretariat, and were then conveyed to the committees for ratification.

The evidence for the change is in the sequence of decisions rather than in any single document. The decision to support Charan Singh's government in July 1979, and the decision to withdraw that support on 20 August 1979, before his government had faced the Lok Sabha, were announced as the leader's decisions; no Working Committee deliberation preceded either, and the parliamentary party learned the second decision's timing from its announcement. The selection of candidates for the December 1979 and January 1980 general election was run from the centre with a thoroughness the old Congress had not attempted since the 1950s: sitting members were denied renomination in large numbers, new faces were inducted on the leader's and her son's judgement, and state leaders who had expected to distribute tickets in their provinces received lists instead. The making of chief ministers after the 1980 assembly victories followed the same grammar: the legislature party's preference was ascertained where convenient and overridden where not, and a chief minister's tenure depended on the leader's continuing confidence rather than on his majority in the House, a dependence the decade's rapid turnover of Congress chief ministers in state after state made visible to every observer.

It is important to state what this architecture was not. It was not the abolition of organisation. The Congress(I) built Pradesh committees, enrolled members, ran a parliamentary party office and contested elections in nearly every constituency of the country within two years of a founding at which it had neither an office nor a symbol of its own. The leader-as-anchor claim is about where authority sat inside that organisation, not about whether organisation existed. Authority sat in one place, and every other node in the structure derived its power from access to that place: R.K. Dhawan's influence, which puzzled contemporaries who looked for his office, is the type case of a system in which proximity to the anchor outranked every formal position. Pranab Mukherjee's contrasting career inside the same entity, a committee man whose value lay in drafting, negotiating and managing the forms, shows the second kind of power the architecture permitted: procedural competence in the anchor's service.

The scholarly disagreement about this architecture is genuine and should be stated at its strongest on both sides. The defence, made by the entity's own organisers and repeated by sympathetic historians, is that a party reduced to 154 seats and stripped of its machinery had no other principle of cohesion available: the committees had produced the expulsion, the regional barons had produced the deadlock, and only the leader's name held a national following together through 1978 and 1979. The criticism, made by scholars of party organisation in the line that runs from Rajni Kothari's later essays to Atul Kohli's Democracy and Discontent of 1990, is that anchoring a party in a person destroys the institutional capital that lets a party survive its leader, converts every succession into a crisis of the whole structure, and deforms the federal character of a party whose strength had been the autonomy of its state leaderships. The founding record supports the factual premise of both readings: the anchor was chosen, it worked as cohesion, and its costs were structural. The adjudication offered here concerns the sequence. The costs were not an accident of the 1980s; they were priced into the founding, visible in the first year's decisions, and accepted by founders who had watched the alternative, committee government by the men of 1977, at close quarters.

One refinement of the anchor deserves emphasis because it explains the architecture's reach into Parliament. Indira Gandhi's mandate inside the new entity was the presidency of the party, conferred by the convention of January 1978, not the leadership of the parliamentary party, and the difference is structural. A parliamentary leader is, in the Westminster grammar the old Congress observed, accountable to the legislators who elect her and removable by them; the contests of 1964 and 1966, in which the parliamentary party chose the prime minister, expressed that grammar. The Congress(I) inverted the accountability. Its legislators owed their tickets to the centre she headed, its parliamentary leader took direction from its party president, and the same person holding both offices by the same title meant that the parliamentary wing functioned as a department of the organisation she presided over rather than as the sovereign body of the government's life. When the entity won in 1980, the forms were duly observed, the parliamentary party elected its leader, and the observance fooled no one, because the electors had been selected by the candidate's own machinery weeks earlier. The founding convention's choice of the organisational presidency as the anchor's seat, rather than a parliamentary mandate, is one of those procedural decisions that look like etiquette and operate like constitution, and it belongs in any inventory of the founding's architecture alongside the three choices the evidence maps.

## The Secretariat: Access as an Institution

Every architecture has a load-bearing detail, and in the Congress(I) the detail was the leader's secretariat. The pre-1978 Congress had transacted its serious business in identifiable rooms: the Working Committee's chamber, the parliamentary board's table, the Pradesh offices where state bosses received petitioners in durbar. The new entity transacted its serious business at the point of access to one person. Whoever controlled the diary, the telephone list and the door controlled the flow of decisions, and the founders installed at that point the most trusted instrument they possessed: R.K. Dhawan, who had served as Indira Gandhi's aide through the years of power and followed her into opposition without a position in any government or committee to justify his weight. Dhawan's authority puzzled journalists who covered the founding years, because it corresponded to no office in the party's constitution and no seat in any legislature. It corresponded exactly to the architecture. In an anchored party, the secretariat is not an administrative convenience; it is the institution through which the anchor's authority is rationed, scheduled and signalled, and the man who ran it was, functionally, the organisation's chief operating officer.

The practice can be watched in the founding year's small change. A state leader seeking a decision on a Pradesh presidency did not move a resolution; he sought an audience, and he measured his standing by whether the audience was granted and how long it lasted. A legislator uncertain which way to vote on a support question did not consult his committee; he waited for the word that came down through the secretariat and the parliamentary party's managers, of whom Buta Singh was the most visible in the founding period. Even the entity's ideological utterances followed the channel: statements of position emerged as the leader's statements, drafted in the secretariat, and the committees' role was amplification. Pranab Mukherjee's position in the same structure marks the complementary function. Mukherjee, who had managed organisational work in West Bengal and drafted for the leadership through the 1970s, became the entity's indispensable proceduralist, the man who could give a decision taken at the residence the correct committee form, draft the resolution that recorded it and defend it in the vocabulary of the party's constitution. An anchored party needs both kinds of servant, the keeper of the door and the keeper of the forms, and the founding had each ready in its nucleus of twenty-six.

Students of organisations will recognise the pattern as the substitution of a court for a bureaucracy, and the substitution had consequences the founders accepted with open eyes. Courts are fast, personal and deniable; they are also incapable of accumulating precedent, since a decision's authority lasts exactly as long as the favour behind it, and incapable of correcting the anchor, since every channel of information reaching the anchor is staffed by people whose positions depend on the anchor's pleasure. The Congress(I)'s later history, the sycophancy that its own centenary oratory denounced in 1985, the difficulty of conveying bad electoral news upward, the treatment of internal criticism as disloyalty, is the court's pathology working itself out over a decade. The founders of January 1978 cannot be acquitted of foreseeability on this point, because the pathology had already been displayed once, inside the Congress(R) of the Emergency years, when the same secretariat-centred practice had governed a government. They chose the architecture anyway, in full knowledge of its failure mode, because the alternative on offer in that winter was the committee government of the men who had expelled their leader and lost the country, and a court, whatever its pathologies, could at least be counted on to obey.

## Architectural Choice Two: The Dynastic Personnel Pattern

The second architectural choice was to staff the entity's commanding heights through the leader's family, and to treat that staffing not as an embarrassment to be concealed but as a presupposition of the structure. Sanjay Gandhi's position is the founding evidence. He had been the Emergency's most controversial figure, the author of the five-point programme that ran beside his mother's twenty points, the organiser of the Youth Congress's wartime footing inside the old party, and a principal target of the Shah Commission's proceedings. A party rebuilding for respectability might have been expected to retire him to the margins. The Congress(I) did the opposite. Sanjay sat in the founding nucleus, travelled as the leader's emissary to state capitals, vetted candidates, received supplicants, and was understood by every chief minister and ticket aspirant in the country to be a co-decision-maker whose assent mattered and whose displeasure was dangerous.

### What formal offices did Sanjay Gandhi hold in the Congress(I) of 1978 and 1979?

None that corresponded to his power. Through 1978 and 1979 Sanjay Gandhi held no party presidency, no general secretaryship and no seat in Parliament. He was returned to the Lok Sabha only at the election of January 1980, and his appointment as a general secretary of the AICC came in June 1980, days before his death.

His authority in the founding period ran through the Youth Congress, which he directed as a parallel organisation, and through his place in the leader's household, a place every party worker in India could read without the help of an organisational chart. The absence of office was not a weakness of his position but a property of the architecture itself: authority held without portfolio cannot be voted out by a committee, transferred by an election or audited against a written job description, and it lapses only when the anchor withdraws it.

The pattern's significance is that it normalised family succession as the entity's answer to the leadership question before any succession was required. In the old Congress, the question of who would lead after the leader had been answered by contests among organisational barons: the Syndicate's choice of 1964 and 1966, the presidential election of 1969. In the Congress(I), the answer was supplied in advance by kinship, and the party's personnel practices adjusted to the expectation. When Sanjay Gandhi died in an air crash on 23 June 1980, weeks after entering Parliament from Amethi and taking his general secretaryship, the architecture did not miss a beat of its logic: the vacancy was a family vacancy. Rajiv Gandhi, the elder son, an airline pilot with no organisational career and a recorded distaste for politics, was brought forward, won the Amethi by-election of June 1981, and was placed in charge of the Youth Congress and then of the 1982 Asian Games organisation, learning the entity's business inside its parallel structures before he inherited its leadership in 1984. A party in which the second-in-command must be replaced from within one household, twice in four years, without any committee being asked to search more widely, is a party whose personnel pattern is dynastic in structure and not merely in outcome.

Below the household, the dynastic pattern generalised into a staffing principle for the whole entity. Offices in the reconstituted Pradesh committees went, in the founding years, to men whose qualification was certified loyalty to the founding rather than seniority in the predecessor organisation, and the Youth Congress supplied a disproportionate share of the appointees, since its network doubled as the leadership's register of the certified. The parliamentary intake of January 1980 carried the principle into the legislature: a striking share of the Congress(I)'s new members were first-time entrants whose selection had been made at the centre, many of them young, several drawn from the Youth Congress's state leaderships, and all of them aware that their seats were gifts of the anchor rather than conquests of a local machine. A legislature party so composed does not develop an independent corporate spirit, and the Congress(I)'s parliamentary behaviour in the 1980s, its discipline absolute in division lobbies and its initiative negligible in committee rooms, is the staffing principle of 1978 expressing itself in the institution where the old Congress had once housed its barons. Personnel is architecture continued by other means: a party that staffs itself from a household's register has decided, in advance and durably, where its decisions will be made.

The strongest counter-argument deserves its full weight. Defenders of the founding, and some historians with no brief for the family, point out that charismatic authority in mass parties commonly runs in families, that the Nehru-Gandhi name carried an electoral value no committee could manufacture, and that Sanjay's role in 1978 and 1979 was that of an organiser of unusual energy whose network among district-level workers was real organisational capital, not mere nepotism. All three points are true, and none of them answers the architectural claim. The claim is not that the family lacked ability or that voters disliked the arrangement; the voters of 1980 plainly did not. The claim is that the entity's constitution, in practice, located its succession mechanism inside a household, and that this location, chosen at the founding when Sanjay was installed as co-decision-maker without office, distinguishes the Congress(I) from every earlier Congress, including Indira Gandhi's own Congress(R), in which her authority, however personal, had been exercised through and against a baronage that owed her nothing by birth.

The staffing principle had a geography as well as a hierarchy. Because the entity's Pradesh committees were rebuilt from certified loyalists rather than inherited from the predecessor's rolls, the party of 1980 was unusually uniform across states: a Pradesh president in Andhra and a Pradesh president in Rajasthan held their offices by the same tenure, from the same source, on the same terms, and could be exchanged, removed or overruled by the same instrument. The old Congress had been a federation of provincial machines whose variety was its strength and whose autonomy was its constitution. The founded entity was a single machine with provincial branches, and the uniformity, like the rest of the architecture, was a decision taken in the founding months when the committees were first staffed and the precedent was set that no Pradesh leadership would be permitted to constitute itself.

## Architectural Choice Three: The Youth Congress as Parallel Cadre

The third architectural choice gave the entity two spines instead of one. The Indian Youth Congress, founded as the party's youth wing in the late 1940s, had been rebuilt by Sanjay Gandhi during the Emergency into an instrument of a different kind: a cadre body with its own membership drives, its own district and state presidents, its own programme, and a chain of loyalty that ran to Sanjay personally rather than to the Pradesh Congress Committees in whose territories it operated. The Emergency's end disgraced that instrument without dismantling it. In the Congress(I), the Youth Congress was reactivated from 1978 as a parallel organisation in the full sense: parallel recruitment of young workers who had no place in the formal committees, parallel reporting lines that reached the leader's household without passing through state presidents, and a parallel ladder of advancement by which a district Youth Congress president could aspire to a party ticket over the heads of the district Congress committee that nominally outranked him.

The parallelism solved a problem the founders understood well. The formal committees of any Congress they could build in 1978 would be staffed, unavoidably, by the generation of 1977: men whose instincts were those of the old organisation, whose local factions predated the split, and whose loyalty to the new entity was recent and conditional. A parallel cadre body offered the leader a workforce of a different vintage, recruited on loyalty to the founding itself, energetic in the unglamorous work of booths and processions, and available as a counterweight whenever a Pradesh committee dragged its feet. State-level evidence from the founding years shows the mechanism in operation: Youth Congress state presidents functioned as alternative centres of authority in provinces where the Pradesh Congress president was a figure of the older dispensation, and the flow of complaints, reports and requests between the districts and Delhi ran through both channels, with the Youth Congress channel generally reaching the anchor faster.

The costs of parallelism were also structural, and they compounded the costs of the first two choices. A worker's career inside the Congress(I) depended less on the slow accumulation of standing inside a committee hierarchy than on visibility to the parallel network and its patron, so the entity selected for demonstrative loyalty over administrative patience. State leaders learned to treat the Youth Congress not as a feeder organisation but as a rival court, and several of the Congress(I)'s later internal wars, in Andhra Pradesh, in Karnataka and in the capital itself, began as quarrels between a chief minister and a Youth Congress network he did not control. Rajiv Gandhi's apprenticeship was served inside this parallel structure, as Youth Congress in-charge and as the organiser of the Asian Games of 1982, and the succession of 1984 was thereby routed through the cadre body the founding had built rather than through the AICC, one more indication that the architecture of January 1978 was a working constitution and not a temporary expedient of the comeback.

The parallel body's content deserves a paragraph of its own, because it explains the cadre's cohesion. The Youth Congress that Sanjay Gandhi rebuilt carried a programme, the five points he had propagated during the Emergency, family planning, tree planting, the abolition of dowry, literacy and the campaign against caste, recast after 1977 in the softer language of social work and national discipline. The programme's policy weight was modest; its organisational weight was large. It gave young recruits a doctrine of activism that distinguished them from the committee Congress's culture of brokerage, a uniform style of work, camps, enrolment drives, blood donation and relief work, and a direct emotional line to the founding household, whose younger member inspected, addressed and promoted the cadre in person. A political generation was socialised inside this body between 1978 and 1984, and its graduates, district organisers who became legislators, Youth Congress presidents who became ministers, carried the founding's assumptions about loyalty and authority into the party's mainstream for decades afterwards. When later observers describe the Congress's internal culture as one in which demonstrable devotion to the leadership outbids organisational seniority, they are describing the parallel architecture's alumni at work in the parent body.

## What the New Entity Displaced: Three Contrasts

The claim that the Congress(I) was a new entity stands or falls on the contrasts, and the brief for this article requires them to be drawn against three predecessors separately. Table 1, the three-architectural-choice map, sets out the new entity's architecture with its institutional consequences across the years from 1978 to 1984 and its differences from each prior Congress; the paragraphs around the table argue the contrasts in prose.

| Architectural choice | What was built, 1978 to 1984 | Institutional consequence | Difference from the prior entities |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Leader-as-anchor | Decision-making centred on the leader's residence and secretariat; committees ratified | Candidate selection, chief ministerial tenure and alliance decisions became acts of the leader | The Congress System bargained among regional barons; the Congress(R) retained baronial negotiation inside a dominant leader's party |
| Dynastic personnel pattern | The leader's son installed as co-decision-maker without office; succession treated as a household question | The leadership question was answered in advance by kinship in 1980 and again in 1984 | Earlier Congresses settled succession by contest among organisational leaders in 1964, 1966 and 1969 |
| Parallel cadre architecture | The Youth Congress run as a second organisational spine with its own recruitment and reporting lines | Advancement ran through loyalty networks beside the committees; state units faced an internal counterweight | The pre-1978 youth wing was subordinate to the Pradesh committees; the residual Congress kept a single committee hierarchy |

Against the pre-1969 Congress, the contrast is with [the Congress System's four-component framework](/2019/11/18/kothari-congress-system-post-independence/) that Rajni Kothari stated in 1964 and 1970: single-party dominance reproduced by factional pluralism inside the party, opposition parties held at the margin, and regional leaders autonomous within discipline. The Congress(I) kept the dominance, when it could win it, and dismantled the other three components as working principles. Factional pluralism, the mechanism by which the old Congress had absorbed social pressure into its own bargaining, was reclassified as indiscipline; the founders had watched factions conduct the expulsion of December 1977 and drew the lesson that pluralism inside the party was a weapon pointed at the leader. Regional autonomy within discipline became regional subordination with discipline defined at the centre. Opposition marginality, which the old system had produced as a by-product of absorption, the new entity pursued by direct electoral combat and, in office after 1980, by the dismissal of nine state governments held by the opposition in February 1980, an act of central assertion the Congress System's grammar would have found unintelligible.

Against the post-1969 Congress(R), the contrast is finer, because the personnel and much of the rhetoric were continuous. The Congress(R) had been born in defiance of the party organisation, and [the 1969 split's structural-cultural rupture as antecedent](/2019/03/26/1969-congress-split-decisive-moment/) is the necessary backdrop: a leader who had appealed over the Syndicate's heads to the country, won the election of 1971 on a personal mandate, and governed through a party whose senior figures increasingly resembled courtiers. Yet the Congress(R) retained more of the old architecture than its legend admits. Its Pradesh committees still contained men with independent bases, its Working Committee still recorded disagreement, and [the 1969 absorption-mechanism failure](/2019/11/23/1969-split-organisational-pivot/) was a failure of the old absorptive machinery under strain, not a decision to discard the machinery. The Congress(I) made the discard explicit. Where the Congress(R) had drifted toward personal rule while keeping institutional residuals, the Congress(I) was founded on personal rule and treated the residuals, committees that deliberated, leaders with independent bases, organisational elections, as defects inherited from a disgraced past. The distinction explains a puzzle of the comeback years: why a leader who had already dominated one Congress for eight years bothered to found another. The answer the record gives is that domination inside a structure built to bargain was a wasting asset, and the founding replaced the structure.

Against the residual post-1977 Congress, the contrast is between two theories of what a party is. The residual organisation, led in its first phase by Brahmananda Reddy as president and Y.B. Chavan as parliamentary leader, held that the Congress was its constitution, its committees, its office, its funds and its symbol, and that continuity through the split belonged to whoever kept those things. The Congress(I) held that the Congress was its elected representatives, its members and its voters, and that continuity belonged to whoever could convene them and win with them. The Election Commission's proceedings of 1978 to 1981 put the two theories to a legal test, and the test, the majority test of the Sadiq Ali judgment, was written in the Congress(I)'s language: count the wings, organisational and legislative, and award the name to the majority. The residual theory, for all its institutional dignity, could not survive being counted.

## Counting the Wings: The Majority Test as Organisational Philosophy

The legal framework inside which the founding had to prove itself deserves examination, because it silently ratified the founders' theory of what a party is. The Election Commission's jurisdiction over disputed parties ran through Paragraph 15 of the Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order of 1968, and its working test came from the Supreme Court's decision in Sadiq Ali v. Election Commission of India, delivered in November 1971 out of the 1969 split. The Court there held that where a party divides, the Commission may treat as the party the group commanding a majority in both the organisational wing and the legislative wing, testing the claim against the party's constitution and counting heads in the AICC, the Pradesh committees, the parliamentary party and the state legislature parties. The test is usually described as a pragmatic rule for a hard case. It is better understood as a philosophy. It declares that a party's identity resides in its counted adherents at a moment of decision, not in the continuity of its registrations, its property or its paper. A minute book can be inherited; a majority must be re-won every time it is counted.

Both factions understood the philosophy and organised for it, which is why the winter of 1977 to 1978 was fought as a census. The residual leadership's strategy was to keep the counting bodies frozen in its favour: the Working Committee that expelled the loyalists was also the body whose composition would evidence the organisational wing, and the expulsion itself can be read, in part, as a counting manoeuvre, an attempt to define the disloyal out of the denominator before the Commission divided. The founders' counter-strategy was to build a denominator of their own that no committee resolution could shrink: a convention whose delegates were the party's elected personnel, a unanimous presidential election, a two-thirds claim on the AICC, and then the state-level accessions that converted a claim about the centre into facts about legislatures. When the Commission froze the symbol in 1978, it was acknowledging that both counts were colourable; when it decided in July 1981, after the intervening general election had supplied the most reliable count of all, it was applying the philosophy to a record the founders had been manufacturing, deliberately and in the open, since the first week of January 1978.

The test was applied a second time inside the same split's history, and the second application confirms the first's logic. When Devraj Urs broke away in 1979 and organised the Congress(U), his group presented itself not as a claimant to the whole Congress name but as a party seeking its own recognition, and the Commission's treatment of the application, recognition as a separate registered party entitled to its own symbol for the election of 1980, shows the system working at its other setting: a faction that could not outcount the recognised Congress could still exit with a legal personality of its own, provided it asked for separation rather than succession. The distinction between the two applications is the distinction between Reddy's strategy and Urs's. Reddy claimed the inheritance and lost it entire; Urs claimed a divorce and received a settlement. Party law, like the architecture this article describes, rewards the claimant who understands what the counting can and cannot deliver, and the founding generation of the Congress(I), which had counted its wings before it claimed its name, understood the system it was operating in better than the custodians who believed the inheritance was theirs by custody.

The philosophy's deeper consequence is rarely noticed. A test that locates party identity in counted majorities disciplines founders toward the forms of democracy, conventions, memberships, delegate counts, even when the substance of the resulting party is centralised command; the Congress(I) had to perform representativeness to become the Congress, and the performance left deposits, delegate lists, convention precedents, enquiry records, that later democrats inside the party could cite. At the same time, the test prices out the slower currencies of institutional legitimacy: seniority, custodianship, the moral claim of having kept the office open through the bad years. The residual Congress was rich in exactly those currencies and poor in the only one the Commission accepted. There is a lesson about Indian party law in the outcome that runs beyond this split. A legal order that recognises parties by counting their wings will repeatedly reward the faction that can mobilise numbers quickly, which is to say the faction organised around a mobilising person, and will repeatedly disappoint the faction organised around procedure. The founding of 1978 was the first great demonstration, and the Indian party system's later splits have rehearsed its script with a fidelity that suggests the script has become part of the system's working constitution.

## The Residual Congress: The Alternative That Kept the Machinery

The residual Congress deserves a fuller account than the victor's history usually grants it, because its programme was a coherent alternative and its defeat was not foreordained. The men who held it in January 1978 were not lightweights. Kasu Brahmananda Reddy had been chief minister of Andhra Pradesh and Union home minister; his claim to continue as president, pressed after his term expired on 31 December 1977, rested on the Working Committee's authority and was argued before the Election Commission as a claim of institutional continuity. Y.B. Chavan, the parliamentary party's leader, had been Maharashtra's first chief minister, defence minister during the war of 1962 and a member of every Congress high command since the 1950s; his presence gave the residual organisation its claim to be the Congress of the founding generation. Around them stood figures of the weight of Swaran Singh, Siddhartha Shankar Ray and Dev Kant Barooah. What this leadership kept was real: the AICC office and its staff, the party's central funds, the organisational files reaching back decades, and the reserved cow and calf symbol, the emblem under which the Congress(R) had won in 1971, subject to the Commission's freeze once the dispute was joined.

### Did the Congress(I) inherit the old party's offices, funds and files?

It did not. The central office, the organisational records reaching back decades and the party funds remained with the residual organisation, which held physical possession and the Working Committee's paperwork to support its custody. The Congress(I) began with improvised premises arranged around its leader and with whatever its legislators carried across with them.

The point is not sentimental. A founding that starts without the machinery and wins anyway demonstrates that the machinery was no longer the party's operative asset by 1978, and the residual leadership's confidence that possession of the office settled the argument was the core of its misjudgement, repeated at every stage from the symbol freeze of 1978 to the Commission's order of 1981.

The residual organisation's trajectory after 1978 is a study in the limits of machinery without an anchor. Its state units, leaderless at the top in the only sense that mattered electorally, haemorrhaged legislators to the Congress(I) through 1978 and 1979. In the assembly elections of February and March 1978, held in Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka and Maharashtra among other states, the residual Congress was routed by its rival: the Congress(I) took clear majorities in Andhra Pradesh, where Marri Chenna Reddy became chief minister, and in Karnataka, where Devraj Urs, who had joined the Congress(I) at the founding and campaigned as its leader in the state, returned to the chief ministership on 28 February 1978. The Karnataka sequel belongs to this section because it completes the residual story: Urs, the most substantial regional leader the Congress(I) possessed, quarrelled with the leadership over the place being prepared for Sanjay Gandhi, left the Congress(I) in mid-1979, and gathered the scattered non-Indira claim under his own presidency as the Congress(U), drawing in Chavan, Reddy and a generation of younger leaders that included A.K. Antony and Sharad Pawar. The residual Congress thus ended the decade by being absorbed into a vehicle founded by the Congress(I)'s most important defector, a sequence the papers of Devraj Urs preserved at the Karnataka State Archives document from the inside and which no account of the founding should flatten.

### How many seats did the Congress(U) win at the 1980 general election?

Thirteen. The Congress(U), contesting the general election of December 1979 and January 1980 as the standard-bearer of the Congress that had kept the committees, won thirteen Lok Sabha seats against the Congress(I)'s 353, and its claim to be the continuing Congress collapsed with that arithmetic in every region of the country.

Its president Devraj Urs was left holding a majority in the Karnataka assembly while his national claim disintegrated, and he resigned the chief ministership within days of the verdict, in January 1980, as his legislators crossed back to the victorious entity their state leader had abandoned six months earlier. The thirteen seats were the residual theory's final balance sheet: committees, files and continuity, audited by the electorate.

The legal consummation followed the electoral one. By its order of 23 July 1981 the Election Commission, after due enquiry, held that the group led by Indira Gandhi as president, the Congress(I), shall be recognised as the Congress, and that the Urs group was not the Congress, leaving it free to seek recognition under a different name. Urs carried the order to the Supreme Court by special leave petition; on 14 August 1981 the Court dismissed the petition, and the Commission's finding became final. The cow and calf symbol, frozen since 1978, passed under the settlement to the recognised Congress, which preferred the open hand under which it had won in 1980. Sharad Pawar succeeded to the leadership of what remained of the Congress(U) in October 1981 and renamed it the Indian Congress (Socialist); that remnant, its claim extinguished, merged into the Congress(I) in 1986. The residual Congress's papers, the Urs collection among them, survive as the archive of the road not taken: an institutional Congress, governed by committee men through inherited machinery, which the electorate and the Commission, in that order, declined to recognise as the Congress at all.

## Two Congresses in the States: The Federal Evidence of 1978 and 1979

The founding's truth was told most plainly in the states, because a national convention can be staged but a state organisation must exist to be counted. In the weeks after January 1978, every Pradesh unit of the old party faced the same decision, and the pattern of their decisions is the organisational map of the split. Units whose leaderships had been built by the Reddy-Chavan generation declared for the residual organisation and discovered that their legislators had gone the other way; units in the south, where the Congress had survived 1977 in office or near it, declared for the Congress(I) and brought their governments or near-governments with them. The two early state governments of the new entity illustrate the range of its internal arrangements better than any central document.

Karnataka was the entity's prize and its problem. Devraj Urs, who had run the state's celebrated land reforms and built a backward-classes coalition of genuine social depth, joined the founding, delivered the February 1978 election, and governed from 28 February 1978 with an authority in his state that no other Congress(I) chief minister possessed, because his majority was his own work and his social base predated the split. The anchored centre's treatment of Karnataka over the following eighteen months is a compact history of the architecture's friction with federal reality: directives from Delhi on appointments and policy met a chief minister who regarded his mandate as independent, the Youth Congress network in the state reported around him to the household, and his resentment fixed on the position being prepared for Sanjay Gandhi, whose elevation Urs read, correctly, as the dynasty's claim to stand above every elected leader in the party. His exit in mid-1979, taking the state's legislative weight and the residual claim's presidency with him, demonstrated both the architecture's reach and its limit. The anchor could hold the party; it could not hold a baron who possessed a base of his own, because barons with bases were exactly what the architecture had been designed to abolish, and the design worked by expelling what it could not subordinate.

Andhra Pradesh showed the opposite configuration. Marri Chenna Reddy, who became chief minister after the February and March 1978 elections, was a leader of the older generation whose authority in his state was real but who chose subordination as the price of office, and his government's survival depended on a continuing negotiation with the centre in which the centre's terms prevailed. The contrast between Bangalore and Hyderabad in 1978 and 1979 is the federal evidence for the article's claim. The same founding produced, in one state, a chief minister who behaved as a coalition partner and eventually seceded, and in another, a chief minister who behaved as a delegate and endured. The difference lay not in the men's temperaments but in the sources of their majorities: Urs's majority had been assembled by Urs, Chenna Reddy's by the Congress(I)'s statewide wave under the leader's name. An architecture that locates legitimacy in the anchor will always sort its regional leaders into those who need the anchor and those who merely use it, and the founding's state history is the record of that sorting, conducted in public, at the cost of the party's strongest southern government within eighteen months of its victory.

The state evidence of 1980 completed the demonstration from the other side. When the Congress(I) took office in New Delhi in January 1980, its government's first federal act was the dissolution, on 18 February 1980, of nine state assemblies held by the opposition, on the reasoning that the Lok Sabha verdict had withdrawn the people's mandate from the parties governing those states. The act was constitutional in form and revolutionary in organisational meaning: a party whose own legitimacy doctrine located the mandate in a national leader's person applied the doctrine symmetrically, treating state mandates as revocable by the national one. The Congress System's federal grammar, in which state governments possessed their own electoral dignity whatever happened in Delhi, was thereby formally renounced by the party that had invented it. No single act of the founding period states the architectural transformation as economically as this one, and it was performed not by the Emergency's heirs in a moment of siege but by a freshly elected government in the first month of its confidence.

## The Founding Tested: Elections, Prosecutions and the Janata Collapse, 1978 to 1980

An organisational founding is a hypothesis until elections test it, and the Congress(I)'s hypothesis was tested four times in two years. The first test, the assembly elections of February and March 1978, has been described in the previous section from the residual side; from the new entity's side it demonstrated that the founding's legislative following could be converted into state governments within eight weeks, in Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka, and that the leader's name, detached from the old symbol and the old office, still drew the Congress vote. The second test was personal and parliamentary. Indira Gandhi had no seat in the Lok Sabha, having lost Rae Bareli in 1977. In November 1978 she contested the by-election from Chikmagalur in Karnataka, where Devraj Urs's government organised her campaign, against the Janata Party's Veerendra Patil, a former Karnataka chief minister of the old Congress tradition.

### When did Indira Gandhi return to the Lok Sabha after the January 1978 founding?

In November 1978, through the Chikmagalur by-election in Karnataka. Standing as the Congress(I) candidate in a constituency her party's state government administered, she defeated the Janata Party's Veerendra Patil by a margin the returns of the day placed above seventy thousand votes, and re-entered the House she had last sat in as prime minister.

She took her seat as the leader of a parliamentary party that the founding, eleven months earlier, had brought into being around her name, and the by-election's organisation, run through the Karnataka government of Devraj Urs and the Youth Congress's district networks, was itself a demonstration of the new entity's machinery in working order.

The third test was the Janata government's answer to Chikmagalur. On 19 December 1978 the Lok Sabha, on a report of its Privileges Committee concerning the alleged obstruction of officials during the Emergency, resolved to expel Indira Gandhi from the House and to commit her to prison for the remainder of the session. The expulsion was the governing coalition's attempt to settle by parliamentary process what it could not settle politically, and its effect on the new entity was galvanic. A party founded on the proposition that allegiance to a person outranked the verdicts of institutions could hardly have been given a better recruiting ground: the Congress(I) organised protests across the country, its workers courted arrest in large numbers, and the spectacle of its leader imprisoned by a fractious government completed the transformation of the Emergency's author into the opposition's martyr that the October 1977 arrest had begun. Organisationally, the episode also proved the parallel cadre's worth, since the Youth Congress supplied the street presence for a campaign run at short notice while the formal committees were still being rebuilt in several states.

The fourth test was the Janata government's self-destruction, and it displayed the new architecture in operation at the level of national decision. Through the first half of 1979 the Janata coalition tore at itself over the dual membership of its former Jana Sangh component and the ambitions of Chaudhary Charan Singh, who had formed his own formation, the Janata Party (Secular), and sought the prime ministership. Morarji Desai resigned on 15 July 1979. Charan Singh, sworn in on 28 July 1979, could count only with Congress(I) support, which was extended on conditions, including the withdrawal of the cases against Indira and Sanjay Gandhi, that he would not or could not meet. On 20 August 1979, the day before his government was to face the House, the Congress(I) withdrew its support by its leader's decision, announced without prior committee deliberation and defended afterwards as self-evidently correct; Charan Singh resigned, advised dissolution, and the Lok Sabha was dissolved on 22 August 1979. Whatever judgement history passes on the manoeuvre's ethics, its organisational meaning is plain. A party in which a decision of that magnitude could be taken, executed and held to by every one of its members of Parliament within a single news cycle was a party whose decision structure had been rebuilt exactly as its founders intended.

The general election of December 1979 and January 1980 converted the architecture into office. The Congress(I) won 353 of the seats contested, on 42.7 percent of the national vote in the Election Commission's statistical reporting, a tally within a seat of the Congress(R)'s 352 of 1971 and achieved by an entity that, twenty-four months earlier, had possessed neither headquarters nor reserved symbol. Its candidates had been selected centrally, its campaign had been run as a referendum on its leader, and its defeated rivals included both the Janata formations and the Congress(U)'s thirteen seats. Indira Gandhi, returned from Medak and Rae Bareli, the two constituencies she had contested, took office on 14 January 1980. The comeback was complete. The argument of this article is that the comeback is the least interesting thing the election proved: what it proved organisationally is that the entity founded in January 1978 could select candidates, discipline a parliamentary party, run a national campaign and absorb a victory's spoils without reference to the inherited machinery it had renounced, because it had replaced that machinery with an architecture of its own.

## Restoration in Name, Transformation in Substance: The Complication Answered

The strongest objection to the organisational reading is real, and it must be answered rather than waved away. The Congress(I) did not remain a residence-run entity with a borrowed name. It recovered the Congress name by the Commission's order of 1981, it recovered the party's properties and funds through the litigation that followed, its AICC met in fuller session, most visibly at Calcutta in December 1982, it held its centenary at Bombay in December 1985 with the full ceremonial of institutional continuity, and in 1996, ahead of that year's general election, it dropped the parenthetical letter altogether and stood before the electorate simply as the Indian National Congress. If the architecture of 1978 was so substantive, the objection runs, why did the entity spend two decades reacquiring the institutional dress of the old Congress, and does the reacquisition not show that the founding's architecture was a temporary rhetoric of opposition, discarded on contact with government?

The answer turns on a distinction between the nominal-institutional and the architectural-substantive. What was restored between 1981 and 1996 was the name, the property, the symbol's custody and the ceremonial calendar: the institutional dress. What was not restored was the old architecture's working content. No organisational election displaced the practice of nominated committees at any level that mattered. No Working Committee recovered the power to deliberate against the leader's known preference, as the committees of 1969 and 1977 had done with consequences the founders remembered. No regional leader recovered the autonomous standing of a Kamaraj or a Chavan within the structure; the chief ministers of the 1980s were appointed, transferred and dismissed from the centre at a rate the Congress System's historians record with astonishment, and the succession of 1984 was executed inside the household within hours of the assassination, with the party's organs ratifying a decision that the architecture had pre-taken. Rajiv Gandhi, who spoke the language of organisational renewal more fluently than any Congress leader of his generation and used the centenary session of 1985 to denounce the brokers of power who had colonised the party, governed through the same anchor, the same nominated structures and the same household succession that the founding had installed. The centenary speech is in fact the complication's own refutation from within: the leader of the restored, renamed, fully institutional Congress stood before its assembled delegates and described an organisation captured by exactly the pathologies the leader-as-anchor architecture produces, and the organisation applauded and changed nothing.

The Calcutta session of December 1982 shows the restoration's character at its most complete. An AICC session in a great provincial capital, full attendance, committees constituted, resolutions debated in form: every external feature of the old institutional Congress was present, and the session is rightly cited by those who hold that the founding's architecture was an opposition expedient. What the session did not do is the point. It elected no one against the leadership's wish, it altered no channel by which decisions rose, and its organisational resolutions were implemented, where they were implemented, by nomination. Three years later the centenary session at Bombay heard the party's own prime minister describe its structures as captured by brokers of power and promise organisational elections and internal renewal; the elections were announced again in later years and deferred again, and the pattern of deferral is itself evidence, because a party in which committees held real power would not have needed its leader's permission to revive them, and a leader who intended revival would not have needed to announce it more than once. The forms multiplied exactly as fast as the substance permitted, which is the behaviour of an institution decorating an architecture it does not intend to change.

The post-1985 evidence therefore cuts against the objection. Nominal restoration and substantive persistence are compatible, and the Indian record shows both occurring together: the party that contests elections as the Indian National Congress, from headquarters indistinguishable from its predecessors', remains in its decision structure, its personnel pattern and its cadre arrangements the entity constituted in January 1978. Phase Four did not end when the letter was dropped. The letter was dropped when Phase Four no longer needed it.

## The Founding's Long Shadow: 1984 and After

A founding is finally judged by whether strangers to the event still behave as it prescribed. By that test the January 1978 settlement governed the Congress long after every founder had left the scene. The succession of 31 October 1984 was its first posthumous examination. Indira Gandhi was assassinated in the morning; by the evening her son had been sworn in as prime minister, the decision taken in the small circle the architecture had always privileged, the party's formal organs ratifying after the fact a transfer that no committee had debated and no alternative claimant had contested. Compare the succession of 1964, when a prime minister's death set the organisation's recognised power-brokers to their work of soundings and consensus, and the succession of 1966, when the decision was auctioned among state bosses at a meeting of the parliamentary party. In 1984 there were no soundings, because there was no constituency left whose soundings mattered: the anchored party knew its rule, and the rule's name was the household. Rajiv Gandhi's own tenure then supplied the melancholy corollary. A leader installed by the architecture set out, at the centenary session of 1985, to denounce the architecture's pathologies, the brokers, the sycophancy, the absence of internal elections, and the party, organised exactly as the founding had organised it, proved unable to execute its own leader's reform programme, because every instrument through which reform would have had to travel was staffed by the system's beneficiaries and every channel of enforcement ran upward to a single office.

The examination of 1991 was stricter still. Rajiv Gandhi's assassination during the election campaign left the party mid-flight without a leader of the household in the field, and the Working Committee's first instinct, before any consultation of the organisation, was to offer the party presidency to Sonia Gandhi, who had held no party office and won no election, on no ground except her place in the founding household. She declined, and the party eventually turned to P.V. Narasimha Rao, its first prime minister drawn from outside the founding family in the entity's history; but the order of the instincts is the evidence. Thirteen years after the founding and seven after the founder's death, the reflex installed in January 1978 still fired first: legitimacy lives in the household, and offices are filled by descending from it. The Rao years and the Kesri interlude that followed showed the architecture working without its founding family only as an anchor tenantless, a structure of nomination and deference waiting for the household's return, which the party duly arranged in 1998. None of this later history was written into the founding documents of 1978. All of it is legible as the founding's architecture operating under successive managements, which is what it means, in organisational history, to say that an act was a founding and not an episode.

## The Adjudication: A Founding, Not an Episode

The named disagreement can be closed on the evidence assembled. The comeback reading, in Malhotra's biography of 1989 and Gupte's of 2009, explains the leader's recovery: her resilience, her reading of the Janata government's weaknesses, the electorate's forgiveness. It does not explain the organisation, and where it touches organisation it treats the Congress(I) as the old Congress under new management, a vehicle whose interest lies in its destination. The organisational-substantive reading explains things the comeback reading cannot: why the founders surrendered the office, funds and symbol rather than fighting for them from within; why the Commission's majority test, applied in 1981, was won by the side that had spent 1978 counting and convening rather than litigating possession; why the party's decision practice in 1979 already showed the speed and unanimity of an anchored structure; why candidate selection in 1980 could be centralised in a party not yet three years old; why the succession crises of 1980 and 1984 were absorbed by a household mechanism rather than contested by a baronage; and why the restored Congress of the later 1980s, for all its recovered ceremonial, never recovered the bargaining architecture of the Congress System or the residuals of the Congress(R).

The periodisation stake should be made explicit, because it is where the adjudication bites on the wider history of the party. If the comeback reading is right, the Congress's fourth phase begins in January 1980, when office was recovered, and the years 1977 to 1979 are a trough between phases, an interregnum of opposition in which the old party waited out its punishment. If the organisational reading is right, the fourth phase begins on 2 January 1978, in opposition, in defeat's immediate aftermath, and the return of 1980 is not a beginning but the new phase's first dividend. The second periodisation explains features of the 1980s that the first must treat as imports: the candidate-selection regime, the treatment of chief ministers as delegates, the February 1980 dissolution of nine assemblies, the successions of 1980 and 1984 all arrive in office already formed, because they were formed in the opposition years when the architecture was built and staffed. Historians of institutions distrust explanations that locate a system's character in its moments of success; character is set in adversity, when choices cannot be deferred and every design decision carries the organisation's survival as its price. The founding generation chose the anchor, the household register and the parallel cadre while it held nothing, and it held to all three while it held everything. A periodisation that starts the phase at the founding honours that sequence; a periodisation that starts it at the victory mistakes the harvest for the sowing.

The reading can also state what would count against it, which the comeback reading, being a narrative of character, cannot easily do. If the founding were merely an episode, the entity built in 1978 should have converged back toward the predecessor architectures as its circumstances normalised: committees recovering deliberative weight in the fat years after 1980, succession reopening to contest when the founding leader left the scene in 1984, the parallel cadre being folded into the formal structure once the emergency of opposition had passed. None of the three convergences occurred; each movement ran the other way, toward deeper nomination, smoother household succession and a Youth Congress apprenticeship for the incoming leader. A reading that predicts the direction of forty years of institutional behaviour from a two-day convention has earned its claim to describe a founding. A reading that must treat each of those forty years as a fresh accident of personality has described, at most, a very long episode.

Two readings of the same years, one organised around office recovered and one around organisation founded, are not enemies, and this article's claim should not be inflated into a denial of the other. Read together, the office-rebuilding account and the organisational account compose the full description of 1978 to 1984: the founding supplied the instrument, the recovery supplied the occasion, and each is unintelligible at full depth without the other. The adjudication concerns priority, and on priority the record is not ambiguous. The office was recovered because the instrument existed; the instrument was not created by the recovery, having been constituted two years before it, in defeat, by men and women who could not know the Janata government would obligingly destroy itself on schedule. Phase Four of the Congress's organisational history begins, therefore, not with the victory of January 1980 and not with the sympathy wave of 1984, but with the convention of 1 and 2 January 1978 and the twenty-six loyalists who organised it: the moment a party that had been expelled from itself decided that it was not an office, a file or a symbol, but a following gathered around a name, and built accordingly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the Indira Congress(I)?

The Indira Congress(I) is the Indian National Congress (Indira), the party constituted at the national convention of Congressmen in New Delhi on 1 and 2 January 1978 after the expulsion of Indira Gandhi by the Reddy-led organisation. It was organised on three architectural choices: a leader-as-anchor structure, a dynastic personnel pattern and a parallel cadre in the Youth Congress. The Election Commission recognised it as the Congress by its order of 23 July 1981, and it governed India from January 1980; the parenthetical letter was dropped from its name in 1996, but the organisation founded in 1978 continued as the Indian National Congress.

### Q: When was the Congress(I) founded?

The Congress(I) was founded on 1 and 2 January 1978, when a national convention of Congressmen, comprising members of the All India Congress Committee, members of Parliament, state legislators and candidates from the 1977 elections, met in New Delhi, repudiated the claim of the Brahmananda Reddy leadership to continue in office, constituted the Indian National Congress (Indira) and elected Indira Gandhi its president unanimously. She notified the Election Commission of her election by letter dated 7 January 1978, opening the legal dispute over the Congress name and symbol that the Commission decided in her favour on 23 July 1981.

### Q: Who were Y.B. Chavan and Devraj Urs?

Yashwantrao Balwantrao Chavan was Maharashtra's first chief minister, defence minister at the time of the 1962 war, and a Union finance and foreign minister; after the 1977 defeat the Congress parliamentary party chose him as its leader, and he stood as the senior figure of the non-Indira Congress claim. Devraj Urs was Karnataka's chief minister from 1972 to 1977, the author of that state's land reforms and its backward-classes politics. He joined the Congress(I) at the founding, led it to victory in Karnataka in February 1978 and served as chief minister again, before breaking with Indira Gandhi in 1979 and founding the Congress(U).

### Q: Why did Indira Gandhi form a new Congress in 1978?

Indira Gandhi formed a new Congress because the organisation she had led since 1969 had been turned against her: the Working Committee under Brahmananda Reddy, deadlocked with her faction through the AICC meetings of November 1977, resolved on her expulsion in December 1977, and Reddy claimed to continue as president after his term expired on 31 December. Rather than contest the committees for control of the office, funds and files, she located the party in its elected representatives and mass following, convened them in convention on 1 and 2 January 1978, and built an entity whose authority ran from her leadership rather than from the inherited machinery.

### Q: What is the difference between Congress(R) and Congress(I)?

The Congress(R) was the party Indira Gandhi led out of the 1969 split: it governed India from 1969 to 1977, retained the institutional residuals of the old Congress, including committees that still bargained and state leaders with independent bases, and fought the elections of 1971 and 1977 as the Congress. The Congress(I) was founded in January 1978 after her expulsion by the Congress(R)'s own successor leadership. It began without the office, funds or symbol, and was built on different architecture: a leader-as-anchor structure in which committees ratified rather than deliberated, a dynastic personnel pattern centred on Sanjay Gandhi, and a parallel Youth Congress cadre beside the formal organisation.

### Q: When did the Congress(I) revert to INC name?

The legal identity was settled on 23 July 1981, when the Election Commission held after enquiry that the group led by Indira Gandhi, the Congress(I), shall be recognised as the Congress, a finding the Supreme Court left undisturbed when it dismissed Devraj Urs's special leave petition on 14 August 1981. The party nevertheless kept the parenthetical letter in its registered name and in common usage for a further decade and a half. The designation was dropped only in 1996, ahead of that year's general election, from which point the organisation founded in 1978 stood before the electorate simply as the Indian National Congress.

### Q: What is the leader-as-anchor architecture?

The leader-as-anchor architecture is the organisational design in which a party's authority is located in one person's leadership rather than distributed through its committees. In the Congress(I) after January 1978, decisions on candidates, chief ministers, alliances and support to governments were taken by Indira Gandhi, conveyed through her secretariat, and ratified by the Working Committee and the AICC instead of being deliberated there. Access to the leader outranked formal office, regional leaders held their positions at her pleasure, and the party's cohesion depended on the anchor's authority. The design gave the young party speed and unanimity, at the price of institutional capital that a committee-governed party accumulates.

### Q: What happened at the National Convention of Congressmen on 1 and 2 January 1978?

The convention gathered members of the All India Congress Committee, members of Parliament, members of the state legislatures and Congress candidates from the 1977 elections in New Delhi. It refused to accept Brahmananda Reddy's claim to continue as party president after the expiry of his term, constituted the Indian National Congress (Indira) as the party's continuation under new leadership, and elected Indira Gandhi president unanimously. Its organisers, a core of some twenty-six loyalists around the leader, had prepared it as a representative act, so that the new entity could present the Election Commission with a counted organisational majority to set against the residual leadership's possession of the office.

### Q: Who was K. Brahmananda Reddy, and what claim did he press before the Election Commission?

Kasu Brahmananda Reddy was a former chief minister of Andhra Pradesh and Union home minister who became president of the Congress in May 1977, succeeding Dev Kant Barooah after the election defeat. His presidency fronted the organisation's move against Indira Gandhi, culminating in her expulsion in December 1977. When the loyalists' convention elected her president, Reddy claimed before the Election Commission that he, and not Indira Gandhi, remained president of the Congress, his own term having expired on 31 December 1977, and he asked the Commission to reserve the party's cow and calf symbol for his organisation. The Commission froze the symbol and allotted separate symbols to the two groups.

### Q: How did the Election Commission settle the dispute over the Congress name and symbol?

The Commission proceeded under the Election Symbols Order, applying the majority test the Supreme Court had approved in Sadiq Ali v. Election Commission of India in 1971: count each group's strength in the party's organisational and legislative wings. Unable to decide before the impending elections, it froze the cow and calf symbol in 1978 and allotted separate symbols to the Congress(I) and to the rival group, which by then was led by Devraj Urs as the Congress(U). After the verdict of 1980 it completed its enquiry and, by its order of 23 July 1981, recognised the Congress(I) as the Congress. The Supreme Court dismissed Urs's challenge on 14 August 1981.

### Q: What was the parallel-cadre architecture of the Congress(I)?

The parallel-cadre architecture was the Congress(I)'s second organisational spine: the Indian Youth Congress, rebuilt under Sanjay Gandhi, operated beside the formal committee structure with its own membership recruitment, its own district and state presidents and its own programme, and with reporting lines that ran to the leader's household rather than through the Pradesh Congress Committees. It gave the founding a workforce recruited on loyalty to the founding itself, counterweighted state committees staffed by the older generation, supplied the street presence for campaigns such as the protests against Indira Gandhi's imprisonment in December 1978, and formed the ladder by which Rajiv Gandhi later entered the organisation's command.

### Q: How many members of Parliament and state legislators joined the Congress(I) at its founding?

Between seventy and eighty members of the Lok Sabha, out of the 154 the Congress had returned in March 1977, adhered to the Congress(I) in the weeks after the convention of 1 and 2 January 1978, and a majority of the party's state legislators across the country did the same. The figures mattered beyond morale. The new entity had surrendered the central office, the funds and the organisational files to the residual leadership, and its legislative following was its principal countable asset, the evidence on which the Election Commission's majority test would eventually award it the Congress name in July 1981.

### Q: What did the assembly elections of February and March 1978 show about the two Congresses?

The elections, held in Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka and Maharashtra among other states, showed that the Congress vote followed the leader rather than the machinery. The Congress(I), contesting under a separate allotted symbol and without the inherited organisation, won clear majorities in Andhra Pradesh, where Marri Chenna Reddy became chief minister, and in Karnataka, where Devraj Urs returned as chief minister on 28 February 1978. The residual Congress, which held the office, the funds and the claim to institutional continuity, failed to carry any of the major states at stake, and its legislators began the drift toward the new entity that continued through 1979.

### Q: How did the Janata government's proceedings against Indira Gandhi affect the Congress(I) as an organisation?

The proceedings functioned as the young party's consolidation mechanism. The arrest of 3 October 1977, dissolved by a magistrate's release order the next day, the Shah Commission's public hearings through 1978, and the expulsion from the Lok Sabha and imprisonment of 19 December 1978 each converted a legal process into a loyalty test the organisation could pass publicly: workers courted arrest, the Youth Congress supplied crowds at short notice, and membership and fundraising were framed as defence of the leader. A party anchored in a person gains cohesion when the person is prosecuted, and the Janata government's campaign thereby strengthened the very architecture it was meant to destroy.

### Q: What was the Congress(U), and what became of it after 1981?

The Congress(U) was the vehicle Devraj Urs founded in July 1979 after leaving the Congress(I), gathering the older non-Indira leadership, Y.B. Chavan and Kasu Brahmananda Reddy among them, together with younger figures such as A.K. Antony and Sharad Pawar. It won thirteen Lok Sabha seats in 1980, and on 23 July 1981 the Election Commission held that it was not the Congress. Sharad Pawar took over its leadership in October 1981 and renamed it the Indian Congress (Socialist); Urs himself moved on to the Janata Party before his death in June 1982, and the remnant under Pawar merged into the Congress(I) in 1986.

### Q: How did the Congress(I) fill its party committees after 1978, by election or by nomination?

By nomination. The inherited Congress constitution prescribed organisational elections rising from the membership, and the old party had held them, however imperfectly, in its strong decades. The Congress(I) constituted its AICC, its Working Committee and its Pradesh Congress Committees by nomination from the centre, appointed and replaced Pradesh presidents at the leader's discretion, and treated internal elections as a dispensable form throughout the founding period and the years of government that followed. The practice was itself an architectural statement: in an anchored party, office is a delegation from the leader, not a mandate from a constituency inside the party, and nomination is the honest procedure of such a system.

### Q: Why is the Congress(I) committee structure described as ratificatory rather than deliberative?

Because the committees registered decisions instead of making them. In the Congress of the Syndicate era, the Working Committee bargained: regional leaders with independent followings argued, threatened and settled, and outcomes were genuinely uncertain before the meeting. In the Congress(I), the consequential decisions of the founding years, the extension of support to Charan Singh in July 1979 and its withdrawal on 20 August 1979, the selection of candidates for the 1980 election, the appointment of chief ministers, were settled by the leader and her secretariat and then placed before the committees, whose role was to endorse, publicise and implement. A body that reliably endorses what it did not decide is performing ratification, and the description records the fact without needing to deny the committees their administrative usefulness.

### Q: How did the Congress(I) rebuild its state organisations between 1978 and 1980?

The rebuilding followed the legislative following. Where legislators had crossed in strength, Pradesh committees were reconstituted around them under loyalist presidents; where the formal unit was weak or hostile, the Youth Congress network carried the party's presence until a committee could be staffed. The state governments won in February and March 1978, in Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka, gave the entity administrative anchors and patronage in the south, while the parliamentary party's growth at the centre supplied the model for wavering units elsewhere. After the verdict of January 1980 the flow reversed direction entirely, with legislators of the defeated formations, including much of Urs's Karnataka following, crossing back into the Congress(I).

### Q: Why did the Congress(I) claim the Congress name instead of adopting a new party name?

Because the name was the largest single asset in dispute. The Congress name carried the freedom movement's legitimacy, the default allegiance of voters who identified the party with the nation's founding, and the organisational memory of nine decades; to concede it would have been to concede continuity to the residual leadership and to ask the inherited Congress vote to transfer its loyalty to an untested label. The claim was also winnable on the law: the majority test of the Sadiq Ali judgment rewarded counted strength in the organisational and legislative wings, which the founding convention had been assembled to demonstrate. Indira Gandhi therefore pressed the claim by her letter of 7 January 1978 and held it until the Commission awarded her the name in 1981.

### Q: What did the withdrawal of Congress(I) support from the Charan Singh government in August 1979 reveal about how the party took decisions?

It revealed the anchor at work under maximum pressure. Charan Singh's government, sworn in on 28 July 1979, rested on Congress(I) support extended on conditions. When the conditions were not met, the support was withdrawn on 20 August 1979, on the eve of the confidence vote, by the leader's decision, announced without any recorded deliberation of the Working Committee or the parliamentary party, and every Congress(I) member of Parliament held to the line through the dissolution that followed on 22 August. A committee-governed party cannot move that fast or that unanimously on a question of that size; the episode is the cleanest single demonstration that the founding's decision structure functioned as designed.
