The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State is the book in which Friedrich Engels set out to explain how women came to be subordinated, and it did so on a foundation that has since given way entirely. Engels wrote it in 1884, drawing on the anthropology of Lewis Henry Morgan, whose Ancient Society had appeared seven years earlier and whose scheme of human development arranged every known people on a single ladder of stages. That ladder has been dismantled. The evolutionary stage scheme, the universal early stage of mother-right from which Engels derived his history, and the reconstructions of primitive marriage built from kinship vocabularies have all been rejected by the discipline whose evidence he borrowed. Any honest guide to the book has to begin there, in the open, and not in a footnote near the end: the anthropological basis of this text collapsed, and it collapsed comprehensively.

And yet the book would not go away. For a hundred years it was the socialist movement's standard treatment of the woman question, read wherever that movement organised, translated wherever it published, and quarried for arguments long after anthropologists had stopped citing Morgan except as a figure in their own discipline's history. Its programme, that women's emancipation requires entry into social production on a large scale together with the transfer of household labour to social institutions, passed into the policy of parties and then of states. Feminist scholars of a later generation fought over it in both directions, some editing and defending parts of it, others treating it as the point their own work had to leave behind. A book can be refuted in its evidence and remain load-bearing in its influence, and this one is the standing example. The task of this guide is the separation the book's readers have too often skipped: claim by claim, what depended on the collapsed foundation, what did not, and what follows for anyone picking it up now.

That separation has a name in what follows. The separable programme is the name this guide gives to the book's political conclusion, that emancipation requires both waged social production open to women and the socialisation of domestic work, and the name marks a logical fact: the programme does not stand or fall with Morgan's stages. One could grant every objection the anthropologists have made, surrender the matriarchy, the stage sequence and the kinship fossils entire, and the programme would still be there to be argued with on its own terms, because it is a claim about the economics of the household under capitalism and not a claim about prehistory. Conversely, one could defend the programme fiercely and gain nothing for the prehistory. Most writing on the book confuses these two ledgers, scoring points on one and banking them on the other. The claims audit set out later in this guide is the instrument for keeping them apart: a row for each major claim, the evidence its author relied on, the standing of that evidence, and a verdict.

Two boundaries should be fixed before the exposition begins, because the book is habitually credited and blamed for arguments housed elsewhere in this series. It is not a treatise on Marxist feminism as a whole, a tradition that grew by criticising and revising this text as much as by inheriting it; that wider story belongs to the [marxist feminism complete guide](/2016/09/01/marxist-feminism-complete-guide/). Nor is it a book about women alone, whatever its reputation says. Its subject, announced in its own title, is a triple emergence: kinship arrangements, private property and the state arising together and explaining one another, so that the chapters on the Greek and Roman gens and on the formation of states carry as much of the argument as the famous pages on marriage. The state side of that triangle has its own treatment in the [marxist theory of the state guide](/2012/12/01/marxist-theory-of-the-state-guide/), and Engels's life, standing and other writings belong to the [friedrich engels intellectual profile](/2013/05/15/friedrich-engels-intellectual-profile/). What remains here is the book itself: its making, its argument, its evidence, its verdicts and its residue.

## A book written out of another man's notebooks

The Origin of the Family is a posthumous collaboration, and its strangeness as a text begins with that fact. Karl Marx died in March 1883. Among the papers he left were the excerpt notebooks in which, across his final years, he had worked through the ethnological literature of the period with the density he brought to everything: long extracts from Morgan's Ancient Society, from Henry Sumner Maine on early law, from John Budd Phear on Ceylon, from John Lubbock on prehistoric peoples, all interleaved with his own marginal comments, some admiring, some acid. Marx published nothing from this labour. It was Engels, sorting the manuscripts of his friend and literary executor in everything but name, who found the Morgan excerpts and recognised in them the skeleton of a book Marx had apparently intended to write, presenting Morgan's material in connection with the conclusions of the materialist method. Engels decided to write that book himself, quickly, as an act of intellectual executorship. In the preface he described the work, with a modesty that was also a claim, as the fulfilment of a bequest.

The writing was fast even by Engels's standards. He completed the manuscript in roughly two months in the spring of 1884, working, as he put it, from Marx's extensive excerpts rather than from a fresh reading of the whole literature, and the book appeared at Zurich before the year was out, since Bismarck's Anti-Socialist Laws made publication inside Germany hazardous for a text of this kind. The haste matters to a reader because it explains the book's peculiar texture: it is a synthesis written at speed by a man of seventy-hour weeks and formidable memory, fusing three bodies of material that did not naturally fuse. The first is Morgan, supplying the architecture. The second is Marx's notebooks, supplying emphases and some judgements, including the celebrated marginal verdicts on the hypocrisies of bourgeois marriage that Engels wove into his own prose. The third is Engels himself, who had been thinking about the family, property and the state since the 1840s, who had sketched the subordination of women as a historical product in earlier work with Marx, and who brought to the ethnology a polemicist's instinct for the politically usable conclusion.

### What did Marx leave behind for Engels to work from?

Notebooks of excerpts, not a draft. Across his last years Marx filled notebooks with extracts from the ethnological writers, Morgan above all, surrounding the extracts with his commentary, approval and ridicule. Engels found these among the papers, treated them as the plan of an unwritten book, and executed the plan himself within a year of Marx's death.

The notebooks themselves have a publication history that a researcher needs, because arguments about what Marx thought, as against what Engels wrote, turn on them. The Morgan excerpts were first published in full in 1972, when the anthropologist Lawrence Krader edited them as The Ethnological Notebooks of Karl Marx, and scholars have since mined the volume for evidence of the late Marx's interests: his attention to communal property forms, his resistance to reading European categories into non-European institutions, his doubts about unilinear schemes even as he excerpted the century's most unilinear anthropologist. That tension is real and should be stated plainly, because it complicates the pious picture of a book simply dictating itself from the notebooks. Marx's excerpts are critical as often as they are receptive. Engels's book is smoother, more systematic and more confident than the material it was built from, and the difference between the two textures is itself a finding about the text. The manuscript scholarship surrounding the notebooks, including their place in the great scholarly edition of the Marx and Engels corpus, is treated in the [mega2 marx manuscripts guide](/2013/07/01/mega2-marx-manuscripts-guide/), which is the right door for readers who want the documentary trail rather than the argument.

Lewis Henry Morgan, the book's second author in all but title, was a Rochester lawyer who had been adopted into the Seneca nation, had written a pioneering study of the Iroquois confederacy, and had then spent decades assembling, through missionaries, officials and correspondents across the world, schedules of kinship terminology: the words each people used for relatives. From those schedules, and from the ethnographic reports of the period, he built Ancient Society, published in 1877, the most ambitious work of evolutionary anthropology of its century. Its governing idea was that human institutions develop through a fixed sequence of stages, savagery, barbarism and civilisation, each subdivided, each marked by characteristic inventions, subsistence forms and institutions, and that family forms develop in a parallel sequence driven by changing rules of marriage and descent. Engels took over this architecture nearly whole: the subtitle of his book announces that it is written in the light of the researches of Lewis H. Morgan, an acknowledgement quoted here in a shortened fragment because the full German phrasing runs long, and the acknowledgement is exact. Where Morgan supplied stages, Engels supplied a motor: the development of production, the rise of private property, and the class antagonisms that property creates.

### Why does the subtitle carry an American anthropologist's name?

Because the architecture is his. The stage sequence, the succession of family forms, the gens as the unit of primitive organisation and the kinship evidence beneath the reconstructions all come from Morgan's Ancient Society, and Engels, who added property and class as the engine of the sequence, said so on the title page rather than absorbing the debt silently.

A third source deserves naming here, because the book's most consequential idea came from neither Morgan nor Marx. The notion of mother-right, of an early order in which descent and inheritance ran through women and women held high social standing, came from Johann Jakob Bachofen, a Swiss jurist whose Das Mutterrecht of 1861 argued from classical myth and ancient legal survivals that a matriarchal order had preceded patriarchy in the Mediterranean world. Bachofen was a mystical conservative, about as far from a socialist as the century afforded, and his method, reading myths as encrypted history, was speculative even by the standards of the time. A parallel line of speculation ran through the Scottish lawyer John Ferguson McLennan, whose Primitive Marriage of 1865 coined terms that stuck, exogamy and endogamy among them, and who quarrelled with Bachofen's construction even while building his own. Engels fused Bachofen's mother-right with Morgan's gens and kinship schedules and presented the compound as established science. That act of fusion is the original sin of the book's evidence: two speculative systems, each contested in its own field, were bolted together and the bolts hidden by the confidence of the prose. The claims audit will return to this, because several of the collapsed claims trace to exactly this joint in the structure.

The book that resulted went through four editions in Engels's lifetime, and the fourth, of 1891, matters textually: Engels revised it, added material, and supplied a new preface on the early history of the family that engaged some of the accumulating evidence and criticism. Readers citing the book are usually citing that fourth edition, in one of its translations, and the prefaces of 1884 and 1891 frame it as a work its author knew was built on moving ground. He said, in substance, that the material was new and the treatment provisional. The provisional character is worth remembering when the audit turns severe, not as an excuse, since Engels's followers rarely treated the book as provisional, but as a fact about the gap between the text and its canonisation.

## The argument in its own order

Close reading has to precede auditing, because the audit's verdicts only make sense against the argument as its author actually built it, and the argument is more carefully built than its dismissers usually allow. Its opening move is methodological, and it is the move on which everything else depends. Against the assumption, then standard in law, theology and political economy, that the monogamous family of the propertied classes is a natural and timeless form, Engels insists that family forms are historical products. They differ across peoples and across periods, they change with the mode of subsistence and the organisation of property, and they are therefore to be explained, not assumed. In 1884 this was a fighting proposition. The family of the Victorian propertied household presented itself as the terminus of moral progress and the pattern of nature; a literature that treated it instead as one arrangement among many, explicable by the same material forces that explain law and government, was striking at the foundations of respectable social thought. Whatever has happened to the evidence, that opening move has been completely absorbed by the disciplines that rejected the rest: no anthropologist or historian of the family now treats any family form as nature's default. The reader should register the point early, since the claims audit will mark this claim as surviving, and it is not a small survival.

The architecture Engels borrows from Morgan then organises the exposition. Human prehistory is divided into savagery, barbarism and civilisation, each stage in three grades, with the transitions marked by inventions and subsistence revolutions: fire and fishing, the bow, pottery, animal domestication and agriculture, iron smelting, and finally the phonetic alphabet and writing. Within this frame the family passes through its own sequence. First comes what Morgan called the consanguine family, in which marriage groups are organised by generations and only the union of parents and children is excluded. Then the punaluan family, named from a Hawaiian term Morgan took to describe group marriage among sets of brothers and sisters, in which siblings are progressively excluded. Then the pairing family, in which a man and woman form a recognisable couple but the bond is loose, dissoluble by either side, and embedded in a wider kin group that, not the couple, raises and provisions the children. Last, with civilisation, comes the monogamian family: the strict, legally enforced union of one man and one woman, founded, in Engels's account, not on affection or sexual exclusivity in general but on the concentration of wealth in the man's hands and the need to transmit that wealth to heirs of certain paternity.

### What sequence of family forms does the book lay out?

Four, in a fixed order taken from Morgan: a consanguine family organised by generations, a punaluan family of group marriage that progressively excludes siblings, a pairing family of loose and dissoluble couples within a wider kin household, and finally the monogamian family, strict and property bound, arising with civilisation and private wealth.

Running beneath the family sequence, and in Engels's handling more important than it, is the gens: the clan, the kin group tracing descent in the female line in its early form, holding property in common, governed by its own elected and deposable chiefs, and forming the cellular unit of what Engels, following Morgan, calls gentile society. The gens is the hero of the book's middle chapters. Morgan had reconstructed it from his Iroquois material, where he found descent reckoned through women, chiefs chosen from within the clan and removable by it, and a social order that functioned, in Engels's admiring paraphrase, without soldiers, police, prisons or courts of compulsion. Engels reads the gens as proof that elaborate social organisation, including what he is happy to call a kind of primitive democracy, preceded the state and made the state necessary only when property and class division had corroded the gentile order from within. The chapters on the Greek gens, on Athens, on Rome, on the Celts and Germans, then trace, case by case, how growing wealth, trade, slavery and private appropriation dissolve the gens and precipitate the territorial state in its place.

The reader who knows the series will notice that this stage machinery raises a general problem about periodising human history by modes of production and social forms, a problem that reaches well beyond this book: how much violence a universal sequence does to the variety of actual histories. That general question is treated in the guide to the [mode of production explained](/2010/02/15/mode-of-production-explained/), and it is flagged here rather than reopened, because for this text the question has a sharper, local form. Engels's sequence does not merely simplify variety; it asserts a single order of succession through which every people must pass, and asserts it on evidence, the kinship schedules, that was supposed to make the succession readable in the present like strata in rock. The strata turned out not to be strata. But that is the audit's business; the exposition has first to finish the argument, whose centrepiece is the transition that destroyed the gentile order and subordinated women in a single movement.

That centrepiece is the overthrow of mother-right. In the pairing family and the gens, descent, on Engels's account, ran through the mother, because under conditions of group marriage and loose pairing paternity was unknowable, and only the maternal line could be traced with certainty. Children belonged to the mother's gens; inheritance of the gens's common property passed within it; women, as the known line of descent and the managers of the communal household, held a standing that Engels describes in terms of high respect and real authority. Then came the domestication of animals, herds, slaves, and surplus: wealth, and wealth of a new kind, held by individual men, since the herds and the instruments of production fell to the man's sphere in the division of labour the book describes. The man now wanted his property to pass to his own children, and that required a revolution in the descent rule: reckoning through the father, paternity made certain, and monogamy imposed, on the woman, to guarantee it. Mother-right was overthrown and father-right installed, and Engels gives the event its famous name.

### What is mother-right, as the book uses the term?

A rule of descent and inheritance, not a government of women. Under mother-right, children belong to the mother's clan, descent is traced through women because paternity cannot be established, and what property the clan holds passes within the maternal line. Engels treats it as the original form of the gens and the basis of women's standing within it.

The defeat formulation must be quoted carefully if it is quoted at all, and handled as the book's thesis sentence. Engels writes that the overthrow of mother-right was, in his phrase, "the world historical defeat of the female sex", and the sentences around it extend the claim: the man seized the reins in the house as well, the woman was degraded and reduced to servitude, and the first class opposition in history coincides, he argues, with the antagonism between man and woman in monogamous marriage. Three distinct claims are packed into this celebrated passage, and the audit will separate them, because their fates differ. There is a historical claim, that a universal order of mother-right existed and was overthrown as private property arose. There is a conceptual claim, that the subordination of women is a historical event with causes, and therefore neither natural nor eternal. And there is an analogy pressed into an identity, that the husband within the monogamous family stands to the wife as bourgeois to proletarian, the first class oppression being the oppression of the female by the male sex. The historical claim depended on Bachofen and Morgan and has fallen with them. The conceptual claim is the one that reorganised feminist thought. The identity claim has been argued over ever since, including by the Marxist feminists who inherited the book and found the analogy illuminating as metaphor and indefensible as analysis.

Monogamy, in the book's account, must then be read with its asymmetry exposed, because Engels is emphatic that the monogamy introduced by property was monogamy for the woman only. Its purpose being the production of heirs whose paternity is beyond dispute, the wife's fidelity is enforced by law, religion and economic dependence, while the husband's corresponding infidelity is institutionalised in concubinage and prostitution, which the book treats not as aberrations of the monogamous order but as its necessary complements, the shadow cast by the institution itself. The discussion of prostitution is among the book's most quoted and least dated passages of social criticism, since it requires no anthropology at all: it is an analysis of a double standard and its economic basis in the Athens and the Europe Engels could observe. He draws on Greek evidence, the hetaira as the recognised companion of the citizen whose wife was sequestered, and on the legal position of women in Rome and in the Europe of his own century, where the Napoleonic code and its imitators made the wife a legal minor in property. Whatever the verdict on the prehistory, this stratum of the book is social history of a recognisable kind, and its method, treating sexual morality as a function of property arrangements, remains the book's most portable analytical habit.

Against this property bound monogamy Engels sets, in the pairing family and among peoples without significant private wealth, a sexual life he portrays as freer and more equal, and he predicts, famously, that with the abolition of private property in the means of production monogamy will not disappear but will for the first time be realised: freed from economic compulsion, the union of man and woman can rest on individual affection alone, and a generation will arise that has never known the purchase or sale of sexual access. The prophecy is worth pausing on because it displays the book's characteristic argumentative shape, which the reader will meet again in the programme. A historical claim about origins is made to license a claim about the future: because monogamy was made by property, it can be remade by property's abolition. The form of inference is the vulnerable point, and critics from the anthropologists to the feminists have pressed on it. Origins do not govern destinies in that direct way, and an institution that arose for one reason may persist for others. The separable programme, when this guide reaches it, is precisely the part of the book that does not need this inference.

## The household inside the argument

The book's second engine, quieter than the defeat thesis and more durable, is its treatment of the household as an economic institution. In the gentile order, Engels argues, the household was communal: a group of families managing production and consumption together, with the labour of women at its centre and publicly recognised as socially necessary. The monogamous family of private property shrank that household to a single couple and converted its labour into a private service. His formulation, paraphrased here because the original sentence runs beyond a fair quotation, is that within the modern family the wife's domestic work has become a private service excluded from social production, the husband representing the possessing class and the wife the dispossessed within the home's own small economy. The point that matters is not the analogy, which the audit will grade severely, but the reframing: housework is work, its privatisation is a historical arrangement with economic causes, and the dependence of the wife is manufactured by her exclusion from production for society rather than decreed by nature.

This reframing had a long fuse. When feminist economists and sociologists of the nineteen seventies set out to theorise unpaid domestic labour, in what became known as the domestic labour debate, they found in Engels the one classical text that had treated the household as a unit of economic life to be explained rather than a natural background to the economy proper. The debate's participants argued fiercely about whether housework produces value, a question this book does not settle and barely poses in those terms, but the standing of the question, the insistence that the kitchen and the nursery belong inside political economy, descends from this text. The successor framework that reorganised the same territory under a different theory, social reproduction theory, has its own exposition in the guide to [social reproduction theory explained](/2016/09/15/social-reproduction-theory-explained/), and the relationship between the two is one of revision rather than continuation: social reproduction theory keeps the household inside the analysis of capitalism while discarding the prehistory and the property origin story almost entire. That pattern, keep the reframing and discard the anthropology, is the separable pattern again, and its recurrence across the book's afterlife is the strongest evidence that the pattern names something real in the text rather than a charity invented by its defenders.

### How does the book make housework visible as labour?

By historicising the household. Where the household is communal and its work done openly for the kin group, domestic labour counts as social production, Engels argues, and women hold standing accordingly. Where property shrinks the household to one couple, the same labour becomes a private service, invisible to the economy and a ground of dependence.

The programme follows from the reframing with unusual directness, and it is the book's final and most consequential argument. If the subordination of women rests on their exclusion from social production and their confinement to privatised domestic labour, then emancipation has two material conditions, and Engels states both. The first is the reintroduction of women into public industry on a large scale: women must take part, in his phrase, "in production on a large, social scale". The second is the transformation of domestic work itself: cooking, cleaning, laundering and the care of children must cease to be the private burden of individual wives and become social functions, organised as public services, so that private housekeeping no longer claims more than an insignificant portion of women's time. Neither condition is presented as a favour to be granted; both are presented as tendencies that large scale industry itself creates, by drawing women into factories while making the individual household an anachronism, and the role of socialism is to complete a movement that capitalism begins and blocks. The two conditions together are what this guide calls the separable programme, and their independence is the point to hold: nothing in either condition mentions Morgan, mother-right or the gens. They are claims about the organisation of labour under capitalism and its possible re organisation under socialism, assessable by the evidence of wage labour, public services and household technology, and they were so assessed, by supporters and critics, for the century that followed.

It is worth being exact about what the programme is not, because misreading collects here. It is not a prediction that the state will wither the family away by decree; Engels expects the family form to change as its economic basis changes, and he is scornful of legislative moralising. It is not a claim that wage work as it exists under capitalism is itself emancipation; the book is clear that the factory draws women in on terms of exploitation, and that the first condition without the second merely doubles the burden, a point later feminists would develop into the analysis of the double day. And it is not a claim that sexual and emotional life will be administered by the collective; the famous prediction is the opposite, that unions freed from property considerations will be formed and dissolved by the parties alone. The programme is an institutional claim about two kinds of labour, and its clarity on that point is a large part of why it could be taken up as policy, by movements and governments that had no interest in the prehistory from which its author had derived it.

## The claims audit

Exposition over, the audit can begin, and it should be conducted the way this guide promised at the outset: row by row, with the evidence named and the verdicts unsparing where unsparing is due. The claims audit is the findable artifact of this article, and it is offered as a reading instrument as much as a judgement: a student who works through the book with the table open will know, for every major assertion, whether they are reading living argument or the archaeology of an argument. The rows are ordered by their position in the book's structure rather than by severity, so that the pattern of survival can be seen as it develops: the foundations fail first, and the further a claim stands from the prehistory, the better it tends to fare.

Three clarifications belong with the table, and a reader who skips them will misuse it. First, abandoned in this audit means abandoned as a claim about the human past, not expunged from intellectual history: the stage scheme organised two generations of inquiry and still structures the way the nineteenth century is studied. Second, survives in modified form is not a diplomatic grade. The inheritance row illustrates what it means concretely. Engels claimed a tight causal chain from property through paternity anxiety to monogamy and female subordination. The comparative historian Jack Goody, working a century later with African and Eurasian material, argued that systems of property transmission, diverging devolution to children of both sexes, plough agriculture and the control of women's sexuality do indeed form a connected complex in Eurasian societies, while rejecting any universal stage sequence and locating the mechanism in specific productive systems rather than in a general drama of mother-right overthrown. That is a modification so substantial that the surviving content is a research programme, a direction of questions about property and kinship, rather than a doctrine. Third, several rows that the audit grades as surviving survive as descriptions of particular legal orders rather than as laws of development, and the table marks this where it matters: the double standard row rests on Athens, Rome and the European codes, evidence a historian can inspect, and its strength is exactly the strength of that inspectable record.

### Which rows of the audit carry the severest verdicts?

The first four. The universal stage sequence, the universal priority of mother-right, the matriarchy inferred from it, and the reading of kinship vocabularies as fossil records all rest on the Morgan and Bachofen apparatus, and all four fall with it. The verdicts soften as the rows move away from prehistory toward property law and the household: the audit's central pattern.

The pattern in the table deserves to be stated as a finding, because it is not the pattern either camp in the book's long quarrel expects. Dismissive readings expect uniform ruin: a book built on Morgan should fall as Morgan fell. Defensive readings expect a core that survives intact, a set of theses that can be lifted out shining. The audit shows something less tidy and more instructive. Survival correlates with distance from the borrowed science. Where Engels is doing prehistory with Morgan's tools, he is wrong in the ways Morgan was wrong, and wrong with total confidence. Where he is doing the history of law and institutions with evidence open to inspection, he is frequently penetrating and sometimes wrong in interesting, local ways. Where he is theorising the household of his own century and prescribing for it, he is independent of the prehistory altogether. The book is therefore not one book but three, of descending evidential dependence and ascending durability, bound in a single cover and written in a single confident voice that gives the reader no signal when the ground changes. That absence of signalling is the deepest justification for the audit as a form: the text will not tell you where it stands, so the table must.

## The objections, with their authors named

The brief for this article imposes a discipline on the criticism section that this guide adopts as its own: the objections are to be attributed to named scholarship, not waved through as the verdict of a discipline in the abstract. The discipline matters here not as pedantry but because the objections are of different kinds, made by different people at different times, and some of them cut at Engels while others cut at the entire nineteenth century's way of doing anthropology, Engels included only as its most famous political reader. Separating them is the difference between a prosecution and an education.

The first objection is the deepest and belongs to Franz Boas and the school he founded in American anthropology. Boas's methodological writings, from the eighteen nineties onward, attacked the comparative method on which Morgan's scheme rested: the practice of tearing institutions from their contexts, arranging the fragments on an evolutionary ladder, and reading the ladder as history. Against it Boas argued that similar institutions arise from unlike causes, that each people's institutions must be understood through its own particular history of contact, borrowing and invention, and that a universal sequence of stages is an artefact of the arrangement rather than a discovery about the past. The Boasian objection does not merely correct Morgan's order of stages; it denies that the evidence, ethnographic snapshots of living peoples, can be converted into a chronology at all. If Boas is right, and the discipline largely concluded that on this point he was, then the entire prehistoric edifice of The Origin of the Family is not a false history but a category error: an arrangement of contemporaneous diversity mistaken for a sequence in time. Engels cannot be rescued from this by better data, because the objection is to the inferential machine, not to its fuel.

The second objection is narrower, empirical and in its way more damaging, because it operates inside Morgan's own evidential stronghold. Robert Lowie, Boas's student, devoted much of his Primitive Society of 1920 to dismantling the Morgan reconstructions point by point. The kinship schedules, on which the succession of family forms rested, would not bear the weight placed on them: Lowie showed that the classificatory terminologies Morgan treated as survivals of group marriage could be explained by the logic of the terminology systems themselves and by the social groupings of the present, without postulating vanished marriage forms behind them. The gens likewise refused to behave as a universal primitive unit with a fixed history. Lowie's tone is instructive: he treats Morgan with respect as a founder and then removes, one by one, the props of the system, in a manner that leaves the reader in no doubt that the removal is permanent. It is Lowie, more than any single writer, whom a reader should have in mind when the audit records the kinship rows as abandoned.

A third objection inverts the book rather than dismantling it, and it belongs to Edward Westermarck, the Finnish anthropologist whose History of Human Marriage appeared in 1891, seven years after Engels's book and within its author's lifetime. Westermarck argued that monogamy is not a late product of property but primitive: that the durable pair bond, guarded by male jealousy and rooted in the long dependence of human young, precedes property and stages alike, and that group marriage of the Morgan kind is a fiction projected onto peoples whose customs had been misreported or misunderstood. Westermarck's own positive theory has not worn well either; the appeal to instinct explains too much too easily, and his readings of the ethnographic record were as selective as his opponents'. But his objection matters because it demonstrates that the ethnographic evidence of the period could sustain exactly contrary constructions in competent hands, which is itself a verdict on the evidence. Where the same reports yield Morgan's sequence and Westermarck's inversion, the reports are not deciding the question, and a book that presents them as deciding it has overstated its warrant. Engels engaged the controversy in the preface to his fourth edition, adjusting details while holding the scheme; the adjustment reads, against Westermarck's massed counter instances, as a defence of the perimeter while the centre was being surveyed by the enemy.

The fourth objection arrives later and cuts deeper than the stage scheme, at the category of kinship itself. Claude Lévi-Strauss, in The Elementary Structures of Kinship of 1949, rebuilt the study of kinship around alliance: the exchange of spouses between groups, governed by the prohibition of incest, as the elementary social act. In that rebuilding, the question Morgan and Engels asked, which form of marriage came first, dissolves into a structural analysis in which kinship systems are systems of communication and exchange, intelligible in their own logic rather than as stages of anything. David Schneider pressed the dissolution further in his Critique of the Study of Kinship, arguing that kinship as Western scholars defined it, a universal domain grounded in the facts of blood and reproduction, is itself a cultural artefact of the observers, projected onto peoples whose own categories organise relatedness otherwise. Schneider's critique, if accepted, does not just falsify Engels's reconstructions; it withdraws the ground from the question of a universal family history, since the family whose history was to be traced is not a constant object across cultures. A guide should record, in fairness, that Schneider's position has itself been contested within anthropology, and that kinship studies after him rebuilt parts of the field on new foundations. But the rebuilt field does not rebuild Morgan. On the specific apparatus Engels used, the scholarly verdict delivered across these four objections is not in serious dispute, and this article has stated it in the audit without varnish.

### How did the Boasian objection differ from the others?

In kind. Lowie corrected the reconstructions from within the same evidential enterprise; Westermarck inverted the sequence while keeping the idea of one. Boas denied the enterprise itself: arranging living institutions into a ladder of stages was, for him, a methodological illusion, so the failure lies not in the data but in the inference from present diversity to past chronology.

One further attribution is required for completeness, because the strongest statement of the objection that the brief asks this article to confront comes not from any single anthropologist but from the accumulated literature the four objections represent. It runs as follows, and this guide states it at the strength its proponents would want. A book whose entire evidential apparatus has been rejected, whose chronology is a category error, whose kinship evidence does not record what it was said to record, and whose central historical event, the overthrow of a universal mother-right, is an occurrence for which no evidence exists, retains its authority, where it retains it, through the political tradition that adopted it. Its continued citation in that tradition is therefore a case study in how canons protect texts: the movement needed a doctrine of the family, the book supplied one with the prestige of science, and the prestige outlived the science by a century because the institution, not the evidence, was doing the preserving. That objection deserves to be felt. It explains something real, the sociology of the book's survival in party education long after the scholarly ground had gone, and any defence that does not reckon with it is a defence of a different, easier book.

## The defences, with their authors named

The steelman discipline runs both ways in this article, and the defences of the book's components must now be given with the same specificity as the attacks, credited to the scholars who actually made them, with the limits those scholars themselves acknowledged. The defences are of three kinds, and they do not add up to a rehabilitation: a defence of the ethnography in part, a defence of the method and questions, and a defence conducted by rebuilding the argument on new evidence. Each is instructive about which part of the book it had to leave undefended in order to proceed.

The most consequential defender was Eleanor Leacock, the American anthropologist who edited the standard English edition of the book in 1972 with a long, scholarly introduction, and who defended its core in her own fieldwork based writings, collected in Myths of Male Dominance. Leacock's position is the model of a partial defence conducted honestly. She granted that Morgan's stage scheme was superseded and that the book's prehistory could not stand as written. What she defended was a different stratum: the claim that in egalitarian foraging societies, of the kind she had studied among the Montagnais Naskapi of Labrador, the relations between women and men were substantially more equal than in class societies, that women controlled their own labour and its products, and that the subordination of women is therefore tied historically to the emergence of stratification and private property in the broad sense, even if Engels's specific mechanism and sequence were wrong. Her ethnography documented how colonial trade and mission pressure had themselves eroded the autonomy she described, which gave her defence an empirical spine Engels lacked: a documented process, running in the observable direction, from relative equality toward subordination as property and hierarchy advanced. Readers should notice what Leacock's defence concedes by its shape. It saves the book's directional intuition by abandoning its chronology, its universality and its mechanism, and it relocates the claim from a single prehistoric drama to a recurring, inspectable relationship between property, stratification and the standing of women.

Karen Sacks, writing as a feminist anthropologist in the nineteen seventies and later publishing under the name Karen Brodkin, took a different route in her widely read essay Engels Revisited. Sacks argued that the book's framework could be repaired from inside by distinguishing two statuses that Engels had run together: women's position as wives within a domestic unit and their position as sisters within a kin network that gave them independent standing, rights and allies. On her reading, the decisive historical variable is whether adult women participate in social production as full social adults, and her revision preserved the book's organising intuition, that women's oppression is a function of their place in production and property, while discarding the mother-right machinery and correcting the book's tendency to treat the wife's position as the whole of women's position. The essay is a useful exhibit for the audit's method, because it shows a defender performing the separation operation on the text in public: this part is the living argument, this part is the scaffolding, and the scaffolding can go.

The third kind of defence is reconstruction, and its exemplary instance is the volume Women's Work, Men's Property, edited by Stephanie Coontz and Peta Henderson in 1986, in which anthropologists and historians set out, explicitly, to ask whether an Engels style argument could be rebuilt with the evidence available a century later. The contributors took up the correlation Engels asserted, between the development of private property, class stratification and the institutional subordination of women, and tested it against records he never had: the early state societies of Mesopotamia, the legal archives of the ancient Near East, the ethnography of stratified chiefdoms. Their collective conclusion, roughened by disagreements the editors did not hide, was that something like the correlation survives disciplined statement: the rise of heritable property and class does tend to bring intensified control over women's labour, mobility and sexuality, through identifiable mechanisms rather than a single overthrow. What does not survive the rebuilding is every element of the dramatic form: no universal mother-right, no single defeat, no sequence. The reconstruction is the strongest defence the book has received precisely because it is the most conditional, and a reader who wants the book's central intuition in its most defensible modern form should go to that literature rather than to the text of 1884.

Two further names belong in the record because their uses of the book shaped its feminist afterlife as much as any defence. Kathleen Gough, an anthropologist who had worked on the Nayar of Kerala and on kinship in changing societies, wrote on the origin of the family with sympathy for the historical questions Engels had posed and with an anthropologist's impatience for his answers, insisting that the near universality of male dominance, which any honest account must face, required explanations that ran through the sexual division of labour and the organisation of child rearing rather than through a single property event. Gayle Rubin's essay The Traffic in Women, published in 1975 and among the most influential feminist texts of its decade, performed the book's most famous partial appropriation: Rubin took from Engels, and from Lévi-Strauss, the insight that kinship systems are systems in which women are transacted, and coined the term sex and gender system for the arrangements by which a society transforms biological sexuality into social subordination, while explicitly discarding the evolutionary apparatus and the expectation that economic change alone would dissolve the system. Rubin's essay is the cleanest demonstration available that the collapse of the anthropology did not end the book's usefulness even to its critics: what she kept was the structural gesture, the treatment of kinship and marriage as a system with its own logic of exchange and power, and what she discarded was everything Morgan had supplied.

### What did Leacock's defence concede, and why does the concession matter?

Nearly everything specific. The stage scheme, the universal mother-right, the mechanism of a single overthrow: all surrendered. What she defended was the directional claim that women's standing falls as stratification and private property advance, supported by her fieldwork. The concession matters because the book's most credible defender saved it by performing the separation this audit performs, not by denying the collapse.

The answer to the strongest objection can now be given in its proper form, and it has two parts, one of concession and one of rebuttal. The concession is the larger part. The objection from the canon is correct about the sociology: within the political tradition that adopted the book, its authority was institutional long after its evidence was gone, citations in party literature were acts of loyalty more often than of inquiry, and the book is, in the plain sense, cited far more often than its evidential status warrants. A guide that flinched from that sentence would be joining the protection racket it describes. The rebuttal is narrower, and it is the rebuttal the whole audit exists to make precise. The objection proves too much when it moves from the canon to the text. The text contains claims of three evidential kinds, and only the first kind, the prehistory, depends on the rejected apparatus. The second kind, the social history of the double standard and of wives' legal position, rests on evidence any reader can inspect in the legal record. The third, the programme, rests on the organisation of labour under capitalism and invites assessment by a century of policy experiment, favourable and unfavourable, that the following section reviews. To say that the canon protected the book is true; to say that nothing in the book survives its protection racket is to repeat, in the critical direction, the same refusal to separate that the canon practised in the defensive one. The separable programme is the name for what the refusal misses.

## Mother-right, matriliny and the missing matriarchy

No cluster of errors around this book is more persistent, in popular summary and in polemic alike, than the slide from mother-right to matriarchy, and the close reading this guide exists to provide should slow down and take the distinction apart with care. Three terms are in play, and they name three different things. Mother-right, in the usage Engels took from Bachofen and Morgan, is a rule of descent and inheritance: children belong to the mother's kin group, membership and what property passes are reckoned through women, and the mother's brother, not the father, is the senior male authority in a child's life. Matriliny is the modern anthropological term for descent reckoned in the female line, and it describes a documented and widespread arrangement found, among other places, among the Akan of Ghana, the Minangkabau of Sumatra, the Mosuo of southwestern China and, in Morgan's own material, the Haudenosaunee, the Iroquois peoples whose confederacy he studied. Matriarchy is a different claim altogether: a social order in which women as a sex hold political rule, as men do under patriarchy. The first two terms describe kinship bookkeeping. The third describes a distribution of power. The book's argument, and nearly all careless argument about the book, depends on sliding from the bookkeeping to the power.

### Was there ever a matriarchal stage of society?

Anthropology does not support it. The claim of a universal earlier stage of mother-right, which Engels took from nineteenth-century sources, has been rejected, along with the evolutionary stage scheme it belonged to. Matrilineal descent exists and is not the same as matriarchy.

The ethnographic record, examined without the stage scheme's demands, is uncooperative with the slide in both directions. Matrilineal societies are real and numerous, and in several of them women hold substantial economic and ceremonial standing: Minangkabau women, for example, hold the rice land and the houses in the female line and occupy the centre of the kinship system, in a society often described as the largest matrilineal order in the world. Yet formal political office among the Minangkabau rests overwhelmingly with men, with the mother's brothers and appointed male lineage heads conducting public affairs. Among the Haudenosaunee, clan mothers nominated and could depose the male chiefs of the confederacy, a real and remarkable constitutional power, exercised within a system in which the council benches themselves were occupied by men. The pattern repeats: matriliny reorganises men's authority, routing it through the avunculate, the authority of the maternal uncle, rather than abolishing it, and it coexists with every degree of female standing from the formidable to the subordinate. This is the cleanest empirical refutation of the matriarchy thesis available, and it has the additional bite that much of it was latent in Morgan's own Iroquois material, where descent ran through women and councils were filled by men, had the material been read without Bachofen's mythic template held over it.

Engels himself is not innocent of the slide, but he is less guilty of it than his popularisers. His considered claim is that under mother-right women held a position of high respect and real influence within the gens, grounded in their control of the communal household and their position as the known line of descent; he speaks of the power of women in the house and of their honoured standing in the clan, and the famous defeat is the loss of that position, not the fall of a female state. The word matriarchy does the work in later summaries, his own and others', that his argument does not strictly need, and the audit has graded the claim at the strength at which it was asserted: asserted as a stage, it fails. What remains genuinely open, and should be reported as open rather than settled in either direction, is the comparative question Leacock pressed: how the standing of women varies with the organisation of property and production across documented societies. That question survives the matriarchy's fall because it never depended on a matriarchy. It depends only on variation, and variation is the one thing the ethnographic record supplies in abundance.

Two misreadings that attach to this section of the book deserve explicit correction, since they recur in examination answers and in journalism. The first is that Engels proved an original matriarchy: he argued for a stage of mother-right from sources he trusted, the argument failed with the sources, and proof was never available in the material. The second is the mirror error, that because no matriarchy existed the book's whole account of women's standing under gentile communism was fantasy. The ethnographic record does contain societies of substantial sexual equality, particularly among foragers, and contains wide variation in every direction; the fantasy was the universal sequence, not the variation. A reader who can hold both corrections at once has understood the audit's method, which is to grade the claim at the level at which it was made and resist the gravitational pull, in both camps, toward verdicts on the book entire.

## The programme's century

The separable programme would be a logical curiosity if it had stayed on the page. It did not. It became, in the century after publication, the nearest thing the socialist movement possessed to a doctrine of the family, and its career in policy is the fairest test of the audit's claim that the programme is independent of the prehistory: governments and movements applied it, amended it and in part reversed it, without any reference to Morgan, because the levers it named, women's employment and the social organisation of domestic work, were levers a ministry could actually pull.

The transmission began before the book. August Bebel's Woman and Socialism, first published in 1879, five years before Engels's treatise, had already carried the argument that women's emancipation required economic independence and the socialisation of household labour to an enormous working class readership, and it remained, through dozens of editions, the movement's most widely read text on the question, absorbing Engels's authority after 1884 in its later revisions. Clara Zetkin, who edited the Social Democratic women's paper Die Gleichheit and built the socialist women's movement in Germany, worked in the same framework, insisting that the woman question would be settled by women's entry into production and by social provision, not by alliance with bourgeois feminism on the suffrage question alone. When the Second International's parties wrote programmes, the two conditions of 1884 are what they wrote: protective and equal access to employment on one side, public provision for the work of the household on the other. The anthropology travelled in the baggage, cited in popular editions and party schools, but the operative doctrine was the programme, and its operatives never had to decide whether mother-right had existed in order to demand a municipal laundry.

The programme's largest field test came in Soviet Russia after 1917, and it deserves a factual summary rather than either of the legends, heroic or horrified, that attach to it. The early Soviet government legislated at speed in the programme's direction: civil marriage and divorce on demand, the legal equality of spouses, the abolition of illegitimacy as a legal category, and in 1920 the legalisation of abortion, the first such measure in the world. Alexandra Kollontai, the programme's most systematic advocate in office, pressed its second condition, the socialisation of housework, through the Zhenotdel, the women's department of the party founded in 1919, and through campaigns for communal dining halls, public laundries, nurseries and maternity provision, arguing in print that the individual household was an economic relic and that only its social replacement would free women for full participation in production and public life. The first condition advanced on paper and in the industrialisation drive, which drew women into the wage labour force in vast numbers. The second condition advanced far less: communal services remained scarce, underfunded and overwhelmed in a poor country devastated by civil war, and the gap between the programme and the kitchen was filled, in practice, by women's double burden, wage work added to undiminished domestic work rather than substituted for it.

The reversals followed, and they are part of the test. The Zhenotdel was abolished in 1930. In 1936 abortion was recriminalised, divorce made harder, and official policy turned pro natalist and familial, celebrating motherhood and the stable family the programme had expected to transform; the later Soviet order combined very high female employment with a domestic division of labour that remained conventional and with political authority held almost entirely by men. A fair reading of the experiment for the purposes of this audit is therefore double. The programme proved actionable: its first condition was achieved at scale, and its second was genuinely attempted, which distinguishes it from utopian texts that cannot be operationalised at all. And its second condition proved to be the load bearing one, exactly as the book's logic would predict: where housework was not in fact socialised, formal equality and full employment did not by themselves deliver the emancipation the programme promised, and the shortfall appeared precisely where the separable programme locates the problem, in the privatised household. Critics of the programme draw the opposite lesson from the same record, holding that the persistence of the family and of domestic inequality under every attempt shows the programme mistook a durable human institution for an economic arrangement. Both readings argue from the policy record. Neither needs to mention Morgan, which is the audit's point about separability, demonstrated by history rather than merely asserted by logic.

### What happened to the programme where it was applied at scale?

Its two conditions fared unequally. Women's entry into wage labour was achieved on a mass scale, while the socialisation of housework, the condition on which the book's logic makes emancipation actually depend, was attempted through communal services that stayed scarce and was later abandoned as policy turned back toward the conventional family.

The afterlife of the book in the later twentieth century ran through scholarship as much as through policy. The feminist revival of the nineteen seventies returned to it as the classical statement to be reckoned with: Leacock's edition of 1972 put it back into circulation with scholarly apparatus, the domestic labour debate took up its household analysis, Rubin transformed its kinship thinking, and a generation of Marxist feminists defined their own positions by specifying where they departed from it, on the autonomy of patriarchy, on reproduction as a sphere with its own logic, on the inadequacy of production alone. The tradition that resulted, Marxist feminism in its mature form, is therefore this book's heir in a peculiar sense: it descends from the text by criticising it, and an account of the tradition that presented the inheritance as simple discipleship would falsify both. The tradition has its own guide in this series, already linked at the outset, and the reading of the present text is best completed by the successor framework that reorganised its surviving claims, social reproduction theory, whose relation to the book is revision from within the same broad school. What no serious participant in that afterlife did, after the anthropological verdicts surveyed in this article, was defend the prehistory. On that point the scholarly argument closed, and the audit's abandoned rows record a closure, not a skirmish.

## The preface's two productions

Every close reading of this book should begin where its author began, with the preface of 1884, because the preface contains, in compressed form, a methodological claim that reaches beyond the family and that generated a literature of its own. Engels there states the materialist premise of the work: that the determining factor in history is, in the final instance, the production and reproduction of immediate life. The phrase is double, and the doubleness is the point. Production of the means of existence, of food, clothing, shelter and the instruments for making them, is one side. The other side is the production of human beings themselves: procreation, the raising of children, the organisation of kinship through which persons are made and placed. Labour and the family, Engels says, stand together at the origin, and the less developed labour is, the more the social order appears to be dominated by ties of kinship. As production develops and property accumulates, the kinship order is progressively displaced and overlaid by the property order, until the state arrives to manage what the gens no longer can.

This dual formulation has been fought over with an intensity out of proportion to its two paragraphs, because it appears to concede, at the foundation of the materialist method, that kinship and reproduction form a second determinant alongside production, and the whole subsequent argument about whether Marxism can theorise women's oppression without remainder turns on how those paragraphs are read. One reading treats the preface as a youthful dualism its author would have outgrown: reproduction matters in early society and fades as production develops, so the mature theory remains a theory of production with a primitive exception clause. Another reading, pressed by feminist scholars who wanted a materialism of reproduction in its own right, treats the preface as a charter: the production of life is permanently double, and a theory that attends to only one term will systematically miss the organisation of childbearing, child rearing and sexuality as social facts with their own weight. Social reproduction theory, whose guide is linked elsewhere in this article, can be read as the second reading developed into a system, and the domestic labour debate was, among other things, an argument about which reading of this preface was correct. A close reading should note what the text will and will not bear. The preface does not present two equal and parallel systems persisting through history; its own narrative has the kinship order receding as property advances, which is the first reading's strongest card. But it does grant kinship an independent determining role in the periods it governs, and it never retracts the grant, which is the second reading's. The book that follows can be read as oscillating between the two, treating the family now as an effect of property and now as a structure with inertia and force of its own, and much of the criticism surveyed in this guide is, at root, a complaint about that oscillation.

The preface also performs the book's act of piety and its act of appropriation in a single gesture, and the two should be distinguished. The piety is genuine: Engels presents the work as the execution of Marx's intention, down to the detail that Marx had meant to present Morgan's results in connection with his own materialist conclusions, and a reader who consults the notebooks can see that the intention existed. The appropriation is equally real: what Marx left was excerpts and marginalia, critical, exploratory and unfinished, and what Engels published was a system, confident, linear and closed. Between the notebooks and the book there is an act of authorship for which Engels bears sole responsibility, and the scholarly habit of attributing the book's doctrines to Marx, or of defending them by appeal to Marx's notebooks, mistakes the documentary situation. The book is Engels's. Its strengths, the sweep, the legal and institutional learning, the polemical architecture, are his, and its overconfidence is his: the notebooks hedge where the book declares.

### What does the preface claim about production and reproduction?

That the production of life is twofold from the start: the making of the means of existence and the making of human beings themselves, organised through kinship. In early society, on this account, kinship ties dominate the social order, and they recede only as production, property and class advance and displace them.

There is a further feature of the preface that the audit reader should file away, because it explains a recurring peculiarity of the book's reception. Engels wrote as an advocate of the materialist method demonstrating its reach: the family, the most apparently natural and moral of institutions, was to be shown as tractable to the same kind of explanation as the state and the law. The demonstration required the explanation to look complete, and the book's tone of completeness, its march from stage to stage with every institution assigned its place and cause, is a rhetorical requirement of the demonstration rather than a report of the evidence's actual condition. Later critics have sometimes read the tone as the whole offence, as though a humbler book would have survived. The audit's verdicts suggest otherwise: the abandoned rows fail on evidence, not on manners, and a tentative Morganism would still have been a Morganism. But the tone had consequences of its own, because it taught two generations of readers to receive the book as settled doctrine, and it is against that reception, as much as against the text, that the modern criticisms are aimed. The separable programme reading is, among other things, an attempt to read the book at a temperature its author declined to use.

## The family chapter under the lens

The second chapter, titled simply The Family, is where the book's method is most exposed and where a close reading earns the most, because it is here that Engels performs, in sequence, every operation his critics later indicted: the conversion of kinship terminologies into vanished institutions, the arrangement of reports into a succession, and the narration of the succession as a moral history with a direction. It is also, read attentively, a chapter with real analytical pleasures that the indictment should not be allowed to confiscate: the comparative eye for the variety of marriage arrangements, the refusal to treat European monogamy as the measure of the rest, and the insistence that the emotional content of family forms, love, jealousy, parental attachment, has a history and is not a constant of nature onto which institutions are draped.

The chapter's machinery is the kinship schedule. Morgan had gathered, through printed questionnaires circulated by the Smithsonian Institution and through correspondence, the terms used in dozens of societies for the relatives a European vocabulary distinguishes: father, mother, brother, sister, son, daughter, and their extensions. He found two great types. In the descriptive type, roughly the European one, terms track biological closeness. In the classificatory type, found across much of Asia, Africa, Oceania and the Americas, a man applies the term translated as father to his mother's husband's brothers and to many men of that generation and clan, and the term translated as brother to a wide class of male kin. Morgan's inference, which Engels reproduces as established, was that such terminologies are conservative: they preserve, as fossils preserve extinct organisms, the marriage arrangements of the time when the terminology formed. A system in which many men are called father must, on this view, descend from a time when fatherhood was in fact collective, in group marriage; the terms outlasted the institutions and testify to them. From a graded series of terminologies and reported customs, the succession of family forms was then read off, consanguine to punaluan to pairing to monogamian, each form leaving its terminological stratum behind.

The inference is the weakest joint in the book, and it failed for a reason Lowie's critique made canonical: terminologies classify the living. A classificatory system sorts kin by generation, clan and relative age because those are the distinctions the society needs its members to observe, for obligations, marriage prohibitions and inheritance, and it does so whether or not any group marriage ever existed among that people. The vocabulary is a map of present social structure, not a sediment of past sexual arrangements, and the fossil reading, on which the whole succession of the first two family forms depends, is an artefact of treating language as a recording instrument for institutions it merely labels. The consanguine and punaluan families, the first two terms of Engels's sequence, have no independent existence outside this inference: no observer has ever reported a society organised in the consanguine form, and the punaluan form rests on contested readings of Hawaiian custom that later fieldwork did not sustain. The pairing family, by contrast, corresponds to arrangements ethnographers have abundantly documented, loose and dissoluble unions within kin households, and monogamy is a legal and historical fact. The sequence therefore runs from the conjectural to the documented as it approaches the present, an inversion of what an evidential ladder should look like, and the reader is entitled to feel that the chapter is most confident exactly where its material is thinnest.

### Why did the kinship schedules seem to prove vanished marriage forms?

Because the classificatory terminologies were read as fossils. Where many men bore the term translated as father, Morgan inferred a past in which fatherhood had genuinely been collective, in group marriage, and held that the words survived the institution as strata survive in rock. Later kinship studies showed the terms map present obligations and groupings, and record no such past.

Set against that failure, the chapter's durable achievement should be recorded with equal precision, since the audit's surviving rows have their textual home here as much as anywhere. Engels's treatment of sexual morality as an institution with a history, his observation that individual sexual love, the passionate attachment of one person to one other as a good in itself, is a historically late ideal rather than a human universal, and his scepticism toward the marriage customs of his own class, displayed in the chapter's asides on marriages of convenience contracted for estates and alliances, all belong to the stratum of the book that requires no Morgan at all. The passage in which he predicts that a coming generation of men and women who have never known the buying of a woman's surrender will judge sexual arrangements by standards the present cannot foresee is rhetoric, not evidence, but it is rhetoric with a serious methodological content: the refusal to grade other arrangements by the standards of one's own. That refusal, consistently applied, is the best of the book, and it sits in the same chapter as the fossil fallacy, which is why this text defeats every simple verdict and why the audit, with its patience for rows, is the honest instrument for it.

The gens chapters that follow the family chapter change the evidentiary weather, and a careful reader can feel the change of ground underfoot. The Iroquois gens, as Morgan described it and Engels adopts the description, is a documented institution of a documented people: a matrilineal clan with named animal totems, communal burial, elected sachems and chiefs removable by the clan, including by its women, adoption of outsiders, and obligations of mutual aid and vengeance, federated with other gentes in tribe and confederacy. Whatever one thinks of the use made of it, this is ethnography of real societies, made by a man who had lived among the Seneca and been adopted into their nation, and the chapters on Greece and Rome work with the classical and legal record, with gentile names, burial plots, inheritance rules and the traditions of the Roman gentes and Greek phratries, material a historian can weigh. Engels's construction of the argument is that the gens, organised on kinship, is the universal primitive constitution, and that the state arises, in Athens most legibly, when wealth, slavery, trade and territorial settlement dissolve it: Solon's and Cleisthenes' reforms, registering citizens by district and property class rather than by birth in a genos, mark for him the exact point at which the political order ceases to be an order of kin and becomes an order of territory and class. As a reading of Athens this has been contested in detail by classical scholars, and the universality of the gens as a stage has gone the way of the other universals. But the analytical skeleton, kinship organisation displaced by territorial and propertied organisation as the frame of political life, remains a live way of posing questions about early states, and it is no accident that the chapters on the state have worn better than the chapters on marriage: their evidence was always of the inspectable kind.

## Bachofen, McLennan and the making of mother-right

Mother-right did not fall from the sky of Morgan's schedules. It had a genealogy of its own before Engels touched it, and tracing that genealogy is part of the audit, because the claim's failure is partly a failure of its sources' methods, which were speculative in ways that were already visible to careful contemporaries. Bachofen, a Basel jurist and scholar of Roman law, published Das Mutterrecht in 1861, arguing that the earliest Mediterranean societies had been ordered by mother-right and had passed through it to father-right, leaving traces in myth, in funerary practice and in legal survivals. His prize exhibits were literary: the Oresteia of Aeschylus, in which the Erinyes defend the mother's claim against Apollo's argument for the father, read by Bachofen as the dramatised record of a constitutional transition; the accounts of Herodotus concerning the Lycians, who, the historian reports, took their names from their mothers and reckoned descent through them; and the Amazonomachy, the myths of women warriors, read as distorted memory of a gynaecocratic age. Bachofen's learning was vast and his intuition, that the patriarchal family of classical antiquity had a prehistory and was not eternal, was a genuine intellectual event. His method, though, was the method of the myth as document: stories treated as encrypted annals of institutions, decoded by the interpreter's sense of the whole. An interpreter with a different whole could decode the same stories differently, and the history of the mother-right literature is largely the history of such decodings colliding.

McLennan arrived at a neighbouring construction from legal anthropology rather than myth. His Primitive Marriage of 1865 argued from bride capture symbolism in wedding rituals across many peoples: the mock seizure of the bride, he proposed, is the survival of an age of actual capture, when women were scarce in each group because of female infanticide, and men stole wives from outside, a practice that generated exogamy, the rule of marrying out, as its institutional deposit. From these materials McLennan built a history in which polyandry, the marriage of several men to one woman, figured as an early form and in which kinship through females only was, at a certain stage, the necessary consequence of uncertain paternity. Engels took from McLennan the uncertainty of paternity and the kinship through females, discarded the capture apparatus, fused the result with Bachofen's mother-right and Morgan's gens, and produced the synthesis the audit has examined. The synthesis had one decisive advantage over each of its parents: it looked empirical. Myth and ritual symbolism were the weakest of its ingredients, and they disappear from the finished book's foreground, while the schedules, the gens and the classical record, its strongest ingredients, carry the narrative. A reader of 1884 met a work of apparent science. The conjectural stratum had been poured into the foundations where it could not be seen.

### What did Bachofen actually argue from?

Myth and legal survival, read as encrypted history. The trial of Orestes, Herodotus on the Lycians naming children from their mothers, and the Amazon legends supplied his evidence for an age of mother-right preceding father-right. The intuition that the patriarchal family had a prehistory was fertile; the method was speculation disciplined by erudition, and it could yield contrary readings.

The fate of the mother-right idea inside anthropology is a useful epilogue to this genealogy, because the discipline did not simply forget it: it professionalised the question and found the question ill posed. Descent, residence, inheritance, succession to office and domestic authority, the components that the nineteenth century bundled into a single variable called mother-right or father-right, turned out to vary independently. A society may reckon descent through women, require the married couple to live with the wife's kin, transmit land through men and vest office in a chiefly line that mixes the principles, in combinations that no single scale of mother-right to father-right can order. The bundle came apart in the hand, and with it went the possibility of the universal sequence the bundle was meant to travel along. This is a more interesting failure than mere error, and the guide records it as such: mother-right was not a fiction like phlogiston, it was an over unification of real phenomena, descent through women emphatically among them, into a single institution with a single history. The over unification is the error, and it is the error of the whole evolutionary school, Morgan included, in its most instructive form.

## The bourgeois marriage and its shadow

The chapters on monogamy contain the book's most sustained piece of social analysis, and it is here, if anywhere, that a modern reader unacquainted with the text discovers why it was feared and quoted in its own century. Engels's claim is that the monogamous marriage of the propertied classes is an economic transaction wearing a sacrament. Among those who have property to transmit, marriage is contracted for the property: alliances between families, the consolidation of estates and firms, the production of legitimate heirs, with the inclinations of the parties, and especially of the bride, admitted only so far as they do not disturb the settlement. His evidence is the law and literature of his own Europe plus the classical record, and his tone is that of a man reading the marriage columns as balance sheets. The wife within such a marriage occupies, in his analysis, a position defined by dependence: excluded from production for the market, maintained by her husband, and bound to fidelity by the entire apparatus of law, religion and economic necessity, while the husband's infidelity is provided for by the institution of prostitution, which the book treats as the monogamous order's own product and necessary complement, the organised unfaithfulness that the official faithfulness requires.

This analysis survives its century better than the prehistory for reasons the audit has already given: it rests on evidence in the open. The legal position of married women in the Europe of 1884 is a matter of record: in most jurisdictions a wife's property passed under her husband's control, her earnings were his, her capacity to contract was limited or extinguished, and divorce, where it existed at all, was asymmetrically available and ruinous to her in a way it was not to him. The double standard in sexual morality was not a subterranean fact requiring excavation; it was codified in the adultery laws of several states, which punished the wife's adultery as a property offence and the husband's as a peccadillo, and it was architectural in the great cities, where the tolerated districts and the registered women formed part of the municipal order. Engels's Greek excursus, in which the Athenian citizen's sequestered wife is shadowed by the hetaira, the educated companion who supplied the conversation and company the wife was not permitted to supply, makes the same structural point in classical dress: a system that confines respectable women produces, alongside itself, the class of women it uses and despises. The analysis generalises dangerously when it is promoted into a law of all monogamy everywhere, and the audit's modified verdict on the monogamy row reflects exactly that promotion. As a reading of propertied marriage under the legal regimes Engels knew, it remains formidable.

### Why does the book treat prostitution as part of monogamy rather than as its opposite?

Because its monogamy binds one side only. Where fidelity is enforced on the wife by law and dependence, while the husband's access to other women is tolerated and organised, the tolerated traffic is, for Engels, the institution's own complement and product, the shadow the official arrangement casts, not an external corruption of it.

There is one passage of this analysis that a close reading should isolate, because it has been quoted carelessly in every direction. Engels argues that within the proletarian household, where there is no property to transmit, the economic foundations of the old monogamy are absent: no inheritance to secure, and the wife, drawn into industry, is no longer maintained in the same dependence, so that the material basis exists, for the first time, for unions founded on inclination alone, though the conditions of proletarian life, its insecurity and brutalisation, deform what they make possible. The passage has been read as romanticisation of working class marriage, and Engels is not innocent of a certain warmth in it, but its analytical content is the reverse of sentimental: it is the separability logic applied to his own century. Change the property relation and you change which parts of the institution have a basis and which persist as mere habit and coercion. The same passage quietly refutes the lazy reading of the book as holding that sexual morality is a simple reflex of economics: habits, law and religion persist in it after their basis has gone, and the argument throughout is about bases and conditions, not reflexes. Readers preparing to criticise the book for economic reductionism will find, if they read this passage carefully, that the reduction on offer is of institutions to their material conditions of persistence, a subtler and more defensible operation.

## The feminist criticisms, stated from the other side

The defences have had their section; the criticisms that come not from anthropology but from within feminist scholarship itself require one too, because a guide that credited the book with founding a tradition while suppressing the tradition's own indictment of it would fail the neutrality its brief demands. These criticisms accept much of the audit: the prehistory is gone, the household reframing stands. Their target is what the audit grades as surviving, the programme and the causal story, and they argue that the survival is thinner than the book's heirs admitted.

The most systematic of these criticisms is associated with Christine Delphy, the French sociologist whose work, from the nineteen seventies onward and collected in Close to Home, argued that the book's deepest error is not its anthropology but its economics. On Delphy's reading, Engels located women's oppression in their exclusion from production, and prescribed entry into production as the remedy, while the material reality in front of him was that married women already worked, enormously, inside the household, and that this work was appropriated by the husband as the head of a domestic economy with its own relations of exploitation. Housework, for Delphy, is not a pre economic residue awaiting socialisation; it is labour performed within a domestic mode of production in which the husband appropriates the wife's labour power wholesale, and the wage relation outside the home is therefore not the sole, or even the primary, site of the exploitation of married women. If Delphy is right, the separable programme is mis specified at its core: it treats the household as a site of idleness to be emptied into social production, when the household is a site of production whose relations must themselves be transformed, and a century of women entering wage labour while continuing to perform the domestic labour, the double burden the Soviet record displayed, is exactly what her theory predicts and what the programme, on her reading, cannot explain. The criticism does not touch the prehistory. It strikes at the survivor, and it is the strongest internal objection the book faces.

Michèle Barrett's study of women's oppression, published in 1980 and for a generation the standard theoretical statement of British socialist feminism, delivered a different but complementary judgement. Barrett argued that the legacy of the book within Marxism had become an obstacle: its account encouraged the tradition to treat women's oppression as a by product of property and class, destined to dissolve with them, and thereby to under theorise the ideological and familial structures, what she called the ideology of familialism, that reproduce gender division inside the working class itself and survive changes in property form. The family household system, on her account, is not merely an effect of capitalism; it is one of capitalism's own supports, organising the reproduction of labour power and the division between waged and unwaged work in ways the system actively requires. Where Engels saw an institution with a basis that socialism would remove, Barrett saw an interlocking settlement that a change of ownership alone would leave standing. The distance between the two is the distance the whole Marxist feminist tradition travelled, and it is measured accurately by setting her first chapters beside his last ones.

A third line of criticism, associated with the historians and anthropologists who examined the book's treatment of evidence about women specifically, notes a silence in the text that its programme throws into relief. The women of the book are acted upon by structures: mother-right falls, monogamy is imposed, the wife is degraded, the proletarian woman is drawn into industry. Female agency, resistance, negotiation and the making of institutions by women themselves figure hardly at all, and the ethnographic record, even of Engels's own century, contained much that a less architectonic writer would have found: women's control of markets and trade in parts of West Africa, female religious and political office in a range of societies, the clan mothers of his own prized Iroquois material exercising constitutional power in the narrative he adopts and then hurrying past. A history of women's subordination in which women never act is a history with a hole in its centre, and the criticism has bite precisely because the material for a better account lay inside the book's own sources. The guide records this criticism as one of the standing ones, while noting the reply that defenders make: that a short programmatic treatise of 1884 cannot be indicted for lacking the historiography of a later century, and that the book's subject was structures, not heroines. The reply explains the silence without filling it.

### What is Delphy's objection to the programme itself?

That it misdiagnoses the household. For Delphy, married women's domestic labour is already production, appropriated by the husband within a domestic economy of its own, so oppression does not consist in exclusion from production and will not be cured by entry into wage labour. The remedy aimed at the wrong relation: the household's relations, not its privatisation alone, are the problem.

These criticisms set the terms on which the book is now most profitably read, and they also complete the audit's picture, because they reveal that the surviving rows survive as questions and programmes under active dispute, not as settled doctrine in anyone's keeping. The separable programme, graded in the table as surviving as a programme, survives in the way programmes survive: as a set of institutional proposals that organise argument, attract amendment and withstand or fail particular tests, the Soviet test chief among them. Delphy and Barrett did not discard it; they rewrote it, which is what the strong survival of a programme looks like in practice, and the successor theories treated elsewhere in this series, the domestic labour debate's descendants and social reproduction theory above all, are best understood as the programme's rewritten forms, carrying its central insistence, that the household is inside the economy, into frameworks its author would not have recognised.

## Morgan before Engels: the lawyer, the league and the schedules

To see what Engels was borrowing, and why intelligent men believed it, the reader needs Morgan in his own right for a section, because the caricature of a Victorian armchair speculator will not explain the authority the borrowed system carried in 1884. Morgan's formation was practical and, by the standards of his time, empirical. As a young Rochester lawyer he became involved with the Seneca, one of the nations of the Haudenosaunee confederacy, in their resistance to a fraudulent land purchase, was adopted into the Seneca nation, and turned the experience into The League of the Iroquois in 1851, a study of the confederacy's political organisation that remains a document of value: the clan structure, the council system, the role of the clan mothers in nominating chiefs, all described from proximity rather than from travellers' tales. Whatever was later made of it, the Iroquois material at the base of the gens theory came from the most sustained participant observation any of the evolutionary anthropologists could claim.

From the Iroquois, Morgan generalised outward in two great works. Systems of Consanguinity and Affinity of the Human Family, published by the Smithsonian in 1871, assembled the kinship schedules on a world scale and founded the comparative study of kinship terminology, with the classificatory and descriptive distinction as its durable contribution. Ancient Society, six years later, built the evolutionary edifice on that foundation: the stages, the succession of inventions and institutions, the family forms, the gens as the archaic unit, and the reconstruction of Greek, Roman and Hebrew antiquity as late chapters of a single human story. The work was received as a landmark across the political spectrum, and its author's credentials were those of a founder of a science, not of a pamphleteer: he was elected to the presidency of the American Association for the Advancement of Science, and his books were printed by the great institutional presses. When Engels described his own book as written in the light of Morgan's researches, he was claiming the authority of the best regarded anthropological system then available, and a reader of 1884 had no professional consensus to set against the claim. Boas was twenty six, and the demolition had not begun.

The demolition, when it came, had therefore to destroy a reputation as well as a theory, and the manner of it contains a lesson the audit has already drawn but which is worth stating at this point in the guide. Morgan's data, the schedules and the Iroquois descriptions, did not evaporate: kinship terminology remains a field, and the League study is still read. What evaporated was the theoretical frame that converted the data into a chronology. The same terms that Morgan arranged as strata, later scholars arranged as systems, functioning in the present; the same gens that he placed at the origin of a universal sequence, later scholars described as one form of kin organisation among many, with histories of its own that run in no single direction. The distinction between data and frame is the reader's best protection in this whole literature, because Engels inherited both from Morgan, fused them with Bachofen and with his own property theory, and presented the compound in a prose that does not distinguish the strata from the system either. The claims audit is, in effect, the distinction applied after the fact.

### Why did the stage scheme command belief in its own century?

Because it organised real and rapidly accumulating data into an intelligible order at the moment the data arrived. Ethnographic reports from every continent demanded arrangement, the scheme arranged them, its author had unmatched credentials and genuine fieldwork behind him, and no rival method of equal sweep existed until the Boasian critique rebuilt the discipline on particular histories instead.

One further feature of Morgan's system explains a peculiarity of Engels's book that readers often register without understanding: its treatment of the Americas as a window onto the Old World's past. Morgan held that the institutions of the indigenous peoples of the Americas preserved, in living form, the stages through which Greek and Roman society had passed in antiquity, so that the Iroquois gens could be used to decode the Roman gens as a zoologist uses a living species to decode a fossil. Engels adopts the procedure entire, reading Athens and Rome through the Iroquois and the Germans through both. The procedure is the comparative method in its purest form, and its rejection is the core of the Boasian objection described earlier: peoples are not living fossils, their institutions have their own histories as long and as eventful as Europe's, and the window is a mirror. Modern anthropology's insistence on this point is sometimes mistaken for political delicacy; it is in fact a methodological finding, earned in the field, and it removes the single most characteristic device of Engels's middle chapters. What survives that removal is the classical material itself, the gentile institutions of Greece and Rome as the record gives them, which historians continue to study without the American key.

## The state in the book: Athens, Rome and the Germans

Although this guide's assignment is the family, the book's final architecture cannot be understood without the state chapters, because in Engels's construction the two dissolutions are one: the same growth of private property that overthrew mother-right and installed monogamy also dissolved the gens and precipitated the state, and the title's three nouns name a single process viewed from three sides. The chapters on Athens, Rome and the Germans are therefore not a second book stitched to the first; they are the first book's argument completed at the level of political organisation. The state, on this account, is not the eternal form of social order and not a divine or contractual invention. It is a product of society at a certain stage, arising where the gentile organisation, government by kinship, can no longer administer a society split by wealth into classes with irreconcilable interests, and its defining marks are the organisation of subjects by territory rather than by blood, and the creation of a public power, officials, soldiers, prisons, standing above society and increasingly alienated from it.

The Athenian case carries the argument, and it is the most carefully worked in the book. Engels traces the Attic gentes and phratries, the growth of maritime trade and money, the appearance of a nobility enriched outside the gentile structure and of a debtor class ruined inside it, and then the reforms: Solon cancelling debts and ranking citizens by wealth, Cleisthenes reorganising the citizen body by territorial demes so that the unit of politics is the district and no longer the clan. The reading has been quarrelled with by classical historians in its details, in its Morgan derived reconstruction of the early Attic constitution and in the weight it lays on single reforms, and the quarrel is legitimate: the evidence for the early period is thin, literary and late. But the analytical claim at the centre, that the classical polis represents a reorganisation of political life on territorial and propertied lines displacing an older kinship organisation, is one that classical scholarship in its own idiom continues to work with, and the chapter is the best place in the book to watch Engels's method operating on inspectable evidence: legal traditions, constitutional reforms, the vocabulary of institutions, rather than on kinship schedules.

The Roman and German chapters extend the pattern with diminishing ethnographic risk. Rome supplies the patrician gentes and their long contest with the plebs, read as the gens order decaying from within as wealth and conquest stratify it. The Germans, drawn largely from Tacitus and from the evidence of the mark, the communal land association, supply the case of a gentile order meeting the Roman state from outside and generating, in the barbarian kingdoms, the feudal order to come. A modern reader should be warned that the German material carries the nineteenth century's romantic investment in Tacitus and in primitive Germanic freedom, an investment Engels shared with much of his century's scholarship and which later work has heavily qualified; the free Germanic mark community, like the matriarchy, is in part a projection. The pattern of the audit repeats itself with almost comic regularity: the further the text stands from documentary and legal evidence, the more of its century's mythology it absorbs.

For the purposes of this guide, the state chapters matter chiefly as the destination of the book's master inference, and the reader should mark the inference's shape. From the premise that the family, property and the state arose together, Engels draws the conclusion that they stand and will fall within the same historical frame: the state, he argues elsewhere and implies here, is not eternal, and the family form bound up with private property shares the property system's transience. The inference has the same structure as the prophecy about monogamy examined earlier, origins made to govern destinies, and it is subject to the same objection. Institutions acquire functions and supports beyond their origins, and a genealogy, even a true one, is not by itself a prognosis. The separable habit of reading is the corrective: the genealogy can be graded on its evidence, the prognosis on its own, and the programme, which is the practical residue, on the record of its application. The book fuses all three into a single swell of argument. The reader this guide is training keeps them in separate accounts.

## The final chapter and its prophecy

The ninth chapter, Barbarism and Civilisation, closes the book by gathering the whole sequence into a single narrative of civilisation's price, and it is the chapter most often anthologised and least often read with the audit in hand. Its first movement is a summary of the stage scheme at its most confident: the rise of the division of labour, of commodity production and exchange, of merchants, money, usury and landed property, told as the solvent in which the gentile order dissolves. Its second movement is a judgement on civilisation that borrows its cadence from Morgan himself, whom Engels quotes, in fragments, on the career of property: on the mastery of production turned to accumulation without end, on a society organised around acquisition arriving at a point where the career of property threatens the society conducting it. The closing pages predict that a society which has made property its sole end will be superseded, and that the mastery human beings won over nature and over their own social organisation will be turned to ends beyond accumulation. The cadence is magnificent. The audit's footnote to it is plain: the chapter's confidence in the sequence it summarises is exactly as well founded as the sequence, which is to say, in its stage form, not founded at all, while its judgement on the property order of the nineteenth century stands or falls as social criticism, on evidence of a different and inspectable kind.

A detail of the final chapter deserves isolation because it shows the book's theory of the state completing the theory of the family in a way readers miss. Engels argues that civilisation, in dissolving the gens, also completes the degradation of women begun with the overthrow of mother-right, by confining them within the individual family just as the affairs of society migrate to the state and the market, institutions from which women are excluded. The public and the private harden together: the man's sphere becomes the state, production and exchange; the woman's becomes the household, now a private establishment outside society's account. The formulation prefigures, in a vocabulary its author did not live to see, the modern critique of the public and private divide, and it is one of the places where feminist scholarship of a later period found the book genuinely generative, a structural insight detachable from the prehistory that frames it. Detachable is the operative word. The insight concerns the organisation of industrial and state society, which can be examined in the record of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and it requires nothing of Morgan at all.

The prophecy about monogamy belongs to the same chapter's argument and should be graded by the same rule. Engels predicts that with the transfer of the means of production to common ownership, the individual family ceases to be the economic unit of society, private housekeeping is transformed into a social industry, the care and education of children becomes a public matter, and monogamy, relieved of its property foundation, will for the first time become general and real, for men as well as for women, while unions will be formed and dissolved by inclination without economic penalty falling on either party or on children. Read as a prediction, the package has fared unevenly across the century that tested parts of it: the legal and economic dependence of wives has been dismantled in many jurisdictions by routes the book did not foresee, through labour market change, contraception, welfare provision and feminist legislation within capitalism, while the socialisation of housework in its strong form was attempted only in the Soviet case and abandoned there. Read as an analytical claim, that the form of marriage tracks the organisation of property and domestic labour and changes when they change, the passage states the book's most general surviving thesis, the one the audit's surviving rows collectively support. The reader who learns to perform that double grading, prediction in one ledger, analysis in another, has learned the book's proper use.

## The book in the movement: orthodoxy and its uses

Between its publication and the feminist revival, the book lived the life of a classic inside a movement, and that life is itself evidence in the canon question the strongest objection raised. The German Social Democratic Party, the largest socialist organisation of the pre war world, adopted the book into its educational bloodstream: it was summarised in party schools, quarried in the women's movement press, and treated as the authoritative word on the origin of women's oppression by the generation of Karl Kautsky, the movement's leading theorist, who wrote on the family and population in its spirit. Its standing was of the kind that forbids revision: a text by the surviving founder, on a subject where the movement wanted doctrine, carrying the borrowed prestige of science. When Lenin, in The State and Revolution in 1917, expounded the theory of the state, he leaned on this book's state chapters as on a foundation, quoting its account of the state as a product of class irreconcilability and its prediction of the state's eventual disappearance into the museum of antiquities alongside the spinning wheel and the bronze axe, an image from the book's pages that became, through Lenin's use, one of the most repeated in the revolutionary tradition. The state chapters thereby acquired a second canonisation inside the first: to question the book was to question authorities in series.

This history explains a feature of the book's twentieth century reception that puzzles readers who approach it as scholarship: the lag between the anthropological verdict and the tradition's acknowledgement of it. Boasian anthropology was ascendant in the universities by the nineteen twenties; Lowie's demolition was available in English from 1920; yet party literature continued to reproduce the stage scheme and the matriarchy as settled fact for decades, in popularisations whose authors cannot all have been ignorant of the scholarly position. Some were: movements educate from their own shelves. But the structural explanation the strongest objection offers, that the institution was preserving a doctrine it needed, fits the pattern better than individual ignorance does, for the need was real. A movement recruiting women in industrial numbers required an account of women's oppression that made it historical, soluble and connected to the general programme, and no replacement text of equal authority existed. Doctrine abhors a vacuum. The lesson the guide draws is not contempt for the tradition, which contained many who argued the questions honestly, but a standing instruction to the reader: the fame of this book is a datum about institutions, not about evidence, and the two must never be converted into one another, in either direction. The same institutional fame also preserved the book's genuine contents, its programme and its household analysis, through decades when academic fashion would have let them drop, and the feminist scholars who revived it found it on the movement's shelves. Canonisation distorted the book and saved it, in the same act.

### Why did the tradition keep teaching the scheme after the scholars dropped it?

Because the doctrine did work the institution needed. The stage scheme and the defeat narrative gave the movement a complete, teachable account of women's oppression as historical and soluble, backed by the founder's authority, and no replacement of equal authority existed. The preservation was institutional before it was intellectual, which is what the canon objection means.

The collapse of the Soviet order and the end of the movement's educational empires changed the book's situation once more, and largely for the better as far as reading it goes. It is now one classic among others, assigned in universities in courses on feminist theory and on the history of anthropology, where it is read alongside the Morgan it summarises and the criticisms it attracted, an arrangement that would have been unthinkable in the party school and that suits the text surprisingly well. Stripped of doctrine status, its qualities as writing come forward: the lucidity, the legal learning worn lightly, the comparative range, the polemical energy that made it the most readable of all the Marxist classics after the Manifesto. Its defects come forward too, no longer protected. The university afterlife has also produced the scholarly genre this guide belongs to, the annotated reckoning, of which Leacock's edition was the precursor: editions and studies that print the text whole and the verdict alongside, on the principle that a foundational work whose evidence has failed is more instructive, not less, when its successes and failures are mapped claim by claim.

## Kinship studies after the collapse

A reader is entitled to ask what the discipline that rejected the book put in its place, since a guide that reported only the demolition would leave the impression of a vacant lot where a science had stood. The answer matters for the audit, because the surviving rows are graded against what the successor scholarship actually found, and the picture is neither that kinship turned out to be uniform after all nor that nothing general can be said. What the successor field established, across a century of fieldwork of a depth Morgan could not have imagined, is variation as the datum: descent reckoned through women, through men, through both, or through chosen and adoptive ties; residence with the wife's kin, the husband's, or neither; inheritance and succession following rules that combine these principles in locally intelligible ways; and marriage forms ranging across monogamy, polygyny, polyandry in a few documented regions, and arrangements that the European vocabulary of marriage fits awkwardly at best. Against that datum, every universal sequence fails, Engels's included, and fails instructively, because the failure taught the discipline that kinship systems are solutions to local problems of group membership, property and alliance, not stations on a common road.

Within the variation, though, disciplined generalisation proved possible, and its shape vindicates a fragment of the book in the qualified way the audit records. Jack Goody's comparative work, already cited for the inheritance row, is the exemplary case: comparing African and Eurasian systems across large bodies of historical and ethnographic evidence, Goody argued that where agriculture supports substantial heritable property, societies tend to develop what he called diverging devolution, property passing to children of both sexes, and with it an intensified concern with the control of women's sexuality and marriage, the protection of the property line. The correlation is regional, mechanism specific and laden with exceptions he himself catalogued, and it is stated without stages, dramas or overthrows. It is also recognisably a descendant of the question Engels asked, whether the organisation of kinship and the position of women are connected to the organisation of property, receiving at last an answer of the kind the evidence supports: yes, in these places, by these mechanisms, with these limits. A foundational text whose questions survive its answers has not lived in vain, and the audit's modified grades are calibrated to exactly that condition.

The later turns of kinship studies pushed still further from the book's categories, and two deserve record for completeness. The study of relatedness, associated with Janet Carsten and others from the nineteen nineties, set aside the assumption that kinship everywhere rests on the biological facts of procreation as the West understands them, and studied instead how relatedness is made, through shared food, shared houses, care and memory, in the ethnography of particular places. The new kinship studies of the same period examined the reproductive technologies and chosen families of the industrialised world with the same instruments, collapsing the distinction between the anthropology of elsewhere and the sociology of home. Nothing in these turns rehabilitates Engels's apparatus, and much in them would have astonished him, yet their founding gesture is one he would have recognised: the insistence that the forms of relatedness in one's own society are as constructed, as historical and as much in need of explanation as those of any other. The naturalness of the family, the assumption his first chapter attacked with the weapons of 1884, remains the target; the weapons are new. The separable pattern one last time: the polemic survives its evidence, and the evidence is not missed.

It remains to record, for the symmetry of the audit, the one contemporary in spirit revival the stage idea has received and why it does not disturb the verdicts. Writers of grand synthesis, most prominently David Graeber and David Wengrow in their work on early social history, have argued against the stage schemes of archaeology and anthropology from the opposite side of Engels: not that the sequence is wrong in its order, but that early peoples experimented with social forms, including seasonal alternations between hierarchy and equality, with a self consciousness the stage schemes deny them. Their argument, contested in its turn by specialists, treats the deep past as politically various rather than as a ladder, which places it, whatever its authors' intentions, on the Boasian side of the question at issue here: diversity without a single order. No party to the modern argument defends the Morgan and Engels sequence, and the mother-right stage has no contemporary scholarly defender as a universal claim. The abandoned rows of the audit are as closed as scholarly questions get.

## How to read the book now

Practical counsel can now be given, and the audit makes it specific. Read the book in the fourth edition text, the one Engels revised, in a translation that prints both prefaces, the first edition's and the fourth's, since they contain the method and the author's own adjustments, and a reading that skips them mistakes the book's claims. The edition prepared by Eleanor Leacock, published by International Publishers in 1972, remains the most serviceable single volume for a serious reader, not because its editor defends the text, though she does in part, but because her long introduction performs an early version of the audit, marking the ethnographic strata and their condition, so that the novice reads with a surveyor's map already in hand. The text is also available in the collected works of Marx and Engels in English, where it sits among the writings of the eighteen eighties, and the scholarly reader will want the notebooks volume beside it, for the reasons the opening sections gave: the distance between Marx's excerpts and Engels's system is one of the book's central facts, and it can only be judged by those who look at both.

As to order and emphasis within the book itself, the audit translates into a reading plan. The prefatory material and the second chapter, The Family, come first and repay the closest attention, the chapter read with its machinery exposed: the schedules, the inferred forms, the slide from mother-right toward matriarchy, all marked as they pass. The ninth chapter, Barbarism and Civilisation, comes next in importance, since it contains the programme, the judgement on civilisation and the prophecy, the living matter of the book in concentrated form. The Iroquois gens chapter, the third, is the best of the ethnographic chapters, because its source had lived his material, and it can be read as a document in the history of anthropology in its own right. The Greek and Roman chapters reward readers with classical interests, as institutional history argued from legal evidence. The remaining chapters, on the Celts and Germans and the formation of the German state, are the most dated in their ethnography and the most skippable for a first reading, though the reader of the state argument will want the Athenian material complete. Two chapters, in short, carry the book for most purposes, the second and the ninth, and a teacher setting the text will find that the audit's whole argument can be taught from those two plus the prefaces.

### Which two chapters repay the closest reading, and why those?

The second, The Family, because it contains the book's machinery entire, the schedules, the sequence and the defeat, in the form in which they must be examined; and the ninth, Barbarism and Civilisation, because it contains the programme and the judgement on the property order, the parts of the argument that survive and organise everything else.

Three cautions complete the counsel, each keyed to an error this guide has seen in print often enough to name. Do not cite the book for the ethnography: any sentence of the form Engels showed that early society was thus and so should be struck and rewritten, since what he showed was what Morgan believed, and Morgan's beliefs on these points are no longer evidence. Do not cite the book as Marx's: the attribution error is common in journalism and inattentive scholarship, the notebooks notwithstanding, and it matters because the book's authority has been doubled by the borrowed name. And do not cite the programme as a quotation when a paraphrase is wanted in the other direction either: the two conditions, entry into social production and the socialisation of domestic work, are frequently summarised as Engels demanding that women be sent into factories, a summary that omits the second condition, the one his own logic makes decisive and the Soviet record proved out by its absence. A citation practice that honoured those three cautions would remove most of the mischief the book's afterlife has generated, on all sides.

## The verdict this guide leaves

The separation is now complete enough to be summarised without the table, and the summary is the answer to the question with which the guide opened: what is a reader to do with a foundational text whose evidence base has collapsed? The answer the audit supports is neither of the inherited reflexes. Against dismissal: the book's reframing of the family and the household as historical and economic institutions reorganised two fields and a political tradition; its programme set the agenda of a century of policy and criticism; its social history of propertied marriage remains a formidable piece of analysis; and its guiding question, how the organisation of property bears on the organisation of kinship and the standing of women, is still being answered, in disciplined form, by the comparative scholarship that succeeded it. Against defence: the prehistory is gone entire, stage scheme, mother-right, matriarchy and kinship fossils together; the mechanism of a single overthrow is a drama, not a finding; and the book's authority within its tradition was for decades an institutional fact that protected it from the verdict its own evidence had earned. Both halves are true, and the discipline of holding them together, claim by claim, is what the claims audit and the separable programme, the two instruments this guide has named, exist to make habitual.

One more consideration belongs in the summary, because it concerns the reader's own discipline rather than the book's merits. The audit method practised here, grading a classic claim by claim against the standing of its evidence, is sometimes mistaken for a debunking exercise, a prosecutor's art. It is the opposite. It is the form that respect takes when a text is too important to be either worshipped or discarded, and it is the only form of respect that survives contact with a century of scholarship. The movements that protected this book from grading did not honour it; they embalmed it, and the embalmed text taught nothing to anyone who did not already believe. The graded text teaches in every direction at once: to the anthropologist, the history of a method; to the feminist, the genealogy of a programme; to the historian, the workings of a canon; and to any reader, the transferable skill of separating what a powerful book knows from what it merely inherits. That is the spirit in which this guide has proceeded, and it is offered as the right spirit in which to open the book itself.

There is a last reason to read the book that belongs to neither ledger, and it is the reason it will continue to be read when the grading is forgotten. It is the record of a powerful mind attempting, with the best science available to it, to denaturalise the most naturalised of institutions, and failing in the attempt in ways that are themselves a complete education in how such attempts fail: through borrowed systems trusted past their warrant, through the fusion of speculation with data until the seam vanished, through the confidence of a style that could not admit the gradient between what it knew and what it surmised. A student who learns to detect those failure modes in this magnificent and ruined text has acquired an instrument that transfers to every foundational work in every field, including the ones whose evidence still stands. That is the sense in which the book's collapse is part of its value. The foundation fell; the ruin is instructive; and the programme its author built on the ruin's edge, detached and examined, remains on the table of political argument where he left it, to be amended, contested and used.

## The Iroquois material re-examined

Since the gens chapters lean hardest on the Iroquois, and since the Iroquois material is the strongest ethnography the book possesses, it deserves a section of its own in the audit, with the later scholarship on the Haudenosaunee set against Morgan's account and Engels's use of it. The confederacy Morgan described, the league of the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca and later Tuscarora nations, was a real and sophisticated political order, and his description of its clan system, its councils and its condolence rituals for installing chiefs drew on genuine intimacy with Seneca informants, above all the Seneca leader and scholar Ely S. Parker, who served as Morgan's collaborator and whose own role in the making of the League study is now recognised as foundational in itself. Later scholarship, including work by Haudenosaunee scholars and historians of the confederacy, has corrected Morgan on points of emphasis and detail: the league's antiquity and the dating of its formation are debated; the balance between its peace keeping function and the warfare of the mourning war complex is drawn differently; and the authority of the clan mothers, the senior women of the matrilineal clans who nominated the chiefs, held the titles in trust and could initiate a chief's removal, is in several accounts stronger and more institutional than the use Engels made of it suggests.

The corrections matter to the audit in an unexpected direction. Engels used the Iroquois as the type of the matrilineal gens at the threshold of its overthrow: a society in which mother-right still organised descent while the forces that would destroy it gathered. The fuller record shows a durable, functioning matrilineal order of great antiquity in its own terms, not a transitional fossil, with women's institutional power embedded in a constitution that also seated men in council and sent them to war. It is, in other words, a standing refutation of two of the book's habits at once: of the treatment of living peoples as earlier stages of other peoples, since the confederacy was a contemporary political achievement and not a survival; and of the slide from matriliny to female subordination awaiting its defeat, since here matriliny anchored a division of authority that lasted centuries without being either a matriarchy or a way station. The best ethnography in the book thus turns out, on fuller knowledge, to testify against the frame the book built on it, a pattern the guide has met before, in the kinship schedules, and which constitutes the deepest single lesson the text offers about borrowed science: the data were better than the theory, and they have outlived it.

A second re-examination concerns Morgan himself as a historical actor, since the audit of a borrowed system should include the terms of the loan. Morgan's relations with the Seneca were honourable by his period's standards and his defence of their land claim was effective advocacy; his evolutionary scheme, at the same time, arranged Native peoples on the lower rungs of a ladder whose top was occupied by the civilisation dispossessing them, and it was used in that arrangement by others for purposes he did not control. Engels, reading against the grain of his century in most respects, accepted the ladder while reversing its moral sign: the lower rungs became, in his hands, the lost world of gentile freedom against which civilisation is indicted. The reversal is rhetorically magnificent and methodologically worthless, since a ladder does not become sound when its valuations are inverted. Modern scholarship on the confederacy, which treats it as a political creation of particular peoples at a particular time, abandons the ladder altogether, and stands as the audit's closing exhibit on the ethnographic side: what the material needed was history, and history is what the stage scheme was invented to avoid supplying.

## The first division of labour

One sentence of the book has had a career almost independent of the rest, and a close reading should attend to it: the assertion that the first division of labour is that between man and woman for the propagation of children, from which the text derives the first class opposition in the antagonism of the sexes within monogamy. The sentence does three things at once, and its career illustrates the rewards and dangers of the book's aphoristic method. It proposes a natural history, a division of tasks arising from reproduction itself and predating property. It proposes a moral history, in which this innocent division becomes the template and seed of domination. And it proposes an analogy, class, which imports into the household the entire analytical machinery of exploitation developed for the economy. Each of the three has been separately argued ever since. Anthropologists have examined the sexual division of labour as a near universal of documented societies while disputing every attempt to derive it from a single cause or to read its contents as fixed: the tasks assigned to women and men vary enormously, women hunt in some societies and men gather in others, and the near universality of some division coexists with radical diversity in its terms. Feminist theorists disputed the second move, asking whether domination can really be generated from a division of tasks without an intervening account of power, and disputed the third most of all, since the class analogy, pressed literally, makes every husband an exploiter in the technical sense and dissolves the specific mechanisms, law, violence, ideology, property, by which subordination is actually maintained.

Engels's own text is more careful than the sentence's career suggests, and the care is worth recovering. The division of labour between the sexes is presented in the early chapters as arising within the gentile order without domination: woman's sphere, the household and its communal production, is honoured and indispensable, man's, hunting, herding and war, no more so, and the catastrophe arrives only when the products of man's sphere become private wealth and the spheres are revalued by property. Domination, on this account, is not the unfolding of the division but its capture by a new institution. The feminist criticism surveyed earlier can be restated against this fuller version, and survives the restatement in part: Delphy's objection, that the household contains its own appropriation and does not need capture from outside to subordinate the wife, targets exactly this innocence of the original division, and the anthropological objection, that the contents and valuation of the spheres vary beyond any single story, targets its universality. What the fuller text does rescue is the analytical point beneath the aphorism: that the organisation of reproduction is a division of labour, subject to the same historical analysis as any other, and not a biological backdrop. That point, like so many in this book, survives in the act of being separated from the sentence that carried it.

## Athens and Rome: the inspectable evidence

The audit grades the book's classical chapters more kindly than its prehistory, and a reader should know concretely what that kindness rests on, because the evidence in question can be examined in an afternoon and the examination is the best training the book affords. Take Athens first. The citizen wife of classical Athens lived under a kyrios, a male guardian, father, husband or son, without whom she could not conduct legal business beyond trifling amounts; she was married by a contract between men, the engye, in which her own consent was not the operative element; her dowry, though protected in principle, was administered by her husband; and the legitimate transmission of citizenship and property ran through her body under guard, in a city whose funeral orations praised wives for being least spoken of among men. The hetaira stood in structural counterpoint, as the book says. None of this requires Morgan. It is the record of the orators, the laws quoted in the courts and the inscriptions, and Engels's arrangement of it into a property analysis, the wife as the instrument for producing heirs whose legitimacy the estate requires, is an interpretation laid on evidence that readers may weigh without intermediaries.

Rome offers the complementary case, and the book uses it with a lawyer's pleasure. Early Roman marriage placed the wife in the hand, the manus, of her husband, legally in the position of a daughter in his household; across the Republic the alternative form, marriage without manus, spread, leaving the wife in her father's family or, if he died, legally independent under a guardian whose control steadily thinned into formality, while her property, dowry apart, remained her own to a degree unusual in the ancient world. Engels reads the sequence as the gens order's Roman decay, property and individualisation dissolving the old family from within. Roman legal historians tell the story with more causes and less system, citing the demographic regime, the frequency of early death and remarriage, and the interests of great families in keeping property allied rather than absorbed. The readings compete on open ground, which is the point: here the book is doing history of the kind that can be argued with, footnote against footnote, and its claims enter the common scholarly traffic without special protection. The contrast with the schedules could not be sharper, and a student who grasps why the Roman chapter can be argued with while the punaluan chapter can only be believed or disbelieved has grasped the audit's organising distinction between inspectable and uninspectable evidence.

There is a further lesson in the classical material for the separable programme, and it is the strongest card the programme's defenders hold. The legal dependence of wives in Athens and in early Rome, and its gradual modification as property arrangements changed, exhibits the mechanism the programme generalises: the standing of women within marriage tracks, with lags and resistances, the organisation of property and production around it. The Greek and Roman wives were not subordinated by a memory of mother-right's fall; they were subordinated by the law of their cities in the present tense, for reasons their own jurists could state in terms of estates and heirs. Engels's universal drama is a myth laid over this real and local machinery. Stripped of the drama, the machinery remains, and the programme is the machinery's modern address: change the organisation of production and of domestic labour, and the legal and social standing of women within the family is put in motion, as the long modification of the European codes across the century after his death, under pressures he did not foresee and some he did, was to demonstrate in the event.

## The book and the women of its own century

A foundational text should also be audited against its own moment, and the moment of this book contains a fact its reputation tends to obscure: it says almost nothing about the political demands that women of its own century were actually organising around. The suffrage campaigns were underway across Europe and North America as Engels wrote; married women's property acts were being fought through legislatures, in Britain successfully in 1870 and 1882, within his working lifetime and in his own country of residence; the first generations of women were entering universities and professions in contested trickles. Of this landscape the book gives no account and takes no positions. Its woman question is structural and futural: emancipation will arrive with the transformation of production and the household, and the organised feminism of the century, dismissed within the socialist tradition this book armed as bourgeois, appears in its pages not at all.

The silence was consequential, because the tradition took its cue. Clara Zetkin's address to the founding congress of the Second International in 1889, the charter document of socialist women's politics, drew the line the book implied: the emancipation of women, like that of labour generally, would come only through the social transformation, and cooperation with the bourgeois women's movement, which sought equality within the existing order and chiefly for women of property, was refused as a matter of principle, while the suffrage itself was demanded as a weapon of the class struggle rather than as a feminist end. The position had a logic, and it mobilised hundreds of thousands of working women into the socialist women's movement, the largest women's political organisation of the pre war world. It also subordinated every specifically female grievance to the timetable of the general revolution, and the grievances had a way of outlasting the timetables: when the revolutions came, the women's departments were wound up within a generation, as the Soviet record in this guide has shown, while the suffrage and property reforms the despised campaigns pursued were won within the unreformed order and proved irreversible in a way the revolutionary settlements did not. A balanced audit records both halves. The book's structuralism gave its tradition an analysis of women's oppression deeper than the liberal feminism of its day possessed, an account of why formal equality would not reach the household; and its silence on the immediate gave that tradition an excuse, used for a century, to postpone the household question in practice while honouring it in theory.

Later Marxist feminists made this criticism from inside the inheritance, and it belongs with the feminist criticisms already surveyed, as their historical corollary. The charge is not that Engels was a man of his time, which is true of everyone and explains nothing, but that the book's architecture licensed the postponement: if the oppression of women originates in private property and is destined to fall with it, then the rational political course is always to attend to property first, and the woman question becomes, in the movement's own revealing phrase, a secondary contradiction. The phrase and the practice long outlived the book, and the feminist revolt inside the tradition in the nineteen seventies was aimed as much at the licence as at the text. Reading the book with this afterlife in view sharpens the separable programme's meaning one final time: the programme's two conditions were, in the movements that canonised the text, the very parts most often postponed, the socialisation of housework above all, so that the tradition's practice inverted the text's logic, keeping the prehistory as doctrine and shelving the programme as promise. The audit's separation is thus also a restitution. It returns to the centre of the book the part its custodians found least convenient.

## The household in later economic thought

One measure of the book's surviving core is the company it keeps in unexpected places, and the clearest instance is the treatment of the household by economic thought that owes Engels nothing. When Gary Becker published A Treatise on the Family in 1981, founding the modern economic analysis of the household within the mainstream of the discipline, the organising claim was that the household is a productive unit, combining time and goods to produce the commodities of life, and that its internal division of labour can be analysed as specialisation. Becker's framework differs from Engels's in nearly every commitment: it treats the household's arrangements as efficient responses to the productivity of its members, naturalises a benevolent head whose altruism aligns the unit's interests, and derives the sexual division of labour from comparative advantage in a manner that critics, including feminist economists such as Nancy Folbre, charged with dressing the existing order as an optimum. Yet the shared premise is the one this guide has been tracking: the household is an economic institution, its labour is labour, its arrangements are analysable and have distributional consequences for the persons inside it. That premise, a fighting proposition in 1884, became across the following century a commonplace available to schools that agree on nothing else, and its migration is the cleanest demonstration that the separable core of the book was detachable in fact: it travelled without the anthropology, without the programme and without the politics, into the heart of the orthodoxy its author spent his life opposing.

Folbre's own work, and the feminist economics that followed her, can be read as the point at which the two traditions this book straddles, the Marxist and the economic, resumed conversation on the household with the myths cleared away. The questions she pressed, who does the caring work, under what obligations, at whose cost, and how the answers are enforced by what she called structures of constraint, are Engels's questions restated at the standard of evidence the audit demands: no stages, no defeats, no universal drama, but the household as a site of labour, obligation and unequal power, connected to the market and the state by traceable mechanisms. The line of descent is not simple piety; feminist economics criticised the Marxist tradition's own record on these questions as sharply as it criticised the mainstream's. But the existence of the conversation, on this ground, with these questions, is a fact about the book's legacy that no verdict on Morgan touches.

### What did the mainstream's household economics share with the book?

Its founding premise and little else. Becker's household is a productive unit whose internal labour is analysable, which is the book's reframing exactly; but where Engels historicised the unit and indicted its arrangements as domination founded on property, the mainstream modelled it as an efficient, benevolent arrangement, and feminist economists attacked the model for naturalising the order it described.

Population supplies a second such meeting ground, worth a paragraph for completeness. Engels's correspondence and the book's margins engage the Malthusian question that haunted his century's political economy: whether population presses on subsistence by a law of nature. The position the tradition took from such remarks, that every mode of production has its own law of population and that abstract laws of population exist only for plants and animals, was made programmatic in his letter to Kautsky of 1881 and is reflected in this book's treatment of the gens, whose institutions regulate marriage and reproduction socially rather than by famine. As demography the claim is too sweeping, and the demographic transition of the following century, in which fertility fell with development across every property regime, fits no simple version of it. As a methodological refusal, the insistence that reproduction is socially organised and historically variable rather than a natural constant, it is the preface's dual production thesis again, and it has worn the better of the two. The reader will by now recognise the pattern without prompting, and may take the recognition as a sign that the guide's work is nearly done: the same operation, separating the refusal from the system built on it, grades this claim as it graded the rest.

## Bebel beside Engels: the two classics compared

No account of this book's place in its tradition is complete without the comparison its first readers made instinctively, between Engels's treatise and Bebel's Woman and Socialism, because the two texts divided the labour of the doctrine between them and their differences explain the tradition's shape. Bebel wrote first and wrote for agitation: his book is long, miscellaneous, passionate and encyclopaedic, moving from the position of women in primitive society through the history of marriage to the conditions of working women in the Germany of the Anti-Socialist Laws, and closing with a vision of the future society in which the socialised kitchen and laundry figure as concretely as in any text of the century. It was prosecuted, banned and read to pieces in workers' households, running through edition after edition, and it reached readers who would never open a treatise on Morgan. Engels wrote second and wrote the theory: compressed, systematic, sourced, and largely innocent of the contemporary factory floor. Where Bebel described the seamstress's working day, Engels described the fall of mother-right. The movement read Bebel for its lives and Engels for its doctrine, and cited Engels in the footnotes of its soul while learning its arguments from Bebel.

The comparison is instructive for the audit because the two books failed differently. Bebel's ethnology was derivative, taken from the same evolutionary sources and from Engels himself in the revised editions, and it has dated in the same places. But Bebel's book does not stand or fall by the ethnology, because its centre of gravity is the documentation of the present: the wages, hours, health and housing of working women, the law as it stood, the double burden described from life. Those chapters are social reporting of a high order and can be read now with profit and without an audit. Engels's book inverts the ratio: its present is thin, its past is everything, and so the collapse of the past strikes nearer its centre. The separable programme is the point where the two books meet and agree, and it is no accident that the shared programme, forged in Bebel's agitation as much as in Engels's theory, is the part of the inheritance that survived both texts' evidence. The tradition's two classics thus perform the separation between them: read side by side, they show a doctrine whose documentary and programmatic parts were gathering the strength that its prehistory was about to lose.

There is a human footnote the guide should not omit, because it conditions how the doctrine was heard. Bebel's book was written by a man who championed women's full equality inside a party whose daily practice was as male as any institution of the empire, and its author supported the women's movement of his party against considerable internal resistance, just as Engels lent his authority, in correspondence and in the book's authority itself, to the same cause. The tradition's women, Zetkin pre-eminently, used both texts as weapons in internal struggles their authors did not have to fight. A text's meaning in a movement includes the uses its allies make of it under fire, and by that measure the book served, at times, better than it argued: it gave the movement's women a founder's warrant for demands the movement's men preferred to schedule for after the revolution, and Zetkin's generation wielded the warrant with skill. The audit grades claims; history also grades uses, and the two ledgers, as this guide has insisted throughout in the other direction, must not be fused.

## Other sources, other debts: Maine, Kovalevsky and the commune

Morgan and Bachofen were not the only authorities on Engels's desk, and the subsidiary debts repay attention because they show the book's method reaching beyond its main system toward kinds of evidence that aged better. Henry Sumner Maine, whose Ancient Law of 1861 had founded the historical study of legal institutions in English, supplied the tradition with its master contrast between societies organised by status and societies organised by contract, and his account of the patriarchal family as the unit of ancient law stood in direct opposition to the mother-right thesis: for Maine, the father's authority is the archaic fact and kinship through males the original form. Marx's notebooks engage Maine critically, and Engels's book is, in one of its aspects, a sustained refusal of Maine's patriarchal origin, fought on the ground of the gens evidence. The quarrel is instructive because both sides overclaimed a universal from regional evidence, Maine from the Indo European legal record, Engels from the American and classical material, and the truth the successor scholarship settled on, that the early institutions of different peoples differ in precisely these respects, was available to neither as a conclusion, since each man's system required an original form. The episode is the audit's method in miniature once more: two confident universals, each founded on genuine learning, cancelling into a plurality neither could admit.

The Russian connection is the second subsidiary debt, and it links this book to the late Marx's most famous hesitation. Maxim Kovalevsky, the Russian historian and sociologist whose work on communal landholding Marx had studied and who knew both Marx and Engels personally, supplied evidence and argument about the village commune as a surviving form of common property, material that fed the tradition's agonised question whether Russia might pass to socialism through its commune without traversing the full capitalist road. The question belongs to other articles in this series, but its bearing here is the picture it gives of the book's intellectual household: Engels in 1884 was working within a circle of comparative institutional scholarship that included serious historians of law and property, and the Origin's best chapters, on the gens and the classical city, draw strength from that company. The book was not a lone system builder's fantasy; it was the radical wing of a real scholarly moment, which is precisely why its failure is the failure of the moment and not of a crank. Audits should be exact about the dignity of what they grade down, and this book's company was dignified.

### How did Maine's account oppose the book's?

At the root. Maine began from the patriarchal family, the father's authority as the cell of society and kinship through males as the archaic rule, against which mother-right was a corruption or a fable. Engels's book is a sustained refusal of that origin, and the successor verdict, that institutions differ by people and neither universal stands, refused them both.

One more debt deserves naming because it is so easily missed: the classical scholarship of the Germans themselves. Engels read Greek and Roman antiquity with the equipment of a German gymnasium education and the philological seriousness it instilled, and his pages on the Attic constitution, on the Roman gens and on the Germanic mark draw on the historical school of his own country, on Niebuhr's Roman history and the debates it started, and on the legal antiquarianism that made the nineteenth century the great age of institutional prehistory in every sense. The breadth matters for a fair verdict on the man's performance inside his moment. He was not a dilettante seizing on a single fashionable authority; he synthesised the institutional scholarship of several nations at speed, and the synthesis held together for two generations because the scholarship beneath it was, in its documentary parts, substantial. The parts that failed were the parts where the scholarship of the moment itself was building on air, and the audit's abandoned rows are a map of where the air was.

## Children, education and the cost of the next generation

The book's argument has a third term that summaries often drop, and a close reading should restore it, because without it the programme's second condition hangs in the air: the child. In the gentile order of the text, children belong to the clan and are raised within its communal household, their maintenance a charge on the kin group and their education the business of the whole society in miniature. With the monogamous family and private property, the child becomes the individual couple's affair and its economic meaning changes: the child is the heir, the vehicle through which the estate crosses the generations, and the child's legitimacy is the obsession the whole apparatus of enforced fidelity exists to secure. Engels's analysis of legitimacy, of the legal distinction between children born within marriage and outside it, and of the disabilities the law of his century laid on the latter, is among the book's most concrete engagements with the statute book, and it requires no anthropology to follow: the inheritance system of propertied Europe punished children for the circumstances of their conception as a device for policing their mothers, and the book says so with a plainness that still startles.

From this analysis the programme's educational plank follows, and it is the plank whose subsequent history is the most nearly unambiguous. The care and education of children, the text argues, will become a public affair as private housekeeping is socialised, and society will care for all children alike, whether born in marriage or outside it, removing at a stroke the economic anxiety that polices sexuality and the stigma that punishes the child. The century that followed implemented fragments of this in nearly every industrial society, by routes its author did not chart: universal public schooling, already advancing in his lifetime; the legal abolition, across most European jurisdictions in the later twentieth century, of the disabilities of illegitimacy, so complete that the category itself has vanished from the statute books where it once organised inheritance; public provision for early childhood, uneven, contested and real. The fragments do not add up to the book's vision, and the causal credit belongs to many movements besides its tradition. But the direction of the legal record on this single point, the child's standing detached from the parents' marital status, is the direction the text specified, and the audit notes it as the programme's clearest partial vindication in law.

The child also exposes, by an instructive contrast, the limit of the book's method. For a text so attentive to the economics of reproduction, it has remarkably little to say about children as persons, about attachment, about the experience of the household from within, and the gap is not an accident of length. The method treats institutions from the standpoint of their function in the whole, and the interior of family life enters only as ideology or as prophecy. Later feminist writing supplied the missing interior, sometimes in anger at the tradition's abstraction; the literature of the double day, of emotional labour and of care as skilled and costly work, is in this respect a correction administered to the book from within its own school of question. The correction should be recorded as the audit's final substantive note on the programme: what the programme sees, it sees with unmatched clarity, the distribution of labour and dependence; what it does not see, the labour's meaning to those who perform it, had to be added by others, and the added part changed the politics, since a movement that understands care only as a burden to be socialised will design different institutions from one that understands it also as a relationship to be honoured. Both understandings are needed to read the century's experiments fairly, and neither was available entire in 1884.

## The fourth edition and the moving frontier

The book's own publishing history contains a small, revealing episode with which this guide's exposition can close, because it shows the author meeting the collapse in its earliest form and choosing, as his tradition would choose after him, to defend the structure. Between the first edition of 1884 and the fourth of 1891, the ethnological literature did not stand still. New field reports accumulated, the Australian material in particular, on which the theory of group marriage heavily depended, was re examined and contested, and Westermarck's History of Human Marriage mounted its systematic counter case. Engels used the fourth edition to answer: he revised passages, incorporated new material on the pairing family and on the Australian systems, defended the mother-right construction against its critics, and wrote a new preface treating the family's early history, which reframed the book's beginnings. The performance is scholarly in form and should be credited as such: he read the criticism, he adjusted the evidence he presented, he conceded details.

And yet the episode cuts the other way on inspection, and a guide committed to the audit must say so. What Engels adjusted was the perimeter: this report reinterpreted, that custom reclassified, a new authority enlisted for the pairing family. What he did not entertain was the possibility that the frame itself, the sequence, the universal mother-right, the fossil method, was the problem, although by 1891 the grounds for entertaining it existed in print and would within a generation become the discipline's common sense. The pattern is the canon pattern in its first instance, before there was a canon: the founder defending the structure by repairing the details, because the structure was now load bearing for a movement and for a life's intellectual architecture. Readers who find the tradition's later protection of the book inexplicable should study the preface of 1891, where the protection begins, in the author's own hand and with the best scholarly manners. It is a deeply human document, and it teaches the guide's final lesson about foundational texts: that their defence is rarely cynical, that it proceeds by honest adjustments honestly made, and that it fails, when it fails, not for want of integrity but because the adjustments a system permits itself are never the ones that would dissolve it.

The textual moral for the working reader is simple and has already been given in the reading counsel: use the fourth edition, read the two prefaces as part of the argument, and remember, when the prose swells with confidence, that its author revised this book under fire and chose his ground. The ground he chose was the system. The audit has chosen the claims, one by one. Between those two choices lies the whole difference between the book's first century and its second, and the reader who has followed this guide is equipped to inhabit the second knowingly.

## Individual sex love: the book's most original idea

Buried in the family chapter, inside the machinery of the stages, sits the book's most original contribution to the theory of sexuality, and it is the contribution least dependent on anything else in the text: the concept of individual sex love. Engels argues that the passionate, exclusive attachment of one person to one other, experienced as a overwhelming and enduring desire for union with that irreplaceable person, is not a constant of human nature but a historical formation, largely unknown to the ancient world in its modern intensity, developing through the European Middle Ages in distorted forms, in the adulterous romance of chivalry and the troubadours, and achieving social recognition only as the bourgeois order, for its own reasons, began to admit inclination as a ground of marriage in theory while denying it in practice. Antiquity, on his account, knew erotic passion, the Greeks sang it, but knew it outside marriage, directed at the hetaira or the beloved boy, while marriage itself remained an arrangement of property and citizenship in which love was neither expected nor missed. The modern demand, that marriage should be founded on love and on love alone, he treats as a genuinely new claim of human individuality, born with the bourgeoisie and destined to be realised only when its economic hypocrisies are removed.

The concept has been taken up, criticised and transformed by later thought in ways that repay a paragraph. Historians of sexuality and of the emotions have confirmed the broad shape of the claim, that the ideal of companionate, love founded marriage as the universal expectation is historically recent and geographically specific, while disputing every attempt to give it a single birthday or a single cause: the demographic regime, the novel, Protestant teaching on marriage as companionship, and the slow separation of household from production all figure in the modern literature where Engels put the property drama. Theorists of sexuality in the Marxist tradition made the concept do further work: Alexandra Kollontai's writings on love in the new society, her winged Eros and her insistence that the transformation of intimate life was part of the revolution and not its decoration, descend directly from this stratum of the book, and the descent shows the concept's separability once more, since Kollontai had no need of the stage scheme to develop it. Even the critics of the concept instruct: the objection that Engels romanticised a future of spontaneous, unconflicted attachment, underestimating jealousy, asymmetry and the sheer recalcitrance of desire, an objection Westermarck's instinct based theory pressed in its own idiom, identifies the prophecy's weakest joint, its assumption that removing the economic distortions leaves a natural harmony beneath. The anthropology of emotion offers no such harmony. What survives the objection is the concept itself, the historicisation of love, which remains one of the indispensable operations of the human sciences and which this book performed, in passing, inside a chapter whose official business was something else.

## The words the book fixed

A foundational text leaves a deposit in the language of its tradition, and part of a reader's guide is an inventory of the deposit, because the terms continue to do work, and to smuggle the book's assumptions, wherever they are used unexamined. Mother-right itself is the first: a calque of Bachofen's Mutterrecht, it entered the Marxist vocabulary through this book and survives in it as a period term, useful now only with its audit attached. The pairing family, Morgan's syndyasmian family in Engels's rendering, gave the tradition its term for the loose unions of the gentile order and survives in the scholarly literature chiefly as a label for Morgan's system. The gens vocabulary, gentile society, gentile constitution, framed a century of discussion of pre state organisation and left its mark on the theory of the state far beyond this text. The world historical defeat is the deposit's most valuable coin and its most dangerous, a phrase of genuine rhetorical genius that compresses the book's weakest claim into its most memorable form, so that the claim has been remembered, and repeated, in exact proportion to the phrase's power rather than to its evidence. Teachers of the text should make the disproportion explicit: the sentence is the book's best writing and its worst history, in a single breath.

Two further terms deserve entry in the inventory because their careers outran the book in opposite directions. The socialisation of housework, the programme's second condition in its slogan form, became a term of art in the policy debates of two centuries, invoked by movements that had read nothing else of the text and opposed by critics who took it, often fairly, as the emblem of a politics willing to dissolve the private sphere into administration; the slogan's career illustrates how a programme survives by compression, and what the compression costs, since the book's own version is hedged with the prediction that domestic labour will shrink to an insignificant claim on time rather than be bureaucratised in its existing bulk. And the pairing of sex antagonism, the clash of the sexes as the first class opposition, entered the tradition as a warrant for treating gender conflict as a branch of class conflict, a warrant whose adequacy the Marxist feminist tradition spent decades contesting from inside. A reader who meets these terms in the wild, in textbooks, journalism and movement literature, now knows their mint and their metal: some are sound coin from the book's strong strata, some are base metal from the collapsed ones, and the audit is the touchstone that tells them apart.

## The statute book of the author's own Europe

The book's present tense deserves one more examination, because its author was not only a reader of Morgan but a close and angry reader of the family law of the Europe he lived in, and the anger rests on documentation that a student can verify in the codes themselves. The French Civil Code of 1804, the Napoleonic settlement that half of Europe imitated, constituted the wife as a legal subordinate in terms that require no gloss: she owed obedience to her husband, could not appear in court or dispose of property without his authorisation, and was bound to follow his residence, while adultery law punished her lapse with imprisonment and his, where it was punished at all, with a fine and only when committed in the conjugal home. The German territories varied the pattern without lifting it, and the unified German Civil Code that arrived in 1900, after the book, preserved the husband's authority over the wife's property and person in forms the socialist women's movement campaigned against by name. In England the common law doctrine of coverture merged the wife's legal person in her husband's until the Married Women's Property Acts of 1870 and 1882 dismantled it piece by piece, a campaign concluded, in its property aspect, within Engels's lifetime and within a short walk of his Manchester and London addresses, though the book, fixed on its deep history, never mentions it.

Set this record beside the text and a double judgement forms, of the kind the audit exists to encourage. On one side, the book's account of the monogamous wife's position is not rhetoric laid over ethnography; it is a fair description of the legal order of its own continent, and its central mechanism, the wife's exclusion from independent property as the ground of her subordination within marriage, was being demonstrated in reverse by the reform campaigns of the very decades it describes, since each property act measurably altered wives' standing, exactly as the mechanism predicts. On the other side, the book's silence about those campaigns, noted in an earlier section, here acquires its sharpest form: the mechanism was being operated, in public, by identifiable movements with identifiable victories, while the text looked through them to a transformation of the whole order, and the tradition it armed learned to look through such campaigns too. The separable reading performs its final service on this material. The mechanism is the book's; the campaigns were history's; and a politics that had held the mechanism and honoured the campaigns together would have been stronger than either the doctrine or the liberalism it despised. That counterfactual is beyond an audit's competence to grade, but it is the reflection the documentary record most naturally prompts, and the guide leaves it with the reader as the book's most instructive might have been.

## What Engels added to Morgan: an itemised account

Because the book presents itself as Morgan with a motor fitted, the reader deserves an itemised statement of the fitting, since the audit's grades fall differently on the borrowed parts and the additions. Taken from Morgan, nearly unaltered: the three stages and their subdivisions; the succession of family forms with their names; the gens as the unit of archaic society and the reconstruction of Greek and Roman institutions in its light; the kinship schedules and the fossil reading of them; and the Iroquois as the type case of gentile organisation. Added by Engels: the theory of private property as the solvent of the gentile order and the cause of the state's emergence; the derivation of monogamy from inheritance and paternity certainty, where Morgan had treated monogamy's rise more loosely as an achievement of civilisation; the defeat thesis in its dramatic form, since neither Morgan nor Bachofen narrates a single overthrow as the hinge of women's history; the analysis of the household as an economic unit and of the wife's position within it as a relation of dependence founded on exclusion from social production; the whole programme of the final chapter; and the philosophical frame of the preface, the dual production thesis, which has no counterpart in Morgan at all.

The itemisation yields a distribution the audit's table confirms from the other side. Of the abandoned rows, every one sits in the borrowed column: stages, mother-right as a universal, the matriarchy, the fossil terminologies. Of the surviving rows, every one sits in the added column or in the application of the additions to inspectable records: the historical variability of family forms is the frame of the whole, asserted by Engels against the natural family doctrine of his own culture; the property and inheritance connection is his mechanism, however modified by later hands; the household analysis and the programme are his outright. The book is therefore most itself where it is least Morgan, and a judgement that has circulated since the anthropological verdicts, that Engels merely popularised another man's errors, inverts the actual distribution of durability in the text. He popularised another man's errors and added, in the same binding, the arguments by which his tradition thought about the family for a century. The two facts must be carried together, and the reader who has grasped why they must has understood the difference between auditing a book and reviewing its sources.

The one addition that belongs to neither column tidily is the Marx element, and it is fitting to end the itemisation there. From the notebooks came emphases rather than architecture: the savage marginal comments on bourgeois marriage and on the hypocrisy of the respectable family, the interest in communal property forms among peoples the stage scheme placed low, and above all the warrant, the sense that the dead man's authority and intention stood behind the enterprise. What Marx would have made of the same material cannot be known; the notebooks, exploratory and critical, support more than one construction, and the scholarly literature that has grown around them contains a standing argument about whether the late Marx was retreating from the unilinear confidence the book displays. That argument belongs to the manuscript scholarship linked earlier in this guide. For the audit's purposes the position is simple: the book's claims are graded as Engels's claims, on Engels's evidence handling, and the notebooks enter as provenance, not as corroboration.

## Mechanisms in place of the drama: bridewealth, dowry and the traffic in spouses

The modified survival of the property and kinship connection can be made concrete, and should be, since modified form is the audit's most easily abused grade and an example disciplines it. Consider the payments that surround marriage across the ethnographic and historical record. In bridewealth systems, widespread in Africa and elsewhere, the groom's kin transfer goods or livestock to the bride's kin, legitimating the marriage and transferring rights in the woman's labour and in her children to the husband's group. In dowry systems, characteristic of the Eurasian societies Goody studied, property moves with the bride, as her share of the inheritance settled at marriage, and the control of women, their seclusion, the guarding of their premarital chastity, intensifies with the value at stake. In the European propertied marriage of Engels's own analysis, the settlement and the jointure perform the same office in legal dress. The comparative point, established across a large literature that includes Goody's work on production and reproduction and the alliance theorists after Lévi-Strauss, is that marriages are transactions between kin groups as well as unions of persons, that the direction and size of the transfers correlate with the organisation of property and descent, and that the standing of the women transacted varies systematically with the form the transaction takes. Engels asserted the connectedness of marriage, property and descent as a universal drama with a single plot. The record supports the connectedness and refuses the plot. Kin groups in the bridewealth belt were not enacting the fall of mother-right, and Eurasian dowry was not its epilogue; each system is a local settlement among property, labour and alliance, intelligible in its own ecology.

This is the place to acknowledge, with the specificity the steelman rule requires, the strongest version of the claim that something of the defeat survives. It runs, in the form its most careful proponents give it, as follows. Across the broad transition from kin organised, relatively egalitarian societies to stratified, property holding ones, a transition that occurred independently in several world regions, the institutional position of women deteriorated in recurrent respects: control over their mobility and sexuality tightened where heritable property made paternity a property question; their exclusion from the formal political offices of the emerging states was near total; and the ideologies of the new orders regularly represented the arrangement as natural and sacred. The deterioration is a pattern, not an event; it has no date, no single mechanism and no universal reach, and foraging and horticultural societies supply exceptions in both directions. Stated at that strength, with those hedges, the claim is a respectable comparative hypothesis, discussed in the literature on the origins of gender inequality by scholars who owe Engels no allegiance and some who have never read him. It is also recognisably his claim, the defeat with the drama removed and the universality surrendered. The audit's phrase, survives in modified form, was coined for exactly this distance between a claim and its defensible descendant, and the reader who can state both the distance and the descent has the book's measure.

## The style of the book and the uses of confidence

A reader's guide that ignored the writing would miss part of the explanation of everything else, because this book's influence ran through its style as much as through its theses, and the style is implicated in both its triumphs and its failures. Engels writes with a lucidity that the Marxist classics do not always manage: short declarative sentences carrying complex institutional material, technical terms introduced once and then worked without ceremony, classical allusions that assume a gymnasium reader and repay one, and a polemicist's instinct for the concrete instance, the hetaira beside the wife, the bronze axe beside the spinning wheel, by which an abstraction is made to stand in the light. The architecture is equally deliberate: preface as manifesto, stages as foundation, the family, the gens, the states, and the final chapter as peroration, a structure that lets a reader of ordinary stamina finish the book in days and retain its whole shape. Doctrinal texts are usually unreadable; this one is a pleasure, and the pleasure is a historical force in its own right, since it carried the stage scheme into minds that would have resisted a drier exposition and lodged the defeat sentence in the memory of a century.

The audit's interest in the style is diagnostic. Confidence in this prose is a constant, not a variable: the sentence that reports the Athenian law and the sentence that reports the punaluan family have the same gait, the same level tread of a man crossing ground he owns. A style that cannot modulate its certainty cannot signal its evidence, and a reader without external guidance has no internal means of knowing where the record ends and the system begins. This is not a complaint about the author's honesty; it is a structural property of the classical treatise as he practised it, and it explains why the audit as a form, with its external grading imposed row by row, is the necessary supplement the book never received from its tradition. The tradition, for its part, learned the gait: generations of popularisers reproduced the confidence along with the scheme, so that the defeat of mother-right was still being narrated in the level tone of fact in textbooks whose authors had never seen a kinship schedule. Style is not decoration on an argument of this kind. It is the delivery mechanism of its authority, and the mechanism worked perfectly, which is the problem the audit exists to correct.

## The milieu: Tylor, Frazer and the doctrine of survivals

Engels's method had a context wider than Morgan, and placing it there completes the audit's account of why the fossil reading of customs seemed respectable in 1884. The dominant British anthropology of the period was Edward Burnett Tylor's, whose Primitive Culture of 1871 founded the comparative study of custom on the doctrine of survivals: practices carried by force of habit into a new state of society, where they persist as evidence of the older state from which they descend, as a child's game preserves a dead earnest or a saying preserves a dead belief. Survivals made the present legible as an archive of the past across the whole field of custom, and James George Frazer's Golden Bough, appearing from 1890, built its vast edifice on the same principle, reading rituals and myths worldwide as strata of a universal mental history. McLennan's bride capture from wedding symbolism is the doctrine applied to marriage; Morgan's fossil terminologies are the doctrine applied to language; Bachofen's myths are the doctrine applied to literature. Engels drank from a common stream, and the stream's theory of evidence, that the present contains legible deposits of every past stage arranged in one order, is the real defendant at the audit's bar.

The doctrine of survivals failed, where it failed, for the reason the Boasian critique identified and functionalism then entrenched: a practice's presence in a society is explained by what it does there, or by that society's particular history of contact and invention, and its resemblance to a practice elsewhere proves neither descent nor stage. Wedding mock capture turned out to flourish where no capture tradition can be traced, and to be explicable by the drama of alliance and reluctance the ritual itself performs. Myths proved to be charters and entertainments and theologies before they were annals. The stream did not dry up at once; Frazer's influence ran for decades in literature and in the popular mind, and survivals thinking survives in folk scholarship everywhere. But as the warrant for reconstructing universal prehistory it was withdrawn, and with it went the last general licence under which the first two family forms of Engels's sequence could be asserted. The guide has treated the collapse claim by claim; here it is recorded whole, as the failure of a theory of evidence that a whole intellectual age shared, which is the historically exact way to state the book's misfortune and the strongest mitigation it can honestly claim.

## The book's silences: what the universal history leaves out

An audit of claims should end with an audit of coverage, because the book presents itself as a universal history of the family, and its universals have edges that a modern reader will strike against and should be warned of fairly. The history it tells is, for all its American and classical range, a history whose destination is Europe: savagery and barbarism are arranged as the antechambers of a civilisation that arrives in Greece, Rome and the German forests, and the peoples of Asia, Africa and Oceania enter chiefly as evidence for stages of somebody else's past. The great agrarian civilisations of Asia appear at the margins, in the book's brief and unsatisfying engagement with the village community and the oriental forms its tradition called Asiatic, and the family systems of the Islamic world, of India and of China, with their documented legal and literary records of a depth rivalling Rome's, are absent from the argument altogether. Slavery, which the book treats as a stage in the dissolution of the gens, is the slavery of antiquity; the Atlantic slave system and the colonial orders of the author's own century, the largest forced reorganisation of family life in the modern world, the systematic destruction and reconstitution of kinship among the enslaved and colonised, form no part of its history of the family, though no event of the period bears more directly on its subject.

These silences are the common silences of the European scholarship of the moment, and the guide records them as limitations of the book's claim to universality rather than as scandals of intention; a treatise is not diminished by failing to be a different treatise, but a universal history is answerable for its map. Their practical consequence is the one the audit has met repeatedly: where the book generalises, it generalises from a sample that is narrow in place and kind, and the modified grades of its surviving claims are modified precisely by re stating them within the sample that supports them, the propertied orders of the Mediterranean and European record and the ethnography of the Americas as Morgan gathered it. Later scholarship widened the sample beyond recognition, African systems, Asian legal histories, Oceanic ethnography of the first quality, and the widened sample is what converted the book's universals into regional hypotheses. The lesson generalises past this text, and the guide states it as its penultimate word on method: the reach of a claim should be audited against the map of its evidence, and a foundational work is most dangerous exactly where its map is smallest and its voice most confident that the map is the world.

## The century of evolution: Darwin's shadow over the enterprise

No intellectual history of this book is honest that does not reckon with the prestige of the word evolution in its century, because that prestige was the stage scheme's real collateral. Darwin had demonstrated, for species, that present diversity is the deposit of a long development governed by law, and the demonstration reorganised every science it touched. The social thinkers who borrowed the pattern, Herbert Spencer before Darwin and after him, the anthropologists of the ladder, Morgan at their head, understood themselves to be doing for institutions what had been done for organisms: replacing a static creation with a lawful history. The borrowing carried an assumption the biological original does not contain. Species branch; the tree of life is not a ladder, and Darwin's own imagery resisted the single scale of progress that the social evolutionists drew from him. Institutional evolutionism kept the ladder and dropped the tree, arranging peoples as rungs because the arrangement served the comparativist's need for order and the imperial century's flattering self portrait, its own civilisation at the summit by scientific warrant rather than by conquest's courtesy. Engels, who admired Darwin extravagantly and whose graveside eulogy for Marx compared the two discoveries, law in nature and law in history, accepted the ladder in the anthropological case while reversing its moral polarity: the summit became the fall, the rungs a story of loss. The reversal, this guide has argued in the Iroquois context, leaves the ladder standing. The Boasian objection was, at bottom, the demand that human history be allowed to branch like the tree, each people's institutions the product of its own path, and on that demand the whole later discipline was built.

There is an irony in the Darwinian connection that the audit should register, because it shows the book's author betrayed by his own best standard. Engels's writings on science display a sharp nose for the ideological misuse of biology, for the importation of competition and hierarchy into nature and their re export as natural law; his critique of the Malthusian and social Darwinist traffic between the two realms is among the most acute things in the tradition. The same vigilance, applied to the anthropology he borrowed, would have asked what work the ladder was doing for the civilisation that drew it, and whether the peoples arranged on its rungs had been consulted in their own histories or merely in their vocabularies. The question was not asked, because the ladder was needed: the materialist demonstration required a history of the family with the completeness of a natural history, and Morgan supplied the only candidate. Necessity, intellectual and political, is the usual solvent of vigilance, in founders as in followers, and the reader who has watched it operate in this exemplary case has a detector for it elsewhere, including in works whose evidence has not yet had its century to fail.

## The Australian evidence and the marriage classes

One body of material carried more weight in the book's later editions than any other, and it earns a section because its fate is the purest available illustration of the audit's entire argument in small. The Aboriginal peoples of Australia possessed kinship and marriage systems of a complexity that staggered the European observers who reported them: section and subsection systems dividing whole societies into named classes, with marriage prescribed between classes, descent reckoned in intricate patterns, and terminologies that classified kin across the entire group. For the evolutionists these systems were a gift: here, among peoples their scheme placed lowest, were institutions that looked like group marriage organised by rule, the punaluan family caught, as it were, in the act. Morgan built on the reports; Engels, in the fourth edition especially, leaned on the Australian material to defend the reality of group marriage against the sceptics, treating the marriage classes as the institutional deposit of an age in which groups of men and groups of women were married to one another collectively.

The reinterpretation, when the fieldwork deepened, reversed the evidential direction completely. The Australian systems, studied on the ground by professional anthropologists and later by scholars working with Aboriginal communities as authorities on their own law, emerged as systems for regulating marriage between individuals: the classes prescribe whom a person may and may not marry, exogamy rendered as a classificatory machine, and each actual marriage is a union of a man and a woman whose eligibility the machine certifies. Group marriage, as a practised institution, is not what the machine produces; it is what the machine's shadow looked like to observers reading categories as crowds. The terminology that classifies a whole set of men as fathers and a whole set of women as mothers records the class logic, exactly as Lowie argued of the schedules generally, and the actual unions remain individual. Engels's strongest empirical card for his second family form thus turns out, on the discipline's mature reading, to be evidence for the opposite proposition: that classificatory kinship and individual marriage are fully compatible, and always were. A reader who wants one exhibit to carry away from this guide, as a specimen of how the book's evidence behaved under scrutiny, should carry this one, because it is complete in itself: the data were real, the reports were honest, the inference was the error, and the correction came from looking longer at the same material rather than from discarding it.

## Manchester in the background: the young Engels and the working class family

The book of 1884 was written by an old man synthesising a science, but its author had met the family question first as a young man reporting a catastrophe, and the early encounter left deposits in the late theory that a reader should be able to identify. In 1844 and 1845 the twenty four year old Engels lived in Manchester, working in his father's cotton firm and studying the industrial districts, and the book he produced from that residence, The Condition of the Working Class in England, contains a celebrated and harrowing account of what factory labour was doing to the working class household: wives and children drawn into the mills while men stood idle, the breadwinner relation inverted, children raised by strangers or by no one in the courts and alleys of the industrial city, and the family, in his shocked phrase making, dissolved by the very industry that employed its members. The young Engels drew from these scenes a conclusion the old Engels never abandoned: that the family form of a class is a function of its economic position, made and unmade by the organisation of production, and that the bourgeois family, preached as eternal, was being daily refuted in the districts where the bourgeoisie's wealth was spun.

The deposit in the later book is the proletarian marriage passage, where the absence of property is said to remove the economic foundation of the old monogamy and to make possible, for the first time, unions resting on inclination. Critics have treated that passage as theory spun from the armchair, and against the Manchester evidence the charge needs qualifying: its author had watched, forty years earlier, what the wage relation does to a household's internal order, the authority of the unemployed husband, the independence, bitter and real, of the wage earning wife and children, and the passage generalises a witnessed process. The qualification does not rescue the generalisation entire. Manchester showed a family form under violent dissolution by capital; the passage infers a form being born, freer and more equal, and the inference outruns the witnessing, since the districts also showed him drunkenness, brutality and the abandonment of children on a scale his later optimism passes over quickly. The audit's pattern holds for the biography as for the bibliography: the observation is strong, the system built on it is stronger than the observation allows, and the separation of the two is the reader's work. But the biographical root matters for a fair hearing. The book's central intuition, that households are economic institutions whose forms follow the organisation of production, was not borrowed from Morgan. It was carried into Morgan's system by a man who had formed it on the ground in the capital of cotton, four decades before he fused it with the anthropology that failed.

## Before Bachofen: Lafitau and the Jesuit reports

Mother-right also has a prehistory older than Bachofen, and it changes the idea's complexion in a way the audit should register. In 1724 the Jesuit missionary Joseph François Lafitau published his comparative study of the customs of the American peoples, the fruit of years among the Haudenosaunee, in which he described the position of Iroquois women with an astonishment that still reads freshly: the women as the soul of the councils, the keepers of the clan's continuity, the holders of the longhouse and the fields, the nominators of chiefs, nothing done, in his summary, without them. Lafitau even reached for the classical vocabulary his century supplied and spoke of a kind of gynaecocracy among the Iroquois, comparing their institutions to those of the ancient Lycians, the same Herodotean exhibit Bachofen would use a century and a half later. The line from Lafitau through the Enlightenment's debates on the state of nature to the nineteenth century's mother-right is a documented thread of European thought, and Morgan, who knew the Iroquois literature intimately, stood at its end rather than at a beginning.

The thread matters because it cuts against two lazy stories at once. Against the story that mother-right was a socialist invention: it was a missionary's observation, elaborated by a conservative jurist, long before it was a doctrine of emancipation, and its career demonstrates how an ethnographic report can be conscripted by successive and opposite causes as each age reads its own questions into the longhouse. And against the story that the whole idea was spun from myth without observation: at its root lies a real institutional complex, the matrilineal clan organisation of the Haudenosaunee, described by an eyewitness of genius, which does confer on women powers of nomination, deposition and property holding that no European institution of the period afforded them. What the observation never contained was the universal stage or the matriarchy; those were added by the systematists. The audit's closing judgement on the ethnographic side can therefore be put in a single sentence that the whole guide has been travelling toward: the witnesses were frequently honest, the observations frequently valuable, and the architecture erected on them was the error, which is why the ruins of this book, unlike those of a fraud, remain worth the archaeologist's time.

## A word on words: the German beneath the translation

Serious readers should know that the book's key terms sit on a fault line in the German language, because one word does double duty throughout and the doubling is not innocent. Geschlecht means both a lineage or kin group and a sex, and Engels's prose moves between the two senses inside single arguments, so that a sentence about the organisation of descent can acquire, by the word's other resonance, an implication about the relations of men and women that the descent material alone would not carry. Translators have handled the slippage variously, and the standard English version, serviceable as it is, sometimes resolves into one sense what the original leaves resonating in both. The defeat passage is the famous case: its subject in the German is the ordering of the Geschlechter in both senses at once, lineage and sex together, which is precisely the fusion the argument asserts, property reorganising descent and subordination together. A critic who wants to press the text on the slide from kinship bookkeeping to sexual power, the slide this guide examined in the mother-right section, will find the slide installed in the vocabulary itself, and may judge it either as the argument's deepest intuition, that the two orders were never separate, or as its original verbal sin, a pun promoted to a mechanism. The audit takes no side in that judgement, since it grades claims rather than words, but it records the fault line because generations of polemic in several languages have been conducted across it without noticing it was there. Readers working in translation should simply know that at this one point the ground moves, and that scholarly editions flag the passages where it matters most.

## The book as a set text: a century of syllabuses

The last measure of the book this guide will take is pedagogical, because the text's strangest property is that it is assigned, criticised and retained in teaching long after every party to the quarrel agrees about its evidence. In the party schools of the old movement it was a catechism. In the universities that succeeded them it became something more interesting: a specimen. Anthropology courses use it to show students what the discipline was founded against, and to let them watch a first rate mind operate a discarded method at full power. Feminist theory courses use it as the classical statement against which the tradition defined itself, the text students must be able to summarise before they are allowed to surpass it. Courses on the history of socialism use it as the doctrine in its purest form. In each setting the book is taught with its verdict attached, which is the audit's own arrangement institutionalised, and the arrangement works because the text rewards the double treatment: it is lucid enough to be followed by beginners and flawed enough, in documented ways, to train the judgement of the advanced. Few books survive their refutation as teaching instruments; survival of that kind requires that the errors be systematic and instructive rather than random, and that the surviving core be substantial enough to justify the journey. By those criteria the set text status is earned, and it constitutes a fourth afterlife for the book, after the doctrine, the policy and the scholarly reckoning, in which its function is neither to be believed nor to be applied but to be used as the standard exercise in the separation this guide has practised from its first page.

## The translations: how the book travelled

The book's geography is a final piece of evidence about the kind of classic it became, because it travelled as movement literature before it travelled as scholarship. Written in German and published at Zurich to evade the Anti-Socialist Laws, it moved first through the German speaking movement's networks and then, in translation after translation, through every national section of the socialist world. The first English version, prepared by Ernest Untermann and issued by the Chicago socialist publisher Charles H. Kerr in 1902, put it into the hands of the Anglophone movement in the plain, sturdy rendering from the fourth German edition that remained the standard English text for most of the century. In that form it was sold at meeting halls, excerpted in movement papers and shelved in the libraries of labour colleges, a distribution system that owed nothing to universities and everything to organisation. The Russian movement read it early and argued with it in the emigre press; the Soviet state later printed it in the millions as part of the founders' canon, with the production values of scripture. Each of these travels reinforced the pattern this guide has tracked: the text arrived wrapped in institutional authority, its anthropology certified by its binding, and the ordinary reader in Odessa or Chicago had no more means of knowing that Morgan's ladder had been removed in the universities than the reader in Berlin. Translation history is usually a bibliographer's corner. Here it is part of the explanation of the canon problem, since a book's authority is manufactured by its carriers as much as by its arguments, and this book's carriers were, for a hundred years, among the most disciplined organisations on earth.

The scholarly afterlife of the text in print forms a counterpoint worth recording. When the book entered the academic world in earnest, it did so in editions that argued with it: Leacock's of 1972, already discussed, which surrounded the text with an anthropologist's running audit, and the version in the English collected works of Marx and Engels, where it keeps company with the writings of the same years and can be read against the notebooks in their separate volume. The contrast between the two kinds of edition is the contrast between the book's two centuries. The movement editions printed a doctrine, clean, with the founder's authority unmarked by doubt. The scholarly editions print a document, with the doubt restored to the margins where a reader can use it. Nothing about the words on the page changed between those printings, which is the final proof of this guide's organising claim: a foundational text is not only what its author wrote, but what its carriers permit its readers to ask, and the recovery of the right to ask, claim by claim and row by row, is the whole of the audit's work.

## For students and exam candidates

Your examination risk with this text is symmetrical: scripts lose marks by repeating its prehistory as fact and by dismissing the whole book because the prehistory failed. The audit is your protection against both. Learn the claims in their graded form, which rows are abandoned, which survive in modified form, and which survive entire, and be able to name the scholars on each side: Boas and Lowie against the apparatus, Leacock and Sacks for the partial defences, Goody for the disciplined descendant of the property and kinship claim, Delphy and Barrett for the feminist criticisms of the programme itself. The separable programme is the single most examinable idea in the article, since it lets you answer accuracy questions and evaluation questions with the same equipment. ## For teachers and seminar leaders

Set two chapters and say, before anyone opens the book, what condition its evidence is in: the anthropology is superseded, the stage scheme and the matriarchy are not to be believed, and the seminar's business is to discover what a text can still teach when its foundations have been removed. The chapters are the second, The Family, and the ninth, Barbarism and Civilisation, the machinery and the programme respectively, with the two prefaces as the frame. The pairing teaches itself if the audit's rows are distributed as exercises: give each student one claim, its evidence and the instruction to prosecute or defend it from the named scholarship alone, Boas against the inference, Lowie against the schedules, Leacock for the directional intuition, Delphy against the programme, and let the plenary discover the pattern, that survival tracks distance from the borrowed science. The exercise generalises, and students who have performed it on this book will perform it unbidden on the next foundational text they meet, which is the transferable skill the seminar exists to build.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Three trails lead out of this article, and the first is documentary. The relationship between Marx's excerpt notebooks and the finished book is a live scholarly question about the late Marx's development, turning on how critically the notebooks handle the material the book presents as settled; the [guide to the manuscripts and their scholarly edition](/2013/07/01/mega2-marx-manuscripts-guide/) maps the editions and the state of the manuscript scholarship. The second trail is biographical and institutional: the author's position as Marx's executor, the writing of this book as an act of executorship, and its canonisation inside the movement he led are treated in the [intellectual profile of Engels](/2013/05/15/friedrich-engels-intellectual-profile/). The third is disciplinary. Historians of anthropology will find the book a fixed point at which to measure the discipline's own history, the moment of Morgan's maximum political influence and the eve of the Boasian reconstruction, while journalists covering the periodic revivals of the matriarchy story in popular media should note the audit's finding: no universal matriarchal stage has scholarly standing, matrilineal societies are real and are a different matter, and any report that fuses the two is repeating an error this article exists to correct.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is The Origin of the Family about?

It is Engels's treatise of 1884 on how family forms, private property and the state arose together. Working from the anthropology of Lewis Henry Morgan, it argues that family arrangements are historical rather than natural, that monogamous marriage developed as an institution for transmitting private property to heirs of certain paternity, and that the subordination of women accompanied the rise of property and class. It closes with a programme: women's emancipation requires their entry into social production together with the conversion of private housework into a social function.

### Q: What sources did Engels use for the book?

Chiefly three. The architecture comes from Morgan's Ancient Society of 1877, with its stages of savagery, barbarism and civilisation, its succession of family forms and its worldwide schedules of kinship terms. The immediate working material was Marx's excerpt notebooks on Morgan and on other ethnological writers, which Engels used as the plan of a book Marx had not lived to write. Behind both stands Bachofen's Das Mutterrecht, the source of the mother-right idea, supplemented by McLennan on primitive marriage and by the classical and legal record for Greece, Rome and the Germans.

### Q: What is the world historical defeat of the female sex?

It is Engels's name for the overthrow of mother-right. In his account, early kinship reckoned descent through women, and women held high standing within the clan; when herds, slaves and surplus wealth accumulated in men's hands, men overturned the descent rule so that property would pass to their own children, imposed monogamy on women to make paternity certain, and thereby degraded the female sex as a whole. He calls that transition the world historical defeat of the female sex, and pairs it with the claim that the first class antagonism in history is the antagonism between man and woman in monogamous marriage.

### Q: Was there ever a matriarchal stage of society?

No scholarly consensus supports one, and the claim in its universal form has been abandoned. Engels argued from Bachofen and Morgan that an age of mother-right, descent and inheritance through women, preceded father-right everywhere, and popular accounts inflated mother-right into matriarchy, the political rule of women. Anthropology has found no society, past or documented, in which women as a sex held political rule, and it has rejected the universal sequence of stages into which the claim was built. Matrilineal descent, in which kinship is reckoned through women, is real and widespread, and is a different thing from rule by women.

### Q: Does modern anthropology support the book?

It supports almost none of its prehistory and much of its framing. The discipline rejected unilineal evolution, the method of arranging living peoples as stages of a single past, through the Boasian critique and the detailed demolition of Morgan's kinship reconstructions by scholars such as Robert Lowie; the stage scheme, the universal mother-right and the reading of kinship terms as fossils of vanished marriages are gone. What the discipline absorbed completely is the book's opening move, that family forms are historical and variable rather than natural, and it continues to investigate, in disciplined regional form, connections between property, inheritance and the standing of women that the book asserted as a universal drama.

### Q: What does the book say causes monogamy?

Property, in the specific form of the wish to transmit it intact. On Engels's account, once substantial wealth had accumulated in the hands of individual men, through herds, slaves and trade, a man required heirs whose paternity was beyond doubt, and that requirement produced strict monogamy for the wife, enforced by law, religion and her economic dependence, while the husband's fidelity was never equally required. Monogamy is therefore presented not as a moral advance or a natural form but as the first family form founded on economic conditions rather than on the older kinship order it displaced.

### Q: What survives of Engels's argument?

More than its critics sometimes allow, and less than its defenders once claimed. Surviving entire: the claim that family forms are historical products, the treatment of the household as an economic institution whose labour is real labour, and the programme linking emancipation to social production and socialised housework. Surviving in modified form: the connection between property, inheritance and the control of women, restated by later comparative work without the universal drama. Not surviving: the stage sequence, the universal mother-right, the matriarchy and the kinship reconstructions on which the prehistory rested.

### Q: What did Engels propose for women's emancipation?

Two linked conditions, which this article calls the separable programme. First, the return of women to production for society on a large scale, ending the economic dependence of the wife on the husband that he took to be the material basis of her subordination. Second, the transformation of domestic labour from a private service performed by each wife in isolation into a social industry, with cooking, cleaning and the care of children organised as public provision, so that housework would claim only an insignificant part of women's time. He expected large scale industry to begin the first and socialism to complete both.

### Q: Which chapters of the book should you read?

For most readers, two chapters and the prefaces. The preface to the first edition and its successor to the fourth state the method, the dual production thesis and the author's own revisions. Chapter two, The Family, contains the whole machinery, the stages, the succession of forms and the defeat of mother-right, and must be read with its evidential condition in mind. Chapter nine, Barbarism and Civilisation, gathers the argument into the judgement on civilisation and the programme. The remaining chapters, on the Iroquois gens, Athens, Rome, the Celts and the Germans, reward readers interested in the state argument and in the history of anthropology.

### Q: Why was the book so influential in the socialist movement?

Because it gave the movement a complete doctrine of the woman question at the moment it was organising women in industrial numbers. It explained women's oppression as historical rather than natural, connected it to the property system the movement existed to abolish, and derived a programme, employment and socialised housework, that parties could print in manifestos. It also carried unmatched authority: written by Marx's closest collaborator from Marx's own notebooks, clothed in the prestige of the most respected anthropology of its day. Bebel's more popular treatment prepared the readership, and party education then preserved the book as doctrine long after its anthropology had been abandoned by scholars.

### Q: How does the separable programme grade the book's surviving claims?

The separable programme is the claim that the book's political conclusion, emancipation through social production and socialised housework, is logically independent of its prehistory. It grades the surviving claims by asking, of each, whether it needs Morgan's stages to stand. The programme does not; neither does the household analysis nor the historicity of family forms. The defeat narrative and the matriarchy do, and they fall with the stages. The grading is therefore not a rescue device but a sorting rule, and it explains the book's strange career: its influence continued because the parts its followers used most never depended on the parts its critics destroyed.

### Q: What should a reader separate between the book's anthropology and its argument about property?

Separate the evidence from the mechanism. The anthropology, the stages, the universal mother-right, the kinship fossils, belongs to Morgan's system and has been rejected with it. The argument about property is a mechanism stated over inspectable records: that where heritable wealth concentrates, marriage and descent rules reorganise around its transmission, and the standing of women within marriage shifts with their access to property and production. That mechanism can be tested, localised and modified on legal and historical evidence, as later comparative scholarship has done, without borrowing anything from the prehistory. Confusing the two is the standard error in both praise and dismissal of the book.

### Q: Why did a book built on superseded sources stay canonical for a century?

Because institutions preserve what they use. The socialist movement needed an authoritative account of women's oppression that made it historical, soluble and connected to its general programme, and this book, written by the surviving founder from Marx's notebooks and dressed in the best anthropology of its decade, supplied exactly that. Party schools taught it, popular editions multiplied it, and Lenin's use of its state chapters in 1917 renewed its authority for another era. Its defenders within the movement then maintained the text by adjusting details, a pattern its own author set in the revisions of 1891, while the scholarly verdict went unacknowledged in the movement's literature for decades.

### Q: How does the claims audit order a reading of the book?

It orders the reading claim by claim rather than verdict by verdict. Each row takes one assertion, identifies what its author built the assertion on, reports how that foundation now stands and grades the claim as surviving, surviving in modified form or abandoned. Read in that order, the book resolves into three strata: a prehistory that fails with Morgan, a social history of marriage and law that rests on inspectable records, and a programme that stands apart from both. The audit's order prevents the two standard misreadings, crediting the programme with the prehistory's authority or discrediting the programme because of the prehistory's collapse.

### Q: What separates mother-right from matriarchy in the sources Engels used?

A rule of descent from a distribution of power. Mother-right, as Bachofen coined the idea and Morgan's gens material seemed to confirm it, means that children belong to the mother's clan, that descent and inheritance are reckoned through women, and that the mother's brother holds authority over her children. Matriarchy means that women as a sex hold political rule. The sources document the first in the form of matrilineal systems, several of which vest office and public authority in men; for the second they offer myth and inference, which is why the audit keeps the terms apart and grades the claims separately.
