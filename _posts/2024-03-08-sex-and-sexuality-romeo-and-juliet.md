## Sex and Sexuality in Romeo and Juliet: The Verdict in Brief

Sex and sexuality are the first language this play speaks, the medium in which its feud is argued, and the clock by which its plot is timed. Before either lover appears, before the word love has been spoken by anyone who means it, two servants are trading boasts about thrusting women to a wall and cutting off maidenheads. The verdict of this article is plain: the play's physical life is not decoration laid over a pure romance, and it is not an embarrassment that tasteful editing politely removes. It is a graded system of sexual registers, running from predation at the bottom to tenderness at the top, and the tragedy can be measured by the distance between those registers. Verona's public voices can speak of sex only as conquest, appetite, mockery, or transaction. The lovers, meeting inside that city, have to invent a register of their own before they can speak desire at all, and the invention is the most original thing in the play. Quotations here follow René Weis's Arden Third Series edition, cited by act, scene, and line.

The chaste reputation is the first obstacle, because it is an artifact rather than a reading. Generations met this play in expurgated school texts, in productions that trimmed the servants, softened Mercutio, and hurried past the Nurse, and the residue of those cuts hardened into a belief about the text itself: that the romance floats above the body, that the lovers' feeling is spiritual where everyone else's is merely comic. The full text refuses that belief at every level. Its opening exchange is a sexual threat delivered as a joke. Its wittiest character cannot mention love without turning it into an obscene conjuring trick. Its most experienced voice on desire belongs to a wet nurse who measures a girl's whole future by a fall on a dovehouse wall. Its heroine waits for her wedding night in a soliloquy of frank, impatient wanting that no other speaker in the play could pronounce. And its catastrophe is set in motion by a consummation that has to happen on one particular night, in one particular chamber, before a dawn that will exile the bridegroom. Remove the body from any of those points and the machinery stops.

What follows reads the play's sexual discourse as discourse: a set of competing registers, each with its own vocabulary, its own theory of what desire is, and its own speaker licensed to use it. The servants own sex as conquest. Mercutio owns sex as appetite and sport, and polices Romeo with it. The Nurse owns sex as knowledge the body keeps, spoken from inside marriage and childbirth and loss. The parents and Paris own sex as appraisal and possession, the language of a market. Against all four, the lovers build a counter-register out of prayer, touch, and plain first-person wanting, and Juliet becomes its boldest speaker. A final strand, the period double sense of the word die, runs underneath the whole system as vocabulary, a pun the coarse speakers and the dying lovers share without ever agreeing on what it means. The catalogue table later in this article sets the registers side by side, because the pattern is the argument: in this play, how a character talks about sex is the surest index of what that character believes a person is.

## The Servants at Act 1 Scene 1: Sex as Conquest Before Love Is Named

The play begins below stairs, and what begins there is a theory of sex. Sampson and Gregory, Capulet servingmen carrying swords and bucklers through a public place, pass the time before the brawl in a duet of boasts, and every boast is sexual before it is martial. Sampson vows that when he has fought the men he will "be civil with the maids: I will cut off their heads," and when Gregory checks the sense, Sampson sharpens it: "Ay, the heads of the maids, or their maidenheads, take it in what sense thou wilt" (1.1.24-26). Gregory's answer, "They must take it in sense that feel it," and Sampson's crowing reply, "Me they shall feel while I am able to stand, and 'tis known I am a pretty piece of flesh" (1.1.27-28), complete a small system. Sex in this register is something done to the enemy's women, an extension of the feud by other means, in which the woman's body is territory and her virginity a kind of outwork to be demolished. The joke requires the audience to hear rape and defloration inside a pun and to laugh, and the laughter is the datum: this is the sexual common sense of Verona's street, spoken by its lowest licensed voices before any gentleman has drawn a sword.

### What is the first claim the play makes about sex?

The first claim is that sex is a weapon of the feud, an act of aggression one house performs on another's women. Sampson's maidenhead boast (1.1.24-26) makes defloration a form of combat, and Gregory's "draw thy tool" (1.1.33) fuses sword and phallus in a single word. No voice in the scene corrects either man.

The play opens by defining desire downward, as domination, so that every later register can be measured against this floor. The fusion of sword and phallus is the register's grammar, and the scene keeps declining it. Sampson has already promised to "push Montague's men from the wall and thrust his maids to the wall," because "the weakest goes to the wall" and "women, being the weaker vessels, are ever thrust to the wall" (1.1.15-17). Gregory's "Draw thy tool; here comes two of the house of Montagues" (1.1.33) and Sampson's "My naked weapon is out" (1.1.33-34) make the equation explicit: the weapon is naked in both senses, and readiness for the fight and readiness for the boast are the same readiness. Even the pair's cowardice is phrased through the body, in the thumb-biting choreography that lets each man provoke while denying provocation. Nothing in this register admits a woman as a speaker. Women exist in it as maids, heads, walls, and vessels: surfaces on which men of one house write insults to men of the other. The point is structural, not merely atmospheric. When the gentlemen take the stage minutes later and begin the same quarrel in verse, the audience has already been taught what the feud's language of honor conceals. The servants say plainly, in the sexual idiom, what Tybalt will later say in the idiom of the duel: that the other house exists to be entered, beaten, and shamed.

Two cautions keep the reading honest. The first is that this coarseness is conventional. Elizabethan audiences were trained hearers of bawdy, and the punning exchange of insults, the flyting that runs from street comedy through the histories, treated sexual boasting as a standard weapon in a verbal duel. Eric Partridge's glossary of Shakespearean bawdy demonstrated how much of this vocabulary was common coin, a shared lexicon of double senses that any playgoer could spend. The servants are not deviants; they are fluent speakers of their culture's ordinary obscenity, and their fluency is the point. The second caution cuts the other way. Convention explains the vocabulary, but it does not explain the placement. Shakespeare puts this register first, before the Prologue's dignified sonnet has finished echoing, before Romeo sighs a single oxymoron. The play's own order of speaking is an argument: appetite armed is the ground note of Verona, and the love story will have to be built on top of it, in hearing of it, against it. Every tender line the lovers later exchange is spoken in a city whose opening definition of sex was a threat against maidenheads, and the audience is never allowed to forget the definition, because the play keeps restating it in higher and higher rooms.

## Mercutio's Registers: Queen Mab, the Conjuring, and the Prick of Noon

If the servants own the floor of the system, Mercutio owns its most brilliant and most corrosive middle. His bawdy is not one register but three, and the three harden as the play proceeds. In Act 1 Scene 4 he is the physician of desire, prescribing violence as the cure for longing: "If love be rough with you, be rough with love. Prick love for pricking, and you beat love down" (1.4.27-28). The advice is a complete philosophy in a couplet. Love is an itch and an erection, a bodily accident to be beaten down by force, and the tender sufferer is a fool for treating appetite as destiny. In the Queen Mab speech that follows, the philosophy expands into a cosmology. Mab is "the fairies' midwife" (1.4.54), and her night rounds visit every rank of dreamer with the fantasy that rank desires, until the catalogue darkens into sex outright: "This is the hag, when maids lie on their backs, that presses them and learns them first to bear, making them women of good carriage" (1.4.92-94). The hag is the nightmare as sexual initiation, desire arriving as weight and pressure on a sleeping body. Mercutio's imagination, even at its most lyrical, keeps returning to the same doctrine: wanting is something the body suffers, dreams confess it, and no vocabulary of souls will launder it.

### Why does Mercutio greet Romeo's desire with obscenity?

Because obscenity is his instrument of correction. Mercutio hears Romeo's Petrarchan sighing as a category error, appetite mistaken for transcendence, and answers in the vocabulary he believes the feeling deserves: pricks, hags, and anatomy. The conjuring in Act 2 Scene 1 is his desecration of the lover's liturgy, staged to shame desire back into the flesh.

The conjuring is the register's masterpiece and its ugliest moment. Romeo has vanished into the Capulet orchard, and Mercutio, who believes the quarry is still Rosaline, calls him back with an anti-blazon: "I conjure thee by Rosaline's bright eyes, by her high forehead and her scarlet lip, by her fine foot, straight leg, and quivering thigh, and the demesnes that there adjacent lie" (2.1.17-20). A blazon in the Petrarchan tradition catalogues the beloved's beauties upward into praise; Mercutio's catalogue travels downward into anatomy and stops at the parts adjacent to the thigh, which courtly verse exists to avoid naming. The speech act is a conjuring, a spell to raise a spirit, and Mercutio knows its obscenity is the engine: "To raise a spirit in his mistress' circle of some strange nature, letting it there stand till she had laid it and conjured it down: that were some spite" (2.1.23-26). The circle is at once a magician's ring and a woman's body, the spirit at once a demon and an erection, and the joke's cruelty is that it drafts Rosaline, absent and silent, as the apparatus of a spell she never consented to. The capstone wishes Romeo transformed into fruit and lover into one obscene image: "O Romeo, that she were, O that she were an open-arse, thou a poperin pear" (2.1.37-38), where the open-arse is the medlar, a fruit proverbially rotten-ripe and shaped for the pun, and the poperin pear carries the Flemish place name Poperinghe inside a verb any hearer could supply. Editors from Partridge onward have glossed these lines, and the glosses matter, because a reader who misses them misses the fact that the play's most famous wit spends his longest solo turn performing an act of verbal sexual possession over a woman who is not there, on behalf of a friend who no longer wants her.

By Act 2 Scene 4 the register has decayed from doctrine into reflex. Mercutio greets the Nurse's entrance with "A sail, a sail!" (2.4.100), reduces her to her clothing and her fan ("for her fan's the fairer face"), and marks the hour with a clock joke that turns the dial itself bawdy, its hand upon "the prick of noon" (2.4.103-105). The wit is still rapid, but its object has shrunk: an old woman on an errand of love, mocked for the crime of carrying desire's messages at an age when Verona permits her only to be laughed at. Even his diagnosis of Romeo has become an anatomical sneer, "O flesh, flesh, how art thou fishified!" (2.4.38), as though love had pickled his friend into cold meat. The full anatomy of this material, its wit and its wordplay, is traced in the companion study of [Mercutio's bawdy, its sex jokes, and its verbal fencing](/2022/09/16/mercutio-bawdy-wit-wordplay/); the point for the catalogue is the trajectory. Mercutio's bawdy begins as an argument against illusion and ends as a habit that cannot stop, a register that consumes every subject it touches, including, at the last, death itself, which he meets with the pun "you shall find me a grave man" (3.1.99). A man who can only speak of desire as appetite can only speak of dying as a joke about a grave, and the play lets the register complete itself in his mouth before silencing it forever.

Joseph Porter's reading of Mercutio remains the strongest account of why this voice carries such voltage. In Porter's analysis the bawdy is not ornamental clowning but a competing mode of speech, a dramatic argument conducted in obscenity: Mercutio's conjuring competes directly with Romeo's love language for possession of Romeo, spell against sonnet, appetite against adoration. That competition explains the aggression. The bawdy is aimed, always, at a listener it hopes to convert, and when Romeo escapes conversion by falling into a love Mercutio cannot see, the register turns on substitutes: the Nurse, Rosaline's ghost, Romeo's own fishified flesh. What looks like comic relief is a campaign, and its failure is one of the play's quiet catastrophes, because Mercutio dies still believing the campaign was the friendship.

## The Nurse's Register: Sex as Knowledge the Body Keeps

The Nurse is the only speaker in Verona whose sexual talk comes from inside the experience it describes, and the play marks the difference by giving her prose where the gentlemen perform in verse. Her great set piece in Act 1 Scene 3 is a memory of weaning, told while Lady Capulet attempts to open the subject of marriage, and its centerpiece is her dead husband's joke over the toddler Juliet, who had fallen on her face and raised a bump "as big as a young cock'rel's stone" (1.3.54). The husband picked the child up and teased her: "Thou wilt fall backward when thou hast more wit; wilt thou not, Jule?" and the child, "by my halidom," stopped crying and answered "Ay" (1.3.41-45). The Nurse has told the story so often that its punchline has become her measure of a life: "I warrant, an I should live a thousand years, I never should forget it" (1.3.46-47). Critics sometimes treat the anecdote as rambling. As discourse it is exact. A fall forward in childhood, a fall backward in bed: the joke compresses a whole female biography into two postures, and the Nurse's delight in it is the delight of a woman for whom sex is neither conquest nor appetite but a known country, entered through marriage, paid for in childbed, remembered with laughter because it is also where her own dead child and dead husband live.

### What does the Nurse know about desire that no parent will say aloud?

She knows it from the body's side: that a girl's future includes a marriage bed, that the bed is work and risk as well as pleasure, and that pretending otherwise is a luxury of rank. Her wedding-night talk treats consummation as an errand to be provisioned, ladders fetched, burdens borne (2.5.72-77).

Where Lady Capulet appraises and Capulet disposes, the Nurse instructs, because her knowledge was earned in her own flesh and in the flesh she nursed. Her register runs on earned knowledge, and the play keeps certifying the credential. She nursed Juliet at her own breast after her own daughter Susan died; her sexuality is inseparable from maternity, labor, and bereavement, which is why her bawdy never sounds like Mercutio's. When Mercutio performs desire, he performs mastery over it. When the Nurse jokes, she reports from within a life the joke has already happened to. The distinction governs her wedding-day usefulness. She is the one who carries the marriage's logistics, who warns Romeo in Act 2 Scene 4 against leading Juliet "into a fool's paradise" (2.4.166-167), who knows that "women grow by men" and says so in the street, and who, in Act 2 Scene 5, converts the wedding night into a set of practical arrangements: "There stays a husband to make you a wife... Hie you to church; I must another way, to fetch a ladder, by the which your love must climb a bird's nest soon when it is dark" (2.5.72-77). The famous tease in the same speech, that Juliet shall "bear the burden soon at night," is bawdy, but it is a midwife's bawdy, prospective and instructional, aimed at preparing a girl for an event the Nurse has survived and Juliet has not. Even her delight is diagnostic: the "wanton blood" rising in Juliet's cheeks (2.5.72) is read by the Nurse the way a nurse reads a symptom, as confirmation that the body knows what the household's euphemisms will not name.

The register has limits, and the play enforces them in Act 3 Scene 5, when the Nurse advises Juliet to marry Paris because Romeo is banished and the second husband will excel the first "in all likelihood" of use. The counsel is the register applied past its competence. Knowledge of the body can price a husband's convenience; it cannot weigh a vow. Juliet's revulsion, "Ancient damnation! O most wicked fiend!" (3.5.237), is partly a discovery that the voice which taught her desire's vocabulary has no vocabulary for fidelity. Yet even the failure confirms the register's importance to the system. The Nurse is the play's proof that sexual speech can be frank without being cruel, experienced without being cynical, and comic without being contemptuous, a combination no other adult in Verona achieves. When she vanishes from the play's counsel after Act 3, the lovers lose the only translator who could move between the household's language and their own, and the loss is one of the tragedy's quiet mechanisms.

## The Household Register: Appraisal, Possession, and the Older Generation's Appetites

Above the servants and below the lovers stands the register of the great house itself, where sex is neither joked about nor desired but administered. Lady Capulet introduces Paris to her daughter as a text for appraisal: "Read o'er the volume of young Paris' face, and find delight writ there with beauty's pen" (1.3.81-82), until the metaphor completes itself in the language of binding and ownership: "This precious book of love, this unbound lover, to beautify him only lacks a cover" (1.3.87-88). A cover, in the period's sexual slang and in plain bookbinding sense alike, is what a wife supplies. Desire, in this register, is a reading assignment; the girl is to inspect the merchandise's face, find the delight already written there, and supply the covering that makes the volume complete. It is the most genteel speech about sex in the play and in some ways the coldest, because it converts a fourteen-year-old's wanting into an act of literary appreciation performed on command, with the conclusion supplied in advance by the person assigning the book.

Paris speaks the register's other dialect, possession. Alone with Juliet in the Friar's cell, pressing a courtship she is evading with double meanings, he claims the spoils of a contract she never made: "Thy face is mine, and thou hast slandered it" (4.1.35). The sentence deserves its place in any catalogue of the play's sexuality, because it states the market's theory without adornment. Her face, her body's most public surface, is his property under the bargain her father struck; her tears are damage to his goods, slander against his own face. Nothing in the servants' opening boasts is as chilling, because the servants only fantasized about women they would never touch, while Paris speaks as an owner inspecting a purchase the law of the household is about to deliver. The play takes care to make him courteous, even gentle, elsewhere. That is the register's horror: it does not need cruelty. The grammar of ownership does the work all by itself, in the politest voice in Verona.

The older generation's own appetites surface only in asides, which is itself a fact about the register: rank licenses appetite in memory, never in the present tense. In the bustle of Act 4 Scene 4, dressing the wedding feast before dawn, the Nurse teases Capulet as a reformed "mouse-hunt," a chaser of women in his time, and Capulet, far from denying the past, boasts of nights spent watching "for lesser cause" (4.4.10-15). The exchange lasts seconds and vanishes under the errands, but the catalogue should record it, because it completes the household's sexual map. The father who will rage at his daughter's disobedience as a pollution of his house was himself a hunter in the streets; the mother who prices a husband's face was herself married and a mother at her daughter's age, as she states without embarrassment in Act 1 Scene 3. Desire in the great house is not absent. It is retrospective for the old, transactional for the suitors, and forbidden as a subject for the one person whose desire the plot actually concerns. The system's hypocrisy is structural, and Juliet's tragedy begins inside it: every adult around her has a sexual past and a sexual vocabulary, and every one of them requires her to enter marriage as though she had neither.

## Bawdy as Convention: What an Elizabethan Audience Heard

No register in the catalogue can be read correctly without the convention that produced it. Bawdy in the Elizabethan theatre was not a lapse in taste interrupting an otherwise decorous art; it was one of the art's standing resources, a licensed idiom with its own rules, and audiences were virtuoso hearers of it. The double sense was the sport. A word like tool, stone, prick, stand, or die carried its innocent meaning on the surface and its sexual meaning underneath, and the pleasure lay in hearing both at once, in catching the second sense a half-second after the first. Playwrights wrote for that split-second delay the way musicians write for an instrument's resonance. The public theatres held apprentices, merchants, servants, gentlewomen, and gallants in one yard, and the bawdy was pitched to travel across all of them: plain enough for the groundlings, quick enough to reward the educated, deniable enough, if challenged, to have been only a word about a sword, a clock, or a fruit. Plausible innocence was built into the form. Sampson can bite his thumb and swear he bites it at no one; Mercutio can conjure by a thigh and call it a joke about a missing friend. The convention's doubleness is social as much as verbal: it lets a whole culture say what it is officially not saying, in public, at two in the afternoon.

Deniability was the convention's legal architecture, and the play builds its first scene out of it. Sampson, having bitten his thumb at the Montague servants, retreats to counsel: "Is the law of our side if I say 'Ay'?" Gregory's answer, "No," is the whole doctrine in one syllable. The gesture must be performed and disowned in the same breath, or it becomes actionable, and the bawdy obeys the identical law. A pun can always plead its innocent meaning before any magistrate: the tool was a sword, the stone was a jewel, the maidenheads were a figure of speech about decapitation. This doubleness is why the sexual idiom belongs so naturally to a feuding city, where every aggression is simultaneously committed and denied, and why the servants, who understand the law of the thumb perfectly, are its most fluent speakers. It also explains the idiom's appeal to Mercutio, whose wit consists of saying the actionable thing in the unpunishable form, and its uselessness to the lovers, whose whole project is speech that cannot be disowned: a vow, a name exchanged, a wanting confessed in the first person and never retracted.

Partridge's Shakespeare's Bawdy, published in 1947 after decades in which editors had declined to explain what the words meant, established the scholarly baseline for this material: a glossary demonstrating that the sexual senses were systematic, recurrent across the canon, and recoverable by evidence rather than by prurient guesswork. The importance of the demonstration for this play is that it relocates the bawdy from the margins of taste to the center of meaning. If the double senses are systematic, then their distribution is a fact about the play's design. Who gets them, when, and at whom they are aimed becomes evidence as hard as any imagery pattern. And the distribution in this play is pointed. The bawdy is densest where desire is being policed, mocked, or sold, and it thins to nothing where desire is mutual and answered. A reader does not need to find every pun profound to see the architecture: the convention gives Shakespeare a public language for sex, and he assigns that language, with great precision, to everyone except the two people the play most concerns.

The convention also explains the play's strange afterlife as a school text. Bawdy built on double senses has a unique vulnerability: remove the second sense, by silence, by a gloss that explains only the sword, or by cutting the passage outright, and the text still reads smoothly. A soliloquy cut leaves a scar; a pun cut leaves nothing, because the innocent surface closes over the gap. Generations of editors exploited exactly this property, as the section on bowdlerization below sets out, and the result was a version of the play in which the registers below the lovers simply went quiet, and the lovers' own frankness, deprived of its contrasting floor, could be misread as the play's only sexual note and then softened in its turn. Restoring the convention is therefore not antiquarian pedantry. It is the recovery of the play's measuring scale. Without the servants' threats, Mercutio's spells, and the Nurse's midwifery, there is no way to hear what is new in Juliet's voice when she finally uses the first person to want something.

## The Lovers' Counter-Register: Prayer, Touch, and the First Person

The lovers enter the system with the odds stacked against them. Every sexual vocabulary Verona offers is corrupt for their purpose: conquest would make Romeo a Sampson, appetite would make him Mercutio's patient, appraisal would make Juliet her mother's pupil, possession would make her Paris's face. Their solution, improvised across two scenes, is to build a register out of what the system lacks: mutuality, consent negotiated line by line, and desire spoken by the woman as plainly as by the man. The shared sonnet at the feast is the register's founding document. Romeo approaches through the pilgrim conceit, his lips "two blushing pilgrims, ready stand to smooth that rough touch with a tender kiss" (1.5.95-96), and the conceit works because Juliet does not merely receive it; she corrects, extends, and out-argues it, granting the palm-to-palm contact, refusing the escalation, and finally naming the result with a critic's detachment: "You kiss by th' book" (1.5.109). The erotic charge of the scene is real, and its vehicle is sacred vocabulary, prayer, shrines, sin, purgation, but the charge depends on the exchange being a negotiation between equals conducted in the open, at a dance, in fourteen lines whose rhymes they must cooperate to complete. Against the servants' monologue of threat and Mercutio's monologue of mockery, the sonnet is a dialogue, the first sexual speech in the play in which a woman answers back and the answer changes what happens.

### How does the lovers' language differ from every other register in the play?

It is the only register built on reply. The servants boast at absent women, Mercutio conjures an absent Rosaline, and Paris claims a silent face. Romeo and Juliet speak to each other, and each adjusts to what the other says: a sonnet divided between two mouths, a vow renegotiated line by line.

Desire elsewhere in Verona is monologue, and the balcony scene extends the invention and exposes its mechanics. Romeo's longing is tactile and distanced at once: "O, that I were a glove upon that hand, that I might touch that cheek!" (2.2.24-25). He wishes to be an object she owns, touching her by her own choice of wearing him, a fantasy of contact that cedes all initiative to her. Juliet, overheard, speaks the register's charter of honesty: "Thou knowest the mask of night is on my face, else would a maiden blush bepaint my cheek for that which thou hast heard me speak tonight" (2.2.52-54). The lines admit what no other speaker in Verona admits, that modesty is a performance daylight enforces and darkness suspends, and that her spoken wanting is true precisely because it was not composed for his hearing. From there the scene becomes a negotiation in earnest: oaths offered and refused as too lightly sworn, the contract named too rash and too sudden, satisfaction defined as "the exchange of thy love's faithful vow for mine" (2.2.127). Sex is present everywhere in the scene's pressure and nowhere in its vocabulary of parts and acts, because the lovers are building a language in which desire is a relation between persons rather than a claim on a body. The separate study of [female desire in the play and its claim on the action](/2024/03/01/female-desire-romeo-and-juliet/) follows what that language does to Juliet's agency; here the point is the register itself, a new instrument the play had to construct because none of Verona's existing ones could play this music.

The register reaches its fullest statement in Juliet's epithalamium, Act 3 Scene 2, and the catalogue must be exact about what happens there while compressing what belongs to another article. Alone, married that afternoon, waiting for the night and the ladder, Juliet summons darkness in her own voice: "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds, towards Phoebus' lodging" (3.2.1-2), and calls for "love-performing night" (3.2.5) to bring Romeo to her. The speech is the most frankly desiring utterance in the play, and its frankness is grammatical: first person, imperative mood, the bride as the desiring subject rather than the desired object. She has "bought the mansion of a love, but not possessed it" (3.2.26-27), a property metaphor stolen from the household register and turned to her own use, purchase completed, possession awaited, the awaited thing being not a face to own but a night to share. The full reading of this speech, its falconry and its stars, belongs to the study of [Juliet's 'Gallop apace' soliloquy and its verse of desire](/2022/07/15/juliet-gallop-apace-soliloquy-desire/); within the system of registers, its function is to prove that the counter-register can carry explicit wanting without borrowing a single word from the bawdy below it. When the news of Tybalt's death poisons the same speech's close, Juliet's cry that "death, not Romeo, take my maidenhead" (3.2.137) lands with its full horror precisely because the audience has just heard the maidenhead's intended destination spoken by its owner, in joy, twenty minutes of stage time earlier.

The counter-register does not survive contact with Verona, and its extinction is part of the design. After the consummation, which the next sections take up, the lovers never again share a scene of negotiated speech; their remaining exchanges are a dawn argument about birdsong conducted in the shadow of exile, and then silence across a city's width, letters, and lies. The tomb gives them no dialogue at all. Romeo addresses a body he believes dead; Juliet wakes to address one that is. The register built for two voices ends with each voice speaking alone to someone who cannot answer, in a vault where the only other sexual vocabulary left is the one examined next: the pun on dying, which the coarse speakers coined as a joke and the lovers inherit as a fate.

## The Word Die: Sexual Vocabulary at the Edge of the Tomb

One word threads the whole system together, and this article claims it only as vocabulary. In the English of the 1590s, to die could mean to perish and could also mean to reach sexual climax, a double sense as familiar to the play's first hearers as the double sense of tool or stand. The pun is everywhere in the period's erotic verse, and Shakespeare's audience brought it to the theatre as part of their listening equipment. Its distribution in this play is a small map of the registers. Benvolio prescribes desire as medicine in its terms: Romeo should take "some new infection to thy eye, and the rank poison of the old will die" (1.2.49-50). The old love dies, perishes, and the cure is a fresh object for the eye, appetite treated as a fever that one infection drives out. The word there is medicinal, the pun dormant. In the servants' and Mercutio's mouths the sexual sense is the active one, the smirk under the surface. In the lovers' mouths, almost never. Romeo and Juliet, who speak of wanting with a directness no one else attempts, do not joke with this word. The play reserves their encounters with it for the places where the other meaning has become literal.

That reservation is a fact of vocabulary before it is anything else, and it deserves to be stated with care, because the fusion of love and death as the play's governing pattern is the owned subject of [the Liebestod reading of the lovers' entwined ends](/2024/01/05/love-and-death-liebestod-analysis/), not of this catalogue. What belongs here is narrower and, for the discourse argument, decisive. The same small word serves the street's smirk and the tomb's silence, and the audience hears both senses whether the speakers intend them or not. When Juliet, fearing Romeo dead in Act 3 Scene 2, cries that death should take her maidenhead in his place, the line's obscenity and its horror are a single effect: the vocabulary of the wedding night and the vocabulary of the grave have collapsed into one word, and the collapse is the sound of the counter-register being repossessed by the registers beneath it. When Romeo in the vault imagines "unsubstantial Death" as "amorous," keeping Juliet "in dark to be his paramour" (5.3.103-105), the personification is the servants' philosophy returned as metaphysics: a male rival who takes women by force and keeps them, Sampson's boast enlarged to cosmic scale. And when Juliet takes the dagger with "This is thy sheath; there rust, and let me die" (5.3.169-170), the pun completes its journey through the system, spoken at last by the play's most honest voice, in the one register where neither sense can be separated from the other.

Editors have not always let readers see this. A gloss that renders die as expire, full stop, in every occurrence, quietly abolishes the word's second life and with it the audience's ability to hear the system working. Partridge's glossary restored the sexual sense to the word's entry, and modern editions in Weis's line print the double meaning where the context activates it. The editorial question, which words carry the bawdy sense and which do not, is one of the places where the chaste reputation was manufactured, gloss by gloss, and it returns in the discussion of the critics below. For the catalogue, the lesson is simpler. A vocabulary is a system of permissions. Verona permits the word die to mean sex in a joke and forbids it to mean sex in a vow, and the play's ending is what happens when the permissions fail and the word means everything at once.

## The Consummation and the Clock: Why the Wedding Night Decides the Plot

Sexuality in this play is not only a set of voices; it is a mechanism, and the mechanism is the consummation's placement in time. The sequence deserves to be stated flatly, because its flatness is the argument. Romeo and Juliet meet on Sunday night, marry on Monday afternoon, and are separated within hours by the duel that kills Mercutio and Tybalt. The marriage is consummated on Monday night, in Juliet's chamber, reached by the rope ladder the Nurse fetched, while Romeo's banishment hangs over the room and dawn will enforce it. The lovers' whole married life, from vow to parting, occupies a single night, and every catastrophe after that night is shaped by the fact that the consummation happened when it did: after the wedding, so that Juliet is a wife and the Paris marriage would be bigamy; before the exile, so that the parting is a sundering of an accomplished union rather than a postponed one; and in secret, so that no one who could protect the marriage knows it exists when Capulet sells it again on Thursday.

### What changes in the play once the marriage is consummated?

Everything the plot can still do. Before the wedding night, the lovers' danger is discovery; after it, the danger is a second marriage, which would make Juliet a bigamist. Consummation converts a romance into a legal and sacramental fact Verona cannot be told, and the potion plot, the false death, and the tomb all follow from that single conversion.

The play insists on the consummation's reality while keeping it, by the period's stage decorum, entirely offstage and unspoken in any coarse term. What it gives instead is the before and the after, in the counter-register's own language. Before: Juliet's epithalamium, the impatient summons of night examined above, a bride awaiting a husband she has already wed. The Friar's instructions in Act 3 Scene 3 send Romeo to "ascend her chamber" and comfort Juliet before fleeing to Mantua, and the errand is ordered with a strategist's clarity: the night is not a romantic interlude but the marriage's completion and its only possible completion, the last hour in which the union can become fact before exile makes it a memory. After: the aubade of Act 3 Scene 5, the lark and the nightingale, two people arguing with the dawn from inside a shared bed's aftermath. "Wilt thou be gone? It is not yet near day" (3.5.1) is the first line of the play spoken by a wife to a husband who has spent the night, and the scene's every subsequent horror, the mother's announcement of Paris, the father's rage, the Nurse's defection, draws its voltage from the audience's knowledge of what the night contained. Juliet's double meanings to her mother, her vow that the bridal bed be made "in that dim monument where Tybalt lies" (3.5.200-201), are a wife's coded speech, unintelligible to the household precisely because the household does not know she has a husband to be coded about.

Set against the haste argument, the consummation is where sexuality and speed lock together. The Friar warned that violent delights have violent ends and counseled wisely and slow, but the plot he serves permits no slowness: the marriage had to be consummated that night or risk never being consummated at all, since Romeo's exile began at dawn and Capulet's counter-wedding was already being scheduled. Desire in Verona is not hasty because the lovers are impatient children; it is hasty because the city's machinery, the feud's violence, the father's market, the Prince's sentence, leaves desire exactly one night in which to be lawful. The bawdy registers understand this in their way. The servants treat sex as something seized in passing. Mercutio treats it as something that cannot wait to be laughed at. The Nurse treats the wedding night as an appointment to be provisioned against a deadline. Only the criticism that reads the play as pure romance misses what every coarse voice in it knows: in this city, the body keeps the schedule, because the schedule is set by men with swords, and the tomb is simply the last and narrowest of the rooms in which the lovers are permitted to meet.

## Romeo's Two Desires: The Siege of Rosaline and the Silence Afterward

One speaker tests the catalogue's whole scheme, because Romeo alone migrates between registers, and his migration is the play's sexual plot in miniature. He begins inside a literary imitation of desire, the Petrarchan siege of Rosaline, and the imagery gives him away. Rosaline "will not stay the siege of loving terms, nor bide th' encounter of assailing eyes, nor ope her lap to saint-seducing gold" (1.1.211-213); she "hath Dian's wit" (1.1.207), the chastity of the moon goddess, and Romeo's complaint is the complaint of a besieger locked out of a fortress. The register is borrowed from the sonnet tradition, but its underlying picture is the servants' own: a woman as a walled place, a man as an army, sex as the breaching of defenses by persistence or bribery. Romeo speaks it in verse and oxymoron, "O brawling love, O loving hate" (1.1.176), which raises the diction without changing the doctrine. He has never spoken to Rosaline in the play's present; she exists for him exactly as she exists for Sampson's imagined maids, as a body defined by its refusal of access. The Friar diagnoses the condition precisely when the new love replaces the old overnight: the earlier love "did read by rote, that could not spell" (2.3.87), recitation without literacy, desire as a memorized part.

The proof that Romeo learns a new register is negative, and it is one of the play's most telling patterns: Romeo never jokes bawdily. He is surrounded by the idiom. Mercutio performs it at him, the servants open the play with it, Benvolio prescribes cures in its spirit, and Romeo, who matches Mercutio pun for pun when the subject is anything else, never once answers in kind about a woman's body. His desire, once it has an object who answers him, sheds the idiom entirely. What replaces it is the tactile, devotional language of the sonnet and the orchard, and, in extremity, silence about the body altogether: his great speeches concern names, stars, banishment, fortune, and death, while the one night that defines his marriage passes without a single speech of his describing it. Even his jealousy is displaced into metaphysics, the tomb fantasy of Death as paramour examined above, in which the sexual threat he could never joke about returns as a cosmic rival he cannot fight. The catalogue therefore records Romeo twice: once among the besiegers, in the borrowed register of the siege, and once at the system's top, in a register he could only enter by abandoning the first. His tragedy has many causes, but one of them is linguistic. Verona gave him two vocabularies for desire, conquest and worship, and nothing between them; the second served him for five days, and neither could be spoken aloud in the city that killed him.

## Partridge, Porter, and the Editors: The Critics' Quarrel over the Bawdy

Three bodies of work frame the modern study of this material, and they do not agree. Eric Partridge, whose Shakespeare's Bawdy recovered the sexual lexicon in 1947, stands for the philological position: the bawdy is a vocabulary, the common property of the period, and the critic's first duty is to establish what the words meant before presuming to say what any passage does. On Partridge's account, Mercutio's obscenity is the age talking. The puns were the small change of Elizabethan wit, minted by convention and spent by everyone, and reading a private psychology into them mistakes the currency for the man. Joseph Porter stands opposite. His study of Mercutio argues that the bawdy in this play is dramatically placed with an exactness that convention cannot explain: Mercutio's obscenity is a mode of action, a sustained attempt to govern Romeo's desire by ridicule, and its escalating bitterness tracks the failure of that attempt. Where Partridge hears an idiom, Porter hears a campaign. The third party to the quarrel is editorial. Thomas Bowdler's Family Shakspeare, prepared with his sister Henrietta and published in 1807 and enlarged in 1818, rested on the stated principle that passages "which cannot with propriety be read aloud in a family" could be omitted without injury to the work, a principle that treats the bawdy as detachable decoration. René Weis's Arden edition represents the modern editorial consensus against that principle: the text printed whole, the double senses glossed where the context activates them, the bawdy treated as evidence a reader is owed.

The disagreement between Partridge and Porter is genuine, and the catalogue adjudicates it. Partridge is right about the words and wrong about the conclusion he invites. The vocabulary is conventional; Sampson's maidenheads, Gregory's tool, the hag, the prick of noon, all spend the common coin, and any reading that treats each pun as a unique invention collapses. But convention is a repertoire, not a distribution, and the distribution in this play is Porter's evidence. Shakespeare gives the bawdy overwhelmingly to speakers who are policing, selling, or mocking desire, and withholds it from the lovers at exactly the moments when desire is mutual. He gives Mercutio not merely the idiom but the idiom in concentration, at a dosage no other character approaches, aimed with increasing desperation at one listener. A convention can explain why the words exist; only intention explains why they are here, in this mouth, at this pitch, and never in that one. The verdict, then: Partridge supplies the dictionary, Porter supplies the drama, and the play needs both, because its method is to take the period's ordinary sexual small change and spend it so selectively that the spending becomes characterization.

The editorial quarrel admits a cleaner verdict, because its consequences are measurable in the text's survival. Bowdler's principle assumes that the passages omitted are ornaments on a structure that stands without them. The catalogue refutes the assumption at three load-bearing points. Cut the servants' opening, and the feud loses its stated sexual logic, the gentlemen's honor code floats free of the appetite it encodes, and the play begins with a sonnet about dignity that its own action never cashes. Cut or soften the Nurse, and Juliet's education in desire vanishes, leaving her epithalamium to appear from nowhere, a frankness without a source, which is precisely how generations of readers misread it, as an inexplicable impropriety in a pure heroine. Cut Mercutio's conjuring and his clock, and Romeo's silence about the body loses its contrast; there is no longer a register he is refusing, and his tenderness can be mistaken for the play's default rather than its invention. An ornament, by definition, can be removed without changing what the structure means. These passages change what everything else means. They are not ornaments, and the editions that removed them did not protect the play; they rewrote it, substituting a romance about two innocents for a tragedy about a city's sexual order and the two children who briefly spoke outside it.

## Bowdler's Shadow: School Editions and the Chaste Reputation

The Family Shakspeare was a publishing success because it answered a real anxiety, the wish to give Shakespeare to households and daughters without giving them the words the age used for the body. Its method was omission, announced in advance as a virtue, and its success founded a tradition far longer than any single edition: the expurgated school text, in which the play circulated to the young through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries with its bawdy thinned, its Nurse trimmed, its Mercutio softened, and its glosses tactfully explaining the swords and clocks and fruits at their innocent face value only. The verification record matters here, because the softening was not random. The lines that disappeared or were glossed into innocence cluster exactly where the catalogue's lower and middle registers operate: the maidenhead exchange in the opening scene, the hag passage at the close of Queen Mab, the anatomy of the conjuring, the fall-backward joke, the wedding-night teasing about burdens and ladders. What survived intact, in edition after edition, was the balcony, the sonnet, the tomb: the counter-register, preserved and then misunderstood, because a register can only be heard against the system it answers. School readers met lovers who spoke beautifully about desire in a city that apparently had no other way of speaking about it, and drew the available conclusion, that the play's subject was a feeling too pure for the world. The purity was the cut's artifact.

Performance kept the same accounts in a different currency. Stage tradition trimmed the Nurse's reminiscence as a digression, hurried Queen Mab as a showpiece detachable from the plot, and treated the servants' opening as dispensable warmth for the groundlings, long after the groundlings' descendants were writing the textbooks. Each cut was defensible in the evening's terms and ruinous in the library's, because the play's sexual argument is cumulative: it depends on the audience carrying the lower registers in memory when the higher ones sound. A production that cuts the conjuring has not merely saved four minutes; it has decided that Romeo's desire needs no antagonist, that Juliet's frankness needs no genealogy, that the tomb's paramour needs no ancestry in a servant's boast. The chaste reputation of the play, the one the brief for this article sets out to test, is the sum of those decisions, repeated until they hardened into a fact about Shakespeare rather than a fact about his editors.

Restoration has been the work of the last century, and it is philological before it is theatrical. Partridge's glossary made the second senses undeniable; editors in the line of Weis print the whole text and gloss the bawdy in the commentary where earlier editions printed asterisks or silence; Porter and his successors gave the material dramatic standing, so that a modern production cutting the conjuring must now argue for the cut against the criticism rather than assume it. The result is a play measurably more physical than the one the school tradition transmitted: more threatened, more knowing, more obscene in its streets and more daring in its heroine, and, by the same restoration, more intelligible in its tenderness. The lovers' language reads differently when the audience knows what it costs to speak that way in Verona. It reads as an achievement under pressure, which is what the text, whole, always said it was.

## Sex and Violence: One Vocabulary for Two Appetites

The catalogue's deepest pattern is one the rows state only by accumulation: in Verona, the language of desire and the language of fighting are the same language, and every speaker who treats sex as conquest is rehearsing for the duel. The opening scene builds the identity into its smallest units. Sampson's maidenheads are heads to be cut off; defloration and decapitation share a verb and a joke. Gregory's "draw thy tool" arms the body and the sword in one gesture. The thumb is bitten, the weapon is naked, the maids are thrust to the wall: the scene's erotics are a weapons drill, and when the drill becomes a brawl minutes later, nothing has changed except the target. The gentlemen inherit the fusion at a higher polish. Tybalt's honor is a sexual sensitivity translated into steel, a readiness to feel insult in the groin of his pride and answer it with penetration by rapier. Mercutio, describing that style, reaches instinctively for the fencing manual's numbered thrusts and couples them with his usual anatomical contempt, as though the duel were only the bedroom's grammar conjugated in public. Even the Friar, the play's moralist, cannot separate the appetites when he warns the bridegroom: "These violent delights have violent ends, and in their triumph die, like fire and powder, which, as they kiss, consume" (2.6.9-11). Delight and destruction share the sentence; the kiss is the explosion's trigger; the word die does its double work in a churchman's mouth, at a wedding, as a prophecy.

The fusion runs in the other direction as well, and the tomb is where the accounts are settled. Romeo's final scene imagines death itself in the predation register, a rival male who has "sucked the honey of thy breath" (5.3.92) and keeps Juliet as a paramour in the dark. The image is often praised as poetry and rarely indicted as what it is: the servants' philosophy, returned. Sampson boasted of taking the enemy's women; Romeo, at the end of the play's education, can conceive of no power in the universe, not even death, except as another Sampson, taking the woman he cannot keep. The counter-register he built with Juliet has no jurisdiction in the vault, because she cannot answer there, and the answering was the whole invention. Juliet's own last act repeats the fusion from the other side: the dagger finds its "sheath" (5.3.170) in her body, a sexual image of penetration and enclosure performing a killing, spoken by the play's frankest voice as her final line of agency. The play does not present these fusions as ornaments on its tragedy. They are its mechanism made audible. A city that can only imagine desire as an act of force will eventually perform its desires by force, in the street, in the square, and in the tomb, and the five deaths are the moments when the metaphor stops being one.

Haste is the fusion's tempo, and the catalogue's timing evidence belongs with the violence, because in this play the two appetites share not only a vocabulary but a clock. Feuds and desires both refuse delay: the insult answered tomorrow is honor lost, the wedding postponed is desire mocked, and every authority who counsels slowness, the Friar with his wisely and slow, Capulet with his two more summers, is overrun by the same impatience wearing different clothes. The consummation's single night, examined above, is the clock's masterpiece, a lawful act performed at unlawful speed because the city's other appetite, violence, had already consumed the intervening day. Read the play's sexuality whole and a verdict about its violence follows: the tragedy is not that Verona desires too much and fights too much, as separate sins. It is that Verona has only one way to want anything, by seizure, at once, against resistance, and it applies that way to women, to honor, to marriages, and to graves. The lovers' counter-register was an attempt to want otherwise, slowly, mutually, in answered sentences. The city gave it five days and one night, and then resumed its single grammar over their bodies.

## Virginity and the Maidenhead: The Play's Most Contested Word

No single word in the play's sexual vocabulary is fought over like maidenhead, because the word names the one asset every register prices differently. To the servants it is spoil, a thing to be cut off the enemy's maids as a preliminary to cutting off heads. To the marriage market it is inventory: Juliet's virginity is the Capulet house's contribution to the Paris bargain, the sealed condition in which the merchandise must be delivered, and Capulet's whole choreography of delay, display, and sudden acceleration is inventory management conducted in the language of paternal care. To the Church's representative it is a state to be lawfully transferred, which is why the Friar's secret wedding matters so much to the plot: the transfer happens off the market's books, in a cell, on a Monday, and every later speech about Juliet's availability is spoken over a fact no one in the room possesses. To Juliet herself, alone among the speakers, the maidenhead is hers, a part of her own body whose destination she has chosen, announced, and awaited in the first person. The word's journey through the play is the system in miniature: the same anatomy, priced as spoil, stock, sacrament, and selfhood, depending entirely on who is speaking.

Rosaline is the word's negative pole, the virginity that refuses all transfers. She never appears, yet her vow organizes the play's first two acts: she has "forsworn to love," armed herself with Diana's wit and chastity's proof against Cupid's bow, and Romeo's siege language breaks against her absence like a wave against a harbor wall. The play is careful never to let Rosaline speak, never to let her refusal become a position argued in her own voice; she exists only as reported by the men her refusal frustrates. That silence is itself a datum for the catalogue. Verona's discourse has no register in which a woman's no to desire can be stated as anything but a defect, a siege not yet won, a humor to be cured by infection of the eye. Juliet's yes, when it comes, is revolutionary partly because the play has shown the audience what happens to a woman's no: it is talked about, endlessly, by everyone except her.

Against Rosaline's withheld virginity the play sets Juliet's bestowed one, and the contrast is drawn in vocabulary. Where Romeo's Rosaline speeches borrow the market's and the army's words, siege, terms, gold, Juliet's speeches about her own state use the language of gift and exchange between persons: her bounty boundless as the sea, given and grown by giving. The wedding night's approach sharpens the distinction to a point. The Nurse prices the night as burden and ladder, an errand completed. Juliet prices it as possession of a mansion already bought, a self taking up residence in its own desire. Even the catastrophe honors the distinction. When Juliet believes Romeo dead, her cry assigns the maidenhead to death as a robbed inheritance, the one outcome in which the word returns to the servants' register against her will, spoil taken by the ultimate enemy house. And when she wakes in the vault to find the transfer reversed by death altogether, her final act reclaims the word's ownership in the only way left, on her own body, by her own hand. The catalogue records virginity, then, not as a theme but as a title deed, disputed by every register in the play, and legible in the end only in the handwriting of the girl it named.

## Reading the Bawdy Clinically: Neither Prudery nor Titillation

The brief for this article names its own danger, and the danger is real: writing about a play's sexuality invites two corruptions, the prudery that will not look and the titillation that will not stop looking, and both falsify the evidence in the same way, by making the material about the reader's feelings rather than the play's design. The clinical method this article has followed is the corrective, and it can be stated as three rules. Quote exactly, and cite the edition, because a paraphrase of bawdy always drifts toward one corruption or the other: soften the words and the passage's aggression evaporates; relish them and its function disappears into the smirk the passage itself may be indicting. Gloss from evidence, from Partridge's lexicon and the editors' commentaries, not from ingenuity, because a sexual sense that cannot be documented in the period's usage is the critic's invention wearing the play's clothes. And keep the question functional at all times: not what the joke does to a modern reader's comfort, but what work it does in the scene, whose power it builds, whose it punctures, what it permits the plot to do next. Judged by those rules, the servants' maidenheads are not an embarrassment to be skipped but a thesis to be answered; Mercutio's conjuring is not a party piece but an attempted exorcism of a friend; the Nurse's fall backward is not comic relief but a curriculum. The bawdy is the play thinking, in the idiom its city gave it, about what people are for. Taking it seriously means refusing both to blush for it and to leer with it, and that refusal, maintained across sixteen registers, is what separates a catalogue from a snigger.

## The Friar's Register: Celibacy, Physic, and Desire as Husbandry

The catalogue has one more licensed voice to record, the strangest of all: a celibate friar who speaks of desire with the confidence of a gardener discussing grafts. Friar Laurence's sexual register is natural philosophy. His dawn soliloquy in Act 2 Scene 3 sorts the world into generation and decay in a single couplet: "The earth that's nature's mother is her tomb; what is her burying grave, that is her womb" (2.3.9-10). Womb and grave, birth and burial, are one organ in his botany, and the herbs he gathers are potent precisely because the same plant can cure or kill "being misapplied." Desire, in this register, is a virtue of nature that turns vice only when misapplied, a force to be husbanded rather than a sin to be scourged or an itch to be beaten down. It is the most systematic sexual theory in the play, and its owner has renounced the practice it theorizes. The play enjoys the paradox too much to resolve it: the man who understands desire best as a system is the man sworn never to feel it, and his understanding, flawless as doctrine, proves helpless as counsel, because every application he attempts in the plot goes wrong at the speed of the appetites he is managing.

His diagnostic skill is genuine, and its idiom is the body's fluids. Faced with Romeo's overnight conversion, he reads the evidence like a physician: "Young men's love then lies not truly in their hearts, but in their eyes" (2.3.67-68), and counts the cost of the old love in brine, the salt water "hath washed thy sallow cheeks for Rosaline" (2.3.69-70). Love as ophthalmia, grief as discharge, desire as a humoral event: the Friar's register medicalizes what Mercutio mocks and the servants boast about, and the medicalizing is a form of power, because a physician may say to a young man what no parent may. He uses the license to conduct the play's most consequential sexual act, the marriage itself, and his stated motive belongs to the husbandry register throughout: "For this alliance may so happy prove, to turn your households' rancour to pure love" (2.3.91-92). The marriage is a graft, two feuding stocks joined so that hatred's sap runs into love's fruit. Even his wedding-day management is horticultural scheduling, "we will make short work" (2.6.36), the ceremony hurried like a planting before weather turns. No other speaker in Verona thinks about sex as a system of nature to be cultivated. The Friar's tragedy is that systems of nature do not take instructions from gardeners once the season turns: the potion, his masterpiece of applied botany, works perfectly as physic and catastrophically as policy.

The register's limit appears where the play tests it against a body in despair. In Act 3 Scene 3, with Romeo prostrate and suicidal in the cell, the Friar's counsel deserts philosophy for the oldest sexual policing in Verona's repertoire: gender shame. "Art thou a man? Thy form cries out thou art; thy tears are womanish" (3.3.109-110), he demands, and drives the taxonomy home, an "unseemly woman in a seeming man" (3.3.112-113) being his diagnosis of a bridegroom's grief. The moment matters to the catalogue because it shows the registers are not sealed compartments. Under pressure, even the celibate philosopher reaches for the street's equation, in which manhood is proved by dryness of eye and mastery of feeling, the same equation Sampson performed with a sword and a boast in the play's first minute. The Friar recovers, builds his plan, and articulates in the same scene the counter-doctrine of the "pack of blessings," but the slip is recorded, and it completes the system's bleakest finding. Verona has many registers for desire and only one for a man in tears, and that one is contempt. The lovers' counter-register, which had room for a weeping bridegroom and a desiring bride, was the city's only alternative, and by Act 3 it is already under sentence of exile.

## The Eye as Organ of Desire: Looking, Watching, and Being Seen

One more strand runs through every register, the strand of sight, and the catalogue is incomplete without it, because in this play desire almost always enters through the eye before it reaches any other organ. The Friar states the doctrine as diagnosis: young men's love lies "not truly in their hearts, but in their eyes" (2.3.67-68). Benvolio prescribes by the same physiology, a new infection of the eye to kill the old one's poison. Romeo's siege of Rosaline was conducted by "assailing eyes" she would not bide; his conversion happens at a feast, by looking, across a crowded room, at a girl dancing with another man; and his first speech about Juliet is a speech about what she does to light, torches taught to burn bright, a jewel in an Ethiope's ear. The lovers' story can be told as an itinerary of looks: the feast's exchanged glances, the orchard's overheard soliloquy, where Juliet's desire becomes audible only because she believes herself unseen, the window partings, the vault's final inspection of a face misread as dead. Even the catastrophe is an error of the eye: Balthasar reports what he saw, a body laid in a tomb, and Romeo dies of a true report of a false appearance.

The gaze in Verona is not innocent, and the play grades it like every other register. At the bottom, looking is surveillance and appraisal: the fathers watch their children's faces for obedience, the Prince's watch patrols the streets, and Paris inspects a face he calls his own. In the household register, looking is the bride's assigned labor, "look to like" being Lady Capulet's whole instruction, an eye ordered to manufacture the desire it reports. Mercutio's gaze is predatory in a different key: his conjuring strips Rosaline by inventory, his mockery reduces the Nurse to a sail and a fan, and his Queen Mab grants him the voyeur's ultimate privilege, a tour of every bedroom and battlefield in the city while the dreamers lie still. Only the lovers convert looking into a mutual act. The shared sonnet begins in a look across a dance floor and ends in touch negotiated by speech; the balcony scene reverses the feast's geometry, the man hidden and listening below, the woman exposed at the window and speaking first, so that the play's most desired body is also its most articulate voice. Juliet's complaint, when the machinery turns, is precisely that the economy of sight has trapped her: seen by Paris as property, by her parents as inventory, by Romeo, at the end, as a corpse. Her last look in the play is the one she turns on Romeo's body, and her reading of it, lips still warm, a churl who drank all the poison and left none, is the only accurate act of seeing in Act 5. The catalogue therefore adds its final row in prose rather than in the table: sight itself, the medium all the registers share, graded from surveillance at the bottom to recognition at the top, and measuring, as faithfully as any pun, the distance between Verona's desire and the lovers' invention of it.

The tomb completes the itinerary of looks by inverting it, and the inversion is the play's final comment on desire's eye. Romeo opens his vault speech by ordering his own sight to its end, "Eyes, look your last" (5.3.112), and then performs the most careful inspection of Juliet's body in the whole text, noting that "Death's pale flag is not advanced there" and that beauty's ensign "is crimson in thy lips and in thy cheeks" (5.3.94-96). The audience, which knows the inspection is true in its observations and false in its conclusion, watches a man read a living face as a dead one, because the register of looking has no room for the fact that could save him: that the body he is studying is not a corpse but a wife asleep. Juliet's counter-look, when she wakes, is the play's only correction, brief and terminal: lips still warm, a dagger still poisoned with nothing left to drink, a body warm enough to kiss and already cold to argument. The stage itself has been complicit in this economy from the first scene. A public theatre asks hundreds of strangers to watch a chamber, a wedding bed, a tomb, and to feel the looking as participation, and the bawdy that seems coarse in print is, on stage, the audience's own share in the city's gaze, licensed by laughter. The catalogue's final finding is therefore also a warning to its own readers. A study of the play's sexuality that treats its looking as a neutral theme misses the one sense in which every reader is a character in the play: Verona's eye, its appraising, desiring, misreading eye, is also the eye with which the play is read.

## The Verdict: How Central Is the Body?

Central enough that removing it removes the play. The evidence assembled here supports three conclusions, in ascending order of weight. First, the play's sexuality is systematic: a graded set of registers, distributed by speaker and rank with an exactness that survives counting, in which desire is imagined as conquest, appetite, knowledge, appraisal, and finally, in two voices only, as mutual speech. Second, the system is causal, not decorative: it sets the plot's clock through the consummation, it arms the feud with its founding jokes, it educates the heroine through the Nurse's curriculum, and it supplies the tomb with its last metaphors. Third, the play's celebrated tenderness is an event inside the system, not an escape from it: Romeo and Juliet's counter-register is built against the bawdy, priced by it, and finally extinguished with it, when the vault returns their story to the registers of seizure and silence. A romance that floats above the body could survive the loss of any of these elements. This play survives the loss of none of them. The chaste reputation is therefore not a misreading of tone but a misdescription of fact, maintained for two centuries by editions that cut the evidence, and the restored text delivers a harder, funnier, more dangerous play, one in which the body is not the romance's obstacle or its reward but its language, its clock, and its grave.

The verdict re-reads the famous scenes in passing, and they do not survive unchanged. The balcony, anthologized as innocence communing with the stars, becomes something braver: a girl who has been taught desire by a servant's anecdotes negotiating her own wanting aloud, in a city that punishes female speech, with a boy who has discarded the siege language of his class and not yet found any other. The shared sonnet, so often exhibited as the play's purity in miniature, reveals its actual mechanism, an erotic bargain conducted in the vocabulary of the shrine because every secular vocabulary in Verona was a vocabulary of seizure. And the tomb, the reputation's shrine of shrines, shows its true composition at last: a monument built by the lower registers, stocked with their metaphors of paramours and sheaths, into which the counter-register's two speakers enter and cannot speak to each other at all. Nothing in these scenes is diminished by the catalogue's evidence. They are larger with it, because their tenderness is now legible as the achievement it was, two voices briefly inventing, against the whole weight of their city's sexual order, a way of wanting that did not require anyone to be taken.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What do the servants' opening boasts treat women as, and why does Shakespeare give that view the play's first words about desire?

They treat women as spoil of the feud, bodies on which one house inscribes its victory over the other. Sampson's vow to cut off the maids' heads, or their maidenheads, taking it "in what sense thou wilt" (1.1.24-26), prices a woman's virginity as an outwork to be demolished after the men are beaten, and Gregory's "draw thy tool" (1.1.33) arms the joke. Shakespeare gives this register the play's first words about desire because it is the baseline every other register will be measured against. Before the Prologue's dignity, before any love poetry, the audience learns Verona's sexual common sense in its crudest licensed form, spoken by servants in prose in a public street. The placement is the argument: appetite armed is the city's ground note, and the lovers' tenderness will have to be invented in hearing of it, against it, and finally over its dead.

### Q: Why does the play's sexual frankness fall as a speaker's rank rises?

Because rank in Verona is a license to euphemize, and the play distributes plain speech downward as a deliberate inversion. The servants say maidenhead and mean a sexual threat; Mercutio, a gentleman, says it in puns that can be denied; the Nurse, a servant with a mother's authority, says it in anecdote and instruction; and the great house, at the top, will not say it at all, preferring the book and the cover, the face read as a text. The higher the speaker, the more desire must be translated into property, liturgy, or silence before it may be mentioned. The pattern exposes the household's hypocrisy as structure rather than character: Capulet raged at his daughter's disobedience was a mouse-hunt in his youth, Lady Capulet was a mother at Juliet's age, and both require the subject to arrive in their presence costumed as a marriage contract. Frankness falls with rank because frankness is information, and information about desire is power the great house hoards by refusing to speak it.

### Q: Why does Mercutio conjure Romeo with a woman's body parts instead of with Rosaline's name?

Because the conjuring is an argument about what desire is, conducted in the only vocabulary Mercutio trusts. A name would concede that Romeo's longing has a person for its object and a language of devotion for its expression. The catalogue of parts, bright eyes, scarlet lip, fine foot, straight leg, quivering thigh, and "the demesnes that there adjacent lie" (2.1.17-20), dismantles that concession piece by piece, reducing the beloved to anatomy and the lover to a spirit to be raised in her circle and laid again. The spell is meant to work by embarrassment: if Romeo can hear his passion translated into organs and farmland, he will abandon the translation's rival, the sonnet. That the conjuring fails, because Romeo is no longer in love with Rosaline and is standing in an orchard having heard Juliet instead, does not soften its intent. Mercutio's bawdy is always aimed; here its target is the idea that desire could be anything besides the body, and its weapon is the inventory.

### Q: What does the hag in the Queen Mab speech teach maids, according to Mercutio?

She teaches them to bear. The hag is the nightmare figured as a sexual weight: "when maids lie on their backs, that presses them and learns them first to bear, making them women of good carriage" (1.4.92-94). In Mercutio's telling, Mab visits every rank with the dream that rank desires, lawyers with fees, soldiers with throats to cut, lovers with love, and maids with this, a pressure in the night that initiates them into the burden of adult sexuality before any husband arrives. The image is the darkest in the speech because it makes female desire begin as something suffered, a weight imposed in sleep, and because its joking delivery licenses the audience to laugh at an initiation imagined as assault. Editors in the expurgating tradition found the passage indefensible and cut or softened it, which is one way the speech acquired its reputation as a charming fairy digression. Restored, the hag is the speech's confession: Mercutio's cosmology of desire ends where the servants' boasts began, with a woman's body as the ground on which other forces practice.

### Q: What does the Nurse's memory of her own husband contribute to the play's account of desire?

It contributes the play's only testimony from inside a completed sexual life. Every other adult voice speaks of desire as appetite, doctrine, or transaction; the Nurse speaks of it as biography, a marriage remembered through a joke about a toddler's fall, a husband dead these many years, a daughter buried, a breast that fed another woman's child. Her husband's tease, that the fallen Juliet would fall backward when she had more wit (1.3.41-45), survives in her telling as tenderness rather than obscenity, because the marriage it came from is complete and its costs are known to her: desire led to childbed, childbed to loss, and loss to the fierce, funny custodianship she exercises over Juliet. The contribution is structural. When the Nurse instructs Juliet about the wedding night, ladders fetched and burdens borne (2.5.72-77), the instruction carries a credential no other speaker holds. She is not guessing, boasting, or selling. She is reporting, and the play lets her report stand as the measure by which the boasts and the sales talk are judged.

### Q: Who was Thomas Bowdler, and what did the Family Shakespeare do to this play?

Thomas Bowdler was the editor, working largely with his sister Henrietta, whose Family Shakspeare of 1807, enlarged in 1818, undertook to remove from Shakespeare everything that "cannot with propriety be read aloud in a family," so that the plays could enter households and schools without offense. For this play, the method of omission fell with surgical consistency on the sexual registers: the servants' maidenhead exchange, the anatomy of Mercutio's conjuring, the hag passage of Queen Mab, the Nurse's fall-backward anecdote and wedding-night teasing, all thinned, softened, or removed, while the balcony and the sonnet passed intact. The consequence outlasted the edition. Expurgation became the school tradition's default, generations met a Romeo and Juliet whose city had apparently no sexual language but the lovers' own, and the lovers' frankness, deprived of its contrasting floor, could be misread first as the play's impropriety and then, once it too was softened, as proof of a pure and bodiless romance. The chaste reputation this article tests is, in large part, Bowdler's bequest.

### Q: Why does the duel fall between the wedding and the wedding night?

Because the placement makes the consummation, and everything after it, happen under sentence. The wedding is Monday afternoon; the duel that kills Mercutio and Tybalt follows within hours; Romeo spends his wedding night in Juliet's chamber as a banished man who must flee at dawn. Had the duel fallen a day later, the lovers would have had a night of ordinary union and the play a different catastrophe. Had it fallen before the wedding, there would have been no marriage to consummate and no bigamy to fear from Paris. Shakespeare's timing locks sexuality to violence at the joint of the plot: desire is granted its single lawful hour only after the feud has already made that hour a farewell. The aubade's argument about larks and nightingales is therefore not decoration on the morning after. It is two people disputing the terms of a deadline the duel imposed, in a bed the feud has already condemned.

### Q: Why are the lovers given no bawdy of their own, even in their wedding-night scenes?

Because the bawdy is a public idiom, and the lovers' desire is defined by its refusal of the public. Every bawdy register in the play is a monologue performed for an audience of men, servants, or cronies, in which the woman spoken of is absent, silent, or asleep. Romeo and Juliet speak to each other, in shared forms whose rhymes they must cooperate to complete, and the idiom of parts and conquests has no grammar for that reciprocity. Their erotic language works instead through prayer, touch imagined and bargained for, and first-person wanting, because those are the vocabularies in which a woman can answer back. Shakespeare's restraint is also a matter of measurement: give Juliet one line in Mercutio's idiom and the counter-register collapses into the system it was built against; give Romeo one jest at her expense and the sonnet becomes a technique rather than a discovery. The wedding night itself is kept offstage by the period's decorum, but the speeches around it, the epithalamium before and the aubade after, show a desire more explicit than any bawdy in the play, conducted entirely in a language two people invented because Verona offered them no words fit to share.

### Q: From whom does Juliet learn the words for desire before she speaks to Romeo?

From the Nurse, and almost entirely from the Nurse. Her mother's contribution to her education is a single interview conducted in metaphors of appraisal, Paris as a book to be read and covered, in which the operative instruction is to look until liking follows. The Nurse's contribution is a lifetime of overheard and then direct instruction: the weaning anecdote that encodes a woman's biography in a joke about falling backward, the frank talk of burdens and ladders around the wedding, the street wisdom that women grow by men. Juliet grows up bilingual in consequence, fluent in the household's euphemism because she must be, and grounded in the body's plain facts because the woman who raised her never pretended otherwise. The genealogy matters when the epithalamium arrives. Juliet's frankness in Act 3 Scene 2 is often read as a miracle of characterization, a pure girl suddenly speaking desire. In the play's own economy of speech it is an inheritance: the Nurse's curriculum, completed by a pupil who adds to it the one thing the teacher lacked, a vocabulary of mutual vow.

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch sexuality catalogue, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch sexuality catalogue is this article's table of the play's sexual registers, sixteen entries running from the servants' predation to the tomb's silence, each entry fixed by its speaker, its signature passage, its theory of what sex is, and its answer to one question: whether the person spoken of may answer. The pattern the catalogue exposes is grammatical before it is moral. At the bottom of the scale, desire's object is a surface, spoil, anatomy, property, and cannot reply; in the middle, she replies in codes, evasions, and scripted assent; at the top, in the scenes the lovers construct, she answers in her own syntax and changes outcomes by answering. Read whole, the catalogue shows that the play grades its sexual speech by consent's presence in the sentence, and that Verona's tragedy is partly a failure of grammar: a city with sixteen ways to speak about a woman's body and, until two children improvise a seventeenth, no way for the woman to be a subject in the speaking.

### Q: How do editors decide whether a word carries a bawdy sense, and what is at stake in the decision?

Editors decide from evidence of period usage, gathered across the whole body of Elizabethan writing, the standard Eric Partridge set when his glossary of Shakespearean bawdy demonstrated that words like tool, stone, stand, and die carried sexual senses systematically rather than occasionally. A single suggestive context proves nothing; a sense attested in jest books, ballads, medical writing, and a dozen other plays is a fact of the language that an editor suppresses at the text's peril. What is at stake is the play's system of registers. Gloss only the innocent sense, as the expurgating tradition preferred, and the servants' menace dissolves into nonsense about carpentry, Mercutio's conjuring becomes inexplicable rudeness, and the Nurse's jokes lose their punchlines, leaving later readers to conclude that the passages are dull where they are in fact pointed. Gloss every possible sense everywhere, and the opposite falsification follows, a text sniggering at itself in scenes built for tenderness. The responsible edition, in the line of René Weis's Arden text, prints the whole play and marks the double sense where the surrounding words activate it, trusting the reader with the same evidence the first audience heard.

### Q: Why is Romeo the one young man in Verona who never makes a sexual joke?

Because his desire, once it finds an answering object, no longer needs the joke's protection. The sexual joke in Verona is a performance for other men, a way of handling longing in public without confessing it, and every young man around Romeo depends on it: Sampson boasts to Gregory, Mercutio conjures for Benvolio, even Benvolio prescribes desire as a cure for other men to admire. Romeo begins the play inside a borrowed version of the same evasion, the Petrarchan siege of Rosaline, all oxymoron and assault, aimed at a woman who never hears it. Meeting Juliet ends the evasion. She answers, corrects, and out-argues him in the sonnet, and from that scene forward his speech about desire is either devotional, contractual, or silent; he will pun with Mercutio about geese and shoes, but never about a woman's body. The pattern is the counter-register's signature. A man who can say what he wants to the woman he wants it from has no use for the idiom that lets Verona want in public while feeling nothing in particular.

### Q: What happens to the play's sexual voices after Mercutio dies?

They go quiet in ascending order, and the play's remaining sexual speech belongs to its worst registers. Mercutio's death in Act 3 Scene 1 removes the bawdy's most brilliant practitioner mid-sentence, his last utterance a pun about a grave. The Nurse, the register of experience, defects within the same act, counseling bigamy in Act 3 Scene 5 and shrinking thereafter to errands and lamentation. Juliet's frank voice survives longest but is driven underground, into the coded double meanings with which she answers her mother, the feigned obedience of Act 4, and a potion soliloquy spoken to an empty room. What remains in public is the household register at its rawest: Capulet disposing of his daughter in the language of baggage and grazing, Paris claiming a face as his property, Lady Capulet commissioning a poisoning. The design is exact. Verona kills its sexual honesty from the top of the catalogue downward, wit first, experience second, the bride's own voice last of all, and the tomb, where the final sexual metaphors of paramour and sheath are spoken over bodies, is what a city sounds like when the only registers it has left are possession and force.

### Q: How does the play's sexual language keep borrowing the language of fighting?

Because in Verona the two appetites are one appetite wearing two costumes, and the vocabulary knows it before the characters do. The borrowing begins in the first minute, when cutting off heads slides into cutting off maidenheads and a drawn sword becomes a drawn tool, and it never stops: Romeo besieges Rosaline with loving terms and assailing eyes, Tybalt's honor is a sensitivity that answers insult with penetration, the Friar warns that violent delights kiss and consume like fire and powder, and the tomb's presiding fantasy is of Death as a rival male who takes and keeps women by force. The shared verbs, thrust, stand, prick, die, are not decoration on two separate subjects. They are evidence that the city's imagination has a single grammar for wanting anything: seize it, against resistance, at once. The lovers' counter-register is legible precisely as an escape from that grammar, a language of exchange and answer in which nothing is taken. When the escape fails, the old grammar repossesses even their deaths, and the play's last sexual images are a paramour who keeps a corpse and a dagger finding its sheath.

### Q: Does the ending punish desire itself, or a city that left desire no lawful room?

It punishes the city, and the distribution of the corpses is the evidence. Desire in the play is not indiscriminate: the desire that is mutual, marital, and honestly spoken is the only force in Verona attempting to build anything, and the deaths arrive not as its wages but as the sum of the obstacles the city's order placed around it, a feud that forbade the match, a market that sold the bride twice, a haste that left the lawful consummation one night before exile, and a chain of messages entrusted to a system that failed them. The Prince's closing audit spreads the punishment accordingly, fathers, households, his own winking authority, all are punished, while the lovers receive statues. Readings that treat the ending as desire's sentence inherit the moral of Shakespeare's source poem, whose preface condemned the lovers' lust outright; the play, which gives its lovers a sacrament, a friar's blessing, and a language of mutual vow, has quietly reversed the source's verdict. What dies at the tomb is not wanting but the brief experiment in wanting lawfully, and Verona is left to its old registers, which is the punishment.

### Q: What does Paris mean by telling Juliet her face is his, and which register does the claim belong to?

He means it as a statement of property law. "Thy face is mine, and thou hast slandered it" (4.1.35) informs Juliet that her face, her body's most public surface, passed to him under her father's bargain, so that her tears for Tybalt are damage done to his goods and a slander against his own appearance. The claim belongs to the household register, the dialect of appraisal and possession in which Lady Capulet first presented him as a book for Juliet to read. Its horror is its courtesy. Paris is not a villain, not a boaster, and not, by his lights, unkind; he is a purchaser inspecting his acquisition in the seller's absence and finding it tear-stained. The line earns its place in the catalogue because it shows what the market's language does when it is spoken by a decent man in good faith: it abolishes the person it describes more completely than the servants' threats ever managed, because the servants only imagined women as spoil, while Paris, contract in hand, addresses one as an entry in his accounts.

### Q: Why do so many of the play's sexual meanings exist only when the lines are heard aloud?

Because the double sense is a timed event, and print cannot hold its timing. On the page a word like tool, stone, or prick sits still and lets a reader choose its meaning at leisure, which usually means choosing the innocent one and moving on. In the theatre the word arrives at speaking speed inside a rhythm of other words, and the second sense detonates a half-second behind the first, in a laugh that spreads through the audience faster than any individual decision to laugh. The Elizabethan public theatre was built for that detonation: a mixed crowd, standing close, expert in the idiom, hearing the play in daylight with the actor's emphasis steering the sense. Editors who gloss only the innocent meaning are therefore not merely being delicate; they are describing a different object, the read play, and silently demoting the performed one. The catalogue's evidence is weighted accordingly, toward passages whose bawdy is structural in delivery, boasts timed as challenges, conjurings timed as spells, teasing timed as foreplay to a wedding night the audience has been invited to anticipate.

### Q: How does the word maidenhead change its meaning as it passes from the servants to Juliet?

The anatomy stays constant and the ownership reverses, which is the whole story of the word. In Sampson's mouth the maidenhead is a thing other people have and he can take, spoil of war priced between a joke and a threat. In the household's mouth it is inventory, the sealed condition of the goods Capulet is negotiating to Paris, never named because the market prefers its euphemisms. In the Friar's cell it becomes a state lawfully transferred by sacrament, off the market's books. In Juliet's own mouth, at last, it is hers: awaited in the epithalamium as the destination of a night she has summoned, mourned in the same scene as an inheritance death might rob, and never once spoken by her as shame. The word's itinerary is the catalogue compressed into a single term, spoil, stock, sacrament, selfhood, and it demonstrates the article's method better than any definition. A sexual vocabulary is a system of permissions about who may speak of a body and in what grammar, and this play can be read, scene by scene, as the record of one word changing hands until it reaches the person it names.

### Q: What does the play gain by giving its sexual knowledge to a nurse, a friar, and a wit rather than to the lovers' parents?

It gains an epistemology: the knowledge lives where the experience is, not where the authority is. The parents, who hold every legal power over the marriage, are the play's most ignorant speakers about desire, appraising faces and striking bargains for a transaction whose human content they cannot discuss. The Nurse knows the marriage bed from inside a life; the Friar knows desire as a system of nature, its humors, doses, and seasons; Mercutio knows it as the street's cynical wisdom, appetite unmasked. Between them the three licensed voices triangulate the subject completely, experience, doctrine, and mockery, while the household that owns the bride contributes only pricing. The gain for the tragedy is that Juliet's education and Romeo's counsel both arrive from this unofficial faculty, which is why both lovers are wiser than their parents and powerless against them: knowledge in Verona is real but carries no vote. When the unofficial faculty fails, the Nurse defecting, the Friar's physic miscarrying, Mercutio dead, the lovers are left with the play's most complete understanding of their situation and no institution through which to act on it, which is a serviceable definition of the catastrophe.

### Q: How can an essay argue that the body drives the play without reducing the lovers to appetite?

By keeping the registers distinct, because the reduction happens only when the play's sexual speech is treated as one thing. The strong essay grants the bawdy its full weight first: the servants' predation, Mercutio's doctrine of appetite, the Nurse's curriculum, the market's pricing, the consummation's clock, showing at each point that the body is not imagery but machinery, setting the plot's timing, the feud's language, and the heroine's education. It then turns to the counter-register and shows the lovers building, inside that machinery, a use of desire the machinery cannot process: mutual, verbal, sacramental, and owned by the woman as plainly as by the man. The conclusion writes itself from the contrast. Appetite is Verona's theory of the body; the lovers' tragedy is that they disprove the theory in practice and are destroyed by a city that has no other. An essay that flattens the registers into a general celebration or condemnation of lust will sound like the source poem's preface. One that maps them can show what the play actually argues: that the body drives everything in Verona, and that the single attempt to drive it by consent rather than seizure is the most radical act in the tragedy.
