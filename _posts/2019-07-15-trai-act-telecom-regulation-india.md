Every argument about Indian telecom regulation is, at bottom, an argument about a single line drawn through Section 11 of the TRAI Act. On one side of the line, the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India can fix the terms on which rival networks connect, set the standards of quality they must meet, notify the rates they may charge, and prosecute them if they disobey. On the other side of the line, the same Authority can only advise. It cannot decide who may enter the market, on what terms a licence is granted, whether a licence is revoked, or how the spectrum on which the entire industry runs is to be managed. Those decisions belong to the Central Government, and the Authority's word on them, however expert and however publicly reasoned, is a recommendation the Government may lawfully set aside. The TRAI Act, 1997 is therefore best understood not as the charter of a powerful regulator or a toothless one, but as a deliberate division of authority by subject, and most disputes about the regulator's influence dissolve once a reader can say which side of the line a given question falls on.

![TRAI Act 1997 and telecom regulation in India, showing the division between the Authority's binding powers and its advisory role - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-71.webp)

That division was not the original design. As enacted in 1997, the Authority combined regulation with adjudication: it was to settle disputes as well as supervise the sector. Four years later, after open warfare between the Authority and the Department of Telecommunications and a ruling of the Delhi High Court confining its powers, Parliament amended the Act, removed adjudication to a new tribunal, the Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal, and rewrote the functions clause so that the boundary between recommending and deciding was spelled out in the statute itself. The institution a reader meets in the present text of the Act is the product of that settlement of 2000, adjusted again by the Telecommunications Act, 2023, which changed who may be appointed to the Authority and lengthened the procedure by which the Government disagrees with it. This article profiles the institution as the statute designs it: what it was built to do, how its members are chosen and removed, which of its powers bind and which merely persuade, how the tribunal and the courts police its boundaries, and why its most consequential weakness is structural rather than personal. The wider field of Indian technology law within which this institution sits is mapped in [the complete guide to technology law in India](/2019/03/01/technology-law-india-complete-guide/), and the new telecommunications statute that re-founded the licensing system is treated in its own article, linked below where the 2023 changes are discussed.

## Why a regulator had to be invented

The problem the Act was built to solve was a conflict of roles inside a single government department. Telecommunications in India was, for most of the twentieth century, a state monopoly run by the Department of Telecommunications. The Department made policy for the sector, it granted the permissions without which no one else could operate in the sector, and it was itself the largest operator in the sector, supplying services through its own network and through the state-owned Mahanagar Telephone Nigam Limited in Delhi and Mumbai. A private company entering the market under the liberalisation of the 1990s therefore competed against its own licensor, appealed for interconnection to its own competitor, and depended for the terms of its licence on the very organisation whose revenues its success would reduce. No arrangement of goodwill could cure that structure; only a separation of functions could.

The National Telecom Policy of 1994, which opened the sector to private participation in earnest, recognised the point and promised an independent regulator. Private capital, domestic and foreign, was being asked to commit sums that could be recovered only over decades, and it would not commit them on the strength of assurances from a department that was also a market participant. A statutory regulator, insulated in some measure from the licensor and obliged to act transparently, was the institutional form that promise took. The Authority also answered a consumer problem: a monopoly department answerable to no external standard-setter had little incentive to publish quality benchmarks, and a competitive market with several operators needed a neutral hand to fix the terms on which their networks interconnected, because a dominant network could otherwise strangle entrants simply by refusing or delaying connection.

### Why was a separate telecom regulator created in India?

Because the Department of Telecommunications simultaneously made policy, granted licences and operated the largest network, so private operators competed against their own licensor and depended on a rival for interconnection. The National Telecom Policy of 1994 promised an independent regulator, and the TRAI Act created one to separate regulation from operation and licensing.

The instrument arrived in two steps. An ordinance first established the Authority in January 1997, and the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India Act, 1997 (Act 24 of 1997) received the President's assent on 28 March 1997, deemed to have come into force on 25 January 1997, the date of the ordinance. The long title states the design plainly: to establish the Authority to regulate telecommunication services, to protect the interests of service providers and consumers, and to promote and ensure the orderly growth of the telecom sector. Each element of that formula mattered. Regulation of services, not ownership of networks. Protection of providers as well as consumers, because a regulator captured by consumer rhetoric could destroy the investment on which service depended. Orderly growth, because the sector was expected to expand at a pace that would punish improvisation. The statute that followed from this preamble was, however, ambiguous on the question that would dominate its first three years: how much of the governing of the sector had actually been handed over.

## The body the TRAI Act built: composition, appointment and tenure

Section 3 establishes the Authority as a body corporate with perpetual succession and a common seal, headquartered at New Delhi. Its composition is deliberately small. Under Section 3(3), as substituted by the amendment of 2000, the Authority consists of a Chairperson and not more than two whole-time members and not more than two part-time members, all appointed by the Central Government. The original body of 1997 was larger, a Chairperson with up to six members, and the reduction in 2000 was part of a general reconstruction discussed below. A body of at most five persons, deciding by the processes the Act and its regulations prescribe, is closer in scale to a commission than to a department, and its smallness has consequences: each appointment carries unusual weight, vacancies bite quickly, and the Act accordingly requires in Section 5(9) that a vacancy in the office of Chairperson or a member be filled within three months of its occurrence.

Section 4 prescribes the qualifications. The Chairperson and members are to be appointed from among persons who have special knowledge of, and professional experience in, telecommunication, industry, finance, accountancy, law, management or consumer affairs. That formula is a field-of-expertise test, not a service test, and on its face it opens the Authority to the professions and to industry as much as to the civil services. A proviso, substituted in 2000, superimposes a seniority condition on candidates from government: a person who is or has been in the service of Government may not be appointed unless that person has held the post of Secretary or Additional Secretary, or the posts of Additional Secretary and Secretary, to the Government of India or an equivalent post in the Central or a State Government, for a period of not less than three years. The combined effect of the section and its proviso was to make the senior civil services the natural pool without formally excluding anyone else. The record reflects that pull. The first Chairperson, Justice S. S. Sodhi, was a retired Chief Justice of the Allahabad High Court; his successor in 2000, M. S. Verma, came from the chairmanship of the State Bank of India; the chairpersons who followed, Pradip Baijal, Nripendra Misra, J. S. Sarma, Rahul Khullar, Ram Sewak Sharma and P. D. Vaghela, were drawn from the senior ranks of the Indian Administrative Service, and Anil Kumar Lahoti, appointed in 2024, came from the chairmanship of the Railway Board. The 2023 changes to Section 4, which formalised a route for candidates from outside government with long board-level experience, are examined in their own section below.

Tenure is short, and its shortness is a considered feature rather than an accident. Section 5(2) provides that the Chairperson and members hold office for a term not exceeding three years, as the Central Government may notify, from the date they enter upon office or until they attain the age of sixty-five years, whichever is earlier. The original Act of 1997 allowed five years; the amendment of 2000 cut the term to three, at the same moment as it terminated the tenure of the incumbents. A three-year term is brief by the standards of Indian regulatory design, and its brevity interacts with the appointment power: a government appointing a regulator for three years appoints, in practice, for the period of its own choosing, and a chairperson hoping for the extensions that practice sometimes grants serves the whole term within sight of the appointing authority. Section 5 softens the dependence in two ways. Conditions of service cannot be varied to a member's disadvantage after appointment, and Section 5(8) imposes a two-year cooling-off period after office: the Chairperson and whole-time members may not, for two years from ceasing to hold office and except with the previous approval of the Central Government, accept employment under the Central or a State Government or any appointment in a company in the business of telecommunication services. The cooling-off clause is aimed at the revolving door in both directions, toward the licensor and toward the regulated.

Removal is confined by Section 7 to stated grounds: a member may be removed by the Central Government if the member has been adjudged an insolvent, has been convicted of an offence which in the Government's opinion involves moral turpitude, has become physically or mentally incapable of acting, has acquired a financial or other interest likely to affect prejudicially the member's functions, or has so abused the member's position as to render continuance in office prejudicial to the public interest. For the two judgment-laden grounds, financial interest and abuse of position, Section 7(2) requires that the member first be given a reasonable opportunity of being heard. There is no removal at pleasure. But there is also no parliamentary or judicial screen of the kind that protects a judge, and the episode of 2000 demonstrated a route around the section altogether: when Parliament rewrote the Authority's composition and tenure, the incumbents' terms ended by force of the amending statute itself, with compensation of up to three months' pay and allowances under Section 5(3), and no question of Section 7 arose. Tenure security under the Act is therefore real against executive displeasure and unreal against legislative reconstruction, a distinction that has shaped the institution's behaviour ever since.

Money completes the picture of dependence and insulation. The Authority is financed by grants made by the Central Government after parliamentary appropriation under Section 21, together with the fees and charges it levies, which are credited to the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India Fund constituted by Section 22. Its accounts are audited by the Comptroller and Auditor-General under Section 23. A regulator funded by annual grants from the department it must sometimes oppose is not thereby compromised in any particular decision, but the design choice is consistent with everything else in the Act: the Authority is an expert body operating inside a frame the Government continues to own, not a fourth branch set over the sector.

Beneath the five office holders sits the institution's permanent machinery, a Secretariat headed by a Secretary to the Authority and staffed by advisers and officers responsible for the subject divisions, economic, technological, legal and consumer facing, through which consultation papers are drafted, comments processed, surveys commissioned and orders prepared. A large part of that staff has historically arrived on deputation from the Indian Telecommunication Service and other government services, a staffing pattern that cuts both ways in the assessment of the institution. Deputation supplies the Authority with engineers and administrators who know the networks and the licence conditions from inside, at no training cost to a small organisation. It also means that the regulator's working levels are substantially populated by officers whose parent cadres belong to the department the regulator must confront, and whose careers after deputation return them to it. The criticism that the Authority can think like the Department at the level of its files, whatever its leadership thinks, is a criticism of this staffing economy rather than of any individual officer. The Act's accountability provisions point the same way: under Section 24 the Authority furnishes returns and information to the Central Government, its annual reporting travels through the Government to Parliament, and its revenues, as the funding position above shows, arrive as grants in aid. None of this makes the Authority a department; its orders bind private parties and its reasoning is published in a way no department's internal noting is. But the machinery explains why the independence the statute confers has always had to be exercised from inside a structure of governmental supply lines, and why occupants of the office who wished to assert that independence have had to assert it consciously rather than inherit it from the building.

## The division of Section 11: what the Authority may only recommend

Section 11 is the heart of the Act, and it was substituted wholesale by the amendment of 2000, which is why the boundary it draws is so carefully drafted. Section 11(1) opens with a non-obstante clause overriding the Indian Telegraph Act, 1885, and then divides the Authority's functions into clauses whose legal consequences differ completely. Clause (a) lists the matters on which the Authority makes recommendations, either of its own motion or on a request from the licensor. Clause (b) lists the functions the Authority discharges, in the statute's own mandatory register. Clause (c) empowers it to levy fees and other charges as determined by regulations. Clause (d) is a residuary charge to perform such other functions, including administrative and financial functions, as the Central Government entrusts to it or as are necessary to carry out the provisions of the Act. The division between clause (a) and clause (b) is the division between advice and power, and every subject in the sector falls on one side or the other.

The recommendatory list in clause (a) repays slow reading, because it contains the questions that decide the structure of the industry. The Authority recommends on the need and timing for the introduction of a new service provider; on the terms and conditions of licence to a service provider; on the revocation of a licence for non-compliance with its terms and conditions; on measures to facilitate competition and promote efficiency in the operation of telecommunication services so as to facilitate growth in such services; on technological improvements in the services provided; on the type of equipment to be used after inspection of the equipment used in the network; on measures for the development of telecommunication technology and any other matter relatable to the telecommunication industry in general; and on the efficient management of the available spectrum. Entry, licence terms, exit, competitive structure and spectrum: the five levers by which the shape of the market is determined are all on the advisory side of the line.

### Why did licensing stay with the government when a regulator was created?

Because the licence is the instrument of the Central Government's exclusive privilege over telecommunications, and the Act of 1997 created an adviser and a conduct regulator, not a second licensor. Entry, licence terms, revocation and spectrum remained executive decisions, taken by the Government on the Authority's recommendation but never transferred to it.

The first proviso to Section 11(1) then states the legal character of the clause (a) list in terms that admit no argument: the recommendations of the Authority specified in clause (a) shall not be binding upon the Central Government. That single sentence is the answer to the most frequently asked question about Indian telecom regulation, and it explains why controversies that appear to be about the Authority's timidity or boldness are usually about the Government's choices. But the provisos that follow build a procedural structure around the non-binding core, and the structure is not decoration. The second proviso obliges the Central Government to seek the recommendations of the Authority on the two most consequential matters, the need and timing of new licences and the terms and conditions of licence, in respect of any new licence to be issued to a service provider. Consultation on those two matters is not a courtesy; it is a statutory precondition. The Authority, for its part, must forward its recommendations within sixty days of the Government seeking them, and the third proviso entitles it, before recommending, to require the Government to furnish information or documents, which the Government must supply within seven days. The fourth proviso protects the licensing process against the Authority's silence: if no recommendation arrives within the period, or within such further period as the two sides mutually agree, the Government may issue the licence anyway.

The fifth proviso is the reference-back procedure, and it is the mechanism by which disagreement between the licensor and the regulator is disciplined. If the Central Government, having considered a recommendation, comes to a prima facie conclusion that the recommendation cannot be accepted or needs modification, it must refer the recommendation back to the Authority for reconsideration. The Authority may then, within fifteen days of receiving the reference, forward its recommendation again after considering the Government's reference; the period was fifteen days as the amendment of 2000 left it, and the Telecommunications Act, 2023 lengthened it to thirty days and recast the Authority's step as an obligation to communicate any further recommendations it may have. Only after that exchange does the statute say, in words of studied finality, that the Central Government shall take a final decision. The procedure thus guarantees the Authority a second word but never the last one. It also creates a documentary trail: a government that departs from a recommendation must first say why, at least to the regulator, and must receive and consider the regulator's answer. Much of the Authority's real influence has been exercised through this trail, because a recommendation published after consultation, defended in a reasoned document and reaffirmed on a reference back, raises the political cost of departure even though it cannot raise a legal barrier to it.

Two further features of Section 11 frame everything the Authority does. Section 11(3) forbids the Authority, in discharging its functions, to act against the interest of the sovereignty and integrity of India, the security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality. Section 11(4) imposes the discipline that has done more than any other to shape the institution's working method: the Authority shall ensure transparency while exercising its powers and discharging its functions. Transparency is not an aspiration in this statute; it is a statutory duty, and the consultation-paper method examined later in this article is its operational form. And behind Section 11 stands Section 25, the channel through which the Government's formal supremacy runs. Under Section 25(2), the Authority is bound, in the exercise of its powers and the performance of its functions, by such directions on questions of policy as the Central Government may give it in writing, with the safeguard that the Authority be given, as far as practicable, an opportunity to express its views before a direction is given. Section 25(3) makes the Government's decision on whether a question is one of policy final. The Authority's binding powers over service providers are therefore held subject to a written policy override in the hands of the licensor, a fact any assessment of its independence must hold in the same frame as the appointment and funding arrangements described earlier.

## Recommend or decide: the Section 11 map

The table that follows is the reference object for every dispute about the Authority's role. It takes the subjects that recur in telecom controversy and records, for each, which side of the Section 11 line it falls on, the provision that puts it there, and who decides when the Authority does not.

| Subject | Recommendatory or binding | Provision | Who decides if the Authority does not |
|---|---|---|---|
| Introduction of a new service provider | Recommendatory | Section 11(1)(a)(i) | The Central Government as licensor, which must first seek the recommendation |
| Terms and conditions of a licence | Recommendatory | Section 11(1)(a)(ii) | The Central Government, subject to the same duty to consult and the reference-back procedure |
| Revocation of a licence for non-compliance | Recommendatory | Section 11(1)(a)(iii) | The Central Government as licensor |
| Efficient management of available spectrum | Recommendatory | Section 11(1)(a)(viii) | The Central Government, which assigns spectrum and conducts auctions |
| Interconnection between service providers | Binding | Section 11(1)(b)(ii) and (iii) | The Authority fixes the terms; disputes go to the Tribunal |
| Revenue sharing among service providers | Binding | Section 11(1)(b)(iv) | The Authority regulates the arrangement |
| Quality of service standards | Binding | Section 11(1)(b)(v) | The Authority lays down the standards and surveys performance |
| Tariffs for telecommunication services | Binding | Section 11(2) | The Authority notifies the rates by order |
| Consumer protection within services | Binding, exercised through regulations | Sections 11(1)(b)(i) and (v), 36 | The Authority regulates; individual complaints lie to operators and consumer forums |

Reading the rows together states the namable claim of this institution profile in its exact form. The Authority is a regulator on the questions that follow and an adviser on the questions that decide: it binds service providers on interconnection, quality and tariffs, and merely advises the Government on who may enter the market, on what terms, and on how the spectrum is managed. The decisions that shape the structure of the industry are therefore taken by the licensor, and the decisions the regulator controls operate inside a structure it did not choose. Readers preparing for the civil services examination, for whom this division is a standing topic, can convert the table into a revision deck using [the ReportMedic presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), which is the natural way to rehearse the subject row by row.

## The powers that bind: compliance, interconnection, quality and the register

Clause (b) of Section 11(1) lists the functions the Authority discharges rather than recommends, and its register is imperative throughout. The Authority must ensure compliance with the terms and conditions of licence; fix the terms and conditions of interconnectivity between service providers, a power whose drafting history matters and which the next section takes up; ensure technical compatibility and effective interconnection between different service providers; regulate the arrangement among service providers for sharing their revenue; lay down the standards of quality of service to be provided, ensure the quality of service and conduct periodical surveys of the service so provided, expressly in order to protect the interests of consumers; lay down and ensure the time period for providing local and long distance circuits between different service providers; and maintain a register of interconnect agreements and of such other matters as the regulations provide, keeping the register open to inspection by any member of the public on payment of the prescribed fee. To these the amendment history added one more, inserted as clause (b)(ix): to ensure effective compliance of universal service obligations, the commitments, funded through the sector's universal service arrangements, to extend service beyond what the market alone would reach.

The instruments by which these binding functions are exercised are three. The first is the regulation. Section 36 empowers the Authority to make regulations, by notification, consistent with the Act and with the rules made under it, to carry out the purposes of the Act, and Section 36(2) illustrates the subjects without confining them. Regulations are subordinate legislation: they bind everyone within their terms, they are laid before Parliament under Section 37, and the Supreme Court has read the power in Section 36 as wide and pervasive rather than as a sum of the section's illustrations, a point developed in the discussion of the case law below. The second instrument is the direction. Section 13 empowers the Authority, for the discharge of its functions under Section 11(1), to issue such directions to service providers as it considers necessary, but its proviso confines directions to the matters specified in clause (b): a direction is an enforcement instrument for the binding list, not a free-standing power to command. Section 12(4) adds a connected power to issue directions for the proper functioning of service providers, and Section 12 equips the Authority with the investigative apparatus of a regulator, the power by written order to call on any service provider to furnish information or explanation about its affairs, to appoint persons to inquire into a provider's affairs, and to direct its officers to inspect books of account and documents. The third instrument is the tariff order made under Section 11(2), examined in its own section next.

### What separates a TRAI regulation from a TRAI direction and a TRAI recommendation?

A regulation is subordinate legislation made under Section 36 after consultation, binding generally and laid before Parliament. A direction under Section 13 is an enforcement command to a service provider, confined to the binding functions in Section 11(1)(b). A recommendation under Section 11(1)(a) binds nobody and is addressed to the Government.

The enforcement tail of the binding powers is deliberately prosecutorial rather than penal, and the distinction is one of the design's most consequential features. The Authority cannot itself fine a service provider for disobeying it. Section 29 makes violation of the Authority's directions an offence punishable with a fine extending to one lakh rupees, rising to two lakh rupees for a second or subsequent offence, with an additional fine extending to two lakh rupees for every day a continuing contravention persists. But a fine under Section 29 can be imposed only by a court, and Section 34 confines cognizance of offences under the Act to a court not below the rank of a Chief Metropolitan Magistrate or a Chief Judicial Magistrate of the first class, acting on a complaint by the Authority. The Authority's role on non-compliance is therefore that of a complainant: it investigates, it directs, it records a prima facie view, and if the provider still does not comply, it prosecutes. The Supreme Court, in the Polimer litigation discussed below, restated this boundary in terms that leave no room for doubt: the Authority may record, for the limited purpose of enforcement, a prima facie finding of non-compliance, but it cannot adjudge guilt, levy or recover the fine, award damages or quantify arrears between parties. Those acts belong, respectively, to the criminal court and to the Tribunal. Licence conditions supply an indirect second lever, because compliance with the Authority's tariff orders and regulations is woven into the conditions on which licences are held, so a provider defying the regulator also places its licence relationship with the Government in issue; but that lever, too, is pulled by the licensor, not by the Authority.

### What can the Authority do when a service provider disobeys its direction?

It can investigate under Section 12, direct compliance under Section 13, and then prosecute: violation of a direction is an offence under Section 29, punishable with fines a court imposes on the Authority's complaint under Section 34. The Authority itself cannot levy the fine or award damages.

The result is a regulator whose binding powers are genuine but whose sanctions are borrowed. Against a provider that treats prosecution as a cost of business, the Authority's practical strength lies less in Section 29's modest fines than in the publicity of its findings, the licence consequences that the Government may attach to defiance, and the Tribunal's power, on a dispute being brought before it, to decide the underlying controversy with the force of a decree. That distribution of sanctions was a choice, made first in 1997 and renewed in 2000, and it should be kept in mind whenever the Authority is criticised for failing to punish: punishment, under this statute, is something it must ask other institutions to inflict.

## Tariffs: the power to notify rates and the practice of forbearance

Section 11(2) contains the Authority's most visible power, and it is drafted as a non-obstante clause overriding the Telegraph Act: the Authority may, from time to time, by order notified in the Official Gazette, fix the rates at which telecommunication services within India and outside India shall be provided, including the rates at which messages are transmitted to countries outside India, with liberty to notify different rates for different persons or classes of persons for similar services so long as it records its reasons. The power on its face is a price-fixing power of the classic public-utility kind. The institution's history is the story of how that power came to be exercised, for most services and most of the time, by not fixing prices at all.

The first great exercise of the power was the Telecommunication Tariff Order of 1999, which restructured a tariff regime inherited from the monopoly era, rebalanced charges between rentals, local calls and long distance, and set ceilings intended to move prices toward cost as competition developed. Thereafter the Authority settled into the posture that has defined it: forbearance. For the great majority of services, the Authority has declined to fix a tariff and has instead allowed service providers to set their own prices in the market, reserving its intervention for defined failures. Forbearance is not abdication of the Section 11(2) power but a standing exercise of it, and it is hedged with reporting discipline: under the tariff order framework, every tariff a provider offers must be reported to the Authority within seven working days of its implementation, must comply with the standing requirements of non-discrimination and non-predation, and remains liable to intervention if the Authority finds it inconsistent with the order. The consumer-facing consequence is the structure Indian subscribers take for granted, in which prices are set commercially and fall through competition, while the regulator audits the market's outputs rather than approving its inputs.

Two episodes show the tariff power at its outer edges. The first is the Prohibition of Discriminatory Tariffs for Data Services Regulations, 2016, issued on 8 February 2016 after a consultation on differential pricing that drew an exceptional public response. The regulations prohibit a service provider from offering or charging discriminatory tariffs for data services on the basis of content, and from entering into any arrangement whose effect is to produce such discrimination, with a financial disincentive of fifty thousand rupees for each day of contravention, capped at fifty lakh rupees. Their immediate consequence was the end of zero-rated offers of the kind then being promoted, under which access to selected sites was free while the rest of the internet was metered. The decision was taken under the Authority's tariff powers and is the foundation of net neutrality in India: a rule about prices accomplished what a general principle, unaided, could not. The second episode cuts the other way. By the Telecommunication Tariff (Sixty Third Amendment) Order, 2018, the Authority rewrote the tests for predatory pricing, tying the prohibition to operators holding a position of significant market power, defined by a thirty per cent share threshold, and requiring the reporting of tariffs in terms the incumbent operators challenged as opaque. On 13 December 2018 the Tribunal set aside the amendment insofar as it changed the concepts of significant market power and non-predation, holding that the yardsticks had to be objective and known to all service providers, that the Authority could not impose a penalty through that route, and describing the exemption of operators below the threshold from the non-predation requirement as an extreme step and an unnecessary abdication of the Authority's own regulatory powers. The Supreme Court, in November 2020, declined to disturb the Tribunal's order except as to the remand, and directed disclosure of segmented offers so that the Authority could examine them. Parliament's later contribution was to write a targeted power into the statute itself: the Telecommunications Act, 2023 inserted into Section 11(2) a further proviso empowering the Authority to direct an authorised entity or a class of authorised entities to abstain from predatory pricing that is harmful to competition, the long term development and the overall health of the sector. The sequence is a miniature of the institution's constitutional position: the Authority acts, the Tribunal reviews and sometimes rebukes, and the legislature, when it wishes the power exercised in a particular way, says so in the Act.

Forbearance has a consumer facing complement. Alongside the Tariff Order, the Telecom Consumers Protection Regulations, 2012 prescribed the transparency framework within which forborne prices are offered: the formats in which tariff plans must be published and made available to subscribers, the taxonomy of plans and vouchers, including the special tariff vouchers through which benefits other than talk value are sold, and protections against the activation of paid value added services without the subscriber's informed consent, reinforced by the complaint and refund machinery for unauthorised activations. The design assumes that a price the regulator does not fix can still be disciplined if it must be legible. A subscriber who can compare plans on standard disclosures, and a competitor who can read a rival's filings, together supply a part of the pressure that a notified tariff would otherwise have to supply alone. The assumption is tested constantly by the density of the market's plan architecture, in which combinations of validity, data allowances and bundled services multiply faster than any disclosure format stabilises, which is why the transparency instruments are amended nearly as often as the pricing instruments themselves, and why the boundary between a complex plan and a confusing one is a standing subject of the Authority's consumer protection work.

## Quality of service: standards measured in public

Quality of service is the binding function the subscriber can feel. Section 11(1)(b)(v) commits two linked powers to the Authority: laying down the standards of quality of service to be provided by service providers, and conducting periodical surveys of the quality actually provided, so that subscribers are ensured quality service. The standards are set in regulations, principally the Standards of Quality of Service regulations consolidated in 2009 and amended since, which prescribe measurable parameters for each service. For mobile networks the parameters address accessibility, retainability and voice quality, and include the benchmark that the proportion of calls dropped within a service area should not exceed two per cent; for broadband they address activation time, speed, latency and fault repair; for basic services, provisioning, fault incidence and rectification. Each parameter carries a benchmark, providers report their performance against the benchmarks quarter by quarter, and the reports are published, so that the quality of a network is not a matter of advertising claim but of returns filed with the regulator and open to comparison across operators and circles.

Measurement alone does not discipline, and the framework adds two further instruments. The first is financial: the quality regulations prescribe financial disincentives payable where a provider's reported performance fails a benchmark, scaled by the parameter and the service area, so that poor quality acquires a price even before any subscriber complains. The second is independent measurement. The Authority commissions drive tests through agencies on city roads and transport routes, measuring call drops, voice quality and data speeds on instruments independent of the providers' own counters, and publishes the results city by city; and it crowdsources experience through its own measurement application, which records the data speeds subscribers actually achieve. The three pictures, the provider's return, the drive test and the crowd, do not always agree, and the disagreements are themselves regulatory information, pointing to where reported quality and lived quality diverge, and to where the Authority's audit of the returns, conducted through agencies of its own appointment, should look harder.

The quality function also marks the boundary of what conduct regulation can achieve. A standard cannot create capacity: where a network is congested because spectrum is scarce, sites are unavailable or backhaul is thin, no benchmark will lift performance to it, and the Authority's quality reports have repeatedly recorded failures that are symptoms of the structure questions, of how much spectrum providers hold and on what terms they hold it, which lie on the advisory side of the line. The call drop crisis of 2015 and 2016, in which quality failures became a national political question, ran precisely along this seam: the Authority tightened its measurement and prescribed compensation by regulation, the compensation scheme fell in the Supreme Court, and the durable residue was a tougher benchmark and reporting regime rather than any transfer of the structural powers that governed the underlying shortage.

## Interconnection and revenue sharing: the plumbing the regulator controls

If tariffs are the Authority's most visible power, interconnection is its most fundamental. A telephone network that cannot terminate calls on rival networks is not a service but an island, and in a market where one network begins with every subscriber, the terms of connection are the terms of competition itself. Section 11(1)(b)(ii) and (iii) place those terms in the Authority's hands: it fixes the terms and conditions of interconnectivity between service providers and ensures technical compatibility and effective interconnection between them, and clause (b)(iv) adds the regulation of the arrangements by which providers share the revenue their interconnected services generate. The drafting history of clause (b)(ii) contains a deliberate override: the power is expressed to operate notwithstanding anything contained in the terms and conditions of licences granted before the commencement of the amendment of 2000. That clause exists because of a specific defeat. In September 1999 the Authority, acting under the unamended Act, prescribed revenue sharing under a calling-party-pays regime for calls between fixed and mobile networks. The incumbent operators challenged it, and in Mahanagar Telephone Nigam Limited v. Telecom Regulatory Authority of India, reported at AIR 2000 Delhi 208, the Delhi High Court held that the Authority had no power to change or vary the rights of parties under their contracts or licences. The amendment of 2000 answered the judgment directly, conferring the interconnection power in overriding terms for pre-existing licences, while the High Court's reading continued to mark the boundary for newer arrangements, a tension the Tribunal worked through in the Reference Interconnect Offer litigation of the following years.

The working instruments are the interconnection regulations and the charges regime built under them. The Telecommunication Interconnection (Reference Interconnect Offer) Regulation, 2002 required providers with significant reach to publish a reference offer on which interconnection would be given to seekers, converting a negotiation the stronger party could stall into an entitlement the weaker could invoke. The Telecommunication Interconnection Regulations, 2018 restated the framework, prescribing the process and timelines by which interconnection agreements are to be entered into, the provisioning of points of interconnection, and the consequences of delay. Alongside the framework sits the price of termination. The interconnection usage charge regime long required the network on which a call originates to pay the network on which it terminates, and the level of that charge distributed income between established operators with balanced traffic and entrants whose subscribers made more calls than they received. By the usage charges regulations of 2017 the Authority reduced the mobile termination charge from fourteen paise per minute to six paise per minute with effect from 1 October 2017, and prescribed a bill-and-keep regime, under which no termination charge is payable at all, from 1 January 2020; the move to bill-and-keep was deferred by a year as traffic patterns settled, and took effect on 1 January 2021. Few regulatory decisions anywhere in Indian economic law have transferred as much money between competitors by a single number, and the ferocity of the litigation and lobbying around six paise is the surest evidence of where, on the binding side of the line, the Authority's power is real.

The same regime extends beyond voice. Termination charges were prescribed for text messages by the usage charges amendments of 2013, at two paise per message, after the Authority found that the absence of an agreed charge had itself become an instrument of competitive pressure between networks of unequal size. Its power to require interconnection at prescribed points, and to police the provisioning of those points against the timelines in the interconnection regulations, has generated a further line of Tribunal litigation about congestion at points of interconnection, in which the entrant's complaint is characteristically that the incumbent has complied with the letter of the framework while starving it of capacity in fact. The pattern recurs across the binding functions: the rule is the Authority's, the engineering is the providers', and the dispute is about the space between the two.

The register function belongs in the same picture. Section 11(1)(b)(vii) obliges the Authority to maintain a register of interconnect agreements, open to public inspection, and clause (b)(vi) requires it to lay down and ensure the time period within which local and long distance circuits are provided between providers. Interconnection delayed is interconnection denied, and a regulator that controls the timetable, the default terms and the public record of agreements holds the three levers that determine whether a new network can reach the subscribers of an old one. This is the ground on which the Authority's binding powers most plainly presuppose the competitive conditions that its advisory powers cannot guarantee: it can ensure that entrants connect, but it cannot ensure that entrants exist, because entry is licensed by the Government on the advisory side of Section 11.

## The fracture and the settlement of 2000

The Authority's first three years were consumed by a jurisdictional war with the Department of Telecommunications, and the amendment of 2000 is unintelligible without it. The original Act had given the Authority a mixture of functions that included, in its early understanding, a say in the licensing decisions by which the market was being built. The first Chairperson, Justice Sodhi, took an expansive view of the institution's remit and insisted publicly that the Department was obliged to consult the Authority before taking major regulatory and licensing decisions. The Department, unaccustomed to taking instructions from a body it regarded as subordinate, proceeded with key policy and licensing decisions without the consultation the Authority demanded. Underlying the quarrel was a genuine ambiguity in the 1997 text about whether the Authority's recommendatory functions were the ceiling of its role in licensing or a floor beneath a wider supervisory claim, and about whether its dispute-settlement reach extended to contests involving the licensor itself.

The courts narrowed the Authority before Parliament rebuilt it. The calling-party-pays judgment described above held that the Authority could not vary rights under licences and contracts. In October 1999 the President of India stated publicly that the Authority required to be strengthened by suitable amendments to the Act, and a Group on Telecom and Information Technology Convergence was constituted, whose recommendations led to the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India (Amendment) Ordinance promulgated on 24 January 2000 and replaced in the same session by the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India (Amendment) Act, 2000 (Act 2 of 2000), expressed to operate from the ordinance's date. The Statement of Objects and Reasons recorded the design intentions with unusual candour: to draw a clear distinction between the recommendatory and regulatory functions of the Authority, to empower it to fix the terms and conditions of inter-connectivity, and to establish a separate appellate tribunal. The amendment substituted Section 11 in the form analysed in this article, with the mandatory consultation and reference-back provisos; it substituted the composition and tenure provisions, cutting the term from five years to three and the membership to a Chairperson, two whole-time and two part-time members; and by Section 5(3) it vacated the offices of the incumbent Chairperson and members, with compensation capped at three months' pay and allowances, so that the Authority of the new design began with new occupants.

The haste of that reconstruction drew criticism that belongs to the record. The Standing Committee on Information Technology, in its report on the functioning of the Authority presented in November 2000, questioned the need to proceed by ordinance at all, observing that the immediate effect of the ordinance was the termination of the terms of the Chairman and Members of the erstwhile Authority, and noting that the Tribunal whose urgency had justified the ordinance had not come into existence even by the end of August 2000, which in the Committee's view disproved the case for urgency. The episode fixed a durable ambiguity in the institution's self-understanding. The settlement of 2000 at once strengthened the Authority, by clarifying its regulatory powers and arming it on interconnection, and disciplined it, by removing adjudication and its first office-holders. A regulator that owes its clearer charter to the same instrument that dismissed its first board has been given a lesson in the location of ultimate power that no subsequent chairperson has needed to relearn.

One further episode of the period fixes the fracture's meaning, because it concerned not the Authority's powers over providers but the Government's duty toward the Authority. The New Telecom Policy of 1999 changed the basis of the private licences themselves, offering licensees a migration from the fixed licence fee regime, under which the first private entrants were failing beneath debts their bids had created, to a revenue sharing regime. The Authority's position was that a change of licence terms of that magnitude was a matter on which the statute required its recommendations to be sought, and the migration was settled without the reference it claimed. Whatever the merits of the migration, which averted the collapse of the first private networks and forms part of the disorder of the period the Standing Committee described, the episode demonstrated that the pre-2000 statute protected the advisory function in name only: there was no procedure the Government was obliged to follow, no back-reference it was obliged to make, and no consequence when advice was not taken because it had not been asked for. The second proviso to the substituted Section 11, obliging the Government to seek recommendations before licensing on the subjects of entry and licence terms and to refer disagreements back, is unintelligible except as the answer to this episode. It also marks the proviso's limit with equal clarity, for the obligation fastens on the subjects the episode concerned, and leaves the wider advisory field where the migration package had left it, dependent on the Government's willingness to ask.

What the amendment took away was adjudication, and it took it away on a stated principle: the body that makes regulations and issues directions should not also sit in judgment on disputes arising under them, still less on appeals against its own orders. The New Telecom Policy of 1999 had gestured at an interim arrangement under which the Authority might arbitrate between licensor and licensee; it was notified and then withdrawn without ever operating. The 2000 settlement abandoned the idea in favour of the orthodox separation, and the next section examines the institution that received the judicial function.

## The Tribunal: what TDSAT hears, and the single appeal beyond it

Chapter IV of the Act establishes the Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal, and its jurisdiction under Section 14 is both original and appellate. In its original jurisdiction, the Tribunal adjudicates any dispute between a licensor and a licensee, between two or more service providers, and between a service provider and a group of consumers. In its appellate jurisdiction, it hears and disposes of appeals against any direction, decision or order of the Authority. Section 14A governs the making of applications, and the Tribunal, a body chaired by a person drawn from the higher judiciary with members bringing administrative and technical experience, is the final fact-finding forum for the sector: under Section 18, an appeal from its orders lies to the Supreme Court only on one or more of the grounds specified in Section 100 of the Code of Civil Procedure, which is to say on a substantial question of law, and not against an interlocutory order at all. Section 27 bars the jurisdiction of civil courts over matters the Tribunal is empowered to determine, and the Act arms the Tribunal with the machinery of enforcement, including a penalty for wilful failure to comply with its orders. Telecom thus belongs to the family of Indian regulatory designs in which Parliament has interposed a specialised tribunal between the regulator and the constitutional courts, the same architectural instinct that produced [the National Company Law Tribunal and its appellate tribunal](/2012/05/01/nclt-nclat-explained/) for company law, though with the difference that the telecom tribunal took its jurisdiction away from an existing regulator rather than from courts alone.

The exclusions in the proviso to Section 14(a) are as instructive as the grant. The Tribunal's dispute jurisdiction does not extend to matters of monopolistic, restrictive and unfair trade practices, which at the time of enactment belonged to the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Commission; it does not extend to a complaint by an individual consumer that is maintainable before the consumer redressal forums under the consumer protection legislation; and it does not extend to the disputes between the telegraph authority and other persons contemplated by Section 7B of the Telegraph Act. A group of consumers may come to the Tribunal; an individual consumer may not, because the individual's route was deliberately left with the consumer forums and, in the regulatory plane, with the operator-level complaint machinery the Authority prescribes. The Supreme Court confirmed the width of what remains in Cellular Operators Association of India v. Union of India, (2003) 3 SCC 186, treating the Tribunal's jurisdiction as circumscribed only by those exclusions, and restated the position a decade later in the BSNL litigation discussed in the next section.

Two features of the appellate design deserve emphasis because they determine how the regulator behaves. First, the appeal against the Authority lies to a specialist body that hears the sector's disputes daily, and the Authority must therefore write its regulations and directions for a reader that understands interconnection costing and spectrum engineering and will not be dazzled by regulatory rhetoric. Secondly, the appeal is not confined to the Authority's directions and orders: the Tribunal has entertained challenges to the validity of the Authority's regulations themselves, on the reasoning the Supreme Court later endorsed, that regulations are subordinate legislation whose legality a statutory appellate forum may test even though it could not test the parent Act. The Authority thus regulates under a standing appellate discipline that is quicker and more expert than writ review, while the constitutional courts remain available above the Tribunal and, in the call drop case, were reached directly.

The Tribunal's docket has also outgrown the sector that created it. In the tribunal rationalisation carried out by the Finance Act, 2017, the appellate functions of the Cyber Appellate Tribunal under the information technology legislation and of the airports economic regulatory appellate tribunal were transferred to the Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal, which accordingly hears appeals in fields far from telephony. The consolidation made the tribunal built for one sector's regulator into a standing appellate resource for several regulated fields, chosen because a specialised forum accumulates expertise in the economics of regulated networks that generalist courts cannot maintain. The development matters for this profile because it repeats, on the adjudicatory side, the logic of 2000: the specialist was trusted with more, and the Authority's own disputes share a forum whose jurisprudence ranges across the regulated economy and whose members must divide their attention accordingly.

The Tribunal's procedure completes the design. It is not bound by the procedure laid down in the Code of Civil Procedure but is guided by principles of natural justice, and for the purposes of its inquiries it holds the powers of a civil court: summoning and enforcing the attendance of witnesses, requiring the discovery and production of documents, receiving evidence on affidavit, requisitioning public records and issuing commissions. In practice its most consequential instrument is interim protection, for the appeal against a regulation or direction that will govern a market from a notified date is usually won or lost at the interim stage, when the Tribunal decides whether the Authority's instrument operates while the challenge is heard. Regulators draft with that first hearing in mind, and the knowledge that a stay application will be argued before specialists within days of notification disciplines the commencement dates the Authority chooses for its own instruments.

## The courts and the standard of review

The case law on the Authority clusters around three questions: how wide the regulation-making power is, by what standard the Tribunal and the courts review its exercise, and where regulation ends and adjudication begins. Bharat Sanchar Nigam Limited v. Telecom Regulatory Authority of India, (2014) 3 SCC 222 answered the first two at the level of principle. The Supreme Court held that the power conferred by Section 36 to make regulations to carry out the purposes of the Act is wide and pervasive, subject to the Act and to the rules made under Section 35, and not confined by the illustrative list in Section 36(2) or controlled by Sections 11, 12 and 13. The same litigation confirmed the Tribunal's authority to examine the validity of regulations framed by the Authority, since subordinate legislation stands on a different footing from the statute that parents it. Read together, the propositions enlarge and discipline the Authority at once: its legislative hand is long, and the legality of what that hand produces is examinable by the specialist forum beneath the courts.

Cellular Operators Association of India v. Telecom Regulatory Authority of India, decided on 11 May 2016 and reported at (2016) 7 SCC 703, supplies the discipline in action. The Authority had made regulations requiring service providers to compensate subscribers one rupee for every dropped call, up to three rupees a day. The Supreme Court struck the regulations down as manifestly arbitrary and therefore violative of Article 14, and as an unreasonable restriction on the freedom of business under Article 19(1)(g). The reasoning is the instructive part. Regulation-making is a legislative function, and the Court did not pretend otherwise; but subordinate legislation remains reviewable for manifest arbitrariness, and the Authority had condemned itself out of its own record, having published, days after the regulations, a technical paper recognising that a large share of call drops arose from causes at the consumer's end, and having imposed a penal consequence without any adjudicatory finding that a given drop was the provider's fault or caused any measurable loss. The judgment stands for a proposition every Indian regulator must internalise: the transparency duty in Section 11(4) and the consultation record it generates are not merely good practice but the very material on which the validity of a regulation will be judged, so that a regulator which consults carelessly litigates defencelessly.

The third question, the boundary between regulation and adjudication, reached the Supreme Court in Telecom Regulatory Authority of India v. Polimer Cable Network, decided on 24 July 2026 by a bench of Justices S. V. N. Bhatti and N. V. Anjaria. A multi-system operator had disconnected the signals of local cable operators with whom it had a commercial dispute; acting on the operators' complaint and a High Court direction, the Authority directed restoration of signals under the interconnection regulations and, on reported non-compliance, issued a show cause notice proposing a complaint under Section 34. The Tribunal held that the Authority had trespassed into adjudication and declared the direction void. The Supreme Court set the Tribunal aside. A direction to obey a regulation, the Court held, operates on the regulated entity in discharge of the regulatory function under Section 11(1)(b) read with Section 13; it is not a determination of a lis between parties, it awards no damages, quantifies no arrears and moulds no relief, and it therefore bears none of the essential attributes of adjudication. The Court collected the boundary in propositions that will govern the institution's enforcement practice: the direction must be referable to securing compliance with a subsisting regulation, licence condition or other matter within Section 11(1)(b); the Authority may record a prima facie finding of non-compliance for enforcement purposes only; on non-compliance its role is confined to that of complainant under Section 34, the fine under Section 29 being exclusively for the criminal court; and where the true substance of a grievance is a dispute between two service providers rather than the breach of a regulation, the matter lies before the Tribunal under Sections 14 and 14A. The judgment matters beyond its facts because it refuses both of the easy errors: it does not let the Tribunal convert every enforcement step into a usurpation of its own jurisdiction, and it does not let the Authority convert enforcement into the decision of the parties' rights.

A fourth authority fixes the boundary where the broadcasting jurisdiction granted in 2004 met a neighbouring statute. In Star India Private Limited v. Department of Industrial Policy and Promotion, (2019) 2 SCC 104, broadcasters contended that the Authority's interconnection and tariff framework for television distribution trespassed on the Copyright Act, under which the licensing of content belonged to the rights owners. The Supreme Court upheld the framework. The Authority's power under Section 11 extends to the terms on which broadcasting services are carried and priced through the distribution chain; the two statutes operate in different fields, the Copyright Act protecting the content itself and the TRAI Act regulating its carriage; and the existence of copyright in a channel does not immunise the economics of its transmission from the sector regulator. The case is the broadcasting counterpart of the BSNL principle, and it explains why the Authority's second field of jurisdiction rests on firmer judicial ground than its critics expected: what the notifications of 2004 granted, the courts have construed as a real jurisdiction over carriage, leaving content, the province of copyright and of the programme and advertising codes, outside it.

## The consultation method: how the Authority builds its record

The Act prescribes almost nothing about how the Authority is to proceed before it recommends, regulates or directs, beyond the transparency duty of Section 11(4) and the laying requirement of Section 37. The institution filled that silence with a method that has become its signature: the consultation paper. On any substantial question the Authority publishes a paper that sets out the problem, the options and its own tentative analysis, invites written comments from stakeholders and the public, invites counter-comments on what others have said, frequently holds open house discussions, and then issues its regulation, order or recommendation accompanied by a statement of reasons or an explanatory memorandum that answers the principal objections received. The method is co-regulatory in structure: the regulated industry supplies most of the data and much of the technical argument, consumer groups and academics contest the framing, and the Authority decides on a record that is public from beginning to end.

### How does the Authority make a regulation, and why does the method matter?

By consultation, conducted in public. The Authority publishes a paper that sets out the problem and the options, invites written comments and counter-comments, often holds open house discussions with stakeholders, and then issues its regulation or recommendation with reasons. The method matters because the record built in that sequence is what a reviewing court later reads.

The differential pricing consultation of 2015 and 2016 shows the method at its largest scale, with responses running into lakhs and a public argument about the future structure of the internet conducted through the Authority's process before the regulations of February 2016 settled the tariff question. Spectrum pricing recommendations travel the same road: a consultation paper on valuation and reserve price, responses from operators whose bids the price will govern, and a reasoned recommendation whose assumptions the Government must confront if it wishes to depart from them. The method matters legally as well as politically, for the reason the call drop judgment exposes: a court reviewing subordinate legislation for manifest arbitrariness reads the consultation record, and a regulation whose explanatory memorandum ignores the regulator's own evidence invites the fate that befell the compensation scheme of 2015. The method matters institutionally because it is the main source of the Authority's soft power. A licensor may reject a recommendation, but rejecting a recommendation that emerged from a public process the licensor did not conduct requires the Government to explain itself against a documented record, and explanations of that kind accumulate into convention. The weakness of the method is the mirror image of its strength: consultation is dominated by those with the resources to respond, the industry the Authority regulates writes the bulk of the record it decides on, and the consumer interest the statute charges it to protect is the least organised voice in its own proceedings.

## The advisory record: where persuasion worked and where it did not

Measured across three decades, the advisory function's record is mixed in a pattern that confirms the design analysis. Where the Government of the day already wished to move and needed expert cover and technical design, the Authority's recommendations have repeatedly become policy. Its recommendation in 2003 of a unified access licensing regime, under which a single licence would let an operator offer fixed and mobile services within a service area, was adopted by the Government within weeks and reorganised the market's basic categories. Its recommendations shaped the design of the 2010 auctions of third generation and broadband wireless spectrum, the first successful large auctions of the liberalised era. Its net neutrality recommendations of 2017, proposing a multi-stakeholder body and core principles against discriminatory treatment of content, were accepted by the Department in substance in 2018 and written into the licence conditions, extending the settlement the 2016 regulations had reached on tariffs into the management of traffic itself.

Where the recommendation touches the exchequer or the structure of state advantage, the record reverses. Spectrum reserve prices are the running example. After the Supreme Court's judgment of 2 February 2012 in Centre for Public Interest Litigation v. Union of India, (2012) 3 SCC 1 cancelled one hundred and twenty-two licences granted in 2008 and condemned the first-come-first-served policy under which they had been issued, the Authority recommended reserve prices for the re-auction that much of the industry and many commentators attacked as pitched at levels that would defeat the auction itself. The auction that followed in November 2012 left much of the spectrum on offer unsold. In the auction of October 2016, the 700 megahertz band, the most valuable coverage band on offer, attracted no bids at all at the recommended reserve price. The Government was within its rights in each instance, since pricing spectrum is an advisory subject and the final decision is the licensor's; that is precisely the point. The Authority's valuation expertise is real, its models are published and contestable, and none of that converts its number into the number at which spectrum is sold. The friction in these episodes is also not one-directional: the Authority's 2012 recommendations were themselves defended as the honest consequence of the judgment's insistence that spectrum be sold by auction at market value, and a government that had just been condemned for giving spectrum away was poorly placed to instruct its regulator to recommend less.

The later history of the same function shows the advisory mechanism working as designed when both sides treat the consultation as real. For the bands intended for fifth generation services, the Authority's recommendations of 2018 addressed the auction of spectrum in the 3300 to 3600 megahertz range together with the wider bands. When the auction came to be prepared in 2022, the Authority returned to the question and recommended reserve prices substantially below the levels whose failure the auction of 2016 had demonstrated, applying valuation approaches that weighted past auction results, the revenue the bands could carry and indexation, rather than anchoring on prices first set in a scarcer era. The Government adopted the broad framework, and the auction of 2022 assigned spectrum across the advertised bands, including the first assignments for fifth generation services. The sequence is the procedure's best case: the adviser published its arithmetic, the licensor priced within reach of it, and the resource moved into use. It is equally a demonstration of the allocation this article describes, since the success depended throughout on the licensor's willingness to price as advised, a willingness the same procedure could not compel in 2012 or 2016.

One advisory subject ties the Authority's economic functions to the map rather than the market. The Universal Service Obligation Fund, financed by a levy on providers' revenues, subsidises service in rural and remote areas where commercial operation alone does not reach, and among the binding functions of Section 11(1)(b)(ix) is the Authority's duty to recommend the terms and conditions on which service providers access that fund, while its recommendations on licence conditions have carried the rollout obligations through which unserved areas are to be connected. The Authority's advice on the fund has pressed, across successive recommendations, for the levy collected in the sector's name to be spent on the sector's gaps, for support to follow shared infrastructure rather than a single operator's network, and for the obligations bought with subsidy to be measured and published like any other quality commitment. The fund and its successor arrangements belong to the article on the statute of 2023; what belongs here is the pattern, that even where money rather than permission is the subject, the Authority's instrument is the published recommendation, and the spending decision, like the licensing decision, rests with the Government.

The reference-back machinery remains in daily use rather than in the museum of drafting. In July 2024 the Department referred to the Authority, under Section 11(1)(a), the terms and conditions of the network authorisations to be granted under the Telecommunications Act, 2023. The Authority, after consultation, sent its recommendations on 17 February 2025. On 3 July 2025 the Department sent a back-reference, recording its prima facie conclusion that some of the recommendations could not be accepted or needed modification, and on 13 August 2025 the Authority published its response to the back-reference, reconsidering and answering the Government's objections. The exchange is the fifth proviso operating exactly as drafted, across a quarter of a century and a change of parent statute: consultation, recommendation, prima facie disagreement, reconsideration, and a final decision that the statute reserves, in the end, to the Government.

## The standing friction with the licensor

Friction between the Authority and the Department of Telecommunications is not an accident of personalities but a product of overlapping design. The Department is the policy-maker for the sector, the licensor whose exclusive privilege under the telegraph framework the licences embody, and, through the state-owned operators, a market participant: Mahanagar Telephone Nigam Limited remained a government company serving Delhi and Mumbai, and in October 2000 the Department's own service operations were corporatised as Bharat Sanchar Nigam Limited, which became one of the largest providers the Authority regulates. The Authority's binding functions are directed by the statute at service providers, a term the Act defines to include the Government itself, and its quality surveys, interconnection directions and tariff discipline apply to the state-owned networks as they apply to private ones. A regulator inspecting the licensor's own companies, on funds granted by the licensor, recommending on subjects the licensor decides, will quarrel with the licensor as a matter of structure; the only question is whether the quarrel is conducted inside the statute's procedures or outside them.

The quarrels have had three recurring subjects. The first is consultation itself. The pre-2000 complaint, that the Department took major licensing decisions without referring them, was answered for new licences by the second proviso to Section 11(1); but the duty to consult attaches only to the need and timing of new service providers and their licence terms, and a Department that wishes to proceed on other advisory subjects, or to frame the question it refers narrowly, retains wide room. The second subject is timing: the Authority must answer a reference within sixty days, but no reciprocal discipline governs the Department's consideration of a recommendation, and recommendations have on occasion waited long past the moment their analysis addressed. The third is spectrum, discussed in the previous section, where the advisory character of the function and the fiscal weight of the subject guarantee recurrent disagreement. Overhanging all three is Section 25: the Government's written directions on questions of policy bind the Authority, and its judgment of what counts as policy is final. The direction power has been used sparingly in the open, which is itself a datum: the licensor that can appoint, fund, decline to consult and ultimately direct has rarely needed to direct, because the structure already distributes the final word to it on every subject that carries revenue or security consequences.

The friction with the appellate forum is of a different kind, professional rather than proprietary. The Tribunal exists in part to correct the Authority, and it has corrected it in terms no regulator enjoys reading: striking down the predatory pricing concepts of 2018 and directing reconsideration within six months, holding in the Polimer matter that its enforcement direction was void as an intrusion into adjudication, a holding the Supreme Court reversed only in 2026. The Authority has in turn defended its orders before the Tribunal and appealed the Tribunal's rulings to the Supreme Court, as in the segmented offers litigation that produced the disclosure direction of November 2020. This adversarial loop is the design working, not failing. A regulator reviewed by no one becomes arbitrary; a regulator reviewed by a forum that treats every act of enforcement as adjudication becomes incapable. The case law of the last decade can be read as the two institutions negotiating, through the courts, the width of the space between those failures.

## Broadcasting, cable and the boundary at the internet

The Authority's writ runs beyond the telephone. In January 2004 the Central Government, by notifications dated 9 January 2004, brought broadcasting services and cable services within the definition of telecommunication services for the purposes of the Act, and from that point the Authority acquired regulatory jurisdiction over the carriage side of television: the interconnection of broadcasters, distribution platform operators and local cable operators, the tariffs at which channels and bundles may be offered, and the quality and consumer protection framework for distribution. The broadcasting framework that followed, restated in the interconnection and tariff instruments of 2017, made the Authority the price and access regulator of Indian television and of telephony alike, and generated its own stream of litigation in the High Courts and the Tribunal about the boundary between the Authority's economic regulation and the rights of content owners under copyright law. The Polimer litigation itself arose from this broadcasting jurisdiction, a reminder that the 2004 extension is not a footnote but a second regulatory empire administered under the same section 11.

The boundary that remains contested is the internet. The Authority regulates the access providers through whom internet services reach subscribers, and its differential pricing regulations settled the tariff treatment of content carried on those networks. Whether services delivered over the internet, the communication applications that ride on access networks, fall within the Authority's remit at all is a different question, turning on the scope of telecommunication service in the parent statute rather than on the Authority's preferences. The Authority has consulted on the question repeatedly, including a consultation paper on a regulatory framework for over-the-top communication services in 2018 and a further consultation in 2023 on a regulatory mechanism for such services and on selective banning; its 2020 recommendations on the over-the-top framework counselled against a new regulatory structure for those services at that stage, proposing monitoring instead, while reserving the position. The wider statute book for the internet services themselves sits in [the guide to the Information Technology Act, 2000](/2019/03/15/information-technology-act-2000-guide/), and the definition that will ultimately fix the boundary belongs to the telecommunications statute of 2023, which the dedicated article on that Act owns. What this profile records is the institutional constant: the Authority's jurisdiction is derivative, fixed by the parent statute's definitions and by government notification, and on the largest open question of its remit it has proceeded, characteristically, by consultation rather than by assertion.

## The changes of 2023: who may sit, and how disagreement is timed

The Telecommunications Act, 2023 did more than replace the licensing statute the Authority advises under. In the amendments collected in the new telecommunications statute, Parliament reached into the Act of 1997 itself, and three of the changes bear directly on this institution. The first is definitional: the Act's references to the telegraph framework were replaced by references to the statute of 2023 and the cable television legislation, and the concepts of licensee and licensor were rewired to the authorisation regime, so that the Authority's functions run in the vocabulary of authorised entities. The second concerns appointments. The proviso to Section 4 was substituted so that, for candidates who are or have been in government service, appointment as Chairperson requires that the person has held the post of Secretary to the Government of India or an equivalent post, while a formal route was written in for candidates from outside government: a person with at least thirty years of professional experience who has served as a member of the board of directors or as chief executive of a company in the fields Section 4 names may be appointed Chairperson, and the corresponding threshold for appointment as a member is twenty-five years of professional experience with board or chief executive service. These appointment provisions were among the parts of the 2023 settlement brought into force during 2024. The third change is procedural and has been noted in its place: in the reference-back proviso, the Authority's fifteen days to answer a back-reference became thirty, and its permissive may became a duty to communicate any further recommendations it may have. The 2023 Act also added, as recorded in the tariff discussion, the express power to direct authorised entities to abstain from predatory pricing, and it recast the Tribunal's original jurisdiction to match the authorisation regime, omitting the licensor and licensee dispute from Section 14(a) as disputes under the new statute were routed to its own adjudicating officer and designated appeals committee.

The appointments change is contested, and this article states both cases without adjudicating between them, as the question is one of institutional judgment on which the evidence is not yet in. The case for widening the field is that the seniority proviso superimposed in 2000 had made the Authority, in practice, a post-retirement destination for the senior civil services, and that a regulator of a technology industry benefits from leadership that has run networks, raised capital and answered to boards; the experience thresholds are set high enough, thirty and twenty-five years with board or chief executive service, to confine the field to persons of genuine seniority, and the government-service route remains open alongside. The case against is that the independence of a regulator has historically been underwritten by the predictable distance between the appointee and the appointing government that a defined senior service pool provides; that candidates whose careers were made inside the regulated industry carry the industry's assumptions and, in some cases, its relationships, into the regulator's chair; and that diluting the service anchor widens the discretion of the appointing government at exactly the point, selection of the persons, where the statute's other protections are thinnest. Both cases are live, they cannot both be wholly right, and the institution's history under the widened field is too short to score them.

## The design defended, the design indicted, and a comparison

The strongest case for the recommendatory design is the case from democratic accountability, and it was built into the settlement of 2000 whose Statement of Objects and Reasons distinguished the advisory from the regulatory functions as a deliberate act. Licensing and spectrum decisions commit the exchequer to sums that dwarf departmental budgets, engage the security of the State in the ownership and control of networks, and distribute a scarce public resource among citizens; choices of that character, the argument runs, should be taken by a government answerable to Parliament and removable by electors, advised by an expert body whose reasoning is public, rather than by the expert body itself. A regulator with binding power over entry and spectrum would take fiscal and political decisions without electoral accountability, and would become the target of a capture more consequential than any the present design permits, because its decisions could not be corrected by the political process at all. On this view, the reference-back procedure is the correct constitutional technology: expertise is heard, heard twice, and placed on record, while responsibility rests where votes can reach it.

The strongest case against the design begins from the Authority's own binding functions. Interconnection, quality and tariff regulation presuppose a market with enough independent networks competing on roughly fair structural terms. A regulator that cannot protect the conditions of entry cannot protect the competition its other powers assume: if the licensor delays a new entrant, writes licence terms that favour incumbents, or prices spectrum so that only the largest balance sheets can bid, the conduct rules operate inside a structure already decided against the competitive outcome the regulator is charged to facilitate. The argument gains force from the licensor's interests. The Government is not a neutral seller of spectrum: it is the majority owner of a service provider, the recipient of spectrum receipts on which fiscal arithmetic depends, and the author of industrial policy for the sector. Each of those interests can pull against the competitive structure, and under the present design there is no institution with power to hold the line, only an institution with a voice. The Tribunal's rebuke in the predatory pricing matter, accusing the Authority of abdicating its regulatory responsibilities, lands oddly against this background: the Authority is criticised for not exercising firmly the powers it has, by critics who rarely add that the powers which would matter most were deliberately given to someone else.

A comparison fixes the choice India made by showing the alternative operating in the same legal system. The Securities and Exchange Board of India, whose statute is treated in [the article on the SEBI Act, 1992](/2013/04/01/sebi-act-1992-explained/), was given the powers the telecom regulator was denied. SEBI licenses the intermediaries of its market by registration, and registration is its own act, not a recommendation to a ministry; it adjudicates through its own officers and imposes monetary penalties in its own name, subject to appeal to a specialist tribunal; its chairperson's appointment resembles the Authority's, but the institution's centre of gravity is entirely different, because the decisions that structure the securities market are taken inside the regulator. Nobody suggests the two sectors are identical, and the comparison does not by itself convict the telecom design: securities markets do not consume a scarce sovereign resource or carry the same security charge as networks. What the comparison shows is that the division in Section 11 was a choice among working Indian models, not a necessity of regulatory architecture, and that its consequences, the voice without the veto, should be defended as a choice rather than excused as an inevitability.

## The consumer's route through the system

For the individual subscriber, the Authority's presence is felt less as a forum than as the author of the rules under which other forums work. A consumer with a billing dispute, a failed recharge, a porting refusal or a service that falls short does not, in the ordinary course, petition the Authority, and the Act does not give the Authority the individual complaint to decide. The route the regulatory framework prescribes runs first to the service provider's complaint centre, under the complaint redressal regulations the Authority has framed, with an appeal within the provider's system to its appellate authority, and beyond that to the consumer forums for an individual claim, since the proviso to Section 14 reserves individual consumer complaints to those forums and sends only a group of consumers to the Tribunal. The Authority's consumer protection is systemic: the quality of service standards and the periodical surveys Section 11(1)(b)(v) commands, the published performance reporting against those standards, the tariff disciplines of non-discrimination and reporting, the portability regime under which a subscriber may change providers while keeping a number, which the Authority introduced by its regulations of 2009 and which was extended across the country in January 2011, and the framework against unsolicited commercial communication examined next. Anyone acting on a specific grievance should treat this account as a map of the system rather than as advice on their own case, because the forum, the limitation period and the proof required turn on the facts of the individual matter.

The redressal framework itself is worth describing, because it is the point at which most subscribers actually meet the institution's handwriting. Under the Telecom Consumers Complaint Redressal Regulations, 2012, every service provider must maintain a complaint centre accessible through a toll-free consumer care number, register every complaint under a unique docket number communicated to the subscriber, and resolve it within timelines the regulations prescribe for the complaint's subject, billing complaints carrying their own regime of notice, examination and refund of amounts found to have been wrongly charged. A subscriber dissatisfied with the resolution, or whose complaint remains unresolved beyond the prescribed period, may appeal to the provider's appellate authority, a second tier within the company constituted at a senior level, which must decide within its own prescribed period. Alongside the two tiers, complaints lodged with providers are visible to the Authority through its central complaint monitoring arrangements, so that the pattern of grievances, a provider whose billing complaints spike in one circle, a class of complaint recurring across all providers, becomes regulatory intelligence feeding the systemic instruments described above. The design is characteristically indirect: the Authority never touches the individual complaint, yet the dockets, timelines and escalation rights that govern its handling are the Authority's drafting, and a provider's complaint statistics feed the same discipline of published reporting as its quality returns.

Unsolicited commercial communication, the spam call and the promotional message sent without consent, is the consumer problem on which the Authority has built its most ambitious machinery, and the problem that best exhibits the limits of machinery. The Telecom Commercial Communications Customer Preference Regulations, 2018, notified on 19 July 2018 and in force from 28 February 2019, replaced the preference register approach of the 2010 regulations with a consent architecture run on distributed ledger technology across the access providers' platforms: principal entities that send commercial communication must register, telemarketers must register, the headers under which messages appear must be registered, message templates must be approved, and a customer's consent and preferences are recorded on the shared ledger so that a message can be scrubbed against them before delivery. In the Authority's own reporting in November 2022, complaints attributable to registered telemarketers had fallen substantially, by about three fifths, while the centre of the problem had shifted to unregistered telemarketers operating outside the ledger, through ordinary numbers, spoofed identities and channels the registration net does not reach. The pattern is the institution's pattern at consumer scale: within the regulated perimeter, its instruments work and can be made to work better; at the perimeter's edge, against actors who never sought a licence and fear no register, the Authority is remitted to directions to the providers, to coordination with other agencies, and to the same prosecutorial enforcement, complaint by complaint, that Section 34 prescribes.

## Assessment: a regulator of conduct inside a structure it did not choose

The One Test for an institution profile is whether the reader can predict, from the design, where the institution will be strong and where it will be absent. By that test the Act of 1997 as remade in 2000 explains its subject unusually well. Expect the Authority to be consequential wherever the question is one of conduct within the market: the terms on which networks interconnect, the charges for termination, the standards of quality and their measurement, the tariffs offered to subscribers and their freedom from content-based discrimination, the discipline of complaint systems and communication preferences. Expect it to be advisory, and therefore contingent, wherever the question is one of structure: who enters, on what licence terms, at what spectrum price, and with what consequences for failure. Expect its influence on the second class of questions to vary with the publicity of its reasoning and the political cost of departing from it, and expect disputes about its power to be resolved, on the authorities collected in this article, by asking first which clause of Section 11 the act in question falls under. The reader who can run that classification can answer, without further research, almost every headline question the sector produces about what the regulator did or failed to do.

The verdict on the design follows from the division itself rather than from the performance of any occupant of the office. As a conduct regulator, the Authority is adequately armed and, since 2000, clearly mandated; its failures in that role, the call drop regulations being the standing example, have been failures of evidence and process that the courts could and did correct. As a structural adviser, it is exactly as powerful as the procedure of consultation and reference back makes it, which is to say powerful when the Government wishes to be advised and negligible when the Government has decided; and the considerations that justify that arrangement, accountability for fiscal and security choices, are real considerations and not pretexts. What the design cannot do is have the argument both ways. A system that reserves structure to the licensor cannot then blame its regulator when the structure produces the outcomes, of concentration, of delayed entry, of spectrum left unsold, that structure produces. The institution's history from the ordinance of 1997 to the amendments of 2023 is the history of that allocation being chosen, clarified and chosen again. The new parent statute has changed the licensing world the Authority advises within; whether it changes the allocation itself is a question the dedicated article on [the Telecommunications Act, 2023](/2019/07/01/telecommunications-act-2023-explained/) takes up in the statute's own terms, and it is not answered here.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Telecom Regulatory Authority do?

The Telecom Regulatory Authority of India regulates telecommunication services under the TRAI Act, 1997. It discharges binding functions listed in Section 11(1)(b): ensuring compliance with licence conditions, fixing the terms of interconnection between service providers, regulating revenue sharing among them, laying down quality of service standards and surveying performance, and maintaining a register of interconnect agreements. Under Section 11(2) it notifies the rates at which services are provided. It also makes recommendations to the Central Government on licensing, licence terms, revocation and spectrum under Section 11(1)(a), makes regulations under Section 36, issues directions under Section 13, and protects consumers through the standards and complaint frameworks it prescribes.

### Q: Are TRAI recommendations binding on the government?

No. The first proviso to Section 11(1) of the TRAI Act states expressly that the recommendations of the Authority on the matters in clause (a) shall not be binding upon the Central Government. Those matters include the need and timing of new service providers, the terms and conditions of licences, revocation of licences, competition measures and the efficient management of spectrum. The Government must still seek the Authority's recommendations before issuing a new licence on the first two matters, must consider what it receives, and if it forms a prima facie view that a recommendation cannot be accepted or needs modification, it must refer the recommendation back for reconsideration before taking a final decision. The obligation is procedural; the decision remains the Government's.

### Q: What is TDSAT and what does it hear?

The Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal is the specialised tribunal established under Chapter IV of the TRAI Act by the amendment of 2000. Under Section 14 it adjudicates disputes between a licensor and a licensee, between two or more service providers, and between a service provider and a group of consumers, and it hears appeals against any direction, decision or order of the Authority, including challenges to the validity of the Authority's regulations as subordinate legislation. Individual consumer complaints are excluded and lie to the consumer forums. An appeal from the Tribunal lies only to the Supreme Court, and only on a substantial question of law on the grounds in Section 100 of the Code of Civil Procedure, under Section 18.

### Q: What did the 2000 amendment take away from TRAI?

The amendment of 2000 took adjudication away from the Authority. The original Authority of 1997 combined regulation with the settlement of disputes; the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India (Amendment) Act, 2000 (Act 2 of 2000) transferred dispute settlement and appeals against the Authority's own orders to the newly created Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal. The same amendment substituted Section 11 to separate the recommendatory functions from the binding regulatory functions, reduced the Chairperson's and members' term from five years to three, reconstituted the Authority in a smaller form, and terminated the tenure of the incumbents with compensation. In exchange, the Authority received an express power to fix interconnection terms overriding pre-existing licence conditions.

### Q: What did TRAI decide about differential pricing?

The Authority decided to prohibit it. After a consultation on differential pricing for data services, it issued the Prohibition of Discriminatory Tariffs for Data Services Regulations, 2016 on 8 February 2016, providing that no service provider shall offer or charge discriminatory tariffs for data services on the basis of content, and that no provider shall enter into an arrangement whose effect is such discrimination, subject to an exception for emergency services. A financial disincentive of fifty thousand rupees per day of contravention, capped at fifty lakh rupees, backs the prohibition. The decision ended zero-rated offers under which selected content was free while other content was metered, and it is the foundation of net neutrality in India.

### Q: Can TRAI regulate internet based services?

The Authority regulates the access providers over whose networks internet services are delivered, and its tariff regulations govern how content carried on those networks may be priced. Whether the internet based services themselves, such as communication applications that ride on access networks, fall within its remit depends on the scope of telecommunication service under the parent statute, a definition the telecommunications legislation fixes and the Authority cannot enlarge by its own act. The Authority consulted on a regulatory framework for over-the-top communication services in 2018 and again in 2023, and its 2020 recommendations advised against a new regulatory structure for those services at that stage while proposing continued monitoring. The boundary remains a question of statutory definition.

### Q: How did the 2023 Act change TRAI appointments?

The Telecommunications Act, 2023 substituted the proviso to Section 4 of the TRAI Act. For candidates who are or have been in government service, appointment as Chairperson requires that the person has held the post of Secretary to the Government of India or an equivalent post. Alongside that route, the amendment wrote in a route for candidates from outside government: a person with at least thirty years of professional experience who has served as a member of the board of directors or as chief executive of a company in the fields Section 4 names, which include telecommunication, industry, finance, accountancy, law, management and consumer affairs, may be appointed Chairperson, and the corresponding threshold for a member is twenty-five years.

### Q: Who sets telecom tariffs in India?

Formally, the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India does. Section 11(2) of the TRAI Act empowers the Authority, overriding the Telegraph Act, to notify by order the rates at which telecommunication services within and outside India are provided. In practice, the Authority has for most services adopted forbearance since the Telecommunication Tariff Order of 1999: service providers set their own prices commercially, must report every tariff to the Authority within seven working days of implementation, and must comply with the requirements of non-discrimination and non-predation, which the Authority polices and can enforce by intervention. So the prices a subscriber pays are proposed by operators, but they are proposed inside a statutory power of the regulator to fix them.

### Q: When was TRAI established and under which law?

The Authority was established in 1997 under the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India Act, 1997 (Act 24 of 1997). It was first set up by ordinance, and the Act, which received the President's assent on 28 March 1997, was deemed to have come into force on 25 January 1997, the date of the ordinance. The Act established the Authority to regulate telecommunication services, to protect the interests of service providers and consumers and to ensure the orderly growth of the sector. The statute was substantially amended in 2000, when adjudication was moved to the Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal, and again by the Telecommunications Act, 2023, which among other changes altered the appointment qualifications in Section 4.

### Q: Who appoints the members of TRAI and how long do they serve?

The Chairperson and members are appointed by the Central Government under Sections 3 and 4 of the TRAI Act. The Authority consists of a Chairperson and not more than two whole-time members and two part-time members, chosen from persons with special knowledge of and professional experience in fields such as telecommunication, industry, finance, accountancy, law, management or consumer affairs, with seniority conditions for candidates from government service and, after the amendment of 2023, an experience route for candidates from outside it. Under Section 5(2) they hold office for a term not exceeding three years, as notified, or until they reach the age of sixty-five years, whichever is earlier, and their conditions of service cannot be varied to their disadvantage after appointment.

### Q: What is the difference between TRAI and the Department of Telecommunications?

The Department of Telecommunications is the Government's policy-maker and licensor for the sector: it frames policy, grants the licences or authorisations under which operators provide services, assigns spectrum and owns the state service providers. The Authority is a statutory regulator created by the TRAI Act, 1997, independent of the Department in its constitution but confined by the Act. The Department decides entry, licence terms and spectrum, on which the Authority only recommends under Section 11(1)(a); the Authority regulates the conduct of all service providers, including the Department's own companies, on interconnection, quality, tariffs and licence compliance under Section 11(1)(b) and Section 11(2). The Authority is also bound by the Government's written directions on questions of policy under Section 25.

### Q: Does TRAI regulate cable television and DTH services?

Yes, on the carriage side. By notifications dated 9 January 2004, the Central Government brought broadcasting services and cable services within the definition of telecommunication services under the TRAI Act, and from then the Authority has regulated the distribution of television channels: interconnection among broadcasters, distribution platform operators including direct-to-home operators and local cable operators, the tariff framework for channels and bouquets, and consumer protection in distribution, restated in the interconnection and tariff instruments of 2017. Its regulation addresses the economics of carriage and access rather than the content of programmes, and disputes in this sector go to the Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal, with individual subscriber complaints following the consumer route.

### Q: Can a consumer take a complaint about poor service to TRAI?

Not as an individual case for the Authority to decide. The framework the Authority has prescribed routes a subscriber's complaint first to the service provider's complaint centre, with an appeal to the provider's appellate authority, and the proviso to Section 14 of the TRAI Act reserves individual consumer complaints to the consumer forums under the consumer protection legislation; only a group of consumers may take a dispute to the Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal. The Authority's consumer protection works systemically instead: it lays down quality of service standards under Section 11(1)(b)(v), surveys and publishes provider performance against them, prescribes the complaint redressal machinery providers must run, and regulates tariffs and unsolicited commercial communication.

### Q: What is an interconnect agreement and why does TRAI regulate it?

An interconnect agreement is the contract under which two service providers connect their networks so that a call or data session originating on one network can terminate on the other, covering the points of interconnection, the technical standards for the link and the charges each side pays, including the termination charge for completing the other's calls. The Authority regulates interconnection because a large network could otherwise defeat competition by refusing, delaying or pricing connection beyond an entrant's reach. Section 11(1)(b) empowers it to fix the terms and conditions of interconnectivity, ensure technical compatibility and effective interconnection, regulate revenue sharing, and maintain a public register of interconnect agreements, functions it exercises through the interconnection regulations and the usage charges regime.

### Q: Why did the Supreme Court strike down TRAI's call drop compensation regulation?

In Cellular Operators Association of India v. Telecom Regulatory Authority of India, (2016) 7 SCC 703, the Supreme Court struck down the regulations requiring providers to compensate subscribers one rupee for every dropped call, up to three rupees a day, as manifestly arbitrary under Article 14 and an unreasonable restriction on the freedom of business under Article 19(1)(g). The Court held that although regulation-making is a legislative function, subordinate legislation can be struck down for manifest arbitrariness; the regulations imposed a penalty without any finding that a particular drop was the provider's fault, and the Authority had itself published a technical paper days later recognising that a large share of call drops arose from causes at the consumer's end.

### Q: Does TRAI decide the price of spectrum sold in auctions?

No. Efficient management of the available spectrum is a recommendatory subject under Section 11(1)(a)(viii), so the Authority recommends the reserve price, the auction design and the conditions of assignment after consultation, and the Central Government decides at what price spectrum is actually offered and sold. The distinction has mattered in practice: the Authority's recommended reserve prices for the re-auction of 2012 were widely criticised as too high, the auction that followed left much spectrum unsold, and in October 2016 the 700 megahertz band attracted no bids at the recommended reserve price. The final word on spectrum pricing belongs to the licensor, and the Authority's published reasoning is a discipline on that word rather than a substitute for it.

### Q: How does TRAI try to control spam calls and unwanted messages?

Through the Telecom Commercial Communications Customer Preference Regulations, 2018, notified on 19 July 2018 and in force from 28 February 2019, which replaced the earlier preference register model. Senders of commercial communication, called principal entities, and telemarketers must register with the access providers; the headers identifying senders and the templates of messages must be registered and approved; and customer consent and preferences are recorded on a distributed ledger platform shared by the providers, against which messages are scrubbed before delivery. Subscribers register their preferences and can complain about violations through their provider. The framework has reduced complaints from registered telemarketers substantially, on the Authority's own reporting, while unregistered telemarketers using ordinary numbers remain the harder problem.

### Q: Is TRAI independent of the government?

It is independent in the limited sense the statute constructs. Its members are appointed by the Central Government but hold a fixed term of up to three years or until the age of sixty-five, cannot have their service conditions worsened after appointment, and can be removed only on the grounds stated in Section 7 after a hearing on the two judgment-based grounds. Against that, the Authority is funded by government grants, the Government may issue written directions on questions of policy that bind it under Section 25 and decides itself what counts as policy, and the subjects on which the Authority could most constrain the Government, licensing and spectrum, are recommendatory only. Its independence is therefore strongest in its binding regulation of providers and weakest against the licensor.

### Q: Can TRAI cancel the licence of a telecom company?

No. Revocation of a licence for non-compliance with its terms and conditions is a recommendatory subject under Section 11(1)(a)(iii): the Authority may recommend revocation to the Central Government, and the Government, which granted the licence as licensor, decides whether to revoke it. The Authority's own coercive tools run against conduct rather than existence. It can ensure compliance with licence conditions under Section 11(1)(b)(i), issue directions under Section 13, investigate under Section 12, and prosecute a provider that violates its directions, since a violation is an offence under Section 29 punishable with fines imposed by a court on the Authority's complaint. Cancelling the licence itself remains the licensor's act.

### Q: Who introduced mobile number portability in India?

The Telecom Regulatory Authority of India introduced mobile number portability by regulation, through the Telecommunication Mobile Number Portability Regulations of 2009, made under its powers over the terms of service provision in the TRAI Act. Portability allows a subscriber to change service providers, and later also to move between services and circles in stages, while retaining the same number, removing one of the strongest practical locks a network held over its customers. The service was launched first in one circle in 2010 and extended across the country in January 2011, and the Authority has amended the regulations repeatedly since, including to shorten the time a port is required to take and to strengthen the grounds on which a porting request may be refused or completed.
