The Turin King List is a torn, brown, badly damaged sheet of papyrus in a museum in northern Italy, and it is the closest thing Egyptology has to a spine. Almost every date you have ever read about a pharaoh rests, somewhere down the chain of reasoning, on this document. It is not a monument. It is not gold. It was written on the back of a used tax register by a scribe in the age of Ramesses II, and by the time it reached Europe it had crumbled into scraps small enough to lose in a pocket. Yet the Turin King List does something no temple wall in Egypt does: it gives the names of kings in sequence and tells you how many years, months, and days each one held the throne. That single feature turns a list into a chronology, and a chronology into history.

![What the Turin King List tells us about Egyptian chronology and the Hyksos, explained - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-89.webp)

To understand why a damaged papyrus outranks a wall of polished limestone, consider what the polished limestone actually gives you. The great royal rosters carved at Abydos and Saqqara are handsome, complete-looking, and almost useless for counting years. They name kings. They do not date them. They also lie by omission, dropping every ruler the reigning pharaoh's court preferred to forget. The Turin papyrus does the opposite. It is ugly, broken, and honest in the specific way that administrative documents tend to be honest: it was compiled to be used, not admired, and its compiler had no obvious reason to flatter anyone by editing the record down to a tidy line of legitimate ancestors.

This article makes a claim that runs through everything below, and it deserves a name. Call it the fragmentary-backbone thesis: the Turin King List is the single most important skeleton of Egyptian chronology precisely because it records reign lengths, and it holds that position even though it survives in pieces, even though whole columns are gone, and even though scholars still argue about the order of the scraps. A complete king list without numbers would be worth less. A fragmentary list with numbers is worth more than either, because numbers can be added, cross-checked, and tested against astronomy, archaeology, and other documents in a way that names alone cannot.

The claim cuts both ways, and the honest version of this article has to say so. A skeleton is not a body. The Canon supplies structure, not certainty. Where the papyrus is torn, the structure is a guess. Where the numbers are legible, they may still be wrong, copied from an earlier source that was itself wrong, or garbled by a tired scribe working from a damaged exemplar. Learning to hold both halves of that thought at once, the indispensability and the unreliability, is what source criticism actually is. This document is one of the best teachers of it in the whole field.

The Canon matters with special force for one stretch of Egyptian history. Between the confident, well-documented Middle Kingdom and the confident, well-documented New Kingdom sits a period of political fracture that the monumental record barely acknowledges. Multiple dynasties ran at once. Kings in the Delta claimed titles while kings at Thebes claimed the same ones. Rulers with West Asian names held the eastern Delta from a city called Avaris, and Egyptian tradition later worked hard to write them out of the family album. Anyone trying to reconstruct the tangle of the Second Intermediate Period and the Hyksos who dominated its middle stretch runs out of monuments almost at once and runs straight into this papyrus. The Turin King List names those forgotten kings. Better still, it counts them, and it counts their years.

That combination of coverage and arithmetic is why a study of the Turin King List is not an antiquarian sidebar. It is the method chapter for the entire series. Every article that gives a reign span or a dynasty range is quietly leaning on the reasoning explained here, and every reader who wants to move from consuming dates to interrogating them has to start at the point where the dates come from. The wider question of how anyone reconstructs a period from broken evidence gets its fullest treatment in the discussion of [how we know about Predynastic Egypt](/2000/09/01/how-we-know-about-predynastic-egypt/), which handles the archaeological end of the same problem. This article handles the documentary end.

There is one more reason to spend time with this papyrus. It teaches a habit of mind that transfers well beyond Egyptology. Ancient king lists look objective. They are formatted like data. They have columns, entries, and totals, and the formatting alone persuades most readers to treat them as neutral. They are not neutral. Someone chose to compile them, chose what to include, chose where to begin, and chose what a reign length even meant when two kings overlapped. Reading the Canon well means reading the choices behind it, and the fastest way to learn that skill is to watch a genuinely useful source turn out to be genuinely partial at the same time.

## What the Turin King List Is

The Turin King List, also called the Turin Royal Canon or the Canon of Turin, is a papyrus document written in hieratic script that records the rulers of Egypt in sequence, from divine beings through semi-divine ancestors and on into the human kings, giving each king's name and the length of his reign. It was compiled during the Ramesside era of the New Kingdom, generally placed in the reign of Ramesses II in the Nineteenth Dynasty, which means it is a document looking backward across roughly fifteen centuries of a history that was already ancient to the person writing it.

That backward gaze is the first thing to fix in your mind. The Canon is not a contemporary record of the kings it names. It is a compilation, assembled by a New Kingdom scribe from older material that has not survived, and its authority depends entirely on the quality of sources we cannot inspect. When it tells you that a Thirteenth Dynasty king held the throne for three years and some months, it is not reporting an observation. It is transmitting a figure that had already passed through an unknown number of copies before it reached the papyrus in front of us. The value of the Canon lies in the fact that a New Kingdom archive apparently held detailed year-by-year records for periods that left almost nothing else, and that this scribe had access to them.

### Why is the Turin King List called a canon?

The term canon here means an authoritative measuring line, borrowed from Greek usage where a kanon was a rule or standard. Egyptologists adopted it because the papyrus functions as the reference standard against which other chronological evidence is measured, not because the Egyptians themselves gave it that name.

The physical object is a roll, written in columns, and the surviving text runs across roughly eleven columns of entries in the standard reconstruction. Each column holds somewhere near twenty-six to thirty lines, and each line typically carries one royal name inside a cartouche followed by a reign length expressed in years, then months, then days. Some entries carry only years. Some carry the full triple figure. At intervals the scribe inserted summation lines, totaling the reigns of a group of kings, and these summations are among the most valuable text on the papyrus because they let scholars test whether a reconstructed run of fragments adds up correctly.

The script is hieratic, the cursive everyday hand of Egyptian scribes, written with a reed brush in ink rather than carved. Hieratic is not a separate language. It is the same Egyptian language that hieroglyphs record, written fast. A monumental hieroglyphic inscription and a hieratic administrative note can say identical things in identical words while looking nothing alike, in the way that a printed sign and a rushed handwritten note differ in a modern setting. The Canon uses black ink for most entries and red for certain rubrics and totals, a standard convention that helps a reader find structural markers in a dense text.

The papyrus is also, importantly, a reused sheet. Its front side, the recto, carries an administrative document, a register of taxes belonging to the reign of Ramesses II. The king list is written on the back, the verso. That fact is not a curiosity to mention and move past. It sits at the center of how the document should be understood, and it will come back repeatedly below, because it tells you something about the status, purpose, and likely working context of the compilation.

### How did a tax register end up carrying a king list?

Papyrus was expensive and reusable. Once a tax register had served its administrative life, the blank back of the roll became free writing surface for whatever a scribe needed next. The king list therefore sits on recycled stock, which suggests a working reference document rather than a ceremonial dedication meant for display.

The subject matter of the Canon begins long before any human being ruled anything. The opening columns list gods, naming figures such as Ptah, Ra, Shu, Geb, Osiris, Set, Horus, Thoth, and Maat, and assigning to them reigns of vast length. After the gods come the akhu, the transfigured spirits, and then a group the Egyptians called the Shemsu Hor, the Followers of Horus, treated as a class of predynastic rulers standing between the divine age and the historical kings. Only after all of that does the papyrus reach Menes and begin the sequence of human pharaohs that a reader would recognize as history.

That structure is not an embarrassment to be explained away. It is evidence about how Egyptians thought about their own past. Kingship, in the Egyptian understanding, was not invented at a datable moment. It descended. The office had been held by gods before it was held by men, and the human king was the current occupant of a chair with an unbroken lineage running back to creation. The Canon encodes that theology as a data structure, which is exactly what makes it interesting as a source: the scribe was not distinguishing myth from history and then presenting the history. He was presenting one continuous succession, and it is later scholarship that draws the line at Menes and calls everything above it something other than a record.

The question of where that line actually falls, and what the earliest kings on the papyrus correspond to in the archaeological record, connects directly to the account of [how Egypt was unified under a single crown](/2000/02/01/unification-of-ancient-egypt/). The Canon's opening human entries and the archaeological evidence from Abydos do not map onto each other in any tidy way, and the argument over [whether Narmer and Menes are the same king](/2000/03/01/narmer-menes-first-king-of-egypt/) is partly an argument about how to read documents like this one.

## How the Turin King List Reached Europe

The Canon's modern history begins with Bernardino Drovetti, a Piedmontese soldier who became France's consul general in Egypt in the early nineteenth century and used the post to assemble one of the largest private collections of Egyptian antiquities ever gathered. Drovetti operated in an era with no antiquities service, no export controls worth the name, and an aggressive competitive market between European consuls who were effectively racing each other to strip sites. He acquired the papyrus around 1820, and the acquisition is where the trouble starts.

Nobody knows exactly where the Turin King List came from. The standard account places it in the Theban region, and Deir el-Medina or a related Theban archive is a reasonable guess given the document's nature, but a guess is what it remains. The papyrus arrived in Drovetti's hands through dealers and diggers who kept no records, had every incentive to obscure findspots, and were paid by the object rather than by the information. A chain of custody that would be considered a scandal in any working excavation was, at the time, the normal way material moved.

The loss matters more than it might appear. Archaeological context is data. Knowing which archive a document came from, what was stored beside it, what building it sat in, and what other papyri shared its jar would tell us what the Canon was for, who used it, and how it fit into the working life of a scribal institution. All of that information was destroyed at the moment of extraction, and no amount of later scholarship can recover it. When people say that looted objects lose most of their historical value, this papyrus is the example to reach for, because everything we would most like to know about its purpose is exactly what the looting erased.

In 1824, King Carlo Felice of Sardinia purchased the Drovetti collection, and the material became the founding core of the Museo Egizio in Turin, one of the oldest institutions in the world devoted to Egyptian antiquities. The papyrus has stayed there ever since, and the museum gave the document the name it now carries in every scholarly language.

### What condition was the papyrus in when it arrived?

By the time the Canon reached Turin it had broken into scores of fragments, many of them small, with text running to the torn edges. Reports that it left Egypt substantially intact and disintegrated in transit are traditional rather than documented, but the outcome is not in doubt: the museum received a shattered roll, not a readable one.

Ancient papyrus is not paper. It is a laminate of pressed plant strips, and after three thousand years it behaves like a dried leaf. It does not fold. It shatters. Handling a desiccated roll that has never been conserved is a delicate operation with modern equipment and training, and it was performed in the 1820s by people who had neither. Whatever the precise sequence of events between a Theban dealer's hands and a crate in Turin, the papyrus arrived as a pile, and the pile is the object Egyptology has worked with ever since.

The scale of the damage is worth stating plainly, because the popular image of the Canon as a neat document with a few holes is wrong. Roughly one hundred and sixty fragments have been identified. Large stretches of every column are missing. Some fragments carry a single legible name. Some carry a number with no name attached. Some carry nothing but a rubric line or the tail of a summation. Reconstructing the document means solving a jigsaw with most of the pieces absent, no picture on the box, and no guarantee that two pieces that fit physically also belong together in the original layout.

## Piecing the Canon Back Together

The reassembly of the Turin Royal Canon is a two-century project that has never fully closed, and it is one of the most instructive episodes in the history of Egyptology, because it shows how method improves and how confident readings get overturned.

Jean-Francois Champollion saw the fragments in Turin in 1824, at the moment when his decipherment of Egyptian writing was fresh and his reputation was being made. He recognized what the scraps were and understood at once that they mattered, and his disappointment at their state is one of the well-known stories of the period. What he could not do was reassemble them properly, because the tools for that did not yet exist and the corpus of comparative material was thin.

The first serious attempt came from Gustavus Seyffarth in 1826, and his method was more sophisticated than his reputation in other areas would suggest. Seyffarth worked from the physical structure of the papyrus rather than from the text. A papyrus sheet is made of two layers of strips laid at right angles, and the pattern of those fibers runs continuously across a roll. Two fragments that were once adjacent will show fiber lines that continue from one to the other, and joins made this way rest on material evidence rather than on a scholar's hunch about which king should follow which. Seyffarth's arrangement was a genuine advance and much of it has survived later revision, which is a strong endorsement of the principle.

Fiber matching has limits. It works beautifully for fragments that were physically adjacent and gives nothing at all for fragments separated by a lost stretch. It cannot tell you how wide a gap is. It cannot tell you whether a floating fragment belongs three lines below its apparent neighbor or thirty. For the many pieces that touch nothing, placement has to be argued from content, and content-based placement is where scholarship starts to disagree with itself.

More fragments turned up in the museum's own storerooms. Giulio Farina published additional pieces in 1938 after material that had been separated from the main group was identified and rejoined to the reconstruction. The lesson there is one that runs through the history of many collections: material acquired in bulk in the nineteenth century was often catalogued badly, and pieces of a single object could sit in different drawers for a hundred years before anyone noticed they belonged together.

The reconstruction that shaped a generation of scholarship came from Alan Gardiner, whose facsimile edition of the Royal Canon appeared in 1959 and gave the field a standard reference with a standard numbering system for columns and lines. Gardiner's edition is why Egyptologists can cite a specific entry as a column and line number and expect to be understood. Jaroslav Cerny, one of the finest readers of hieratic the discipline has produced, contributed to the work of establishing what the ink actually says on fragments where the surface has abraded to near invisibility.

Then, in 1997, Kim Ryholt published a study of the Second Intermediate Period that included a substantially revised reconstruction of the relevant columns, reordering fragments, reassigning placements, and reading entries that had been considered lost. Ryholt's work is the reason the Canon's evidence for the Hyksos and their contemporaries looks different in current scholarship than it did in the middle of the twentieth century. Not every specialist accepts every placement he proposes, and the arguments continue, which is the healthy condition of a live source rather than a sign of failure.

### Can a damaged papyrus really anchor a chronology?

It can, because the Canon does not work alone. Its reign lengths are tested against monuments that give a king's highest attested regnal year, against dated administrative papyri, against astronomical observations, and against Manetho's transmitted figures. The Canon supplies the skeleton, and independent evidence checks the bones.

The reassembly story carries a warning that any serious student should absorb. When you read a translation of the Turin King List, you are not reading the papyrus. You are reading a reconstruction, which is to say a scholarly argument about how the fragments relate, presented in a format that looks like a text. Every published version embeds decisions, and those decisions are revisable. Ryholt's revisions changed the reading of an entire period. The next revision may change something else. Treating any edition as final misunderstands what kind of object this is.

## What the Canon Records

The content of the Turin Royal Canon divides into three broad zones, and they behave very differently as evidence.

The first zone is the divine and semi-divine prologue. Gods hold the throne, each with a reign span, and the figures run to lengths that no reader is expected to take as arithmetic. After the gods come the spirits and then the Followers of Horus, and only after that does the papyrus arrive at the human sequence. Because this section is heavily damaged and because its numbers belong to theology rather than to record-keeping, attempts to extract a literal predynastic chronology from it have not persuaded the field. What the section does supply is a clear window into Egyptian ideas about the antiquity and continuity of kingship, and that is a real historical datum about the Ramesside mind even if it is not a datum about the fourth millennium BCE.

The second zone runs from Menes through the periods that other sources also cover reasonably well: the Early Dynastic kings, the Old Kingdom, the First Intermediate Period, and the Middle Kingdom. Here the Canon is one voice among several. Where it is legible it can be compared against monumental attestations, against the year-by-year annals preserved on the Palermo Stone for the earliest dynasties, and against the surviving epitomes of Manetho. Agreement builds confidence. Disagreement forces a decision about which source to prefer and why, and those decisions are where chronological schemes diverge from one another.

The third zone is where the Canon becomes irreplaceable. For the Thirteenth Dynasty and the fractured century and a half that followed, other sources thin out to almost nothing while the papyrus keeps counting. The Canon names a long run of Thirteenth Dynasty rulers, many of whom left almost no other trace, and it carries the sequence forward through the competing lines of the era. Without the Canon, that whole stretch of Egyptian history would be a scatter of scarabs and stray monuments with no framework to hang them on. With it, there is at least a proposed order and a proposed set of durations to argue about.

The single most important structural feature across all three zones is the reign length. This is the thing to understand about the document if you understand nothing else. A name tells you a king existed. A reign length tells you how long, and lengths can be summed. Sum a run of reigns and you get the duration of a dynasty. Anchor one end of that run to a fixed point and every king in it acquires an absolute date. The entire architecture of Egyptian chronology is built from that operation repeated across three thousand years, and the Canon is the source that makes the operation possible for the stretches where nothing else does.

### What makes the Canon different from a temple king list?

Temple rosters were ritual objects. Carved for a reigning pharaoh to honor legitimate ancestors, they omit anyone the court considered illegitimate and give no reign lengths at all. The Canon was a working document on scrap papyrus, it counts years, and it includes rulers the temple walls deliberately left out.

That contrast is the heart of the matter, and it is worth developing rather than asserting. The monumental rosters at Abydos and Saqqara belong to a religious practice. A king making offerings to his predecessors was performing a rite that bound him into a legitimate succession, and the point of the rite was legitimacy, not accuracy. Anyone whose presence would pollute that succession got left out. That is why the Hyksos rulers are absent from the temple walls. That is why Hatshepsut is absent. That is why the Amarna kings are absent. The walls are not lying in the sense of stating falsehoods. They are lying in the more effective sense of presenting a curated subset as if it were the whole.

The Canon does not perform that operation, or at least it does not perform it the same way. It includes rulers the walls exclude, it groups them with summations, and it counts their years. Whatever the scribe was doing, he was not building a legitimating ancestor gallery for a reigning king. He was building something closer to a reference table.

## The Canon and the Second Intermediate Period

The stretch of Egyptian history between the Middle Kingdom and the New Kingdom is a chronological problem in a way that few other periods are, and the Turin Royal Canon is the main reason it is a solvable problem rather than a blank.

The situation is easy to describe and hard to reconstruct. Central authority fragmented after the Middle Kingdom's long peak. The Thirteenth Dynasty produced a rapid churn of short-reigned kings ruling from the north. Rulers of West Asian background established themselves in the eastern Delta and eventually dominated it from Avaris, and Egyptian tradition would later call them the heqau khasut, the rulers of foreign lands, which Greek transmission turned into the word Hyksos. Simultaneous with them, a Theban line held the south. There were probably other lines besides, ruling smaller territories for shorter spans. The full picture of that fragmentation and how it resolved is the subject of the guide to [the Hyksos and the Second Intermediate Age](/2002/07/01/hyksos-second-intermediate-period/), which handles the period as a period; this article handles the source that lets the period be reconstructed at all.

Three features of the era destroy the usual methods. Overlapping reigns mean that adding up king lists produces totals far longer than the period actually lasted, because two or three kings were reigning at once in different places. Short reigns mean that many rulers never lived long enough to build anything, so the monumental record that normally attests a king does not exist for them at all. Deliberate erasure means that the later Egyptian tradition, and the temple rosters that express it, treat much of the period as something to be skipped rather than recorded.

Against all three problems the Canon offers leverage. It names kings who left nothing else, which solves the attestation problem in part. It gives durations, which lets scholars distinguish a king who ruled for a decade from one who ruled for two months, a distinction no name-only roster can make. And it includes the excluded, which means the erasure operating on the temple walls did not fully operate here.

The Thirteenth Dynasty section is the clearest demonstration. The Canon runs through a long series of rulers, many with reigns measured in months rather than years, and the effect of reading it is a lesson in what political instability looks like from the inside of an administrative record. A king holds the throne for three years. His successor holds it for two. The next for a few months. The throne is turning over faster than a scribe can update a register, and the state keeps functioning underneath because the bureaucracy is not the monarchy. That is a historical insight available from this papyrus and from nowhere else, and it is available only because someone bothered to write down the months and days.

### What the Canon says about the Hyksos

The Canon's treatment of the Hyksos is the single most cited element of the document in discussions of this period, and it is also the element where the reader most needs to see the reasoning rather than the conclusion.

In the reconstruction Ryholt proposed, a summation entry in the relevant column groups the rulers of foreign lands and gives a total, reading six such rulers with a combined span of one hundred and eight years. That figure is the anchor for most current attempts to place the Hyksos dominance in absolute time, and it is the number that appears, usually without its supporting argument, in general accounts of the period.

Look at what stands behind the number. It rests on a placement of fragments, on a reading of damaged signs, and on a judgment about which column the entry belongs to. Change the placement and the total changes. Read a numeral differently and the total changes. The one hundred and eight years is not an observation. It is the output of an argument, and it is an argument that competent specialists have disputed in detail. Kim Ryholt's reconstruction commands wide respect and is the standard starting point, but starting point is the correct description rather than final answer.

Notice also what the figure does and does not settle. A total of six rulers across a span of years tells you about duration and about how the Ramesside tradition counted the era. It tells you nothing about how those rulers arrived, whether they came as an army or as generations of settlers who eventually took power, or how Egyptians of the time experienced their rule. The Canon counts. It does not narrate. The question of whether the Hyksos are best understood as invaders or as immigrants who rose from within an already Levantine Delta population turns on archaeology, on burial evidence, on pottery, and on the reading of much later literary sources, and the debate over [whether the Hyksos were invaders or immigrants](/2003/03/01/were-hyksos-invaders-or-immigrants/) is where that evidence gets weighed. The Canon contributes a frame and stays silent on the story inside it.

There is a further subtlety in the Canon's Hyksos entry that repays attention. The scribe grouped these rulers and totaled them, which means he treated them as a recognized unit within the succession. He did not delete them. Whatever the Ramesside court said about foreign rule in its propaganda, and it said a great deal, the reference document on the back of a tax register counted the rulers of foreign lands as part of the sequence of those who had held Egypt. The gap between what a state proclaims and what its record-keepers write down is one of the most useful gaps in historical work, and here it is, visible on a single papyrus.

## The King-List Sources Table

No single Egyptian king list is sufficient on its own, and the practical skill is knowing what each one gives you and where each one fails. The table below sets the Turin Royal Canon beside the other principal sources for the royal sequence, with what each covers and how far each can be trusted. This is the reference to keep beside you whenever a date in any account of Egyptian history needs to be interrogated.

| Source | Date and form | What it covers | Reign lengths? | Includes disfavored rulers? | Reliability and main limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Turin Royal Canon | Ramesside, hieratic on papyrus verso | Gods and spirits through the human kings into the New Kingdom | Yes, in years, months, and days | Yes, including the rulers of foreign lands | The most complete framework available, but fragmentary, with placement of pieces still argued and the end lost |
| Palermo Stone and associated fragments | Fifth Dynasty, carved basalt annals | Earliest dynasties through the Fifth Dynasty | Yes, as year-by-year events with Nile flood heights | Not applicable to its span | Contemporary and precise where preserved, but heavily broken and covering only the earliest stretch |
| Abydos King List | Nineteenth Dynasty, carved relief in the temple of Seti I | Selected kings from Menes to Seti I, about seventy-six cartouches | No | No, and the omissions are systematic | Beautifully preserved and useless for counting years; a ritual roster of approved ancestors |
| Saqqara Tablet | Nineteenth Dynasty, carved for the official Tjuneroy | About fifty-eight kings in reverse order | No | No | Same ritual logic as Abydos, with a shorter and differently selected roster |
| Karnak King List | Eighteenth Dynasty, carved for Thutmose III | About sixty-one kings, not in strict sequence | No | Includes some rulers the later rosters drop | Valuable for names the other rosters omit, but not ordered as a chronology |
| Manetho's Aegyptiaca | Third century BCE, Greek prose, surviving only in later quotations | The full sweep from Menes to the Persian period, organized into thirty dynasties | Yes, but transmitted figures conflict between the surviving versions | Yes, with hostile commentary | The origin of the dynasty system and indispensable for structure, but preserved secondhand through authors with their own agendas |
| Contemporary monuments and dated papyri | Varies by period | Whichever kings happened to build or issue documents | Highest attested regnal year only | Only those who left something | Direct and contemporary, but gives a floor for a reign rather than its actual length, and silent about kings who built nothing |

Read the table row by row and the fragmentary-backbone thesis stops being a slogan and becomes an argument you can check. Every alternative to the Canon fails at one of two points. The carved rosters have names without numbers, which means they cannot generate a chronology no matter how well preserved they are. The contemporary monuments have numbers without completeness, since a highest attested regnal year tells you a king reached at least that year and nothing about whether he reached the next one, and a king who left no monument leaves no number at all. Manetho has both names and numbers but survives only in quotations that disagree with each other, filtered through Josephus, Africanus, and Eusebius, each with reasons to shape what he passed on.

The Canon alone has names, numbers, sequence, and inclusiveness in one document. That it is torn to pieces is a serious problem. It is a smaller problem than being complete and useless.

## The Method: How King Lists Build a Chronology

Understanding what the Turin papyrus is for requires understanding the machine it feeds. Egyptian chronology is not a list of dates someone found written down. It is a construction, assembled by combining several independent kinds of evidence, and the construction has a specific logic worth walking through.

The base layer is relative chronology: who came before whom. Sequence can be established from king lists, from succession statements on monuments, from the stratigraphy of sites where one king's building sits above another's, and from the development of pottery and art styles. Relative chronology tells you the order of the kings without telling you the year of any of them.

The second layer is duration. This is where reign lengths enter and where the Canon earns its position. If you know that a dynasty consisted of nine kings and you know how long each held the throne, you know how long the dynasty lasted. Sum the reigns and you have a span. Do that for consecutive dynasties and you have a floating chronology, a correctly proportioned ladder of years that is not yet attached to anything.

The third layer is the anchor. A floating chronology becomes an absolute one when any point on it can be fixed to a date in a system we can calculate. Astronomical observations recorded in Egyptian documents provide the main anchors, since an observation of a specific celestial event on a specific day of a specific regnal year can, in principle, be matched to a computable date. Synchronisms with other civilizations provide further anchors, since a letter from an Egyptian king to a Babylonian or Hittite one ties two chronologies together, and if either can be fixed both move with it.

The fourth layer is correction. Raw sums always run long, because reigns overlap. Coregencies, where a king raised his heir to the throne beside him and both counted years simultaneously, mean that adding two reign lengths double-counts the shared years. Rival dynasties reigning in different regions mean that adding their lengths as though they were consecutive inflates a period by decades or more. Every serious chronology therefore subtracts, and the size of the subtraction is a judgment call, which is why competing chronologies exist.

The result of running that machine is what specialists call high, middle, and low chronologies, schemes that differ by decades in the second millennium BCE because they resolve the ambiguities differently. This is why the reign dates in any two books rarely match to the year, and why the honest convention is to write circa and give a range. Anyone who quotes an Egyptian date to the year without qualification either has an unusually well-anchored case or has not understood the problem.

### How the Canon fits into that machine

The Canon supplies layers one and two directly, contributes to layer four by grouping rulers with summations, and stays out of layer three, which needs astronomy and foreign correspondence. It is the durations engine. Everything else is either sequencing, anchoring, or subtracting, and none of those can run without durations to work on.

Two more consequences follow from the machine's structure, and they explain why the Canon's damage matters so much more in some places than others. Where a period is densely covered by contemporary dated documents, the Canon is a useful cross-check and its loss would be survivable. The Old Kingdom, with its annals and its enormous building programs attesting kings directly, is like that. Where a period left almost no dated documents, the Canon is not a cross-check but the primary evidence, and a hole in the papyrus is a hole in history. The Second Intermediate Period is like that, and it is why the Ryholt reconstruction of a few damaged columns changed the picture of an entire century.

## Why a Ramesside Scribe Compiled It

The question of purpose is the one that the looted findspot took from us, and it is the question that would most change how the Canon should be weighted. Since the answer is unrecoverable, what remains is reasoning from the object itself, and the object offers real clues.

Start with the material. The compilation sits on the back of a used tax register. Nobody writes a monument on scrap. Nobody dedicates a ceremonial object to a temple on the verso of a discarded account. The physical support alone rules out the possibility that this was a display piece, and it points toward a working document produced inside an institution that had both old registers to recycle and a reason to want the royal sequence written out.

Consider next what a Ramesside institution would want such a document for. Several plausible answers exist and they are not exclusive. A temple or estate managing endowments established by earlier kings would need to know which kings there had been and roughly when, in order to track obligations that predated living memory. Scribes preparing offering rituals for royal ancestors would need a roster. An archive maintaining the state's own historical records would want a synthesis of the material it held. A scribe in training might have copied the sequence as an exercise, which happened with literary texts and could happen with this. None of these can be proven, and the honest position is that the Canon's institutional function is unknown.

What can be said is that the compiler had access to something. The reign lengths for obscure Thirteenth Dynasty kings did not come out of the air. They came from records, kept during or near those reigns, preserved in an archive, and still consultable in the Nineteenth Dynasty. That inference is one of the most important things the Canon establishes and it is often overlooked, because attention goes to what the papyrus says rather than to what its existence implies. The implication is that Egyptian institutions maintained long-run administrative memory across centuries and across political collapses. The state that fell apart after the Middle Kingdom did not lose its paperwork.

### Why did Ramesside Egypt care about the sequence?

The Nineteenth Dynasty had a legitimacy problem. It came to power after the disruption of the Amarna era through a line of military officers rather than an unbroken royal descent, and it invested heavily in demonstrating continuity with the deep past. Documenting the succession served that project as much as any ritual did.

That context matters for weighing the Canon, and it cuts in an interesting direction. The Ramesside kings were the same rulers who commissioned the Abydos roster, which is a work of ideological editing on a grand scale. The same court, in the same century, produced both a carved wall that erases the inconvenient and a papyrus that counts them. The two documents are not in conflict. They are doing different jobs. The wall is a rite. The papyrus is a file. Understanding that a single culture can produce a propagandistic monument and a reasonably candid administrative record at the same time, without hypocrisy on anyone's part, is one of the more sophisticated lessons available in the study of ancient sources, and this pair of documents teaches it cleanly.

The broader question of how Egyptian identity absorbed and reinterpreted periods of foreign domination, including how a later court chose to remember rule it had spent generations denouncing, runs through the discussion of [foreign rule and Egyptian identity](/2002/12/01/foreign-rule-and-egyptian-identity/). The Canon is a data point in that story: the counting document and the erasing document coexisted.

## The Limits, the Gaps, and the Misreadings

The case for the Canon has been made. The case against overconfidence in it needs making with equal force, because the failure mode among readers who have just learned about this papyrus is to treat it as the answer key.

The first limit is damage, and its effect is not evenly distributed. Some columns survive in long readable runs. Others are represented by a handful of scraps. A reader looking at a modern edition sees a continuous text with bracketed restorations, and the brackets do not adequately convey how much of what appears on the page is inference. Where the papyrus is gone, the entries assigned to that space are reconstructions built from what should be there given the surrounding evidence, and the reasoning can be circular if the surrounding evidence was itself established partly from the Canon.

The second limit is the copying chain. The scribe was transcribing from older material, and every transcription introduces error. Hieratic numerals are compact and easy to confuse when written quickly or read from a worn exemplar. A stroke lost from a numeral changes a reign length. A skipped line drops a king entirely. A dittography repeats one. These are not hypothetical failure modes; they are the standard pathologies of any manuscript tradition, and there is no reason to think Egyptian scribes were exempt. When the Canon disagrees with a contemporary monument about a reign length, the monument usually wins, and the fact that this happens at all is a reminder that the papyrus is a copy of a copy.

The third limit is definitional. What does a reign length mean when reigns overlap? If a king ruled twenty years and his son was crowned beside him for the last five, does the son's entry count from the coregency or from his father's death? Different sources answered differently, and the Canon does not explain its convention. It also does not say whether the totals it gives for a group of rulers were computed by summing the entries or taken from another source, which matters because a summation that disagrees with the sum of its own entries is either a scribal error or evidence of a different counting method, and telling those apart requires a judgment.

The fourth limit is coverage. The end of the papyrus is lost. The surviving text does not carry the sequence through the New Kingdom to the compiler's own day, which is a real loss, since a Ramesside scribe's account of the Amarna period and its aftermath would be among the most valuable documents in Egyptology. Whether he included those kings, and how he handled them if he did, is unknowable. The temptation to assume that the Canon would have vindicated one or another modern reconstruction is a temptation to fill a gap with a wish.

### Was the Turin Royal Canon ever forged or faked?

The papyrus itself is not in doubt; its provenance is poor but its authenticity is secure on physical and paleographic grounds. The real risk with this document has never been forgery. It has been overreading, where scholars reconstruct what they expect to find and then cite the reconstruction as evidence.

That distinction is worth pressing, because the language of fakes and forgeries dominates popular writing about Egyptian sources while the actual epistemic dangers are quieter. Nobody manufactured the Canon in a workshop. What has happened repeatedly, and what will happen again, is that a placement made on thin grounds hardens into a standard reading, gets repeated in secondary literature without its qualifications, and eventually appears in general accounts as a fact. The one hundred and eight years of Hyksos rule is the live example. It is a good reading, it is the best available, and it is a reading. The number of general accounts that present it as a measurement rather than an interpretation is not small.

The other quiet danger is selection. Because the Canon is fragmentary, a scholar building a case can find in it support for many positions by choosing which placements to accept. The discipline's defense against this is the requirement that a reconstruction account for the summations, the fiber joins, and the column widths simultaneously, which constrains the possibilities sharply. That defense works. It does not work perfectly, and it works less well the more damaged the column.

## What Remains Debated

Several genuine controversies attach to the Canon, and knowing what they are separates a reader who has understood the source from one who has memorized a summary of it.

The placement of floating fragments is the largest. Pieces that make no fiber join with anything have to be positioned by argument, and arguments differ. Ryholt's reordering of the Second Intermediate Period columns is the most consequential recent example, and while it has been widely adopted it has not been universally accepted, with specialists contesting individual placements and the reconstructions that depend on them. The practical effect is that the number of kings assigned to particular lines of the era, and the identity of some of them, remains open.

The divine prologue is a second live question. Some scholars have argued that the enormous spans assigned to gods and spirits encode something other than pure theology, perhaps a schematic memory of a long predynastic development. Others treat the section as entirely mythological and see no historical content in the numbers at all. The evidence does not resolve the argument, and the damage to the opening columns means it may never be resolved. What can be said is that no reconstruction of Predynastic Egypt built on those figures has held up, and the archaeological account of the period rests on excavation rather than on this papyrus.

The relationship between the Canon and Manetho is a third. Both give sequences and durations. Both derive, presumably, from the same broad tradition of Egyptian record-keeping, though Manetho wrote roughly a thousand years later in Greek for a Ptolemaic audience. Where they agree, confidence rises. Where they disagree, and they disagree often, the question of which to prefer has no general answer, and the choice usually depends on which independent evidence supports which. The dynasty divisions that structure every account of Egyptian history come from Manetho rather than from the Canon, and the Canon's own groupings do not always match them, which is a standing puzzle about how the Egyptians themselves segmented their past.

A fourth debate concerns what the summations were counting. When the papyrus totals a group of rulers, was it summing the reigns as listed, or reporting a span of years during which that group held power? Those are different quantities when reigns overlap, and the difference can amount to decades across a fragmented period. Scholars have taken both positions, and the choice affects how the Hyksos figure and similar totals feed into an absolute chronology.

A fifth concerns the compiler's sources and their reach. If the Ramesside archive held detailed records for the Thirteenth Dynasty, what else did it hold, and where did it come from? The question opens onto the largest unknown in Egyptian documentary history: how much was written, how much was kept, and how much of what was kept has been lost. The Canon proves that the archive existed. Its silence about itself means we cannot measure what we are missing.

### Does the Canon settle when Egypt's dynasties began and ended?

It settles nothing on its own. The Canon proposes durations. Absolute dates require anchoring those durations to astronomy and to foreign synchronisms, and the anchors are contested. What the Canon does is make the argument possible by supplying the quantities the argument works on.

## Reading a Line of the Papyrus

Abstract description only carries a reader so far. It helps to know what an entry on this document actually looks like, because the format itself carries information that summaries strip out.

A typical human-king entry occupies one horizontal line. It opens with a royal name written inside a cartouche, the oval loop that marks a name as royal. In most cases the name given is the prenomen, the throne name a king took at accession, rather than the birth name that popular accounts favor. That preference is a small but real obstacle for readers, since the pharaohs familiar from general books are usually known by birth names while the Canon files them under their accession names, and matching the two requires a reference table of its own. After the cartouche comes the reign length, expressed as a number of years, then months, then days, using the standard Egyptian numeral signs written in hieratic shorthand.

The precision of that triple figure is worth pausing on. A scribe who writes that a king reigned for a given number of years, four months, and thirteen days is not estimating. He is transcribing from a record that tracked the accession and death of the king to the day. Nobody produces that figure from memory or from tradition. It comes from a document that recorded the transfer of the throne as an administrative event, because the Egyptian year system was tied to the reigning king and every dated text in the country depended on knowing which regnal year it was. Dating was bureaucratic infrastructure, and the Canon inherits its precision from that infrastructure.

At intervals the scribe interrupts the run of entries with a summation, a line that names a group and totals it. These lines are structurally the most valuable text on the papyrus for the modern reconstructor. If a summation says that a certain set of rulers held power for a certain span, and the individual entries assigned to that set can be read, then the sum of the entries should match the total. When it does, the reconstruction is probably right. When it does not, something is wrong: a fragment is misplaced, a numeral is misread, an entry is missing, or the scribe was using a counting convention different from simple addition. The summations therefore function as checksums, and a reconstruction that satisfies them is far stronger than one that merely looks plausible.

Where the papyrus is damaged, an entry may survive in fragments that give a name without a number, a number without a name, or a partial numeral whose original value could be several things. Modern editions mark these with brackets and question marks, and the marks are load-bearing. An entry printed as a name with a bracketed reign length is telling you that the scholar supplied the number from reasoning about the surrounding evidence. That is legitimate work and it is not the same category of information as ink on papyrus, and conflating the two is the most common way that readers overestimate what the Canon says.

### Why does the Canon use throne names instead of familiar ones?

Egyptian kings held a five-part titulary, and the prenomen was the official accession name used in administrative dating. A scribe compiling a reference document naturally used the name his records were filed under. The birth names that dominate popular writing were only one element of a much larger royal identity.

The layout also tells you something about the compiler's intentions. Columns of uniform width, consistent entry format, red rubrics marking structural divisions, and periodic totals are the marks of a document designed to be consulted rather than read straight through. You do not need summations if you are reading a narrative. You need them if you are looking things up and want to check that you are in the right place. The Canon behaves like a reference table because that is what it is, and that observation supports the reading of its purpose developed earlier from the recycled papyrus.

## The Thirteenth Dynasty and the Value of Boring Kings

The most underappreciated stretch of the Turin Royal Canon is its treatment of the Thirteenth Dynasty, and it deserves a section of its own because it demonstrates the document's value better than the famous Hyksos entry does.

The Thirteenth Dynasty followed the Middle Kingdom's confident peak and presided over the slide into fragmentation. Its kings are, from a monumental standpoint, close to invisible. Many of them ruled too briefly to build. Many left nothing but a scarab, a stray statue fragment, or a name on a stela that could belong to someone else. If Egyptology had to reconstruct this dynasty from monuments alone, it would produce a scatter of names with no order and no idea how many rulers there were or how long the whole thing lasted.

The Canon changes that. It runs through a long series of these rulers in sequence and attaches durations, and the durations are the revelation. They are short. A king holds the throne for a handful of years. His successor holds it for fewer. Another lasts months. The pattern repeats down the column, and reading it produces a visceral understanding of political instability that no analytical summary conveys as well. The throne was changing hands faster than any king could establish himself, and the Canon lets you watch it happen line by line.

That pattern raises a question the papyrus itself answers by implication. If kings were turning over that fast, how did Egypt continue to function as a state? Monuments got built, if fewer. Taxes got collected. Records got kept, and we know they got kept because the Canon's compiler was reading them centuries later. The answer is that the Egyptian state was not identical with its monarchy. A bureaucracy of scribes, treasurers, and provincial officials ran the country, and it ran through the churn at the top with enough continuity to preserve its own paperwork. The Middle Kingdom's investment in [administration and trade](/2002/02/01/middle-kingdom-administration-trade/) built an apparatus durable enough to outlast the dynasty that built it, and the Canon is the evidence that the apparatus kept working while the throne spun.

That is a substantial historical conclusion and it comes from nothing but a column of names with small numbers beside them. It is also a good illustration of why boring evidence is often the best evidence. Nobody had a motive to falsify the reign length of a forgotten king who lasted eleven months. The entry is dull, and its dullness is a guarantee of a kind. The temptation to distort a record grows with the importance of what is being recorded, which is why the propaganda concentrates on the great reigns and the honest numbers survive around the small ones.

### How many kings does the Canon name for this period?

The surviving text runs to a long series of Thirteenth Dynasty rulers, with the exact count depending on which fragment placements a scholar accepts and how much of the damaged column is restored. That dependence is the honest answer: the number is a function of the reconstruction, not a fact read off the page.

## What the Canon Proves About Egyptian Archives

One of the Canon's most important contributions is not anything it says. It is what its existence demonstrates about the institutions behind it, and this point is regularly missed because it requires thinking about the document rather than reading it.

For a Ramesside scribe to write down the reign lengths of obscure Thirteenth Dynasty kings, several things had to be true. Someone had to record those reigns at or near the time they happened. Those records had to be preserved through the collapse of central authority, through the period of foreign rule in the Delta, through the wars that ended that rule, and through the establishment of a new dynasty at Thebes. They had to be transferred, recopied, or at least stored somewhere that survived. And they had to remain accessible and legible to a scribe centuries later who could locate them and extract what he needed.

That is a chain of institutional continuity across roughly five hundred years and one of the most disruptive stretches in Egyptian history. It tells you that Egyptian record-keeping was not a courtly ornament that collapsed when courts collapsed. It was infrastructure, maintained by a professional class with its own traditions, its own training, and its own storage practices, sufficiently robust that political catastrophe did not erase it.

The implication runs in an uncomfortable direction too. If the archive held that much, what else did it hold? The Canon is a compilation, which means the scribe selected from a larger body of material and gave us the summary rather than the source. Everything he read is gone. The registers, the accession records, the year lists, the whole documentary foundation of Egyptian chronology existed in the Nineteenth Dynasty and does not exist now. What survives is one man's extract, on the back of a tax form, in fragments.

That framing should permanently adjust a reader's sense of proportion about ancient evidence. The problem is not that Egypt wrote little. Egypt wrote enormously. The problem is that papyrus survives only in dry conditions and by luck, that the Delta's damp soil has destroyed almost the entire documentary record of the region where much of the country's population lived, and that what reaches us is a survival sample skewed hard toward the dry south and toward material that happened to be buried rather than stored. The Canon is a message from an archive that no longer exists, and its fragments are a fair emblem of the whole situation.

### Why has so little Egyptian administrative writing survived?

Papyrus rots in moisture. The Delta, where the greatest concentration of population and administration sat for much of Egyptian history, has soil wet enough to destroy organic material almost completely. What survives comes disproportionately from dry desert sites in the south, which skews the entire documentary record toward one region and one set of institutions.

## The Erasure Problem and What Lists Leave Out

The counterpoint to the Canon's inclusiveness is worth developing on its own terms, because the reader who understands why the temple rosters exclude will understand what the papyrus is actually doing.

Egyptian royal ideology required an unbroken and legitimate succession. A pharaoh making offerings to his predecessors was not compiling a bibliography. He was performing a rite that placed himself in a chain, and the chain had to be clean. A ruler who was female and had taken male titulary, a ruler who had attacked the state god, a ruler who was not Egyptian: each of these was a break in the chain, and the ritual solution was to leave the break out. The roster at Abydos runs smoothly from Menes to Seti I with a set of names that never existed as a continuous succession in fact, and it runs smoothly precisely because the awkward names are gone.

That practice is not falsification in the modern sense, and calling it a lie misdescribes it. The roster was not making a claim about the past that it knew to be false. It was performing a religious act whose logic required a particular selection, and the selection was the point. A modern analogy is a family reciting the names it chooses to honor at a memorial rather than reading a complete genealogical register. Nobody thinks the family is committing fraud. The genre is not the genre of the complete record.

The Canon belongs to the other genre, and the coexistence of the two in the same dynasty, sometimes plausibly in the same institution, is one of the most useful things a student of sources can absorb. The Ramesside court both erased and counted. It erased on the wall, where erasure served a ritual and political function, and it counted on papyrus, where counting served an administrative one. Neither document is the culture's real opinion, because a culture does not have one opinion. Documents have purposes, and the purpose determines the content.

This has an immediate practical consequence for reading the Canon itself. The papyrus is more inclusive than the wall, but more inclusive is not the same as complete or neutral. The compiler still chose where to begin, which is to say he began with gods rather than with the earliest king he could document. He still chose to include the divine prologue, which no purely administrative purpose requires. He still grouped rulers with summations, and grouping is a form of interpretation. The Canon has less obvious ideology than the Abydos roster and it does not have none, and the reader who treats it as raw data because it lacks the wall's obvious agenda has been fooled by format.

The full story of how the Egyptians and their successors handled the memory of rule by outsiders, and how thoroughly the pharaonic tradition could absorb, deny, or repurpose an inconvenient stretch of its own past, sits at the center of the same discussion of how Egypt remembered its outsiders. The Canon is the exhibit that proves the denial was never total.

## Source Criticism in Practice

Everything above adds up to a method, and it is worth stating the method plainly, because it is the transferable skill and it applies to every other article in this series.

The first question to ask of any source is what it is for. Not what it says: what it is for. A document produced to be consulted has different failure modes from one produced to be displayed, which has different failure modes from one produced to persuade. The Canon's recycled papyrus and its summation checksums point to consultation. The Abydos wall's polish and its systematic omissions point to ritual display. Establish purpose and the reliability profile largely follows.

The second question is what stands between the event and the document. The Canon is separated from the Thirteenth Dynasty by centuries and by an unknown number of copies. Every intervening stage is an opportunity for error and for editorial choice. A contemporary monument has a much shorter chain, which is why it usually wins a conflict, and it has its own problem, since a king's own monument is the most motivated source about that king that exists.

The third question is what the document cannot say. The Canon cannot say why anything happened. It has no causation in it at all. Reading a period's history out of it requires importing explanation from elsewhere, and the explanation will carry the assumptions of whoever imported it. Being clear about the boundary between what the source supplies and what the historian supplies is most of what separates good historical writing from confident storytelling.

The fourth question is what would change the conclusion. If a fragment moved, would the total change? If a numeral were read differently, would the dynasty shorten? A conclusion that survives those questions is robust. A conclusion that depends on a single contested placement should be presented as depending on it. The one hundred and eight years of the rulers of foreign lands is a figure of the second kind, and the general accounts that present it as the first kind have skipped a step.

The same discipline applied to material evidence rather than documents produces the archaeological method, and the reasoning by which anyone reconstructs a period that left no readable writing at all is worked through in the account of the earliest evidence for Egypt. The two halves of the method belong together. Documents without archaeology float. Archaeology without documents cannot name anyone.

### What is the single most useful habit for reading ancient sources?

Ask where each number came from before accepting it. Almost every misleading claim about Egyptian history survives because readers accept a figure without asking whether it is an attestation, a calculation, or a reconstruction. Those three categories carry entirely different weights, and the difference between them is rarely printed on the page.

## Common Misconceptions

A handful of misreadings attach to this document persistently enough to be worth naming and correcting directly.

The first is that the Turin King List is a complete list of Egyptian kings that scholars consult like a reference book. It is not complete, it was never a reference book in that sense, and consulting it means consulting a reconstruction that embeds contested decisions. The version anyone reads is an argument in the shape of a text.

The second is that the Canon's divine reigns are evidence of a lost predynastic civilization of enormous antiquity. They are not. The figures belong to a theology of kingship, they are damaged where they are most cited, and no reconstruction of Predynastic Egypt built on them has withstood examination. The archaeological account of that era rests on excavation, on pottery sequences, and on cemetery evidence, and it does not need the papyrus.

The third is that the Canon proves the Hyksos ruled Egypt for one hundred and eight years. The Canon, in a widely accepted reconstruction, supports that figure. Support is not proof, the reconstruction is contested in detail, and the figure would change if the underlying placements changed. The distinction is not pedantry. It is the difference between knowing a thing and knowing why someone said it.

The fourth is that because the Canon includes rulers the temple walls omit, it is objective. Inclusiveness is not objectivity. The compiler made choices, beginning with the decision to start with gods, and a document that counts foreign rulers alongside Egyptian ones is expressing a view about what the succession was, not abstaining from views.

The fifth is that the papyrus was found in a tomb by an archaeologist. No archaeologist was involved at any stage. It came out of the antiquities trade with no recorded context, and its findspot is unknown. Every account that supplies a confident provenance is supplying a tradition rather than a record.

The sixth is that the Canon settles Egyptian dates. It does not settle a single absolute date on its own, because absolute dating requires anchors that the papyrus does not contain. It supplies durations. Durations are half the problem and they are the half that nothing else supplies for the periods where it matters most, which is a large claim and a bounded one.

## Coregencies, Overlaps, and Why Sums Run Long

The single most persistent technical difficulty in using any king list is that reigns do not queue politely one after another, and the Canon does not warn you when they fail to.

Two mechanisms create overlap. The first is coregency, the Egyptian practice of raising an heir to the throne beside a living king. Both men were pharaoh. Both had regnal years. Documents could be dated by either. When the senior king died, the junior might continue counting from his own accession to the coregency or might restart, and the practice was not consistent across periods. A king list that gives each of them a full reign length is therefore double-counting the shared years, and adding the entries produces a dynasty longer than the dynasty was. The Middle Kingdom is the classic zone of coregency argument, and the reconstruction of [Senusret III and the Middle Kingdom peak](/2001/12/01/senusret-iii-middle-kingdom/) depends on how the question is resolved for that line.

The second mechanism is simultaneous rival rule. During the fragmented centuries, a Theban king and a Delta king and possibly other rulers held their territories at the same time. A document that lists them in sequence, as a linear roll must, implies a succession that never happened. Adding their reign lengths inflates the period grotesquely. This is not a subtle error at the margins; applied naively to the Second Intermediate Period it produces a span that no archaeologist would accept.

The Canon supplies partial protection against the second problem through its groupings. By totaling a set of rulers as a unit, the compiler signals that the set belongs together, which lets a modern reader treat the group's total as a span rather than as an addition of independent reigns. That is exactly why the summation for the rulers of foreign lands carries so much weight: a total of years for a group is a much more useful quantity than a stack of individual entries, because it may already encode the overlap correction rather than requiring one.

The word may in that sentence is doing serious work. Whether the compiler's totals were computed by adding his entries or were inherited from a source that reported spans is not stated anywhere on the papyrus, and specialists have argued both. If the totals are sums, they inherit the overlap problem and need correcting. If they are spans, they may already be right. The same figure supports two different chronologies depending on which reading is adopted, and there is no way to settle the question from the document alone.

That ambiguity is the deepest reason competing Egyptian chronologies exist. It is not that scholars disagree about what the Canon says in the well-preserved stretches. It is that they disagree about what its numbers mean, and meaning is not printed on a papyrus.

### How much can overlap distort a period?

Substantially. During the Second Intermediate Period, several lines ruled simultaneously in different regions, so treating the Canon's entries as a single queue could stretch a century and a half into several centuries. Every credible chronology subtracts, and the size of the subtraction is a judgment rather than a measurement.

## The Canon Against the Other Evidence, Period by Period

The Canon's usefulness varies enormously across the sweep it covers, and a reader who understands the variation will know when to reach for it and when to reach for something else.

For the Early Dynastic period and the Old Kingdom, the Canon has a formidable competitor. The Palermo Stone and its associated fragments preserve royal annals carved in the Fifth Dynasty, recording events year by year, including Nile flood heights, and that is a contemporary or near-contemporary record of a kind the Canon can never be. Where the annals survive they outrank the papyrus, since they are closer to the events and were carved rather than copied through generations. The annals are also brutally fragmentary, covering only the earliest stretch, so the Canon retains value as a framework where the stone gives out. The broad account of [the Old Kingdom](/2000/11/01/old-kingdom-of-egypt-complete-guide/) rests on both plus a mass of monumental attestation from the pyramid age, which is why the period's chronology, while debated at the margins, is not in crisis.

For the First Intermediate Period and the Middle Kingdom, the balance tips toward the papyrus. Monuments thin during the fragmentation, recover through the Twelfth Dynasty, and never reach the density of the pyramid age. The Canon's sequence carries real weight here, and its interaction with the coregency question shapes how the Middle Kingdom is dated. The narrative of that collapse and recovery is treated in the discussion of [Egypt's first collapse and reunification](/2001/10/01/egypt-collapse-and-reunification/), and the dates in any such narrative are downstream of decisions about this papyrus.

For the Second Intermediate Period, there is scarcely a contest. Monuments are sparse, many rulers left nothing, and the later tradition suppressed rather than recorded. The Canon is the framework, and where the Canon is torn the framework has holes that archaeology fills partially through scarabs, seal impressions, and stratigraphy at sites in the Delta, but cannot fill with names and durations. Attempts to reconstruct the era's political architecture from material evidence alone have produced valuable results and no chronology.

For the New Kingdom, the Canon's contribution is limited by the loss of its ending. The period is otherwise well documented, with monuments, dated papyri, foreign correspondence, and astronomical references providing anchors that the earlier periods lack, so the papyrus is not needed as a backbone here. The frustrating consequence is that the Canon is least necessary exactly where it survives worst, and most necessary exactly where it survives worst as well, which is a coincidence with no cosmic meaning and considerable practical annoyance.

The general shape of that survey supports a claim worth stating plainly: the Canon's importance is inversely proportional to how well a period is otherwise documented. It is a backstop. Where other evidence is rich, the papyrus is a cross-check whose disagreements are interesting but not decisive. Where other evidence fails, it is the load-bearing member, and its cracks become the discipline's cracks.

### Which period depends on the Canon most heavily?

The Second Intermediate Period, without a close rival. Its rulers left few monuments, its overlapping lines defeat simple sequencing, and the later Egyptian tradition suppressed rather than recorded it. Remove the papyrus and the era's political architecture would reduce to a scatter of scarabs with no framework to organize them.

## The Canon and the Making of a Discipline

The two-century argument over these fragments is also a history of Egyptology learning how to be careful, and it is worth reading that way.

The early phase was decipherment-driven and optimistic. Champollion had cracked the writing system, the field expected the documents to start talking, and a papyrus that listed kings with numbers looked like a key that would open the whole chronology at a stroke. The condition of the fragments delivered the first hard lesson: having a readable script does not mean having a readable text.

The middle phase was material. Seyffarth's fiber-matching moved the problem from philology to physical evidence, and the move was a methodological advance whose principle still governs papyrological reconstruction. It also established the pattern that has held ever since: the reconstruction is only as good as its physical constraints, and where physical constraints run out, argument fills the space and consensus becomes fragile.

The institutional phase was slower and less glamorous. Farina's publication of fragments recovered from the museum's own storerooms in 1938 is the sort of episode that never makes a general history and matters a great deal. It says that the object had been mishandled by its own custodians, that pieces of a single papyrus could sit unrecognized for a century, and that the boring work of cataloguing is where discoveries hide. Gardiner's 1959 facsimile did the same kind of unglamorous work at a higher level, giving the field a shared citation system so that scholars could argue about the same line and know it.

The revisionist phase arrived with Ryholt's reconstruction in 1997, and its reception is instructive. A single scholar reordered fragments in a few columns and the political history of a century changed shape. The field largely accepted the revision, contested parts of it, and continues to work through the implications. That sequence is what a healthy discipline looks like. A field where a major source cannot be reinterpreted is a field that has stopped doing history, and the fact that this papyrus remains contestable after two hundred years is a mark in its favor rather than against it.

The larger lesson for a reader is about the shelf life of confidence. Everything anyone believed about the Canon's Second Intermediate Period columns in the middle of the twentieth century was held with the same conviction that current readings are held with now. Some of it was wrong. The appropriate response is not skepticism about all of it, which would be lazy, but attention to which parts rest on fiber joins and which rest on argument.

## Using the Canon in Study

For a student or an examination candidate, the Canon is more useful as an argument than as a fact, and knowing the difference is worth marks.

Almost anyone can memorize that the Turin Royal Canon is a Ramesside papyrus that lists kings with reign lengths. That sentence is a fact and it is nearly worthless in an examination, because it demonstrates recall and nothing else. What demonstrates understanding is the ability to explain why reign lengths matter more than names, why a fragmentary source can outrank a well-preserved one, and why the same court produced both a counting document and an erasing wall. Those explanations show that a candidate has grasped how historical knowledge is made, and that is what a well-set question on sources is testing for.

The most examinable content in this article is therefore the comparison in the king-list sources table and the reasoning that follows from it. Learn why each alternative fails and the Canon's position becomes something you can defend rather than assert. Learn the four-layer structure of chronology building and you can explain why dates carry a circa and why two textbooks disagree, which is a question that catches out candidates who have memorized one textbook's numbers.

The second most examinable content is the erasure contrast. A question about Egyptian propaganda, about the reliability of monumental sources, or about how states manage memory can be answered strongly with the Abydos roster and the Turin papyrus set side by side, because the pair shows a single culture doing both things at once for different purposes. That is a more sophisticated answer than a general observation that ancient sources are biased.

The third is the honest handling of the Hyksos figure. A candidate who writes that the Canon records one hundred and eight years of Hyksos rule has stated the standard line. A candidate who writes that a widely accepted reconstruction of a damaged column yields that total, that the reading depends on fragment placement, and that the figure is the basis for most current dating of the period, has demonstrated source criticism. The second answer is harder and it is the one that separates work at the top of a mark scheme from work in the middle. The broader context that answer sits in is developed across the period pillar for this cluster.

## Where the Canon Stops: Anchoring a Floating Chronology

The Canon gives durations and stops. Turning durations into dates that can be written with BCE after them requires anchors, and the anchors come from outside the papyrus entirely. Understanding what they are clarifies both the Canon's contribution and its boundary.

The first anchor is astronomical. Egyptian documents occasionally record the observation of a celestial event on a stated day of a stated month of a stated regnal year. Because the movements of the sky are calculable, such an observation can in principle be matched to a computable date, which pins the regnal year in question to an absolute year and drags the surrounding chronology with it. The most discussed of these are observations tied to the heliacal rising of the star Sothis, which the Egyptians associated with the flood and with the ideal beginning of the year. The reasoning is powerful and it is also contested at every stage, since the calculation depends on where the observation was made, on how the observer defined the event, and on whether the record is reporting an observation at all rather than a schematic ideal. Different assumptions move the resulting dates by decades, and that spread is the origin of the high, middle, and low chronologies that scholars argue over.

The second anchor is diplomatic. When an Egyptian king corresponds with a Babylonian or Hittite one, and when both kings can be placed in their own dynastic sequences, the two chronologies lock together. Any absolute date established in either system propagates into the other. This is a genuinely strong method and it has a genuine weakness: the neighboring chronologies have their own uncertainties, so locking two floating ladders together produces a bigger floating ladder rather than a fixed one, unless something in the combined structure touches the ground.

The third anchor is scientific. Radiocarbon dating of organic material from securely stratified contexts can provide independent constraint, and its precision has improved substantially over the history of the method. Its resolution is usually broader than the differences between competing chronologies, which limits how much it can adjudicate, but it is capable of ruling out schemes that stray too far.

The Canon touches none of this. It contains no astronomy, no foreign correspondence, and no organic sample beyond the papyrus itself. Its entire contribution is the ladder, and the ladder is useless without the ground and pointless without something to climb to. That division of labor is the cleanest way to state what the papyrus is: it is the measuring instrument, not the measurement.

### Why do textbooks disagree about Egyptian dates?

Because the anchors are contested and the overlap corrections are judgments. Two scholars can accept identical reign lengths from the Canon and still produce chronologies decades apart, since they resolve the astronomical calculations differently or subtract different amounts for coregencies and rival lines.

## The Compiler's Silence

A last observation about the Canon concerns what it does not do, and it is the observation that most changes how the document should be read.

The papyrus never explains itself. It does not say who made it, when, for whom, from what sources, or by what conventions. It does not define what a reign length means when reigns overlap. It does not say whether its totals are additions or spans. It does not justify beginning with gods, does not mark where myth ends, and does not comment on the rulers it counts. There is no preface, no colophon that survives, and no editorial voice anywhere in the surviving text.

That silence is not unusual. Ancient administrative documents rarely explain their own conventions, because they were written for people who already knew them. A scribe compiling a reference table for colleagues had no reason to spell out what everyone in the office understood. The convention was ambient. It has since evaporated, and its evaporation is why a document that was transparent to its intended readers is opaque to us.

The consequence is that every interpretive question about the Canon has to be answered from outside the Canon, by reasoning about Egyptian scribal practice in general, by comparison with other documents, and by testing which reading produces a chronology that fits the archaeology. That is legitimate procedure and it is also the mechanism by which circularity enters. If a placement is chosen because it produces a chronology that matches the archaeological picture, and the archaeological picture was dated using the chronology, the reasoning has closed a loop. Specialists work hard to avoid this, and the summation checksums and fiber joins are the main independent constraints that keep the loop open. The constraints are real. They are also not infinite, and the more damaged the column the weaker they get.

There is something worth noticing in that silence beyond the methodological headache. A scribe sat down with a discarded tax register, turned it over, and wrote out the succession of every ruler who had held Egypt, from the gods to his own era, with the years each had held it. He did not editorialize. He did not exclude the foreigners. He counted, totaled, and moved on. Whatever the Ramesside state was proclaiming on its temple walls, the working record on the back of the scrap kept the arithmetic straight, and three thousand years later a torn version of that arithmetic is what holds Egyptian history together.

## Where the Canon Hands Over

The point at which the papyrus stops being the primary evidence is itself worth examining, because the handover marks the boundary between a period reconstructed from a document and a period reconstructed from everything.

The Theban line that eventually expelled the rulers of foreign lands appears in the Canon's damaged columns alongside its rivals, and the Canon's evidence for the sequence of those Theban kings feeds directly into the reconstruction of the campaign that ended the era. That campaign, and the sequence of Theban rulers who prosecuted it across more than one generation, is the subject of the account of [the war that drove out the Hyksos](/2002/10/01/war-that-drove-out-the-hyksos/), and the order in which those kings are placed rests partly on this papyrus and partly on their monuments, which become steadily more numerous as the Theban line grows stronger.

Once the expulsion is complete and a unified state emerges under the founder of the Eighteenth Dynasty, the documentary situation transforms. Monuments proliferate. Dated papyri survive. Tomb biographies name officials and the kings they served. Foreign correspondence begins to tie Egypt into a wider system with its own chronologies. The reign of the founder of the New Kingdom is attested by a body of evidence that no Second Intermediate Period ruler comes close to matching, and the Canon becomes one voice in a chorus rather than a soloist. The profile of [Ahmose I and the founding of the New Kingdom](/2002/11/01/ahmose-i-founder-of-new-kingdom/) draws on that richer record, and its dates are correspondingly firmer.

Watching that handover happen is a useful exercise in itself. The history of the Second Intermediate Period reads as tentative, hedged, and full of competing reconstructions, while the history of the early Eighteenth Dynasty reads as confident narrative. The difference is not that one era was more interesting or more consequential. It is that one left a documentary record and the other did not, and the confidence of the writing tracks the evidence rather than the importance of the events. A reader who has understood the Canon will notice that pattern everywhere afterward, and will read the hedged periods with sympathy and the confident ones with a little more suspicion than before.

The irony in the handover is that the Canon's own compiler lived on the far side of it, in the confident era, with access to records from the tentative one that we would trade a great deal to see. He read them, extracted the numbers, wrote them on the back of a tax form, and threw nothing else to us. The papyrus is a door left slightly open onto a room that has since burned down.

## The Honest Verdict

How much can we know from the Turin Royal Canon? The answer has three parts, and giving all three is the point of studying a source rather than consuming a summary of it.

We can know the shape of Egyptian kingship as the Ramesside period understood it, and we can know it with real confidence. The succession ran from gods to spirits to men in one continuous line. Kings were counted, grouped, and totaled. Rulers of foreign lands were part of the count. A scribe on the back of a tax register recorded all of this without apparent editorial anxiety. That is a substantial piece of intellectual history and it does not depend on any contested fragment placement.

We can know a great deal about the sequence and duration of reigns, with confidence that varies sharply by period and by the state of the relevant column. For well-preserved stretches that other evidence corroborates, the Canon's figures are as good as ancient chronological data gets. For the Second Intermediate Period, the Canon gives a framework that is better than nothing by an enormous margin and worse than certainty by an equally large one. For the divine prologue, we can know what Egyptians believed and nothing about what happened.

We cannot know what the Canon was for, what archive it came from, what its scribe's sources were, how the papyrus ended after the surviving text breaks off, or whether the placements that current scholarship accepts will survive the next generation of work. That list of unknowns is long, and a reader who finishes this article able to recite it has learned the thing worth learning.

The fragmentary-backbone thesis holds under all of that. The Canon is the skeleton, not because it is reliable in the way a modern record is reliable, but because it is the only document that supplies the one quantity a chronology cannot be built without, across the one stretch of history where nothing else supplies it. A skeleton with cracks in it still holds a body up. It just means you should be careful about how much weight you put on any single bone.

The habit this document teaches is the habit worth carrying into every other article in this series. When you read that a king reigned from one year to another, ask where the number came from. Ask whether it is an attestation or a reconstruction. Ask what would change if a fragment moved. Most historical writing about Egypt presents its dates without showing that work, not out of dishonesty but out of a reasonable judgment about what a general reader wants. The reader who wants the work shown has to go looking for it, and the Turin Royal Canon is where the looking starts.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the Turin King List and what does it record?

The Turin King List, also called the Turin Royal Canon, is a hieratic papyrus of the Ramesside era, generally placed in the reign of Ramesses II, that records the rulers of Egypt in sequence together with the length of each reign in years, months, and days. It opens with gods, moves through transfigured spirits and the Followers of Horus, and then runs through the human kings from Menes onward. Summation lines total groups of rulers at intervals. The text is written on the back of a reused tax register, and the surviving document consists of roughly one hundred and sixty fragments rather than a continuous roll. Its distinguishing feature is the reign lengths, which no carved Egyptian royal roster provides.

### Q: Why is the Turin King List important?

It is important because it supplies the one thing Egyptian chronology cannot be built without and no other single source provides in that combination: names in sequence, durations attached to them, and coverage that includes rulers whom official monuments deliberately omit. Carved rosters at Abydos and Saqqara give names without numbers, so they cannot generate a chronology. Contemporary monuments give a highest attested regnal year, which is a floor rather than a reign length, and say nothing about kings who built nothing. Manetho gives both but survives only in conflicting quotations. The Canon alone offers the full package in one document, which is why every reconstruction of Egyptian chronology leans on it somewhere.

### Q: What does the Turin King List tell us about the Hyksos?

It counts them. In the reconstruction proposed by Kim Ryholt, a summation entry groups the rulers of foreign lands and gives a total of six such rulers holding power for one hundred and eight years, and that figure anchors most current attempts to place Hyksos dominance in absolute time. The Canon also names Thirteenth Dynasty and other contemporary rulers whom no monument attests, which lets the tangle of overlapping lines be sorted into something like an order. What it does not do is narrate. It says nothing about how the Hyksos arrived, whether by conquest or gradual settlement, or how Egyptians of the period experienced their rule.

### Q: How complete is the Turin King List?

Not very, and the incompleteness is uneven. Roughly one hundred and sixty fragments survive from what was once a roll of about eleven columns, with each column holding somewhere near twenty-six to thirty entries. Some columns preserve long readable runs. Others are represented by scraps carrying a single name, a bare numeral, or nothing but a rubric. The end of the document is lost, so the surviving text does not carry the succession through the New Kingdom to the compiler's own time. Published editions present a continuous text with bracketed restorations, which understates how much of what appears on the page is scholarly inference rather than surviving ink.

### Q: When was the Turin King List written?

It was compiled during the Ramesside era of the New Kingdom, and the standard attribution places it in the reign of Ramesses II in the Nineteenth Dynasty, roughly the thirteenth century BCE by conventional chronology. The dating rests substantially on the document on the front of the papyrus, a tax register belonging to that reign, since the king list was written afterward on the blank back of the reused roll. That timing makes the Canon a retrospective compilation looking back across some fifteen centuries, assembled from older records that have not survived, rather than a contemporary record of the reigns it counts.

### Q: How do king lists help reconstruct Egyptian chronology?

They supply the two lower layers of a four-layer construction. Sequence comes first: which king followed which, established from lists, succession statements, and site stratigraphy. Duration comes second, and this is where reign lengths matter, since summing the reigns of consecutive kings yields the length of a dynasty and produces a correctly proportioned but floating ladder of years. The third layer anchors that ladder to computable dates through astronomical observations and synchronisms with other civilizations. The fourth corrects for overlap, subtracting the years double-counted through coregencies and rival dynasties reigning simultaneously. King lists without durations can only serve the first layer.

### Q: What other king lists exist besides the Turin one?

The main comparators are the Palermo Stone and its associated fragments, Fifth Dynasty annals carved in stone that record events year by year for the earliest dynasties; the Abydos King List, a Nineteenth Dynasty relief in the temple of Seti I naming about seventy-six approved ancestors; the Saqqara Tablet, carved for the official Tjuneroy with about fifty-eight kings in reverse order; the Karnak King List, made for Thutmose III with about sixty-one names not in strict sequence; and Manetho's Aegyptiaca, a Greek history of the third century BCE that survives only in quotations by later authors and gave us the thirty-dynasty framework. Only the Palermo Stone, Manetho, and the Canon supply numbers.

### Q: How reliable is the Turin King List?

Reliable enough to build on and not reliable enough to trust without checking. It is a copy made from older material, and every copying stage introduces the standard errors of any manuscript tradition: numerals confused, lines skipped, entries repeated. Where the Canon disagrees with a contemporary monument about a reign length, the monument usually wins. It also does not explain its own conventions, so how it handled overlapping reigns and whether its summations were computed or inherited remain open questions. Its reliability rises where other evidence corroborates it and falls sharply in the damaged columns, where the text is reconstruction rather than survival.

### Q: Where is the Turin King List kept?

It is held by the Museo Egizio in Turin, in northern Italy, which is where the document acquired its name. The papyrus came to the city as part of the enormous collection assembled by Bernardino Drovetti, who served as France's consul general in Egypt in the early nineteenth century and acquired the roll around 1820. King Carlo Felice of Sardinia purchased the Drovetti collection in 1824, and that material became the founding core of the museum, one of the oldest institutions in the world devoted specifically to Egyptian antiquities. The papyrus has remained in Turin ever since, and its condition has shaped two centuries of scholarship there.

### Q: Who discovered the Turin Royal Canon?

Nobody in any sense an archaeologist would accept. The papyrus was acquired around 1820 by Bernardino Drovetti through the dealer and digger networks that supplied European consuls competing to build collections, in an era with no antiquities service and no meaningful export control. No excavator recorded a findspot, no context was documented, and the chain of custody between an Egyptian tomb or archive and a crate bound for Italy is unrecoverable. The Theban region is the traditional guess and a reasonable one, but it remains a guess. Everything we would most like to know about the document's institutional purpose was destroyed at the moment of extraction.

### Q: What script and language is the Turin Royal Canon written in?

It is written in hieratic, the cursive everyday hand of Egyptian scribes, recording the Egyptian language. Hieratic is not a distinct language and not a code. It is the same tongue that hieroglyphs write, set down quickly with a reed brush in ink rather than carved with a chisel, and the relationship is roughly that of rapid handwriting to a formal printed inscription. The Canon uses black ink for most of its entries and red for certain rubrics and summation lines, a standard convention that gives a reader structural markers in a dense column of names and numerals. The decipherment that made such texts legible is covered by the guide to [the Rosetta Stone and the reading of Egyptian writing](/2014/01/01/rosetta-stone-deciphering-egypt/).

### Q: How did the Turin Royal Canon become so badly damaged?

Through age and handling. Ancient papyrus is a laminate of pressed plant strips, and after three millennia it behaves like a dried leaf: it shatters rather than folds. The roll passed through dealers, a consul's collection, and a journey to Italy in the 1820s, handled at every stage by people without conservation training or equipment, since neither existed. Traditional accounts that it left Egypt largely intact and disintegrated in transit are not documented, but the outcome is not disputed. Turin received a pile of fragments. Whatever the precise sequence, the papyrus was destroyed as a physical object before any scholar could read it whole.

### Q: Does the Turin Royal Canon list gods and spirits as rulers?

Yes, and the opening columns do exactly that, naming divine figures including Ptah, Ra, Shu, Geb, Osiris, Set, Horus, Thoth, and Maat, each with a reign span, followed by the akhu or transfigured spirits and then the Shemsu Hor, the Followers of Horus. Only after that sequence does the papyrus reach Menes and the human kings. This is not a scribal confusion between myth and record. It expresses the Egyptian understanding that kingship was not invented but inherited, an office held by gods before men occupied it, and the compiler encoded that theology as a continuous succession rather than dividing it into legend and history.

### Q: How do scholars rejoin the fragments of the Turin Royal Canon?

Primarily by two methods with very different strengths. The first is physical: a papyrus sheet is built from strips laid at right angles, and the fiber pattern runs continuously across a roll, so fragments that were once adjacent show fiber lines that carry over from one to the other. Gustavus Seyffarth applied this principle as early as 1826 and much of his arrangement survived later revision. The second is textual, placing floating pieces by argument from content, column width, and the summation totals they must satisfy. Fiber matching is evidence. Content placement is interpretation, and it is where reconstructions differ.

### Q: How does the Turin Royal Canon compare with the account of Manetho?

They are the two sources that give both names and durations, and they come from opposite ends of Egyptian literacy: a Ramesside scribe writing hieratic on scrap papyrus around the thirteenth century BCE, and a priest writing Greek prose for a Ptolemaic readership roughly a thousand years later. Manetho gave us the thirty-dynasty framework that structures every modern account, but survives only in quotations by Josephus, Africanus, and Eusebius, whose figures conflict with one another. Where the two agree, confidence rises. Where they disagree, which is often, the choice depends on independent evidence, and the Canon's own groupings do not always match Manetho's dynasty divisions.

### Q: What is written on the back of the Turin Royal Canon papyrus?

The relationship is the reverse of what the question assumes, and the reversal is the interesting part. The king list is itself the back, the verso. The front of the roll, the recto, carries an administrative document: a register of taxes belonging to the reign of Ramesses II. The papyrus was first used for accounting, and once that document had served its purpose the blank reverse became free writing surface, on which someone wrote out the royal succession. Papyrus was expensive and routinely reused this way. The detail matters because nobody writes a ceremonial dedication on scrap, which points to a working reference document rather than a display piece.
