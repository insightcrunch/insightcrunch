The prestige of Romeo and Juliet was not found but made, and the workshop where much of it was manufactured stood in Jena and Vienna, in the person of August Wilhelm Schlegel. Before Schlegel, the play's standing in England rested on the stage: audiences loved it, adapters improved it, and critics apologized for its puns, its haste, and its mixture of clowning with catastrophe. Schlegel reversed the polarity of the whole discussion. In his Vienna lectures on dramatic art and literature, delivered in 1808 and published between 1809 and 1811, he treated the tragedy not as a flawed popular success but as a finished poem whose every scene was necessary, whose contrasts were its structure, and whose mingled mirth was part of its design. The verdict of this article is that Schlegel's reading, carried across Europe by his celebrated German translation of Shakespeare, by Madame de Staël's championship, and by John Black's English translation of the lectures, did more than any other single critical act to convert the play from theatrical property into canonical literature. The reading was not flawless. Its unity is partly a unity of impression rather than of construction, and later critics have shown the seams Schlegel preferred to blend. But the prestige the play now enjoys as a poem, studied whole and quoted as a single sustained utterance on love, descends from the German Romantic reframing in a direct line.

That claim requires the evidence to be laid out in order: the critic himself, his early essay on the play, the Vienna passage where the famous formulations stand, the machinery of antithesis he found in the text, his defence of the play's mixed mode, the system of classical and romantic drama that made the play a piece of European evidence, the translation that naturalized Shakespeare in Germany, and the contested transmission of the whole reading into England. Each stage rests on documents, and the documents reward quotation, because Schlegel's phrases have been repeated so often at second hand that their original force, and their original limits, are easily lost.

A note on the evidence and its handling governs everything that follows. Schlegel's formulations circulate in English almost entirely through John Black's translation of 1815, and the quotations in this article are Black's, checked against the translation's text rather than repeated from the anthologies, because the wording matters: it is Black's "picture of love and its pitiable fate," Black's "apotheosis and obsequies," and Black's "single but endless sigh" that English criticism inherited. Where the briefs and summaries of the reading paraphrase Schlegel into slogans, organic unity above all, this article returns to what the lectures actually say, which is usually more precise and more interesting than the slogan. The German side of the record, the translation of 1797 and its completion under Tieck, is treated with the same documentary care, since the reading and the version were one campaign conducted by the same hands. What emerges is not a legend of Romantic enthusiasm but a chain of dated publications, each of which can be inspected, and a verdict that rests on the chain rather than on the enthusiasm.

## Who Was August Wilhelm Schlegel, and Why Did Europe Listen?

August Wilhelm Schlegel (1767 to 1845) arrived in Vienna with credentials no other living critic could match. He was a poet of the Jena circle that gathered around the journal Athenaeum, which he edited with his younger brother Friedrich between 1798 and 1800; he was the husband, until their divorce, of Caroline Schlegel, the circle's formidable letter writer; he was a philologist who would later hold a chair at Bonn and pioneer Sanskrit studies in Germany; and above all he was Shakespeare's German translator, the man whose verse renderings, appearing from 1797, had given German readers a Shakespeare in their own language of a fidelity and music no earlier version approached. When such a man lectured on dramatic art, the audience heard a practitioner of translation, the most intimate form of reading, and not a theorist speculating from outside the workshop.

The Jena background matters because the reading of the play was formed there a decade before Vienna. The early Romantics of Jena were constructing, in fragments, reviews, and conversations, a new account of what a poem is: not an assembly of parts obedient to external rules, but a living whole that grows from an inward principle and justifies its own form. Friedrich Schlegel's fragments supplied the aphorisms; August Wilhelm supplied the sustained applications, in reviews and essays that took individual works apart scene by scene to show the necessity of their construction. His essay on Goethe's Hermann und Dorothea and his essay on Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet were the showpieces of the method. The second of them is the foundation stone of everything the Vienna lectures later broadcast, and it is treated separately below.

Schlegel's authority also rested on an accident of politics. Attached from 1804 to the household of Madame de Staël as tutor to her children and intellectual companion, he travelled with her through Italy, Germany, and Austria, and reached Vienna in her orbit at the moment when the city, squeezed between Napoleonic France and its own imperial court, was hungry for a cultural argument that was neither French nor servile. The lectures of 1808 offered exactly that: a history of European drama in which the French classical stage, the pride of the occupying power's culture, was demoted to a derivative refinement, while the romantic drama of Spain and England, and above all Shakespeare, was raised as the authentic art of the modern world. The play about the Veronese lovers became, inside that argument, a prime exhibit of what romantic drama could achieve.

### Did Schlegel come to the play as a scholar or as a poet?

He came as both, and the doubleness explains his authority. Schlegel had translated the play into German verse before he lectured on it, had reviewed and excerpted it in the Jena years, and had written on its construction as an essayist. His criticism therefore speaks from inside the workshop of the text, line by line, not from rules outside it.

The personal equipment can be overstated, and the record should be kept plain. Schlegel never saw the play performed in England; his Shakespeare was a reader's and translator's Shakespeare, encountered on the page in the English editions circulating in Germany. That limitation shaped the reading's character in ways this article will keep visible: the Vienna account is a poem's account, built from structure, imagery, and the disposition of scenes, and it has almost nothing to say about actors, audiences, or the economics of the stage. Where the English tradition had judged the play as a performance that sometimes succeeded and sometimes embarrassed, Schlegel judged it as a composition that could be demonstrated, like a proof, to hold together. The shift of tribunal, from playhouse to study, is the first German Romantic contribution, and it precedes any particular claim the lectures make.

## Before Schlegel: The Play's First German Lives

To measure what the German Romantics did, the prior German condition of the play must be stated, because Germany had possessed Romeo and Juliet for decades before Jena, in forms that made Schlegel's intervention necessary. Christoph Martin Wieland's prose translations of Shakespeare, published between 1762 and 1766, gave German readers twenty-two of the plays in serviceable prose, the lovers' tragedy among them; Johann Joachim Eschenburg revised and completed the set in the following decade. Prose was the century's compromise: it carried plot and sentiment across the language border and abandoned verse, wordplay, and song at the frontier. Christian Felix Weiße's Romeo und Julie of 1767 refashioned the story as a bourgeois prose tragedy for German readers and stages, trimming the action toward domestic pathos. Friedrich Wilhelm Gotter turned the tale into a libretto for Georg Benda's opera Romeo und Julie, first performed at Gotha in 1776, where the lovers sang their way toward a conclusion the opera house could applaud. The Hamburg stage under Friedrich Ludwig Schröder made Shakespeare a theatrical fact in Germany from the 1770s, in versions cut, rearranged, and adjusted to the taste of the day.

The pattern across all these lives is consistent, and it is the pattern Schlegel's essay was written to break. The play circulated as a story: affecting, famous, and infinitely reworkable. Its German handlers treated the text as the story's rough container, to be poured into prose, into opera, into bourgeois tragedy, into whatever vessel the moment required. Nobody supposed that the arrangement of scenes, the retention of the clowning, or the survival of the verse could be matters of necessity, because nobody in Germany had yet read the play with the assumption that it was a finished poem. Caroline Schlegel's letters from the Jena years catch the transition in the act: she reports a singer performing nearly the whole of Juliet's part from Gotter's opera, finds little of the original's spirit transferred into it, and contrasts the opera's principled lovers with Shakespeare's Juliet, so young and so sincerely ardent. The circle that would shortly produce the verse translation and the scene-by-scene essay was already measuring the adaptations against the original and finding them wanting.

Schlegel's double intervention of the later 1790s answers the condition exactly. The translation of 1797 restored the verse, and with the verse the play's claim to be poetry rather than narrative. The essay restored the whole, and with the whole the claim that the poetry was designed. Germany did not receive a new play in those years; it received a new ontological status for an old one, and everything in the Vienna lectures of 1808 presupposes that the Jena decade had already performed the conversion on German soil.

## The Young Schlegel's Essay: A Scene-by-Scene Defence Written in Jena

The reading begins as an essay. In the later 1790s, in the Jena period, Schlegel composed an extended examination of Romeo and Juliet that went through the whole sequence of scenes in order, asking of each what it contributed to the whole; the essay was collected in the first volume of the Charakteristiken und Kritiken of 1801, the volume he published with his brother Friedrich. In the Vienna lectures he recalled the performance with unusual directness, and Black's translation preserves the summary: in that essay, he told his hearers, he had gone through all the scenes in their order and demonstrated the inward necessity of each with reference to the whole; he had shown why such a particular circle of characters and relations was placed around the two lovers; he had explained the signification of the mirth scattered here and there, and justified the occasional heightening of the poetical colours.

The conclusion he drew from the exercise is the founding sentence of the play's modern criticism. From the scene-by-scene demonstration, he reported, it seemed to follow unquestionably that nothing could be taken away, nothing added, nothing otherwise arranged, without mutilating and disfiguring the perfect work. Two qualifications keep the sentence honest, and Schlegel himself entered both. The first is the exception he allowed: a few witticisms, grown unintelligible or foreign to later taste as imitations of the social tone of the playwright's day, might be surrendered without loss. The second is the modesty of the method's reach: the essay demonstrated necessity against the adapter's habit of cutting and rearranging, and its target was the stage tradition, running from the Restoration adaptations onward, that treated the play as raw material for improvement. Against a Garrick or an Otway, the claim that nothing could be taken away was a fighting claim, and it was meant as one.

### What exactly did the essay claim could not be removed?

Almost nothing at all. The essay argued that each episode, each surrounding character, and each patch of mirth had a function in the whole, so that removal, addition, or rearrangement would mutilate a perfect work. Its only concession was a handful of dated witticisms, which later taste could surrender without structural loss.

The occasion of the essay belongs to its meaning. Goethe's Wilhelm Meisters Lehrjahre, published in 1795 and 1796, had placed Shakespeare at the centre of the German novel of formation, and Schlegel's 1796 piece on Shakespeare, written on the occasion of Wilhelm Meister, engaged the novel's provocation directly: what is this English dramatist to German culture, and by what criticism shall Germany read him? The Romeo and Juliet essay is the same question answered at the level of a single work. Where the novel mused, the essay demonstrated; where Wilhelm's theatrical mission dissolved in irony, the young critic's scene-by-scene audit stood as earnest method. The Jena Shakespeare, in other words, was from the beginning a reply to Weimar, and the rivalry of the two towns' accounts of the poet, Goethe's reverent appropriation against the Schlegels' analytic championship, runs underneath the whole German reception.

The essay's method deserves emphasis because it was new. Eighteenth-century commentary on the play, in England, had proceeded by beauties and faults: a speech admired, a pun deplored, a character praised, an improbability logged. The Jena essay proceeded by relations. The question was never whether a scene pleased but whether the whole required it, and that question, asked forty times in succession, produced a kind of criticism Europe had not previously applied to a modern play. Roger Paulin, Schlegel's modern biographer and the leading scholar of his criticism, treats the essay as a founding document of analytical and interpretative criticism, the point at which appreciation was replaced by demonstration. That judgement stands: whatever the Vienna lectures added in reach, the method was complete in Jena, and the lectures' famous passage on the play is the essay's conclusion expanded into eloquence.

## Vienna 1808: The Lectures That Put the Play Before Europe

In the spring of 1808 Schlegel delivered the course in Vienna, lecturing in German to a brilliant audience that included Madame de Staël, who was present in the city and who left the most famous eyewitness account of the performance. Her verdict, quoted by John Black in the preface to his translation, captures what the audience felt it was hearing: a critic who treated the finest pieces of poetry as so many wonders of nature, in whose pages the fruit of a whole life's labour was reaped, and whose every opinion and epithet seemed beautiful and just, concise and animated. Staël's account is partial, since Schlegel was her household's ornament and her Germany book a shared campaign, but the course's subsequent career confirms the impression. Published at Heidelberg by Mohr und Zimmer in three volumes between 1809 and 1811 as the Vorlesungen über dramatische Kunst und Litteratur, the lectures were translated into most European languages and became, for a generation, the map on which European readers located the drama of every nation.

The Vienna audience itself was part of the argument's force. Schlegel lectured in German, in the imperial capital, to hearers drawn from the court aristocracy, the diplomatic corps, and the travelling intelligentsia of a continent at war, and the course functioned as a demonstration that German letters could survey all Europe's stages from a position of command. A history of drama that began with Athens and ended with the German prospect for futurity, delivered by Staël's own house critic in a city under French shadow, carried its politics in its table of contents. The play about Verona's lovers was heard, in that room, as evidence in a continental case, and Schlegel pitched its treatment accordingly, as the exhibit on which the romantic drama's vindication would stand or fall.

The course's architecture gave the play its moment. Schlegel begins with first principles, the spirit of criticism and the difference of taste between ancients and moderns; proceeds through the Greek stage, Roman imitation, the Italians, and the French classical theatre, whose unities he dismantles in two full lectures; turns to the Spanish and English stages as the twin homes of romantic drama; devotes Lecture XXII to Shakespeare's age and circumstances, Lecture XXIII to Shakespeare's art in general, and then gives the individual plays their hearings, comedies in Lecture XXIV, tragedies in Lecture XXV, histories in Lecture XXVI. Romeo and Juliet stands at the head of the tragedies lecture, paired with Othello, and the placement is itself a critical act: of all the tragedies, the lovers' play is the one through which Schlegel chooses to define what Shakespearean tragedy is.

That definition opens by stripping away a prejudice. Romeo and Juliet and Othello, he begins, differ from the tragedies already examined neither in their ingredients nor in the manner of treating them; it is merely the direction of the whole that gives them the stamp of tragedy. The sentence quietly abolishes the complaint, inherited from neoclassical criticism, that the lovers' play is a comedy spoiled by its ending or a tragedy contaminated by comic matter. Ingredients do not fix genre; direction does. A play full of feasts, serenades, street wit, and marriage plans is a tragedy if all of it is aimed, from the first scene, at the tomb. With that stroke the mixed mode stops being a fault to be excused and becomes the very thing to be explained.

## What Schlegel Said About Romeo and Juliet in Lecture XXV

The passage that follows is the most quoted paragraph ever written about the play, and it should be read in Black's translation rather than in the loose paraphrases that circulate in its place. Schlegel writes that Romeo and Juliet is a picture of love and its pitiable fate, in a world whose atmosphere is too sharp for this the tenderest blossom of human life. Two beings created for each other feel mutual love at the first glance; every consideration disappears before the irresistible impulse to live in one another; under circumstances hostile in the highest degree to their union, they unite themselves by a secret marriage; untoward incidents follow in rapid succession, and their heroic constancy is within a few days put to the proof, until, forcibly separated, they are by a voluntary death united in the grave. The plot summary is deliberately flat, because Schlegel's point is that the story, however simply told, will always excite a tender sympathy, and that Shakespeare's contribution lies elsewhere: it was reserved for Shakespeare, he continues, to join in one ideal picture purity of heart with warmth of imagination, sweetness and dignity of manners with passionate intensity of feeling.

The peroration builds the sentence on which the play's Romantic reputation rests. Under Shakespeare's handling, Schlegel writes, the story has become a glorious song of praise on that inexpressible feeling which ennobles the soul and gives to it its highest sublimity, and which elevates even the senses into soul, while at the same time it is a melancholy elegy on its inherent and imparted frailty; it is at once the apotheosis and the obsequies of love. Apotheosis and obsequies: deification and funeral rites in a single breath. The formulation endures because it names, exactly, the double sensation the play produces, a love exalted to worship and buried in the same gesture, and because it converts the ending from a regrettable accident of plotting into the completion of the meaning. Death is not what stops the poem; death is the second of its two ceremonies.

### What does the nightingale passage claim that plain argument cannot?

The spring passage claims that the play's unity is sensuous before it is logical. The odour of a southern spring, the nightingale's song, and the first opening of the rose are the play's actual medium, the air in which its contrasts dissolve. Argument can list the oppositions; only the image makes a reader feel them breathed as one air.

The spring passage deserves its sentence of honour. All that is most intoxicating in the odour of a southern spring, all that is languishing in the song of the nightingale, or voluptuous in the first opening of the rose, all alike breathe forth from this poem. But the blossoming is already blighted in the telling: more rapidly than the earliest blossoms of youth and beauty decay, the play hurries from the first timidly bold declaration and modest return of love to the most unlimited passion and an irrevocable union, and then hastens, amid alternating storms of rapture and despair, to the fate of the two lovers, who appear enviable in their hard lot because their love survives them and by their death they have obtained an endless triumph over every separating power. Critics who quote the spring sentence without the haste that follows it misrepresent their author: Schlegel's play is not a static idyll but a race, and its beauty is the beauty of speed toward destruction, the dawn whose purple clouds, as he says a paragraph later, already announce the thunder of a sultry day.

The dawn sentence that follows the spring passage should be quoted whole, because it corrects the idyll in advance. If Romeo and Juliet, Schlegel continues, shines with the colours of the dawn of morning, it is a dawn whose purple clouds already announce the thunder of a sultry day; Othello, by contrast, is a strongly shaded picture, a tragical Rembrandt. The pictorial method is characteristic: Schlegel thinks in light. The lovers' play is morning light with weather in it, gold and rose darkening at the edges, and the image silently organizes everything a reader knows of the text, the dawn serenade of the aubade in 3.5, where the lark and the nightingale dispute over a sky already brightening toward separation, the torches of the feast against Verona's noon, the lantern Romeo calls the tomb's light in 5.3. Criticism that can find the picture a play would hang in has done half the work of interpretation before it argues a single point, and Schlegel's pictures are why his formulations outlast his system.

## Unity of Impression: The Antithesis Machinery Schlegel Found in the Play

The passage's analytical core is its catalogue of contrasts. The sweetest and the bitterest, love and hatred, festive rejoicings and dark forebodings, tender embraces and sepulchral horrors, the fulness of life and self-annihilation, are here all brought close to each other; and yet these contrasts are so blended into a unity of impression, that the echo which the whole leaves behind in the mind resembles a single but endless sigh. Every term in the catalogue can be verified against the text. Love and hatred share the play's first scene, where the servants' brawl interrupts a sonnetising lover; festive rejoicings and dark forebodings share the feast of 1.5, where Juliet, dancing toward her marriage, already asks her nurse to identify the stranger and adds, in an aside the audience cannot unhear, that if he is married her grave is like to be her wedding bed (1.5); tender embraces and sepulchral horrors share the potion speech of 4.3, where the bride rehearses the vault's horrors in the very chamber of her wedding preparations; the fulness of life and self-annihilation meet in Romeo's apothecary scene and in Juliet's last line, when she finds the dagger happy and lets her body be its sheath (5.3). Schlegel's catalogue is not decoration. It is a table of the play's load-bearing oppositions, each pair placed by the dramatist within a single scene or a single speech.

The key term is the one translators and quoters most often flatten: unity of impression. Schlegel does not claim that the play observes the unities of time, place, or action in the French theorists' sense; he had spent two lectures dismantling those as mechanical rules externally imposed. The unity he claims is registered in the receiving mind, an echo that behaves like a single sigh although its materials are contraries. This is the organic doctrine applied with precision: the form is organic not because the parts resemble each other but because they grow into one effect, as the organs of a living body, unlike in shape and function, compose one life. The sigh is Schlegel's unit of measurement, and it is chosen with care, since a sigh is involuntary, indivisible, and compounded of pleasure and grief at once, which is exactly the sensation the catalogue predicts.

The catalogue's pairs also map the play's public architecture, which Schlegel's summary names in passing and his system requires. Love and hatred are not private weather in Verona; they are civic institutions, housed in two families, policed by a prince whose opening edict prices the next brawl at the brawlers' lives (1.1) and whose closing judgement prices the feud at the heirs of both houses (5.3). Paris stands inside the structure as the sanctioned alternative, the marriage the world's machinery approves, and his death at the tomb, killed by the outlaw he tried to arrest, completes the pattern by which every separating power is finally gathered into the grave it made. When Capulet takes Montague's hand over the bodies and calls the lovers poor sacrifices of their fathers' enmity (5.3), the antithesis machine stops, because its two terms have consumed each other. Schlegel's sigh is the sound of that stopping, and his catalogue, read against the frame, is exact: the contrasts are public before they are lyrical, and their blending is the city's tragedy as much as the lovers'.

Against this account stands the strongest modern objection, and it must be faced rather than waved past. Susan Snyder's study of the comic matrix in Shakespeare's tragedies demonstrated that the play's first movement is not merely coloured by comedy but built from comedy's actual machinery: the blocking father, the witty servant class, the young lover sighing for an unattainable mistress, the bawd-like nurse, the secret marriage that in comedy would resolve the plot. In a comedy those devices produce a wedding; here the wedding is real and the machinery keeps running past it into catastrophe, so that the tragedy is, structurally, a comedy that has missed its exit. Snyder's reading does not refute Schlegel so much as it localizes his unity. The seam between the comic structure and the tragic outcome is real, and the play's power partly depends on the audience feeling the seam tear. But Snyder's mechanism and Schlegel's impression describe the same event from opposite ends: the machinery is comic, the direction is tragic, and the blending Schlegel named is the experience of both truths at once. Schlegel himself had built the reconciliation into his opening sentence, where the ingredients are comic and the direction gives the stamp of tragedy. On mechanism, Snyder wins; on the achieved effect, Schlegel's sigh still stands as the accurate report.

## First Among the Tragedies: The Company the Lecture Keeps

The ranking implied by Lecture XXV deserves a moment's isolation, because rankings are how lectures manufacture prestige. Schlegel's tragedies lecture opens with the lovers and Othello, proceeds to Hamlet as a tragedy of thought, and moves onward through the great sequence; but the order of exposition is not neutral. By defining Shakespearean tragedy through the lovers' play first, Schlegel fixes lyric intensity, not political or metaphysical scope, as the kind's essence, and every subsequent tragedy in the lecture is measured against a standard set by a pair of teenagers in Verona. The pairings reinforce the elevation. Othello is set beside the lovers' tragedy as its shaded counterpart, dawn against a tragical Rembrandt, and Hamlet follows as the tragedy whose hero thinks where Romeo and Juliet feel. A reader who absorbs the lecture's architecture has learned, before any argument is examined, that the lovers' play is one of the three or four summits of the canon and the purest instance of tragedy's emotional kind. Rankings of this sort, delivered from a European lectern and reprinted for a century, are the machinery by which canons acquire their apparently natural contours.

The ranking had to survive comparison with the English settlement it displaced, and the comparison is instructive. The eighteenth-century English canon had placed the lovers' tragedy high in affection and middling in dignity: a pleasing performance, in Johnson's phrase of qualified warmth, perpetually apologized for in the particulars. The German ranking inverted the relation between affection and dignity, making the play's very popularity, its immediacy of feeling, into the ground of its dignity, since the feeling was demonstrably engineered by a perfect construction. When the nineteenth-century English canon settled the play among the supreme tragedies, studied in schools and edited with the apparatus once reserved for the histories and Hamlet, the contours it settled into were the Vienna contours. Prestige is path-dependent: the order in which a lecture in 1808 chose to present the tragedies is still legible in the order in which the modern world esteems them, and no honest history of the play's reputation can treat that legibility as coincidence.

## Fate or Accident: What Schlegel Does with the Machinery of Mishap

One test of the reading has been reserved until now because it separates Schlegel's eloquence from his analysis. The catastrophe of the play is engineered from accidents: a letter stopped by plague quarantine in Mantua (5.2), a bridegroom's servant who outruns the truth (5.1), a friar who arrives at the tomb minutes too late and flees at the sound of the watch (5.3). The older English criticism had treated this machinery as the play's weakness, a plot that kills its lovers by postal failure. Schlegel's passage meets the machinery head-on and re-describes it. In his summary the lovers act under circumstances hostile in the highest degree to their union, and untoward incidents follow in rapid succession, putting their heroic constancy within a few days to the proof. The accidents are not apologized for; they are promoted into the meaning, the sharpness of the world's atmosphere made mechanical. His phrase for the elegy's subject, love's inherent and imparted frailty, does the theoretical work in two words: the frailty is inherent, seated in the lovers' haste and secrecy, and imparted, delivered by a world whose institutions, feud, forced marriage, plague, and watch, convert every mishap into a fatality.

The re-description succeeds as far as the impression reaches, and its limit can be marked exactly. Schlegel's account explains why the accidents feel inevitable in retrospect: each one strikes a structure already loaded, a secret marriage, a banished husband, a sleeping bride, so that chance operates only where preparation has given it leverage, which is the Friar's herbs argument extended to the plot's whole engineering. What the account does not do is acquit the plotting at the level of the single joint. Friar John's quarantine speech, however thematically convenient, remains a reported accident doing causal work that no amount of atmosphere quite naturalizes, and the criticism that has worried the joint since Schlegel is worrying a real joint. The verdict that fits both truths is Schlegel's own distinction restated: at the level of impression the machinery is fate, and the sigh at the end registers it as fate; at the level of construction the machinery is a chain of contingencies whose links a reader can test one by one. A reading that confuses the two levels, in either direction, misreads the play, and Schlegel, who founded the first level's criticism, is not always innocent of the confusion. His greatness lies in having shown that the first level exists and governs the experience; his limit lies in sometimes writing as if it abolished the second.

## The Circle Around the Lovers: The Removal Test Applied

Schlegel's summary of his essay contains one clause that deserves a section of its own, because it identifies the essay's most original perception: he had shown, he said, why such a particular circle of characters and relations was placed around the two lovers. The claim is that the supporting cast is a designed system, each figure holding a position in a geometry of pressures around the central pair, and the removal test can be run on the circle figure by figure to see whether the geometry holds. Begin with Mercutio. He is the world's brilliance incarnate, its wit, aggression, and erotic knowingness raised to genius, and he must die at the exact hinge of the play, killed under Romeo's arm in 3.1, because while Mercutio lives the world of the feud is still charming and the comedy of the streets still governs the play's weather. His death is the removal test's strongest exhibit in reverse: remove him earlier and the tragedy loses its hinge; keep him longer and the catastrophe cannot darken. Schlegel's circle explains his position as the point where the mirth he defended is converted, by a single thrust, into the feud's first irreversible payment.

The Nurse occupies the opposite station. She is love's earthly parody and its indispensable instrument, the go-between whose bawdy, garrulous, time-serving nature measures everything the lovers are not, and whose counsel in 3.5, advising Juliet to marry Paris and forget the banished husband, marks the moment the world's wisdom formally deserts the heroine. Remove her and the play loses its channel between the great house and the street, its clock of Juliet's childhood, and its proof that the ordinary world's love is negotiation; the geometry requires a figure through whom affection itself becomes unreliable. Friar Laurence stands at the circle's third station as counsel and failed providence: the man whose herbs Schlegel made the emblem of planted design is also the man whose plan, prudent at every step, is defeated by the accumulation of the mishaps his prudence could not govern, and whose flight from the tomb in 5.3 completes the pattern by which every mediator in the play, priest, nurse, prince, and parent, arrives too late or leaves too early. The circle is a ring of failed mediation around a love that can mediate only itself.

Paris and the parents complete the figure. Paris is the legitimate alternative, the husband the world's forms approve, handsome, wealthy, well connected, and fatally external to the heroine's inner life; his presence converts a private passion into a public impossibility, and his death at the tomb draws the sanctioned order itself into the sacrifice. The two fathers embody the feud as inheritance rather than choice, old men whose enmity their children did not make and cannot unmake, and whose reconciliation over the bodies in 5.3 closes the geometry with terrible economy: the circle's outermost figures purchase peace at the price the innermost pair has paid. The Prince frames the whole as law, opening the play with an edict and closing it with a judgement, his own kinsman Mercutio among the dead, his authority confessedly arrived too late. Run the removal test across the ring and Schlegel's claim survives the audit well: each station is load-bearing, and the few figures who might be spared, a Peter, a musician, an apothecary's outline, belong to the small change the essay's own exception anticipates. The circle argument remains the essay's most teachable legacy, the ancestor of every classroom diagram that draws the play as a system of pressures rather than a sequence of events.

## Berlin Before Vienna: The Lecturer's Apprenticeship

Vienna was a summit, not a start. Schlegel had lectured in Berlin in the first years of the century, on literature and the fine arts, before audiences that included the Prussian court's intellectual world, and the Berlin courses were the workshop in which the Vienna method was assembled: the survey of whole literatures, the judgement of works by their inner law, the polemical demotion of French classicism. The Jena criticism had been written for journals and for the circle; Berlin taught the critic to hold a room, to build an argument across an evening, to let a peroration gather the force of an hour's analysis. The Vienna course's famous passages bear the marks of that training. The paragraph on the lovers is not an essay paragraph transferred to a lectern; it is built as a spoken crescendo, its balanced clauses and mounting catalogue designed for the ear of a hall, and Black's translation preserves the oratory in its periods. Staël's astonishment at hearing a critic as eloquent as an orator records the Berlin apprenticeship reaching its Viennese effect.

The apprenticeship matters to the play's history for a further reason. Criticism addressed to a room behaves differently from criticism addressed to a page: it must decide, rank, and perorate, and it cannot hide behind the review's cautious plurals. The Vienna lectures' treatment of the lovers' tragedy is magisterial because the form required magisterium, and the European reception took the reading up in the form in which it was performed, as a judgement delivered from a height. Later scholarship has sometimes regretted the height, preferring its Schlegel tentative and documentary; but the prestige the reading manufactured was a function of its certainty, and the certainty was a lecturer's, practised in Berlin and perfected in Vienna. The play's modern reputation, which presents its unity and its poetry as settled facts rather than as arguable propositions, descends from that lectern manner as much as from any argument the lectern delivered.

## Schlegel Against the Charge of Mixture: Defending Comedy Inside Tragedy

The mixture of tragic and comic was the standing charge against the play in the criticism Schlegel inherited, and Lecture XXIII, the general lecture on Shakespeare's art, contains his systematic answer, of which the Romeo and Juliet passage is the application. His doctrine is that the mixture is not a lapse from purity but the signature of romantic drama, which aims at the fulness of life rather than at the separation of kinds. The mirth scattered through the lovers' tragedy is justified, in the essay's phrase, by its signification: the wit-combats of Mercutio and the nurse's garrulity are not relief pasted onto a serious action but the medium in which the lovers' intensity becomes measurable. Mercutio's Queen Mab speech (1.4) sets the dream-world of appetite and fancy spinning at exactly the moment Romeo is walking, heavy with a foreboding he cannot name, toward the feast where his life will change; the laughter is the sound of the world the lovers are about to leave. The nurse's interminable anecdote in 1.3, with its bawdy punchline about falling backward, performs the same office for Juliet, measuring by contrast the speed and purity of the girl who within two acts will be bargaining for a potion. Remove the mirth, and the love has nothing to outshine.

It is worth placing Samuel Johnson in this argument's genealogy, because England's greatest critic had reached the mixture's defence by the opposite road a generation earlier. Johnson's Preface of 1765 had ruled that Shakespeare's plays are not, in the rigorous and critical sense, either tragedies or comedies, but compositions of a distinct kind, and had justified the mingled drama by its fidelity to the real state of sublunary nature, where good and evil, joy and sorrow, are mingled in endless variety. Johnson's defence is pragmatic: the mixture is excused because life is mixed. Schlegel's defence is constitutive: the mixture is required because romantic art aims at the whole of life under one impression. The difference is the whole distance between the eighteenth century and the Romantic settlement, and the play's criticism still moves between the two defences, Johnson's apology and Schlegel's theory, usually without knowing their names.

The defence has a limit, and Schlegel drew it himself. The essay's exception of a few witticisms, unintelligible or foreign to later taste, concedes that the punning exchanges, especially the flyting of 2.4, carry period matter that no doctrine of necessity can wholly redeem. The concession matters to the argument's honesty: a critic who defends everything defends nothing, and Schlegel's willingness to surrender the small change of Elizabethan wit is what gives his defence of the large structure its credit. Later criticism has mostly honoured the boundary he drew. Editors keep the puns and annotate them; directors cut them and are accused, in Schlegel's exact terms, of mutilation. The argument has never really been settled because Schlegel stated its terms so well that both sides still plead in them.

### Where does Schlegel's defence of the wordplay stop?

It stops at the dated witticism. Schlegel justified the mirth that carries signification, the Queen Mab flight and the nurse's anecdotes, as structural necessities, but he surrendered a few pleasantries that imitated the social tone of the playwright's day and had grown unintelligible or foreign to taste. That concession is load-bearing: it proves the defence an audit, not a reflex.

The genre question underneath the mixture charge has its own literature, and readers who want the structural case in full should turn to [the genre problem of a comedy that turns tragic](/2022/02/25/romeo-and-juliet-genre-comedy-to-tragedy/), where the comic machinery and its derailment are traced scene by scene. Schlegel's contribution to that question is the criterion, not the inventory: he taught criticism to ask what the whole is doing with its mixed materials before condemning the mixture, and that reordering of the question, from ingredients to direction, is the move every subsequent genre discussion of the play has either adopted or been forced to answer.

## The Friar's Herbs: Schlegel's Proof of a Planted Design

Schlegel knew that a claim of total design needs a specimen, and in Lecture XXV he supplied one, chosen with a controversialist's cunning. The most striking and perhaps incredible circumstance in the whole story, he observes, is the liquor given by the monk to Juliet, by which she for a number of hours not merely sleeps but fully resembles a corpse, without receiving the least injury. A hostile critic would call the potion a romancer's convenience, the least probable device in the plot. Schlegel turns the attack inside out by asking how the poet disposes an audience to believe that Friar Laurence possesses such a secret, and answering from the text: at his first appearance the Friar is exhibited in a garden, collecting herbs and descanting on their wonderful virtues, in a discourse full of deep meaning, in which the pious old man sees everywhere in nature emblems of the moral world. In this manner, Schlegel concludes, a circumstance of an ungrateful appearance has become the source of a great beauty.

The passage he is reading is the Friar's entrance at 2.3, gathering his basket of baleful weeds and precious juiced flowers, and meditating that within the infant rind of one weak flower poison has residence and medicine power in the same small body. The speech does everything Schlegel claims for it. It establishes the Friar's competence two full acts before the potion is needed, so that the device of 4.1 arrives as the application of a known art rather than a contrivance produced on demand. It states the play's master antithesis in botanical form, poison and medicine in one rind, grace and rude will in one breast, so that the potion itself becomes an instance of the doctrine rather than an exception to probability. And it characterizes the play's most dangerous counsellor at his wisest moment, in solitude and at dawn, which is what makes his later panic in the tomb, when he flees the watch and abandons Juliet, a failure of nerve rather than of knowledge. Schlegel's specimen is well chosen: if the preparation argument works anywhere, it works here, and his critics from that day to this have generally conceded the point while contesting its extension to the whole.

The concession should be recorded precisely, because the article's verdict depends on the difference between what the specimen proves and what Schlegel built on it. The Friar's herbs prove planted design at the level of the motif: an object introduced early, glossed philosophically, and discharged late. The essay's larger claim, that every scene bears the same necessity, is an extrapolation from such specimens, argued by accumulation rather than demonstrated scene by scene in the lectures themselves, whatever the Jena essay had attempted. Modern criticism accepts the motif argument without reserve; it is now the ordinary language of Shakespearean craftsmanship. The total claim remains an act of critical faith, though a productive one, since generations of readers sent back to the text to test it have kept finding the preparations Schlegel promised them, the earth-treading stars of the Prologue answered by the tomb's lantern, the wedding bed and the grave exchanging images from the first act onward.

## The Adapter Answered: Otway's Caius Marius and the Crime of Rearrangement

Schlegel's doctrine had a criminal case to prosecute, and in the later lectures he prosecuted it by name. Thomas Otway's History and Fall of Caius Marius, first staged in 1679, had transplanted the lovers into Roman history, lifting whole scenes from Shakespeare's play, and for decades the English stage had often known the story in Otway's Roman dress rather than in Shakespeare's Veronese one. Schlegel's sentence on the transaction, in Black's translation, is the coldest in the course: how little Otway understood the true rules of composition may be inferred from this, that he has taken half the scenes of his Caius Marius verbally, or with disfiguring changes, from the Romeo and Juliet of Shakespeare; nothing more incongruous can be conceived than such an episode in Roman manners and in a historical drama; and the impudent plagiarism is in no manner justified by the adapter's confessing it.

The contempt is doctrinal, and its logic deserves extraction, because it is the essay's removal test enforced against history. If the scenes of the lovers' tragedy could be lifted entire into a Roman history, then either the scenes were never necessary to their own whole, or the lifting destroyed the necessity it stole. Schlegel's answer is the second: in Otway the scenes persist as eloquence without function, affecting episodes bolted to a foreign action, and their incongruity in Roman manners is the proof that in Verona they were not decoration but organism. The adapter's century had assumed that a play's beauties were portable property; Schlegel's prosecution asserts that the beauties are positions in a structure, and that what can be stolen has already been killed by the stealing. Garrick's celebrated eighteenth-century version, which cut the Rosaline matter, softened the language, and kept the tomb scene's dialogue between the dying lovers that Garrick added, stands under the same judgement without being named: every improvement is a small Caius Marius, a rearrangement that mutilates while it polishes. The modern stage and the modern edition, which begin from the text whole and argue about interpretation afterward, are living under Schlegel's law in this matter, whether or not they have read his sentence on Otway.

## The Verse Itself: Schlegel on Rhyme, Sonnets, and the Glowing Colour

The lectures attend, as a translator's lectures should, to the play's verse as a carrier of meaning, and the general lecture on Shakespeare's language and versification contains the observation on which the lovers' play's lyricism is theorized. Of some pieces, Schlegel remarks, the rhymes form a considerable part: of the Midsummer Night's Dream and of Romeo and Juliet he says the rhyme is there either because the poet wished to give them a glowing colour, or because the characters appropriately utter in a more musical tone their complaints or suits of love; and in these cases Shakespeare has even introduced rhymed strophes approaching the form of the sonnet, then usual in England. Glowing colour is the exact term for what the play's rhyming does. The Prologue speaks in a sonnet because the story enters under the sign of the love poem; the Chorus of the second act speaks in another; and Romeo and Juliet, meeting at the feast, complete a perfect sonnet between them, his pilgrim's hand and her saint's palm composing fourteen lines in two voices (1.5), so that the form of courtly lyric is shared into existence between two voices at the moment of falling in love. A criticism trained on the French alexandrine could only hear the rhyme as ornament or as primitivism; Schlegel hears it as temperature, the glow by which the play's spring announces itself in the ear.

The point extends beyond rhyme. The play's verse keeps breaking into set lyric forms at its emotional summits, the aubade's stichomythia at dawn in 3.5, Juliet's formal invocation to the night in 3.2, the couplets that snap scenes shut like lids, and Schlegel's account of versification, which treats metre as the poet's chosen atmosphere rather than as a counting of syllables, gave later criticism its warrant for reading those forms as thought. The translator had sweated over every one of them: to render the shared sonnet into German, keeping its form while the pilgrims' imagery crossed the language, is to learn in the hands what the lecture states in the abstract, that in this play the lyric forms are not interruptions of the drama but the drama's native weather. When later editors and critics mapped the play's sonnet culture and its Petrarchan inheritance, they were working a claim Schlegel had filed, in passing, in a lecture on versification.

## Classical and Romantic: The System That Turned the Play into Evidence

The reading of the single play sits inside a general theory, and the theory is what carried the reading across Europe. Lecture I of the Vienna course fixes the terms. Classical art, in Schlegel's account, is the art of the ancient world: finite, self-contained, harmonious, its forms perfected within clear limits, its religion a worship of nature and of this life. Romantic art is the art of the Christian and chivalric centuries and of the modern nations that inherit them: yearning rather than satisfied, mingled rather than pure, forever suggesting an infinite beyond the form it achieves. The French classical stage, on this map, is a late and narrowed imitation of the ancients, preserving their rules while losing their spirit. The Spanish drama of Lope and Calderón and the English drama of Shakespeare are romantic art in its fullness, and their mixtures, their haste, their ghosts and gravediggers, are not barbarisms to be pardoned but the necessary expression of a different and larger conception of life.

Romeo and Juliet functions in the system as a limiting case, and limiting cases are the ones that prove systems. If a play saturated with romantic mixture, written at speed, full of rhyme, wordplay, clowning, and a plot borrowed from an Italian novella, can be shown to possess unity, design, and profundity, then the classical criteria by which such plays had been judged faulty stand refuted at their strongest point of application. That is why Schlegel lavishes his most famous eloquence on this play rather than on Hamlet or Lear: the lovers' tragedy is the exhibit on which the whole antithesis turns. René Wellek, the great comparatist historian of criticism, entered the necessary caution against this machinery. The classical and romantic antithesis, Wellek showed, was not Schlegel's invention from nothing; versions of it circulated through the period's criticism, in Friedrich Schlegel's fragments, in Staël's earlier writings on literature, and in the general European revolt against French rules. Schlegel systematized and broadcast a distinction his age was already minting. The caution is just, and it qualifies the originality of the system; it does not touch the originality of the application, which was Schlegel's own, worked out on this play in Jena a decade before the system had a name.

### Is the play's unity found in the text or in the impression it leaves?

His answer is the impression, and the distinction is his sophistication, not his evasion. He does not claim the play obeys mechanical unities; he claims its contraries blend into a unity of impression, an echo like a single endless sigh. The text supplies oppositions; the unity happens where they are received, in a mind holding feast and vault together.

The system's afterlife in the play's criticism can be traced in the way later writers inherit the map while discarding the metaphysics. The proposition that the play must be judged by laws drawn from its own nature, rather than by rules imported from another literature, passes from Schlegel into Coleridge's lectures, into Hazlitt's Characters, into the Victorian character criticism of Anna Jameson, and eventually into the common sense of modern scholarship, which no longer asks whether the play may mix its kinds but only how the mixture works. Systems are usually refuted; Schlegel's was absorbed. That absorption is the surest measure of the German Romantic contribution, and it is also why the contribution is so often invisible: the air modern readers breathe while reading the play was first bottled in Vienna.

## Romeo und Julia: The Translation That Made Shakespeare German

Criticism persuaded Europe's reviewers; the translation persuaded Germany's readers, and in the long run readers matter more. Between 1797 and 1801 Schlegel published German verse translations of seventeen of Shakespeare's plays, Romeo und Julia among the very first to appear, in the opening volume of 1797. The achievement was methodological before it was poetic. Earlier German Shakespeare had come largely in prose, serviceable for the plot and dead at the level of the line. Schlegel resolved to reproduce the original's forms: blank verse for blank verse, rhyme for rhyme, song for song, prose for prose, with the metre carried over line by line so that a German reader met not a summary of the play but the play's own gait. The lovers who in prose had sounded like a translated novella now spoke in a German verse supple enough for the sonnet embedded in the feast scene and swift enough for the street fights, and a generation of German poets, Goethe among them, treated the versions as events in German literature itself.

The translation bears directly on the play's elevation. A work that exists in a nation's language as a classic of that language acquires a second citizenship, and Romeo und Julia acquired German citizenship in 1797, a decade before the Vienna lectures explained to Europe why the play deserved its passport. The sequence matters for the verdict this article is building. The lectures' reading did not create German devotion to the play from nothing; the translation had already done the intimate work, household by household and theatre by theatre, and the lectures then supplied the theory that told Germany, and after Germany the rest of the Continent, what its devotion meant. German stages took the Schlegel text as their playing text, German quotation absorbed its phrasings, and the German critical tradition that runs through the nineteenth century reads the play, whether it knows it or not, through Schlegel's German before it reads it through Shakespeare's English.

### Why did the translation matter more inside Germany than the lectures?

Because the lectures argued for a devotion the translation had already created. The Vienna course addressed critics and men of letters; the 1797 Romeo und Julia and its sixteen companions entered German homes, schools, and theatres, and were quoted as the play itself. Theory crowned the fact; the fact was made in the workshop of translation, line by line.

One further point belongs to the accuracy of the record. Schlegel translated the play as a young man of the Jena circle, in the same years when the essay on its construction was forming, and the two labours fed each other in ways the documents let a reader watch. Translation at Schlegel's standard is criticism conducted at the level of the syllable: every line forces a decision about tone, register, and relation to the whole, and a translator who has made ten thousand such decisions possesses an intimacy with the text that no reviewer can match. When the lecturer of 1808 asserted that nothing in the play could be rearranged without loss, part of his warrant was that he had tried, in the only laboratory where the experiment is possible, and had found the arrangement load-bearing in his own language.

The craft of the version repays a closer look, because the lovers' play is the severest test in the canon for a verse translator. Its registers run from the servants' prose bickering through Mercutio's improvisations, the nurse's prose garrulity, the lovers' rhymed lyricism, and the Friar's sententious blank verse, often within a single act. A prose translation flattens those registers into one narrative voice, which is why the Wieland tradition, for all its services, could not make the play a poem in German. Schlegel's rule of formal correspondence preserved the map of registers: where Shakespeare writes prose, the German is prose; where the verse tightens into rhyme at the lovers' meeting and the dawn parting, the German tightens with it; where a sonnet is embedded, a sonnet stands. The consequence for the play's German life was that readers encountered the same stratification of tone that English readers meet, and German critics could therefore argue about register, decorum, and lyric intensity on evidence, as Schlegel's own lectures proceed to do. The verse section of this article has shown the theory; the translation is the theory's proof of concept, and the two together explain why the German Romantic reading, alone among the period's national receptions, rested on a text its audience could actually inspect. A reading that can be checked against the work in the reader's own language has a kind of authority no enthusiasm can counterfeit, and Schlegel, uniquely among the play's champions, had manufactured both the reading and the checkable text in the same decade.

## Completing the Work: Tieck, the Household Workshop, and Unser Shakespeare

Schlegel's seventeen plays were a beginning, not a canon, and the completion of the German Shakespeare became the German Romantics' collective monument. Ludwig Tieck, the circle's other presiding intelligence, novelist, dramatist, and the most theatrically minded of the group, took up the enterprise as editor in the 1820s; the remaining plays were translated under his direction, chiefly by Count Wolf von Baudissin and by Tieck's daughter Dorothea, whose share of the labour the title pages long concealed, and the finished set appeared between 1825 and 1833 as the Shakespeare of Schlegel and Tieck. The brand name is itself a piece of criticism: Europe's most influential Shakespeare bore two Romantics' names on its spine, and German readers who said unser Shakespeare, our Shakespeare, were claiming a naturalization the translation had performed.

Tieck's own criticism of the play deserves its place in the record, since the German championship was a circle's work and not a solo. Tieck had written on Shakespeare from the 1790s, championed the early plays and the theatrical energy the neoclassical taste despised, and brought to the lovers' tragedy a stage practitioner's interest that complements Schlegel's readerly account: where Schlegel demonstrates the poem's necessity, Tieck attends to its life in performance, its youth, its night scenes, its fitness for the boards. Friedrich Schlegel, the younger brother, contributed the philosophical frame, the fragments on romantic poetry as a progressive universal art, forever becoming and never perfected, within which a mixed, yearning, lyrical tragedy could be prized precisely for the qualities the classical standard condemned. Caroline Schlegel, later Caroline Schelling, whose letters remain the circle's sharpest informal criticism, wrote about the play with a discriminating warmth the published record long undervalued, preferring the lovers' ardour to the moralized sentimentality of the French Julie and her Saint-Preux. The German Romantic reading, in short, was composed by a household of talents around one central performance, and Schlegel's was the central performance because his was the only one joined to a translation.

The consequence for the play was a change of address. In the German lands the lovers ceased to be an English theatrical property discussed by specialists and became a possession of the general educated public, read in schools, quoted in letters, set by composers, and treated as a standard by which other love poetry was measured. When later German scholarship produced its great editions and its Shakespeare Society, the institution-building rested on a readership the Romantics had manufactured. No other European nation naturalized the play so completely in the same decades, and the difference is traceable, document by document, to the Jena and Vienna campaign.

The monument was contested inside Germany, and the contest confirms its scale. The Voss family, Johann Heinrich Voss and his sons, produced a rival complete Shakespeare between 1818 and 1829 whose governing virtue was literal fidelity, and German criticism argued the two versions against each other for decades: Schlegel's poetry against the Voss school's accuracy, fluency against the letter. The lovers' tragedy stood in the front of that quarrel, since its lyric passages are precisely where fidelity and music pull hardest apart. That the Schlegel text held the stage and the schoolroom through the quarrel settled the practical question, while the theoretical one, what a translation owes its original, had been given its permanent German form. Dorothea Tieck's share in the completed canon, long absorbed into her father's and Baudissin's names, has been restored to view by later scholarship, and the restoration belongs to the same process: the German Shakespeare was a household manufacture, and its history keeps yielding the names of its makers.

## Across the Channel: Staël, Black's Translation, and the English Reception

The reading's return journey to the play's own country ran through three carriers. The first was Madame de Staël herself. Her De l'Allemagne, the great presentation of German thought to the French and English worlds, was printed in Paris in 1810 and destroyed by Napoleon's police for its refusal to flatter the empire; published in London in 1813, it became the channel through which Schlegel's literary system, summarized and dramatized by its most brilliant popularizer, reached readers who would never open the German lectures. Staël's account of the Vienna course, quoted in Black's preface, is advertising of a high order, but the advertisement carried the goods: the classical and romantic map, the rehabilitation of mixture, and the estimate of Shakespeare as the romantic dramatist entire.

The second carrier was John Black's English translation of the lectures themselves, published in London in 1815 as A Course of Lectures on Dramatic Art and Literature. Black, a Scottish journalist and editor, rendered Schlegel into an English vigorous enough to be quoted, and quoted it has been, ever since; the formulations examined in this article, the picture of love, the apotheosis and the obsequies, the single but endless sigh, are Black's Englishings, and their currency in English criticism is a translator's achievement layered on a translator's. English readers who believed they were receiving Schlegel pure were receiving him, fittingly, in the same mediated way German readers had received Shakespeare: through a version good enough to stand as a classic beside its original.

The printed lectures travelled on their own as well. Within the decade of their German publication the course had been rendered into French and was on its way into the other literary languages of the Continent, so that critics from Paris to Milan encountered the classical and romantic map, and the lovers' play enthroned upon it, in Schlegel's own extended form and not only in Staël's summary. The two channels reinforced each other: the book gave the argument, the salon and the review gave the argument an audience, and by the time Black addressed the English reader in 1815 the lectures carried the momentum of a European reputation already made.

The third carrier was the periodical press and the lecture room, where the reading arrived not as a book but as a climate. Henry Crabb Robinson, the indefatigable diarist who had studied in Germany and knew the Jena world, reported and recommended Schlegel to his London circle years before Black's volumes appeared, and the ideas moved through conversation faster than through print. By the time the translation was available, English criticism was already rehearsing its arguments, sometimes with acknowledgment and sometimes without, which brings the record to its most contested transaction.

## France Takes the Banner: Stendhal and the Romantic War

France's prior possession of the play explains the resistance and the eventual conversion alike. The French eighteenth century had known the lovers through Jean-François Ducis's adaptation of 1772, which refitted the tragedy to French taste and French rules, and through the prose translations of Pierre Le Tourneur, whose complete Shakespeare of the later 1770s gave French readers the plays as literature while confirming, by its prose, that their poetry was an English barbarism to be survived rather than a form to be admired. A nation that met the play in prose and in Ducis's tailoring had every institutional reason to receive Schlegel's Vienna doctrine as an insult, and the Parisian journals' hostility, which Black's preface savours, was the sound of a critical establishment defending its jurisdiction. That the jurisdiction fell within two generations, and that France ended the century as Shakespeare's second European home, measures the doctrine's carrying power better than any German success: the reading conquered the capital of the system it was built to destroy.

The reading's continental career has a French chapter that the English transmission should not be allowed to eclipse. France was the enemy territory of Schlegel's map, the home of the classical stage his lectures demoted, and the conquest of France was therefore the system's proof of concept. It came a generation late and by manifesto. Stendhal's Racine et Shakespeare, published in 1823 and enlarged in 1825, took up the romantic cause for the French theatre with Shakespeare as its standard, arguing in the post-Schlegel climate that the national drama must choose between the unities of Racine and the living mixture of the English stage. The lovers' tragedy figures in that quarrel as it had figured in Vienna, as the lyrical exhibit: the play whose beauties no French rule could license and no French audience, once admitted to it, could surrender. When the French Romantics of the following years fought their theatre battles, the critical charter they fought under had been drafted, in its essentials, in the Vienna lectures, and Victor Hugo's later championship of Shakespeare, crowned by his book on the poet in 1864, completed the occupation. The play's European reputation, which the cliche treats as the spontaneous verdict of mankind, can be followed through these campaigns like a dispatch route: Jena to Vienna, Vienna to Paris and London, and from the capitals into the schoolrooms of the Continent.

## Coleridge's Debt and the Plagiarism Charge

Samuel Taylor Coleridge delivered his first London lectures on Shakespeare in the winter of 1811 and 1812, before Black's translation existed, and the parallels with Schlegel struck German-reading auditors at once: the organic form against mechanical regularity, the defence of mixture, the play read as a poem whose parts live by the whole. Coleridge read German fluently, had studied in Göttingen, and moved in circles, Crabb Robinson's among them, where Schlegel's volumes were current. In 1840, after Coleridge's death, James Frederick Ferrier charged him in print with unacknowledged plagiarism from the lectures, and the accusation has shadowed both men's reputations since. The full evidence, and the lecture-by-lecture comparison, belongs to [Coleridge's own lectures on the play](/2026/10/16/coleridge-lectures-romeo-and-juliet/), which this article links rather than repeats; what belongs here is the adjudication, since the case decides how the German reading entered England.

Jonathan Bate's examination of the evidence remains the settlement most later scholarship accepts. The debt is real: Coleridge took from Schlegel formulations, distinctions, and a critical posture, and his habit of acknowledging the loan was, at best, intermittent. But the taking was transformative rather than servile, because Coleridge pressed every borrowed distinction through his own philosophy of imagination until it came out bearing his system's marks, and because several of the parallel ideas had circulated through the same German sources to both men independently. The verdict that fits the documents is neither theft nor innocence but transmission with transformation, the normal metabolism by which criticism crosses borders. Coleridge, on that verdict, is the German reading's English maker as surely as Black was its English translator, and the play's English prestige in the nineteenth century is unintelligible without both labours.

### Did Coleridge steal the reading or rebuild it?

He rebuilt it on borrowed foundations. The distinctions between organic and mechanical form, and the defence of the play's mixture, reached him from Schlegel's lectures, which he read in German; the philosophy of imagination through which he reworked them was his own. Scholarship since Ferrier's charge has settled, on the documentary evidence, at debt with transformation: transmission, not theft.

The second English receiver confirms the pattern from the opposite temperament. William Hazlitt, who owed Germany little and said so, published his Characters of Shakespear's Plays in 1817 with the lovers' tragedy rendered in a rhapsody of youthful passion that breathes the same post-Schlegel air: the play treated as a poem of feeling, its beauties demonstrated by quotation and sympathy rather than excused by rule. [Hazlitt's account of the characters of the play](/2026/10/23/hazlitt-characters-romeo-and-juliet/) stands as the native English flowering of the settlement the German reading had made possible, proof that by 1817 a critic could praise the play at full stretch, in public, without first apologizing for its form. Between Johnson's qualified admiration in the previous century and Hazlitt's unqualified ardour, the intervening event is Vienna.

## What the Reading Could Not See: A Reader's Play

The reservation that has gathered force since Schlegel concerns the tribunal he chose. His Shakespeare was encountered on the page, in editions, in a Germany where the play's stage life ran through adaptations he despised; he never stood in an English playhouse and watched the balcony scene land. The unity of impression he celebrates is therefore a reader's unity, gathered across hours of study, and the sigh he takes for the play's echo is the sigh of the study. The stage knows a different play in part: one whose effects are timed in minutes, whose nurse can steal or sink an act, whose fight choreography and balcony engineering are arguments no collation settles. The nineteenth-century English stage, for all its cuts and pictorial excess, preserved a theatrical knowledge of the play that the Vienna reading cannot supply, and performance criticism in the following century had to win back, against the poem's prestige, the proposition that the play is also a machine for performance.

The blind spot and the insight are, however, the same fact viewed from two sides. Only a reader's tribunal could have produced the removal test, the preparation argument, and the catalogue of antitheses, because those are discoveries made by collation and rereading, the study's instruments. A playhouse of 1808, running the play in Garrick's version, could not have seen the whole that Schlegel demonstrated, for the sufficient reason that the whole was not on its stage. The German reading's limitation is thus the price of its achievement, and the mature criticism of the play, which reads the text whole and tests the reading in rehearsal, is the synthesis of the two tribunals rather than the victory of either. Schlegel would not have accepted the synthesis as a correction; on the evidence of the lectures, he would have been wrong, and the concession is part of this article's verdict.

## The Critics Argue Back: Paulin, Wellek, Snyder, and the Limits of the Reading

A fair verdict on the German Romantic contribution has to weigh the strongest modern reservations, three of which cut in different directions. Roger Paulin, whose biography and studies have done most to restore Schlegel's criticism to serious attention, argues the maximal case with a scholar's evidence: the Romeo and Juliet essay and its Vienna expansion constitute a new method, analytical criticism itself, and the reading's central perceptions, the necessity of the parts, the blending of contrasts, the Friar's planted herbs, have been confirmed by two centuries of subsequent attention to the text. Against Paulin's maximal case stands Wellek's comparatist caution, already entered above: the system's slogans were common European property, and Schlegel's genius was for synthesis and broadcast rather than for invention. Snyder's comic-matrix demonstration cuts from a third side, at the play rather than at the system: the unity Schlegel celebrated conceals a generic fracture that does real work in performance, and a criticism that blends the seam away understands the sigh better than the structure that produces it.

The adjudication this article reaches distributes the honours as follows. On the essay's method and the reading's permanent perceptions, Paulin is right, and the documentary record of subsequent criticism, which keeps rediscovering Schlegel's points in Schlegel's order, is the proof. On the system, Wellek is right: classical and romantic were the age's small change, and the lectures' originality lies in application and eloquence rather than in the antithesis itself. On the play's construction, Snyder is right about the mechanism and Schlegel remains right about the effect, and the two rights are compatible because Schlegel's own opening sentence, ingredients comic and direction tragic, had already conceded the mechanism to anyone who read him closely. What none of the reservations touches is the historical claim at this article's centre: that the play's prestige as a poem was manufactured in these decades, by these hands, and that the manufacture succeeded so completely that its products now pass for the play's natural attributes.

The Victorian inheritance shows the manufacture ageing into common sense. When Anna Jameson came to write the heroines in the 1830s, her Juliet, ardent, impulsive, and read from inside the character's own heart, presupposes the Romantic reframing at every point; [Anna Jameson and the Victorian Juliet](/2026/11/06/anna-jameson-victorian-juliet/) traces that inheritance in the next generation, where Schlegel's poem of love has become the premise from which character criticism starts rather than the conclusion it must argue. A reading has reached its maximum influence when its opponents and heirs alike must begin from it, and by that measure the German Romantic reading of the lovers' tragedy had, within a generation, achieved the maximum.

The twentieth century's academy completed the absorption. The Vienna paragraph on the lovers became the standard opening document of the play's reception history, reprinted in the critical anthologies through which students meet the play's afterlife, and quoted at the head of edition introductions that proceed to qualify it. Its vocabulary went underground into the discipline's common stock: critics who have never opened the lectures speak of the play's organic unity, its impression, its atmosphere, in Schlegel's senses and often in Black's words. Even the reaction against Romantic Shakespeare, the historicizing scholarship that returned the play to its theatre, its sources, and its print shops, defined its programme against the poem Schlegel had made, and therefore inside his jurisdiction. That is the final evidence for this article's central claim. A manufactured prestige is known by this sign: two centuries later, the manufacture is invisible, the product passes for nature, and the workshop has to be reconstructed from documents before the play's reputation can be seen as the historical event it was. The reconstruction is what this article has attempted, and the documents, Jena essay, Vienna paragraph, German version, Staël's witness, Black's translation, and the English settlements that followed, hold together as one continuous record.

## The Verdict on the German Romantic Contribution

The German Romantics did not discover the play's excellence in the sense of noticing a fact no one had met before; audiences had wept at the lovers since the 1590s, and editors had praised beauties for a century. What Schlegel and his circle contributed was a jurisdiction. They moved the play's trial from the court of rules, where it was forever guilty of mixture, haste, and punning, to the court of the work's own nature, where those same features could be exhibited as design. They supplied the vocabulary in which the defence is still conducted, unity of impression, organic form, direction over ingredients. They gave the play a German body in a translation that became a classic of the receiving language, and a European theory in lectures that became the Continent's map of drama. And through Staël, Black, Coleridge, and Hazlitt, they reimported the whole construction into England, where it settled the question the eighteenth century had left open, whether the play is a poem at all.

The reservation entered throughout this article should stand beside the verdict without cancelling it. Schlegel's unity is an impression's unity, his system was a synthesis of his age's ideas, his knowledge of the play was a reader's rather than a playgoer's, and his defence required one small honest surrender, the dated witticisms, which his rhetoric occasionally tempts his readers to forget. A prestige manufactured on those terms is not thereby counterfeit. Criticism does not deal in counterfeit and genuine; it deals in frameworks that disclose and frameworks that conceal, and Schlegel's framework disclosed the play, its antitheses, its preparations, its speed, its sigh, to readers who had been trained by the older framework not to see them. The German Romantic reading of Romeo and Juliet is the single largest reason the play is now read as it is read, and the lectures' paragraph on the lovers, picture of love, apotheosis and obsequies, single but endless sigh, remains the most efficient summary of the play's effect that criticism has produced.

There is a practical corollary for any reader who meets the play through a modern edition, a classroom, or a stage production that advertises its fidelity to the text. The presumption that the whole text matters, that cuts require justification, that the clowning and the wordplay belong to the design, that the lovers' story is a poem before it is a plot, and that its ending completes rather than interrupts its meaning: every one of those working assumptions entered the culture through the campaign this article has traced, and most of them entered through Schlegel personally, in the essay of the Jena years, the paragraph of Lecture XXV, and the German version of 1797. To read the play with those assumptions is to read it as a German Romantic, whether the reader knows the fact or not. Recovering the fact does not oblige anyone to surrender the assumptions; the demonstrations behind them were real, and this article has tested the strongest of them against the text and found them standing. It obliges only honesty about the play's history: its glory was argued into being, by identifiable critics, in identifiable rooms, against identifiable resistance, and the argument remains the best introduction to the play ever written by a man who never saw it performed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Schlegel summary, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch Schlegel summary is the reference table in this article that sets each element of August Wilhelm Schlegel's reading beside the passage of the play that carries it and the later criticism that inherited it. Its rows run from the picture of love and the apotheosis and obsequies, through the unity of impression and the Friar's planted herbs, to the German translation and the transmission through Staël and Black. The pattern it exposes is that the play's modern prestige was assembled piece by piece in the Romantic decades: every familiar claim about the play's unity, poetry, and design can be traced to a specific Schlegelian element and dated to its passage through the lectures, the translation, or the English reception.

### Q: Who was August Wilhelm Schlegel, and why did Vienna listen to him in 1808?

August Wilhelm Schlegel (1767 to 1845) was a German critic, poet, and translator of the Jena Romantic circle, co-editor with his brother Friedrich of the Athenaeum, and the foremost German translator of Shakespeare. Vienna listened because his authority was practical as well as theoretical: he had rendered seventeen of the plays into German verse, had served in Madame de Staël's household as her intellectual companion, and lectured as a philologist who handled texts at the level of the line. His course offered the city, at a moment of French occupation and cultural humiliation, a history of drama in which the French classical stage was demoted and the romantic drama of England and Spain exalted, a programme with political as well as literary resonance.

### Q: What did Schlegel call Romeo and Juliet in the Vienna lectures?

He called it a picture of love and its pitiable fate, in a world whose atmosphere is too sharp for the tenderest blossom of human life. The formulation opens the play's treatment in Lecture XXV of the Course of Lectures on Dramatic Art and Literature, in John Black's English translation, and it sets the terms for everything that follows in the passage: the lovers are two beings created for each other who feel mutual love at the first glance, unite by a secret marriage under circumstances hostile in the highest degree, and are within a few days united by a voluntary death in the grave. The play's plot is thereby framed from the outset as the portrait of a feeling and its fate, not as a chain of improbable accidents.

### Q: What does Schlegel mean by calling the play the apotheosis and the obsequies of love?

He means that the play performs two opposite ceremonies on love at once. As apotheosis, it deifies the feeling: under Shakespeare's handling the story becomes, in Schlegel's words, a glorious song of praise on the inexpressible feeling which ennobles the soul and elevates even the senses into soul. As obsequies, it buries that same feeling: the song is simultaneously a melancholy elegy on love's inherent and imparted frailty, ending in the tomb where the lovers' union and funeral coincide. The double term captures the play's signature effect, in which exaltation and mourning are not successive moods but a single impression. Schlegel's pairing has been quoted ever since because it names, in four words, the sensation audiences had always reported and earlier criticism had lacked the vocabulary to state.

### Q: Which lecture treats the tragedies, and where does the Romeo and Juliet passage stand in it?

Lecture XXV, titled Criticisms on Shakspeare's Tragedies, treats the tragedies, and Romeo and Juliet stands at its head, paired with Othello as the lecture's opening exhibit. The placement is a critical decision: Schlegel defines Shakespearean tragedy through the lovers' play before he reaches Hamlet, Macbeth, or Lear. He begins by observing that Romeo and Juliet and Othello differ from the tragedies already examined neither in ingredients nor in manner of treatment, but that the direction of the whole gives them the stamp of tragedy. Beginning the tragedies lecture with the most lyrical and most mixed of the plays allowed Schlegel to establish his principle, that genre follows direction rather than materials, on the hardest available case.

### Q: What earlier essay on the play did Schlegel remind his hearers of?

He reminded them of his own essay on Romeo and Juliet, written in the Jena years of the later 1790s and collected in the first volume of the Charakteristiken und Kritiken of 1801, published with his brother Friedrich. In the lectures he summarized its procedure directly: he had gone through the whole of the scenes in their order and demonstrated the inward necessity of each with reference to the whole, shown why such a particular circle of characters and relations was placed around the two lovers, explained the signification of the mirth scattered here and there, and justified the occasional heightening of the poetical colours. The Vienna passage on the play is that essay's argument carried to eloquence; the demonstration preceded the broadcast by roughly a decade.

### Q: What test of the play's unity did the young Schlegel apply scene by scene?

He applied a removal test: a scene, character, or patch of mirth earned its place only if the whole would be damaged by its absence. Working through the scenes in order, he asked of each what the total design required from it, and concluded that nothing could be taken away, nothing added, and nothing otherwise arranged without mutilating and disfiguring the perfect work. His single exception was a few witticisms that imitated the social tone of the playwright's day and had grown unintelligible or foreign to later taste. The test was aimed at the adapters' tradition of cutting and improving the play for the stage, and it remains the ancestor of the modern editorial presumption that the text's arrangement is evidence, not raw material.

### Q: How does Schlegel defend the mixture of comic mirth with tragedy?

He defends it as signification rather than relief. The mirth scattered through the play, from Mercutio's Queen Mab flight to the nurse's interminable anecdotes, is the medium against which the lovers' intensity becomes measurable: the laughter is the sound of the ordinary world the lovers are leaving, and its presence inside the tragedy is therefore structural. In the general argument of Lecture XXIII, mixture is the signature of romantic drama, which aims at the fulness of life rather than the separation of kinds practiced by the classical stage. Schlegel's defence has one stated boundary, the handful of dated witticisms he was willing to surrender, and that small concession is what gives the larger defence its credibility with later critics.

### Q: Why does Schlegel single out Friar Laurence's first entrance as proof of design?

Because it converts the plot's least probable device into a planted preparation. The sleeping potion of 4.1 looks like a romancer's convenience, and Schlegel asks how the poet disposes an audience to believe the Friar possesses such a secret. His answer is the entrance at 2.3, where the Friar first appears gathering herbs and descanting on their wonderful virtues, seeing in nature emblems of the moral world, with poison and medicine residing in a single flower. The speech establishes the Friar's art two acts before the potion is needed and states the play's master antithesis in botanical form. For Schlegel the specimen proves that the design reaches forward across the whole play, and the example remains the standard illustration of Shakespearean preparation.

### Q: What is the difference between classical and romantic drama in Schlegel's system?

Classical drama, in the system of Lecture I, is the art of the ancient world: finite, harmonious, self-contained, perfected within clear limits, and shaped by a religion of nature and of this life. Romantic drama is the art that grows from the Christian and chivalric centuries: yearning rather than satisfied, mingled in its kinds, haunted by an infinite that no form exhausts, and native to Spain and England rather than to Greece and Rome. The French classical stage Schlegel treats as a narrowed imitation of the ancients that kept the rules and lost the spirit. The distinction is less a history than a jurisdiction, since it decides by which laws a given play may lawfully be judged.

### Q: Where does Romeo and Juliet stand in that system as evidence?

It stands as the limiting case on which the system is proved. The play is saturated with everything the classical standard condemned: mixture of mirth and catastrophe, rhymed lyricism, wordplay, speed, and a plot borrowed from an Italian novella. If such a play can be shown to possess unity of impression, planted design, and profundity of feeling, then the classical criteria stand refuted at their point of strongest application, and the romantic account of drama is vindicated. That argumentative office explains why Schlegel lavishes his most famous eloquence on the lovers' tragedy rather than on the greater histories or on Lear: the exhibit had to be the hardest one, or the system it carried would persuade nobody who did not already agree.

### Q: What did Schlegel translate, and when did his Romeo and Juliet appear in German?

Between 1797 and 1801 Schlegel published German verse translations of seventeen of Shakespeare's plays, and Romeo und Julia stood among the very first, in the opening volume of 1797. The translations were methodological events: where earlier German versions had mostly given prose, Schlegel carried the original's forms across, blank verse for blank verse, rhyme for rhyme, song for song, prose for prose, so that German readers met the play's gait and music and not merely its plot. The versions were received as achievements of German literature in their own right, admired by Goethe's generation, adopted by German stages as playing texts, and quoted until their phrasings passed into the language. The 1797 Romeo und Julia thus gave the play German citizenship a decade before the Vienna lectures explained its meaning to Europe.

### Q: Who completed the German Shakespeare after Schlegel, and whose names does the finished set carry?

The finished set carries the names of Schlegel and Ludwig Tieck. Schlegel's seventeen plays of 1797 to 1801 left the canon incomplete; in the 1820s Tieck took up the enterprise as editor, and the remaining plays were translated under his direction, chiefly by Count Wolf von Baudissin and by Tieck's daughter Dorothea, whose large share of the labour the title pages long concealed. The completed edition appeared between 1825 and 1833 and fixed the German Shakespeare as the Schlegel-Tieck Shakespeare, a Romantic monument in which the lovers' tragedy had stood from the first volume. German readers who spoke of unser Shakespeare, our Shakespeare, were registering a naturalization that this completion sealed.

### Q: How did Madame de Staël carry Schlegel's lectures across Europe?

Staël carried them by witness, summary, and fame. She was present in Vienna in 1808 and left the classic eyewitness account of the course, praising a critic who treated the finest pieces of poetry as wonders of nature and compressed a life's labour into epithets at once beautiful and just. Her De l'Allemagne, printed in Paris in 1810 and destroyed by Napoleon's police, then published in London in 1813, presented German thought, Schlegel's system prominent within it, to the French and English reading worlds in a form more dramatic than the lectures themselves. Since Schlegel had lived in her household as her children's tutor and her intellectual companion, the championship was also personal, and her book remains the channel through which many European readers first met the classical and romantic map.

### Q: What did John Black's translation of the lectures change in 1815?

It changed the reading's language, and thereby its audience and its afterlife. Black's A Course of Lectures on Dramatic Art and Literature, published in London in 1815, rendered Schlegel into an English vigorous enough to be quoted, and the formulations that English criticism has repeated ever since are Black's Englishings: the picture of love and its pitiable fate, the apotheosis and the obsequies of love, the contrasts blended into a unity of impression whose echo resembles a single but endless sigh. English readers who believed they received Schlegel pure were receiving him in the same mediated way German readers had received Shakespeare, through a version good enough to stand as a classic beside its original, which is the fitting symmetry of the whole transmission.

### Q: How did Friedrich Schlegel's fragments prepare the ground his brother's lectures built on?

Friedrich Schlegel, the younger brother, supplied the philosophy of which the lectures are the application. In the Athenaeum fragments of 1798 to 1800 the brothers' circle defined romantic poetry as a progressive universal art, forever becoming and never perfected, hospitable to mixture, irony, and yearning, against the finished perfection of classical form. Within that frame a lyrical, mixed, swiftly moving tragedy of young love could be prized for exactly the qualities the classical standard condemned, and August Wilhelm's scene-by-scene demonstrations gave the frame its working criticism. The division of labour was fraternal and deliberate: Friedrich minted the concepts in aphorisms, August Wilhelm spent them on the plays, and the Romeo and Juliet essay and lecture are where the currency first bought something permanent.

### Q: What part did Ludwig Tieck play in the German championship of the play?

Tieck played the practitioner's part. The Jena circle's most theatrically minded intelligence, he wrote on Shakespeare from the 1790s, championed the early plays and the stage energy the neoclassical taste despised, and attended to the lovers' tragedy as a living performance, its youth, its night scenes, and its fitness for the boards, where Schlegel's account is a reader's demonstration of the poem's necessity. As editor of the completed German Shakespeare he then gave the championship its institutional form, directing the translations of Baudissin and Dorothea Tieck that filled out his friend's seventeen plays into a national canon. Schlegel supplied the theory and the founding versions; Tieck supplied the stage sense and the finished monument, and the play's German home was built by both.

### Q: Did Coleridge borrow from Schlegel, and how has that charge been judged?

He borrowed, and the charge of plagiarism brought by James Frederick Ferrier in 1840 has been judged an overstatement of a real debt. Coleridge read the lectures in German, lectured on Shakespeare in London in 1811 and 1812 before Black's translation existed, and reproduced Schlegel's distinctions, organic against mechanical form and the defence of mixture among them, with intermittent acknowledgment. Jonathan Bate's examination of the evidence gives the settlement most scholarship accepts: the debt is genuine, but the taking was transformative, since Coleridge pressed every borrowed distinction through his own philosophy of imagination, and some parallel ideas reached both men from shared German sources. The verdict of transmission with transformation acquits Coleridge of theft while confirming that the German reading entered England substantially through his lectures.

### Q: Why does Schlegel compare the impression the play leaves to a single but endless sigh?

Because the sigh is his unit for a unity that is felt rather than constructed. The catalogue of contrasts that precedes the image, the sweetest and the bitterest, love and hatred, festive rejoicings and dark forebodings, tender embraces and sepulchral horrors, the fulness of life and self-annihilation, names materials no mechanical unity could reconcile. Schlegel's claim is that they are so blended into a unity of impression that the echo the whole leaves in the mind behaves like one indivisible act of feeling, compounded of pleasure and grief exactly as a sigh is. The image also measures duration: a sigh ends, but this one is endless, because the impression survives the performance and the reading. In that single figure Schlegel states his whole doctrine of organic form as applied to the play.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch Schlegel summary to argue that the play's prestige was made rather than born?

An essay should argue from the table's three columns in sequence. Begin with what Schlegel claimed, the picture of love, the apotheosis and obsequies, the unity of impression, and show that each claim was an intervention against a named older judgement, the adapters' cuts, the neoclassical charge of mixture, the beauties-and-faults commentary. Move to the textual anchors, the Friar's herbs at 2.3 and the antitheses of 1.5 and 5.3, to prove the claims were demonstrations and not compliments. Close with the inheritance column, Staël, Black, Coleridge, Hazlitt, and Jameson, to show the claims hardening into the common sense of later centuries. Prestige argued that way is a documented manufacture, with dates, documents, and workmen, which is the strongest form the thesis can take.
