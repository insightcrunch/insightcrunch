## The Verdict First: Why the Oxford Edition Settled the Text

Jill Levenson's Oxford edition of Romeo and Juliet, published by Oxford University Press in 2000, is the edition against which every rival text of the play is measured, and the reason can be stated in one sentence: Levenson was the first editor of the play to refuse both of the easy settlements on offer, declining to bury the short 1597 quarto as a corrupt report and declining to patch the long 1599 quarto into a composite that no early printer ever produced. Where her predecessors chose between the two early texts, or conflated them into a third, Levenson printed both, each modernized, each complete, and each treated as an independent witness to what her introduction calls a mobile text. The verdict argued through this article is that the decision was right, that it has worn better than the conflations printed since, and that the edition's second great act, the reconstruction of the play's performance history from some 170 promptbooks, gives its text an authority no competitor matches: this is an edition built from the play's whole life, on the page and on the stage, rather than from one document privileged in advance.

![Jill Levenson and the Oxford edition of Romeo and Juliet, with the two early quartos - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The word settlement, applied to this edition, has a specific content for each constituency it served. For textual scholars it settled the legitimacy of the short quarto as a printable whole: after Levenson, the live question was no longer whether the 1597 text deserved print but what any given study would do with it. For theatre historians it settled the standing of promptbook evidence, which her archive raised from anecdote to data set, catalogued, counted, and deposited where other scholars could test it. For ordinary scholarly readers it settled the default: the Oxford volume became the edition in which the play's doubleness is not a scandal to be managed but the first fact to be learned. Settlements of this kind are not eternal, and Levenson's is already being revised at its edges by editors who conflate where she separated and by scholars who press a single genealogy where she stayed agnostic. A settlement is recognisable, all the same, by the way later work writes from inside it, and the decades since the edition appeared have produced editions, essays, and productions that assume her frame even while contesting her conclusions. The article that follows tests the settlement against the documents: the two quartos, the promptbook archive, the introduction's reading, the reviewers' verdicts, and the successor editions that defined themselves against her.

The scale of the refusal needs measuring against the tradition it broke. For most of the twentieth century, editors of the play worked inside a settlement reached by the New Bibliography: the second quarto of 1599, title page boasting a text "Newly corrected, augmented, and amended," was the good quarto, printed from Shakespeare's own working papers, while the first quarto of 1597, shorter by some 800 lines, was a bad quarto, a memorial reconstruction assembled from the recollections of actors and fit only to be mined for stage directions and the occasional reading where the good text failed. That settlement had the elegance of a verdict at law. It named a winner, assigned the loser a disreputable origin, and licensed every editor to print the winner's words with the loser's stagecraft silently absorbed. Generations of students read a Romeo and Juliet whose title page credited one book and whose directions, punctuation, and occasional lines quietly came from another, with the borrowings marked, if at all, in a collation few readers consult.

Levenson's volume changed the evidentiary basis of the play's editing in two connected ways. The first was textual. By printing the 1597 quarto entire, in modern spelling, with its own title, "An Excellent Conceited Tragedy of Romeo and Juliet," she moved it from the apparatus to the reading experience: a student can now read the short text as a play, compare its pacing, its compressed rhetoric, and its theatrical directness against the long text, and judge the quarrel between the quartos from evidence rather than from an editor's summary of evidence. The second was historical. Her introduction's performance history, and the commentary that carries performance evidence into note after note, rest on a research programme she had pursued since the mid-1980s: the profiling of approximately 170 promptbooks, the marked copies from which productions of the play were staged across four centuries. No other editor of the play had treated the promptbook archive as a primary instrument. The result is an edition whose notes can say, with documents behind the saying, how a scene was cut, doubled, relocated, or restored by the theatres that kept the play alive.

Neither act was safe. Printing the short quarto entire exposed Levenson to the charge of dignifying a derivative text, and building notes from promptbooks exposed her to the charge of mistaking theatrical habit for textual evidence. The charges were made, in reviews and in the rival editions that followed, and this article adjudicates them in turn. The short answer is that the edition anticipated both objections and answered them structurally: the 1597 text is annotated only where it differs significantly from the 1599 text, which marks its status as the secondary witness without suppressing it, and the promptbook evidence is used to illuminate staging questions rather than to manufacture readings. The longer answer occupies the sections that follow, and it ends where it begins. Among [the modern editions compared](/2026/06/12/modern-editions-compared-arden-oxford/) by students choosing a text, Arden, Oxford, Cambridge, and Folger among them, Levenson's Oxford volume remains the one that shows its working most completely, because it alone places both early texts and the whole performance record inside a single set of covers.

What follows examines the edition in the order its own introduction proceeds: the editor's preparation, the textual problem she inherited, the theory of the mobile text, the edition in practice, the performance archive, the introduction's reading of the play, the adjudication of the first quarto's trustworthiness, and the answer the Arden Shakespeare eventually gave. The standard applied throughout is documentary. Claims about the edition are tied to the volume itself, to Levenson's published account of her method, and to the reviews and successor editions that tested her choices in print.

## Jill Levenson's Road to the Edition: Toronto, the Performance Book, and the Promptbook Project

Levenson came to the Oxford commission with a preparation no previous editor of the play could match, because she had already written the play's modern performance history once. A professor of English at Trinity College in the University of Toronto, she published Romeo and Juliet in the Manchester University Press series Shakespeare in Performance in 1987, a study that reconstructed the Elizabethan production and then followed five decisive revivals: David Garrick's of 1748, Charlotte Cushman's of 1845 and 1846, John Gielgud's of 1935, Peter Brook's of 1947, and Franco Zeffirelli's, staged at the Old Vic in 1960 and filmed in 1968. The 1987 book established the pattern of her thinking. Performance, for Levenson, is not the afterlife of a finished literary object. It is the medium in which the play has its continuous existence, and the record of productions is therefore evidence about the play itself: about which scenes bear weight, which speeches theatres cannot do without, and which effects the words were built to produce.

The five productions of the 1987 study were chosen with an argument in view. Each held the stage, or shaped the stages that followed, for decades, and each redefined what the play was taken to be. Garrick, whose version ruled the English stage from 1748, cut the Rosaline material, compressed the action, and rewrote the tomb scene so that Juliet wakes before Romeo dies, giving the lovers a final exchange the early texts deny them. Cushman, playing Romeo opposite her sister Susan's Juliet, recovered a full scale portrayal of the hero as, in Levenson's phrase, the temperamental adolescent whose brief and extreme vicissitudes as a lover make him a pattern of tragic experience. Gielgud's 1935 production, in which he alternated the parts of Romeo and Mercutio with Laurence Olivier, restored large quantities of text that the acting tradition had discarded. Brook in 1947 brought a young company's speed and severity to the play and cut the closing reconciliation, ending closer to desolation than to concord. Zeffirelli, first at the Old Vic and then on film, stripped away half a century of pictorial sentiment, cast unknown teenagers, and cut the text to roughly a third of its lines for the screen, yet produced the version through which the late twentieth century knew the tragedy. An editor formed by this research would not treat the stage as an appendix to the text. The stage was the text's longest running commentary.

### How many promptbooks did Levenson profile for the edition?

Levenson profiled approximately 170 promptbooks, as she reports in her account of the edition's method. Beginning in the mid-1980s, she followed the catalogue Charles H. Shattuck had published in 1965 and accumulated profiles of marked copies from which productions had been staged, about five dozen more than Shattuck listed, spanning the seventeenth century to the end of the twentieth.

The promptbook project requires a word of explanation, because its fruits are invisible unless the instrument is understood. A promptbook is the working copy of a play from which a production was run: the text as cut for performance, marked with entrances, exits, cues, business, and the accumulated decisions of a company about how the words become action. Surviving promptbooks are, as Shattuck warned in words Levenson quotes, tricky and stubborn informants, voluble about the callboy's errands and silent about where an actor stood. Yet they are the only documents that record, night by night and decade by decade, what theatres actually did with the text: which lines generations of actors found unplayable, which scenes they transposed, which speeches they could not bear to lose. Levenson decided early what to extract from them. Where William P. Halstead's great collation of acting editions and promptbooks had tracked the history of individual lines, she profiled whole productions, reconstructing each revival's shape. The method is set out in her essay "Show Business: The Editor in the Theater," published in 1999 in a volume of essays honouring Jay L. Halio, and it governs the Oxford edition that appeared the following year.

The archive also changed what the edition could claim about the play's vitality. Levenson's own summary of her evidence notes that in the second half of the eighteenth century the play achieved 399 performances in London between 1751 and 1800, eclipsing Hamlet in that span, and that from the Restoration forward it has been in production, in one form or another, almost continuously. An editor who knows that the play outran Hamlet on the Georgian stage, and who has read the marked books of the companies that staged it, edits with a different sense of the text's centre of gravity. The speeches the promptbooks preserve uncut across centuries are evidence of theatrical necessity, and the scenes they persistently shorten are evidence of a different kind. Levenson's commentary draws on both. Her edition is thus the product of two apprenticeships served in sequence: the 1987 performance study, which taught her the play's stage shape, and the promptbook survey, which gave her the documents to prove it.

The institutional setting deserves a sentence of its own, because the edition's archive has a geography. The University of Toronto, where Levenson taught, houses the Centre for Renaissance and Reformation Studies, a research centre with strong holdings and a stronger tradition in early modern book history, and the promptbook database found its permanent home there. The edition is thus a Canadian scholarly production about an English play, built in a centre equipped for exactly its kind of documentary patience, and supported by the national research council whose grant Levenson acknowledges in her account of the database. Editorial projects of this scale are institutional achievements as much as individual ones, and the Oxford volume's apparatus of profiles, databases, and grants records the fact on its face.

## Two Quartos, One Problem: The Texts Levenson Inherited

The editorial problem of Romeo and Juliet is created by two books. The first quarto, printed in 1597 by John Danter and Edward Allde, offers "An Excellent conceited Tragedie of Romeo and Juliet, As it hath been often (with great applause) plaid publiquely, by the right honourable the L. of Hunsdon his Servants." The second quarto, printed by Thomas Creede for Cuthbert Burby in 1599, offers "The Most Excellent and lamentable Tragedie, of Romeo and Juliet," and announces itself as "Newly corrected, augmented, and amended." The second book is the longer by roughly 800 lines, the fuller in verse and rhetoric, and the rougher in its printing, carrying the marks of copy that had not been prepared for the press: variant speech headings, a duplicated passage, uneven lineation. The first book is tidier, faster, and poorer in poetry, yet rich in the detail of performance, with stage directions that read like a record of business actually done. Every editor since the eighteenth century has had to decide what these two books are, because the decision determines what the edited play will be.

The twentieth century's answer hardened early. The first quarto was classified among the bad quartos, texts held to derive from memorial reconstruction, in which actors, most often those in smaller parts, dictated a play from their memories of performance, producing a version that preserved the plot and the famous speeches in degraded form while compressing everything else. On this theory the short text's virtues were accidental and its readings suspect wherever the long text offered an alternative; its stage directions, paradoxically, were prized, since a reported text might remember what the playhouse did even when it garbled what the author wrote. The second quarto, by contrast, was held to stand close to the author's foul papers, his working draft, and so to carry an authority that only obvious error could displace. The theory had distinguished sponsors, and it organized the century's editions. An editor's task, on this settlement, was conservative: print the good text, emend it sparingly, borrow the bad text's directions in brackets, and explain the arrangement in a textual introduction that rehearsed the evidence for the two books' respective origins.

Beneath the settlement, however, the documents kept generating friction. The short quarto preserves some lines and one famous phrase that editors could not surrender: the Prologue to Act 1 in the long text begins "Two households, both alike in dignity" (Prologue, 1), and both texts agree there, but elsewhere the short text holds readings, such as Benvolio's "without-book prologue" line in the scene before the feast, that every modern edition prints despite deriving them from the supposedly corrupt witness. The short quarto's account of the tomb scene assigns the closing movements of the plot in an order some directors find more actable. Its stage directions name the clown Will Kemp in the scene of the musicians, a detail no memorial pirate would invent and no literary manuscript would contain. The long quarto, for its part, prints one passage twice over, the dawn quatrain shared between the end of the balcony scene and the opening of the friar's cell scene, as if the author's draft had kept both versions of an idea and the printing house had set both. A theory that made the short book mere corruption and the long book mere authority explained the evidence by explaining parts of it away.

Two later books complete the genealogy an editor must hold in mind. The third quarto of 1609 reprinted the long text, and the First Folio of 1623 was set from that reprint with some correction, so that the line of descent from the 1599 quarto to the Folio runs through a single reprint and its accumulated errors. The short quarto stands outside that line entirely, descended from no surviving print, which is part of what makes its testimony independent: when its readings converge with sense and metre, they cannot be dismissed as a corruption of the long text's line, since its text never passed through that line. The genealogical picture Levenson inherited was asymmetrical, a documented descent on one side and a single orphaned book on the other whose parentage every theory had to guess.

### Why does the edition keep the 1599 quarto as its main text?

The 1599 quarto stands as the edition's main text because it is the fullest and most authoritative witness to the play: roughly 800 lines longer than the 1597 quarto, richer in the verse the short text compresses, and closest to the author's papers on the prevailing bibliographical account. Levenson prints it in modern spelling as the volume's reading text.

Levenson's dissatisfaction with the old settlement was therefore documentary before it was theoretical. The theories of the two quartos' origins, memorial reconstruction against foul papers, were both inferences from the books' physical characters, and both had hardened into permissions: permission to ignore the short text's words, permission to treat the long text's errors as sacred. Her introduction reopened the case by returning to what the two books demonstrably are. They are two substantive texts, the phrase she uses in her account of the edition, which means two versions with independent claims on a reader's attention, differing not as original and copy but as two states of a work that existed in performance before it existed in print and went on changing in performance afterward. The sections of her introduction titled "Date" and "The Mobile Text" work out the consequence: the play was composed in the mid-1590s, printed first in a form close to the stage version of those years, reprinted in an augmented form close to the author's papers, and then never fixed again, since every production from the Restoration onward remade it. An edition that froze one state and suppressed the other would misrepresent the object it claimed to present.

This is the point at which Levenson's preparation mattered most. An editor innocent of the promptbook archive might have treated the quartos' difference as a problem in stemmatics, a question of which book descends from the lost manuscript by the cleaner line. Levenson, who had profiled 170 promptbooks, knew that play texts in the theatre are mobile by profession: they are cut, restored, reordered, and rewritten by every generation that performs them, and the two quartos are simply the earliest surviving instances of a mobility that her archive documents across four centuries. The short quarto's compression resembles what promptbooks do; the long quarto's fullness resembles what an authorial manuscript holds. Neither resemblance convicts either book of fraud. The edition she built takes the resemblance seriously in both directions, and the sections of her introduction work through the consequences in order, date, texts, procedures, before the performance history gathers the whole into the play's stage life.

## The Date of the Play: Her Introduction's Evidentiary Habits

The short section of the introduction devoted to the play's date shows Levenson's evidentiary habits in miniature, and it is worth pausing over because it governs how much weight the rest of the volume asks its documents to bear. The play must have been written before Francis Meres praised it in print in 1598, and the internal evidence clusters in the middle of the decade: the Nurse's recollection that "since the earthquake now eleven years" (1.3.23) points, if the great English earthquake of 1580 is meant, to 1591, a date most scholars find too early for the play's style, so that the line is better read as a fossil of an early draft or a false scent; the verse tests and the play's kinship with the other works of the mid-1590s, the Dream, Richard II, and the sonnet vogue it shares with the narrative poems, gather around 1595; the first quarto's title page shows the play established in the repertory of the Lord Hunsdon's servants by 1597. Levenson's section weighs these scraps without inflating any of them, settles the composition in the mid-1590s, and moves on. The point for her edition is the sequence that follows from the dating: a play composed about 1595, performed by the Chamberlain's Men, printed in a short version in 1597 and an augmented version in 1599, presents its two texts as near contemporaries rather than as an original and a late corruption, and the four years between composition and the second quarto are years in which the author's company was performing the work and the author was, on the evidence of the longer text, still enlarging it. The date section is three or four pages of the introduction, but it quietly removes the chronological scaffolding on which the bad quarto verdict had rested, the long interval of decay the verdict required.

## The Versioning Decade: Oxford's Two Text Precedent and the Suspect Texts Audit

Levenson's edition did not arrive from a clear sky, and its editor's introduction acknowledges the weather that made it possible. In 1986 the Oxford Complete Works, under Stanley Wells and Gary Taylor, had startled the profession by printing two versions of King Lear, the quarto History and the Folio Tragedy, arguing that Shakespeare had revised the play and that a single conflated text misrepresented both states. The decision was contested for a decade, but it established, inside the very series Levenson would join, the principle that a Shakespeare play could legitimately exist in an edition as more than one text. Her Romeo and Juliet is that principle carried to the play where the two texts differ most in kind: not a revision across a decade of theatrical life, as the Lear texts were held to be, but two books from the 1590s whose relationship, draft, report, or adaptation, remains unproved. Where Wells and Taylor had a revision narrative to justify their pair, Levenson had only the documents and the refusal to confiscate one of them. Her edition is in that sense the more radical application of the Oxford house idea, because it prints the second text without claiming to know what the second text is.

The wider discipline was moving in her direction at the same time. The critique of copy text theory associated with Jerome McGann's work on textual criticism had taught a generation of scholars to see the single edited text as an artifact of editorial desire no less than of evidence, and Randall McLeod's provocations in favour of unediting, of presenting documents rather than reconstructions, circulated through the same years. Most concretely for this play, Laurie Maguire's Shakespearean Suspect Texts of 1996 subjected the memorial reconstruction hypothesis to a systematic audit, testing its supposed diagnostic features across the suspect texts and finding the case for memorial reporting weaker, for most of them, than the orthodoxy had assumed. The detailed audit of that hypothesis belongs to other articles in this series; its bearing here is chronological. Levenson prepared her edition through the 1990s, as the bad quarto doctrine lost its evidentiary confidence, and her introduction's refusal to pronounce the short quarto a memorial reconstruction, a refusal that looked cautious in 1980, looked like the emerging consensus by the year her volume appeared. The edition's timing was part of its authority: it codified, for the play most affected, a skepticism the discipline had already reached in pieces.

Against that background, the shape of her achievement comes into focus. The versioning decade gave editors permission to print two texts; it did not tell them how to build a volume around the pair, how to annotate the lesser witness, how to keep the collations honest, or how to connect the doubled text to the play's life in performance. Levenson supplied the architecture. Her main text, commentary, second text, performance history, and procedures form a single system in which each part justifies the others: the mobile text theory explains the pair, the pair exhibits the theory, the promptbook archive documents the mobility the theory asserts, and the commentary delivers the archive to the reader's desk. Imitators could borrow the two text format, and the Cambridge Early Quartos series would soon be printing the short quartos as volumes in their own right, but the system as a whole, text, stage, and archive in one frame, remains hers.

## Brian Gibbons and the Settlement Levenson Broke

The editor whose work Levenson's volume superseded in the scholarly conversation was [Brian Gibbons and the Arden Second Series](/2027/06/11/brian-gibbons-arden-second-series/), whose edition of 1980 had held the field for twenty years. Gibbons's edition was, and remains, a distinguished piece of work: a lucid introduction, a conservative text, and a commentary tuned to the play's language and its sources. Its textual position, however, belonged to the old settlement in its classic form. Gibbons based his text on the second quarto, treated the first as a reported text of derivative authority, and admitted its readings only where the preferred text seemed deficient, a procedure so standard that it scarcely registered as a choice. The interest of the comparison is not that Gibbons was careless. It is that the profession's best practice in 1980 made the short quarto invisible as a play: a reader of the Arden volume met the 1597 text only in fragments, quoted in collations and notes, dismembered into variants.

Levenson's break with that practice is most visible in a single physical fact: her volume prints the short quarto as a continuous modernized text, under its own title, after the long text, so that the book contains the play twice. The decision reversed the burden of proof that had governed editing since the bad quarto doctrine was formulated. Under Gibbons's settlement, a reading from the short text had to justify its admission to the edited play against a presumption of corruption. Under Levenson's, the short text stands as a witness whose testimony is recorded in full, and the presumption to be justified is the editor's decision to set any part of it aside. The consequences run through the whole volume. Her collations treat the short quarto's variants as substantive evidence rather than as curiosities; her commentary discusses its staging solutions as solutions; her introduction can compare the two texts' shapes, the one swift and theatrical, the other ample and rhetorical, because her reader holds both shapes in hand.

Gibbons's practice also contained, half acknowledged, the seed of Levenson's argument. His text, like every modern text before hers, silently depended on the short quarto for the staging of scene after scene: his feast entrance, as the comparative record shows, populates the stage with five named figures where the copy text names none of them, an elaboration descended through the editorial tradition from the variorum Furness, and the directions that make the Arden text actable throughout are a palimpsest of borrowings in which the short quarto's playhouse detail does much of the work. The old settlement, that is, trusted the short text's directions while distrusting its words, a divided trust that no one had justified in principle: if the actors' memories were good enough to preserve how the play moved, the case for despising everything else they preserved needed arguing, not assuming. Levenson's edition can be read as the systematic pursuit of that unasked question. She did not discover the short quarto's value; she refused to let its value be consumed invisibly, in brackets and borrowings, while its text stood condemned in the introduction.

The disagreement between the two editions is genuine and worth adjudicating precisely, because it is not about competence but about what an edition is for. Gibbons's model serves a reader who wants the play as its author finally wrote it, established by the best bibliographical reasoning, with alternatives documented but subordinated. Levenson's serves a reader who wants the play as it existed in the culture that produced and kept it, which includes the stage version the author's own company performed. The adjudication offered here favours Levenson on the evidence of the documents themselves. The short quarto's theatrical features, its actable tomb scene ordering, its businesslike directions, its naming of Kemp, are not the debris of a pirated text but the profile of the play in the playhouse, and an edition of a play, of all literary objects, cannot consistently treat the playhouse profile as contamination. The full account of Brian Gibbons and the Arden Second Series belongs to its own article in this series; here his edition stands as the measure of what Levenson changed, and the measure shows the change was large. Where the Arden text of 1980 presents one play with an apparatus, the Oxford text of 2000 presents a work in two states with an archive behind it.

## The Story Before the Play: Her Narrative History

The first part of Levenson's introduction, the history of the narrative before Shakespeare, is easy to underrate because it looks like the conventional source study every edition supplies. Hers is distinguished by the same habit that marks the whole volume: the documents are allowed to accumulate until a pattern becomes visible, and the pattern is mobility. The tale begins in myth, in the Babylonian lovers Pyramus and Thisbe whom Ovid told, and passes into the Italian novella, where Masuccio Salernitano, Luigi da Porto, and Matteo Bandello gave it the names, the city, the feud, the friar, and the tomb. Da Porto fixed the lovers as Romeo and Giulietta and set them in Verona; Bandello enlarged the tale's rhetoric and its pathos; Pierre Boaistuau carried it into French, moralizing as he went; and England received it twice over, in William Painter's prose, in the Palace of Pleasure, and in Arthur Brooke's long poem, The Tragical History of Romeus and Juliet of 1562, whose preface warns readers against the lovers' disobedience even as his verse lingers on their passion. Levenson traces the tempers of these retellings with care, noting how each transmitter adjusted the story's sympathies, softening or sharpening the lovers' guilt, expanding or contracting the feud, according to the market and the moral climate of his own moment.

The section earns its place in an edition built on the mobile text because it extends the edition's thesis backward across two centuries. The story was never a fixed object that Shakespeare received; it was a travelling property, translated, abridged, expanded, and rejudged at every border it crossed, and Shakespeare's transmutation of it, his compression of Brooke's nine months into days, his lowering of Juliet's age, his invention of Mercutio's scale and brilliance, is the greatest of the remakings but not a different kind of act from the others. An editor who has shown the tale moving through five languages and four genres before it reaches the stage is prepared to find it still moving when it reaches print in 1597 and 1599. The narrative history is therefore not preamble but prologue in the strong sense: it trains the reader, before a single editorial decision is announced, to expect versions.

## Reading the Two Texts: What Changes Between 1597 and 1599

Because Levenson prints both quartos entire, her edition supports a kind of reading no conflated text allows, and the introduction invites it: take a famous passage in both states and watch what the difference teaches. The Prologue is the first exhibit. Both texts open with a Chorus sonnet announcing the whole action, "Two households, both alike in dignity" (Prologue, 1), but the short text's Prologue runs shorter than the long text's fourteen lines, a compression that sets the short version's economy from its threshold: the frame is present, the amplitude reduced. The feast scene sharpens the lesson. In the long text the masquers' approach is heralded at length; in the short text Benvolio carries a line that survives only there, promising "no without-book prologue, faintly spoke after the prompter, for our entrance," a joke about improvising an apology for the masquers' intrusion that every modern edition prints from the short quarto alone. A text held to be corrupt by theory is, at this line, the sole authority the whole editorial tradition obeys, and Levenson's format lets the reader see the fact on the page rather than take it on trust from a collation.

The balcony scene and its sequel provide the deepest exhibit. The short text runs the lovers' exchange with a swifter pulse, trimming the rhetorical amplifications in which the long text delights, and when dawn comes, the long text notoriously speaks the dawn quatrain twice, once for Romeo leaving and once for the friar entering with his basket, while the short text gives the lines once, to the friar. Read in Levenson's two texts, the doublet stops being a bibliographical embarrassment and becomes a window into composition: a writer who tried a quatrain in one place, liked it, and set it in another, leaving both in the papers from which the long quarto was printed. The tomb scene reverses the direction of instruction. There the short text's disposition of the final movements, the order in which the friar and Balthasar are brought before the Prince, the pacing of the explanations, has struck many directors as the more actable arrangement, the record of a scene that works in the theatre because it was shaped by one. Neither text wins the comparison outright. The long text is the greater poem; the short text is, at point after point, the clearer performance. That is precisely the double truth the mobile text theory was coined to hold, and only an edition that prints both lets a reader verify it instead of inheriting it.

Her format also disciplines a temptation the comparison creates. Faced with two states of a speech, a reader, like an eclectic editor, wants to assemble a best text, taking the long version's poetry with the short version's clarity, and Levenson's procedures refuse the assembly: the texts are printed whole and separate, their notes cross refer, and the conflation is left undone on principle. The reader who wants the composite must make it privately, knowing it is a private manufacture. In that refusal the edition teaches its deepest lesson about what texts are: not raw material for an ideal play that never existed, but the surviving records of a real one that existed more than once.

## The Mobile Text: Levenson's Editorial Theory

Levenson named her theory in the introduction section called "The Mobile Text," and the name does the argumentative work. A mobile text, in her usage, is one that changed as its author composed and revised it, changed again as his company adapted it for the stage, and continued to change in every subsequent era of performance, in the theatre and, in the twentieth century, on film, television, in opera, and in ballet. The phrase refuses the governing metaphor of the old bibliography, in which a text is a lineal descendant of an archetype and every deviation is a fall. Mobility is not corruption in her account; it is the normal condition of a play script, and the two quartos are its first surviving exhibits. The 1597 text witnesses the play in an early state, shaped toward performance; the 1599 text witnesses it in the augmented state its title page advertises, corrected and enlarged, presumably from papers closer to the author's hand. Neither is the play entire. Each is the play at a station of its journey.

The theory has a compositional edge that is easy to miss. If the text was mobile in composition, then some of the evidence editors had used to convict the short quarto of memorial reporting can be reread as evidence of authorial process: passages where the short text differs in kind, not merely in quality, may record the play at an earlier stage of its writing rather than a later stage of its forgetting. Levenson does not press this edge into a full first draft theory of the short quarto, and her restraint matters to the adjudication below; she presents the two texts as independent witnesses without staking the edition on any single genealogy for either. The editorial consequence is drawn with complete firmness even though the genealogical question is left open: whatever the short quarto's origin, draft, report, or prompt copy descendant, its testimony is printed whole, because an editor who prints only the winner of a bibliographical argument makes the reader a hostage to that argument.

Her "Editorial Procedures" section translates the theory into rules of the house, and the rules are the theory's proof of seriousness. The main text modernizes the 1599 quarto's spelling and punctuation, regularizes its chaotic speech headings, and emends only under stated discipline, with the collation recording departures. The 1597 text is modernized by the same conventions and annotated selectively, at the points where it differs significantly from the later text, so that the second text functions as its own commentary on the first state of the play. Stage directions from the short quarto that illuminate the long text's staging are discussed in the commentary rather than silently absorbed into the printed directions of the main text, a scruple that distinguishes her practice from the older eclecticism at exactly the point where that practice was least examined. Previous editors had imported the short text's directions into the long text as a matter of routine, marking the import with brackets when they marked it at all; Levenson keeps the two books' integrities visible and makes the reader do the combining, with her notes as guide.

The theory also explains a small decision with large consequences for how the volume reads: the order of its texts. The main text comes first, at full scale, because the 1599 quarto is the fuller witness and the natural reading text; the 1597 text follows, complete, because anything less would restore the old hierarchy in physical form. A reader experiences the edition, therefore, as the discipline experienced the play: first the ample version, learned by heart across four centuries of conflated editions, and then the shock of recognition and difference as the shorter, faster version replays the same story at performance tempo. Critics of the arrangement have observed that placing the short text second still subordinates it, and the observation is just as far as it goes; but the subordination hers enforces is presentational rather than evidentiary, a matter of reading order rather than of suppressed testimony, and it is the mildest hierarchy compatible with a single volume. The alternative, a parallel text edition on facing pages, would have made comparison easier and sustained reading of either version harder, and would have priced the book beyond the students for whom the paperback was always intended.

### Where do the Oxford edition's stage directions come from?

The main text's directions descend from its copy text, the 1599 quarto, supplemented under discipline where that book is defective. Where the 1597 quarto offers a fuller or more actable direction, Levenson's commentary discusses the alternative rather than silently merging it into the printed text, and her supplied directions follow precedents reaching back to Edward Capell's edition of 1768.

One documented example concentrates the policy. In the feast scene, the second quarto's direction for the entrance of the guests is vague, and editors have filled it variously for two centuries: Gibbons prints a crowded entrance naming Capulet, Lady Capulet, Juliet, Tybalt, and the Nurse, while Levenson, following Capell, prints the sparer "Capulet and attendants," declining to populate the stage beyond what the text requires. When the Nurse interrupts the lovers' first conversation with "Madam, your mother craves a word with you" (1.5.107), the quartos supply no direction for Juliet's response, and Levenson prints a modest supplied direction, adapted from John Jowett's practice, that moves Juliet toward her mother, staging the interruption without inventing business. The pattern is consistent: the edition intervenes where a reader needs the stage picture, intervenes minimally, and leaves a record. In an edition whose editor had spent fifteen years among promptbooks, the restraint is itself a finding: the promptbooks taught her how freely theatres invent such business, and therefore how carefully an editor should.

## The Edition in Practice: Text, Directions, and Commentary

A scholarly edition is finally judged at the page, and Levenson's page differs from her rivals' in the density and kind of its attention. The commentary is celebrated and, in some quarters, reproached for its fullness; the Review of English Studies, in its notice of the volume, observed that many students will no doubt relish the fullness of Professor Levenson's notes and be grateful for the care with which she directs readers to fuller discussions elsewhere. The fullness has a specific character. Where a traditional commentary glosses words, traces sources, and identifies allusions, Levenson's does those things and adds a fourth: it reports the stage. Notes on the great speeches register how they have been cut, transposed, restored, or reassigned in the promptbook record; notes on scenes record the staging traditions that have accreted to them, the business that audiences have come to expect whether or not the early texts prescribe it. The commentary is where the promptbook archive enters the edition's bloodstream, not as a separate performance history confined to the introduction but as a running evidentiary layer beneath the reading of every scene.

### What does the edition's commentary record that earlier editions left out?

The commentary records the play's theatrical life inside the notes to the text: the cuts, transpositions, and staging traditions documented in the promptbook archive Levenson assembled. Where earlier commentaries confined themselves to glossing language, sources, and allusions, hers adds the evidence of performance, reporting how generations of companies handled a speech or a scene across four centuries.

The volume's apparatus supports the practice with a completeness that signals its intended reader: scholars and advanced students who will check the editor's work. The collations record the substantive variants of the early texts at the foot of each page of the main text; the abbreviations and references section keys a bibliography that runs from the earliest editions through the promptbook catalogues of Shattuck and Halstead to the performance studies of the 1990s; the illustrations reproduce the visual record, title pages and production images, that the history discusses. Nothing is asked to be taken on faith. An edition that argues the text is mobile must be auditable in its own procedures, and Levenson's book is built to be audited, which is one reason its conclusions have proved hard to dislodge: a critic who would overturn a reading must first work through the evidence she prints beneath it.

The textual notes exhibit the same doubleness. Consider the opening of the play. The 1597 quarto begins with a direction for "two Serving-men of the Capulets," unnamed, where the 1599 quarto names Sampson and Gregory and arms them, "with Swords and Bucklers, of the house of Capulet." Levenson's edited first text regularizes the long quarto's naming into consistent speech headings for her two serving-men, while her presentation of the short text lets a reader watch the scene run on its anonymous, functional designations, closer to a company working from parts than to a reader following characters. The difference is small and emblematic: the long text reads like literature in preparation, the short text like a show in progress, and only an edition that prints both lets the difference be felt rather than asserted. Her handling of the play's most famous cruxes follows the same principle of exhibited evidence. Where the 1599 quarto prints the dawn quatrain twice, once for Romeo closing the balcony scene and once for the friar opening the next, her commentary lays the two assignments side by side and sets the short quarto's simpler disposition, in which the friar alone speaks the lines, before the reader as the standing alternative a director may weigh. The reader is trusted with the fork in the road.

Two further exhibits show the practice at its most characteristic. The first is the Chorus. The long text prints a Chorus before Act 2, the sonnet that begins "Now old desire doth in his deathbed lie" (2 Chorus, 1), recapitulating the first act and promising the new love's course; the short text lacks that speech, moving from the first act's close into the scenes of the night without the Chorus's mediation. Editors before Levenson noted the absence in a collation and moved on. Her edition makes the difference available to experience: a reader can finish the first text's Act 1 and feel the Chorus gather the action into pattern, then read the short text and feel the action run on without the pattern, faster and less mediated. The second exhibit is her commentary's handling of the play's sources. At the famous turns, the balcony, the potion, the tomb, her notes set Shakespeare's lines beside Arthur Brooke's Romeus and Juliet and the other transmitters her narrative history has already introduced, so that the reader watches the transmutation her introduction describes: Brooke's moralizing dilation compressed into stage time, his warnings converted into ironies. Commentary and introduction are thus one instrument played at two speeds, the history arguing the thesis and the notes exhibiting it line by line.

The introduction's remaining sections support this practice with unusual completeness. The narrative history traces the story from its mythic analogues through the Italian novella and its French and English transmitters, Bandello and Boaistuau, Painter and Brooke, showing in detail how the tale changed as it crossed languages and borders before Shakespeare remade it; the section on the play treats its language, its rhetoric, and its dramatic forms; the performance history and the textual essay follow. The volume closes the circle by giving the short quarto the same dignity of print as the long: modern spelling, numbered lines, and annotation at need. Nothing in the book is vestigial. Each part performs the edition's thesis that the play is a mobile text, known fully only when its states and its stagings are known together.

The commentary's literary quality should not be lost inside the praise of its archive. Levenson glosses the play as a critic of the first rank, attentive to the conceits' logic, to the way the lovers' shared sonnet in the feast scene trains the audience to hear their later exchanges as collaborative composition, to the Nurse's prose as a counter rhetoric of appetite and anecdote against the courtly forms, and to Mercutio's Queen Mab speech as the play's one set piece of pure improvisation, a form that exists to be unrepeatable. Her notes resist the schoolroom habit of paraphrase; they identify what a figure is doing, name the convention it invokes, and point to the promptbook or the parallel passage that shows the convention at work. A student who reads the edition through acquires, almost without noticing, an education in the period's rhetoric and in the period's theatre at once, which is precisely the double literacy the introduction's reading of the play requires. Reviewers who praised the fullness of the notes were registering this density: the commentary is not an apparatus attached to a reading but the reading itself, distributed along the text.

## Performance History as an Editor's Evidence

The performance history in Levenson's introduction is the longest and most consequential in any edition of the play, and its position in the volume is an argument in itself. Editions before hers typically relegated stage history to a brisk survey, a pageant of famous Juliets, inserted between the source study and the textual essay as a courtesy to the theatre. Levenson inverts the hierarchy. Having spent the years since the mid-1980s profiling promptbooks, she writes the history from the archive outward, and the history she writes is one of continuous transformation interrupted by five revivals of exceptional durability. Her finding about the Georgian stage, that the play's 399 London performances between 1751 and 1800 eclipsed even Hamlet, repositions the tragedy in the eighteenth century's repertory: not a lyrical curiosity sustained by Garrick's alterations, but the era's most serviceable serious play, remade by every management that staged it. The history continues through the nineteenth century's pictorial stagings and the Cushman sensation, into the textual restorations of the 1930s, the austerity of Brook, the naturalism of Zeffirelli, and the screen afterlife that carried the play to audiences no playhouse could hold.

### Which five productions does the edition treat as the play's durable stage interpretations?

The five productions are David Garrick's of 1748, Charlotte Cushman's of 1845 and 1846, John Gielgud's of 1935, Peter Brook's of 1947, and Franco Zeffirelli's, staged at the Old Vic in 1960 and filmed in 1968. Together they map the play's transformations: adaptation, recovery of the hero, restoration of the text, austerity, and cinematic naturalism.

The archive also lets her write the Georgian chapter of the history with a specificity no earlier edition attempted. The rivalry of 1750, when Garrick's Drury Lane Romeo and Juliet ran against Spranger Barry and Mrs. Cibber at Covent Garden in a twelve night contest the town called a war of the Romeos, is the kind of episode a survey mentions and an archive documents: her performance history can follow the contest's effect on the acting text, the retention of Garrick's altered tomb scene, in which the lovers speak before they die, as the English stage's settled ending for generations, and the slow accretion of business around the balcony, the procession, and the tomb that later editors would mistake for the play itself. Her count of 399 London performances in the half century after 1750 gives the episode its frame: this was the repertory's workhorse, and the promptbooks show managements treating it as capital, to be renovated rather than replaced. The nineteenth century's chapter is written from the same evidence, the era of pictorial staging and of Cushman's Romeo, whose authority her notes register in the promptbooks' preservation of her readings, and the twentieth century's chapter follows the restorations and reactions, Gielgud's fullness against Brook's severity, that the five production framework organizes.

The five are not a hall of fame but a diagnostic set. Garrick's version shows the play surviving by alteration, its ending rewritten to give the lovers a dying dialogue, its text trimmed of Rosaline and of much else, yet dominating the stage for the rest of the century. Cushman's Romeo shows the play surviving by interpretation, a single performance of such authority that it rebalanced the tragedy around its hero for a generation. Gielgud's production shows the play surviving by restoration, reinstating the full text, the Prologue included, against the acting tradition's cuts. Brook's shows it surviving by subtraction of a different kind, speed and bare boards against Victorian upholstery, with the final reconciliation among the casualties. Zeffirelli's shows it surviving by translation into a new medium, the camera's Verona of real streets and real adolescents. An editor who organizes the history this way claims that the edited text should be answerable to all five modes of survival, and her commentary keeps the claim: its notes know what Garrick cut, what Gielgud restored, what Brook refused to play.

The archive behind the history has had an afterlife of its own, and it belongs to the edition's contribution. Levenson's promptbook profiles were entered into a database, built with colleagues and graduate students at the University of Toronto under a grant from the Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council of Canada, and transferred to the web under the care of the Centre for Renaissance and Reformation Studies, where the records remain searchable by production, scene, and line. The edition, that is, generated a research instrument that outlasts its own pages: any scholar can now trace how a given speech fared in the promptbooks, which generations cut it, and which restored it. Researchers assembling comparable profiles of their own, tracking how a scene mutates across a shelf of marked copies, keep their working notes in tools built for exactly that patient collation, and a local-first research notebook such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/) serves the same discipline of accumulating evidence before argument. The methodological point is Levenson's own: theatre history becomes scholarship when its documents are methodized, and the Oxford edition is the proof of concept, the first edition of the play whose performance claims can be audited against a public archive.

The use the edition makes of that archive inside the commentary deserves one concrete instance. In the feast scene, after the masquers enter, the text gives the servants a stretch of dialogue, clearing the stage picture and grumbling about the great chamber, that many readers pass over. Levenson's note reports the promptbook evidence: many promptbooks omit the servants' dialogue, and most have the masquers exit and re-enter for the ball, regularizing a transition the early texts leave fluid. The note does not instruct a director; it documents what directors have done, and in documenting it, explains why the scene feels different in editions and productions that adopt the regularization. Multiply that note by the play's length and the edition's character stands revealed: a text accompanied by the minutes of four centuries of meetings between the play and its performers.

## The Introduction's Reading: A Play Made of Forms

An edition's introduction is also a work of criticism, and Levenson's reading of the play, set out in the section titled "Romeo and Juliet: the Play," holds that the tragedy is an assembly of the set forms its culture prized, sonnet, aubade, blazon, epithalamium, lament, and elegy among them, and that Shakespeare's transmutation of his sources reflects his moment's concerns with love, death, adolescence, and patriarchism. The claim sounds taxonomic until it is tested against the verse. The lovers' first exchange is a sonnet built by two speakers, opening "If I profane with my unworthiest hand" (1.5.92) and closing with the kiss its form has been negotiating, "You kiss by th' book" (1.5.109). The dawn parting is an aubade argued in the form's own terms, "Wilt thou be gone? It is not yet near day" (3.5.1), in which the lovers dispute whether the bird they hear is the lark or the nightingale because the form tells them what each answer costs. Juliet's wait for the wedding night is an epithalamium turned inward, "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds" (3.2.1). Levenson's point is that these are not decorations applied to a story but the story's machinery: the characters think, woo, and deceive themselves inside the period's inherited forms, and the tragedy is what happens when the forms run out.

The same introduction reads the forms socially as well as rhetorically, and here its documented concerns, love, death, adolescence, and patriarchism, come into play. The sonnet the lovers share is a courtship ritual from a culture that scripted courtship; the marriage it accelerates is a transaction in a patriarchal market, announced when Capulet tells Paris that his consent is the larger share but that Juliet's agreement counts within it, and exposed when the market turns coercive: "An you be mine, I'll give you to my friend" (3.5.191). The aubade's lark and nightingale are a young wife's instruments for negotiating a curfew imposed by the household that owns her time. Levenson's reading holds the two registers together: the play's forms are beautiful, and they are the machinery by which Verona's arrangements reproduce themselves, which is why the lovers' mastery of the forms, their sonnets, their aubades, their elegies, cannot save them. The forms belong to the city. Her emphasis on adolescence sharpens the point: the play's principals are teenagers negotiating adult instruments, marriage contracts, feuds, potions, and dirges, with a fluency that outruns their experience, and the introduction treats that mismatch, rather than any flaw of character, as the tragedy's engine.

This reading aligns her edition, in a way its textual radicalism might not predict, with the formalist criticism of the play's structure. [Susan Snyder: comedy turning to tragedy](/2026/12/11/susan-snyder-comedy-to-tragedy/) in her classic account argues that the play's first movement obeys the design of comedy and is broken across Mercutio's death, a reading Levenson's attention to form supports from the editorial side, since the comic forms, the feast, the bawdy servants, the blocking father, and the go-between nurse, are all present in the early acts as working machinery rather than decoration. Where Snyder traces the generic hinge, Levenson documents the formal inventory that makes the hinge possible: the sonnet culture of the 1590s supplied the play its love language, and the elegiac culture supplied its ending, so that the tragedy's shape emerges from the forms it assembles and then exhausts. The introduction's reading is therefore continuous with the edition's textual practice. A play made of mobile, inherited forms, remade by each speaker who takes them up, is a play whose text should be expected to move.

On the ending, Levenson's reading is characteristically measured, and her measure has become the edition's most quoted critical position. The Prince's sentence over the bodies distributes responsibility without relieving it, "All are punished" (5.3.295), and the reconciliation that follows is real but impoverished: two fathers who could not keep their children alive compete to memorialize them, Montague promising "For I will raise her statue in pure gold" (5.3.299) and Capulet answering that Romeo's statue shall lie as rich beside his lady's. The final line of the play's action, the Prince's "A glooming peace this morning with it brings" (5.3.305), names a peace qualified by the adjective that darkens it, and Levenson's introduction dwells on that qualification: the ending restores order without restoring value, and the golden statues stand in the text as the city's admission that it can honour the lovers only as monuments. The reading matters editorially because it is textually alert. It hears the ending's flatness as written, not as a failure of the writing, and it refuses the sentimental tradition, descending from Garrick's rewritten tomb, that supplied the lovers a consummation the early texts withhold.

## How Far Can Q1 Be Trusted? The Complication Adjudicated

The brief for this article names its complication plainly: how far to trust the readings of the 1597 quarto. The question has three live answers in the scholarship, and the edition's distinction is to have changed the terms on which all three operate. The oldest answer, the orthodoxy Levenson inherited, holds that the short quarto is a memorial reconstruction, reported by actors, and trustworthy only in its stage directions and where the long text is manifestly corrupt. The second answer, associated with the revisionist scholarship that gathered force in the 1990s, holds that the short quarto may descend from Shakespeare's own early draft, the play in a first state before augmentation, and is therefore trustworthy as authorial evidence throughout, a version rather than a corruption. The third answer is Lukas Erne's, whose Cambridge edition of the first quarto argued, from the book's bibliographical character, that it was set from a manuscript close to the author's early papers, making the short text a literary document of the play's composition rather than a playhouse souvenir.

Levenson's position declines to choose among these genealogies, and the refusal is the part of her work most often mistaken for evasion. Her introduction weighs the memorial reconstruction hypothesis and finds its traditional form unproven for this play; she weighs the draft hypothesis and finds it attractive where the short text's differences look like composition rather than compression; and she concludes that the responsible editorial act is to give the reader both texts entire, so that the dispute about origins is conducted in the reader's presence with the evidence open. The adjudication offered here sustains her. On the documentary record, no genealogy of the short quarto has been proved to the standard that would justify suppressing the rival text: the memorial theory explains its verbal degradation but not its theatrical intelligence or its Kemp direction; the draft theory explains its alternative dispositions but not its memorial-looking errors; Erne's account explains its authority but relocates rather than settles the question of why it is short. In that evidential situation, the editor who prints both texts is not avoiding a decision. The decision is that no single origin story earns the right to confiscate a witness.

A single famous line shows the apportionment working at the smallest scale. At the centre of the balcony scene Juliet asks, "What's in a name? That which we call a rose" (2.2.43), and the two quartos divide on the sentence's completion: the long text reads that a rose by any other word would smell as sweet, the short text reads by any other name. The variant is tiny and total, since the speech is an argument that names are conventions, and the short text's version makes the argument in the speech's own key word while the long text's varies it. Levenson's main text follows her copy text and prints the long quarto's reading, with the short text's alternative standing in the second text and the collation for any reader to weigh. The eclectic tradition had generally done the same while deploring the witness it declined; her edition does it while honouring the witness, and the difference, repeated across three thousand lines, is the edition.

The memorial case deserves its strongest statement before the adjudication closes, because Levenson's introduction gives it one and answers it. Its fullest form for this play is Harry R. Hoppe's bibliographical and textual study of 1948, which argued that the short quarto was a memorial reconstruction, put together from the memories of actors in the company: the text is fullest and most accurate, on his showing, in and around the scenes its supposed reporters played, and degrades in proportion to its distance from their parts. The argument was the most elegant the memorial theory produced for any play, and its elegance did real work for fifty years. Its weakness, which the revisionist scholarship of the 1990s pressed and Levenson's introduction registers, is that the pattern it explains can be produced by other causes: a company adapting the play for performance would also preserve the crowd pleasing scenes entire and compress the connective tissue, and an early draft would show the author's own unevenness of finish. A pattern consistent with three genealogies cannot convict one of them. Her edition's refusal to convict is therefore not indecision but the correct response to an underdetermined record, and the proof of the refusal's value is practical: whichever genealogy future scholarship establishes, her volume will not need to be redone, since both witnesses stand in it entire, with the evidence for the argument printed beneath them.

Trust, then, is apportioned by category in her edition, and the apportionment can be stated as a rule other editors have since worked with. For the wording of famous speeches, the long quarto governs, and the short text's variants are recorded and weighed case by case, some accepted where the long text errs. For the shape of the play in performance, the short text is a first class witness, its compressions and dispositions evidence of how the work played in its own decade. For the large interpretation of the play, the two texts are read against each other, the swift theatrical version exposing the long text's literary amplitude, the long text exposing the short version's economies. An editor's trust in a document, Levenson's practice implies, should never be a verdict pronounced on the whole book in advance of the questions asked of it. That implication, more than any single reading she adopted, is her contribution's durable core.

## The Introduction as Criticism: The Compendium Reading Assessed

Levenson's introduction also stands or falls as criticism, apart from its editorial architecture, and its central claim, that the play is a distinctive compendium of its culture's literary and dramatic forms, deserves the scrutiny the edition invites for its texts. The claim's great strength is explanatory reach. Romeo and Juliet is a play that shifts register without warning: the street brawl's prose clowning gives way to a sonnet prologue's high formalism; Mercutio's fantastical riff on Queen Mab sits a scene away from the liturgy of the lovers' first exchange; the Nurse's garrulous prose memory of weaning Juliet stands against the friar's rhymed sententiae; the wedding dirge of the fourth act dissolves into the musicians' comedy of cat gut and silver sound. Criticism that reads the play as a pure love tragedy must apologize for half of this material; criticism that reads it as a failed tragedy or a comedy manqué apologizes for the other half. The compendium reading apologizes for nothing. It holds that the miscellany is the method, that Shakespeare built the tragedy out of the period's whole cupboard of forms so that the lovers' story could be told in every voice their culture possessed, and that the voices' failure to harmonize is the tragedy's sound. Her reading of the ending follows: the glooming peace is spoken over a stage full of incompatible forms that have each had their say and none of which has saved anyone.

The reading has an honest friction point, and stating it fairly is part of assessing her. A compendium emphasis can flatten the play's forward drive, making the tragedy sound like an anthology when in performance it feels like a trap closing; the speed of the action, the four days' compression, the missed letter, are not forms but mechanisms, and an account of the play that lingers on its rhetoric may understate its engineering. Levenson's introduction guards against the flattening better than most formalist criticism does, because her performance history insists on the engineering: the promptbooks show generations of managers wrestling with the play's pace, trimming the rhetoric to keep the trap closing, restoring the rhetoric when the pace could bear it. The compendium and the machine coexist in her account because her evidence for both is documentary: the forms from the texts, the pace from the archive. It is the combination, forms in the study and machinery on the stage, that makes her introduction the rare critical essay an acting company could also use, and it explains why the volume is so often read whole rather than consulted in parts.

The practical stakes of the adjudication deserve a final word, because they are felt in rehearsal rooms rather than in journals. A company choosing a script for a touring production, a school tour, or a ninety minute festival slot faces the quartos' difference as a casting and timing problem before it faces it as a theory: the long text runs long, and its rhetoric asks for voices trained to sustain it, while the short text offers the whole arc of the tragedy at a pace a small company can tour. Before Levenson, such a company worked from a conflated text and cut it, reproducing in ignorance the proportions the short quarto already embodies; after her edition, the company can start from the short text itself, printed legibly, and defend the choice in the programme with an editor's authority. The edition's gift to the theatre is the mirror of its gift to the study: it returned a suppressed document to circulation and let practitioners, not only scholars, discover what it is good for.

## The Arden Answer: Weis, Conflation, and the Reviewers

The Arden Shakespeare replied to Levenson in 2012, when René Weis and the Arden Third Series published the successor to Gibbons's volume, and the reply is instructive precisely because it split its allegiances. Weis's introduction is the work of a thoroughly modern editor in its treatment of the play's afterlives, performance possibilities, and screen incarnations, and it argues, with warmth, that the play is ultimately Juliet's. Its text, however, returned to the older eclecticism Levenson had refused: a copy text in the second quarto, liberally patched with readings and directions from the first, the two books blended into a single acting text of the kind the nineteenth century would have recognized. The full study of [Rene Weis and the Arden Third Series](/2027/06/25/rene-weis-arden-third-series/) belongs to its own article in this series; the point here is the contrast, because the Arden Third's textual method drew the sharpest critical fire directed at any edition of the play in the period, and the fire illuminates, by opposition, what Levenson had achieved.

Emma Smith, reviewing Weis's edition in the Review of English Studies, put the objection in its canonical form. The emendations from the first quarto, she found, lack a consistent rationale: of two dozen stage directions from the short quarto, all but two are adopted wholesale, while among its verbal readings some are taken on new scholarly argument and others on literary preference, so that in Mercutio's Queen Mab speech the printed line combines one quarto's adjective with the other quarto's noun phrase to yield a reading, pricked from the lazy finger of a maid, that exists in neither early book. The line Weis prints, Smith concluded, may be more internally coherent than either quarto's, but it has no consistent principle of textual selection behind it. The charge is the classic charge against eclectic editing: the edited text becomes a fourth document, of the editor's manufacture, answerable to no witness. It is the charge Levenson's two text format was designed, twelve years earlier, to render impossible against her own edition. Her text may be argued with at every line, since her collations expose the evidence, but it cannot be accused of existing in no early book: her long text is the 1599 quarto modernized under stated rules, and her short text is the 1597 quarto entire.

The reviewers of Levenson's own volume, writing closer to its appearance, had registered both the achievement and the standing reservation. The Review of English Studies notice praised the performance history as excellent and often very thought provoking, judged the inclusion of the whole first quarto an excellent decision, and forecast that students would relish the commentary's fullness. The reservations that gathered around the edition in the years after were of two kinds. Textual scholars attached to the draft theory of the short quarto wanted the edition to declare that genealogy outright, and treated her agnosticism as a missed conclusion; literary scholars formed by Lukas Erne's work on Shakespeare as a literary dramatist worried that an edition so centred on performance understated the extent to which the long quarto was prepared for readers, its fullness a design for the page as much as a surplus over the stage. Both reservations are answered, in part, by the edition's architecture. The two text format serves the draft theorist's reader as well as the performance historian's, since the short text is there to be read as a version whichever genealogy is preferred; and the long text is presented with a commentary of literary density that no one has accused of thinness. What the reservations establish is the edition's true limit: it is a frame, not a verdict, and readers who want an editor to decide the quartos' origins for them must look elsewhere, accepting the confiscations that such decisions perform.

## The Edition's Second Life: Classrooms, Databases, and the Editors Who Followed

An edition's contribution is completed by its uptake, and Levenson's volume has been absorbed into the discipline's working habits in ways that repay attention. It became the scholarly text of the play most often cited for the introduction's twin authorities, the performance history and the mobile text essay, and its lineation and readings entered the citation stream of the journals. Drama programmes took from it a practice its editor had not explicitly prescribed: scenes from the short quarto began to be assigned and even staged as a legitimate object of study, a ninety minute playing version of the tragedy that student companies could mount entire, because her edition had made the short text readable without a trip to a rare book room. The Cambridge Early Quartos series, which issued Lukas Erne's edition of the first quarto as a volume in its own right, normalized the premise her volume had argued: a short quarto is a book, deserving the full editorial honours of print, introduction, and commentary.

The influence runs through the successor editions as well, including the one that rejected her textual method. Weis's Arden Third introduction, for all its return to conflation, is saturated with the play's performance and screen life, its notes attend to playing the love scenes through the ages, and its appendix prints a facsimile of the first quarto: the gestures of an editor working after Levenson, for whom performance evidence and the short quarto's presence in the volume had become expectations a scholarly edition must meet, even when its text is built by other rules. Gibbons's generation could treat both as optional. After 2000, neither was. That shift in the profession's defaults, more than any single reading or note, is the edition's institutional legacy, and it is the reason the volume continues to be the reference point from which accounts of the play's editing begin.

Her own later career continued the same preoccupations. The scholar who had co-edited Shakespeare and the Twentieth Century with Jonathan Bate and Dieter Mehl turned, after the edition, to a book on Shakespeare and modern drama for the Oxford Shakespeare Topics series, extending into the modern theatre the question her edition had asked of the early one: what happens to these plays as each age remakes them. The edition remains the centre of her reputation, and its database remains in service at Toronto, open ended by design, so that the archive she built to edit a play has outlasted the edition it built and stands ready to serve the next editor who disputes her.

The mobile text idea itself travelled beyond this play. Textual scholarship in the decades after the edition appeared reached increasingly for the language of versions, states, and revision that her introduction had naturalized for Romeo and Juliet, and studies of other two text plays, of the Hamlet quartos and the Lear versions above all, conducted their arguments in a climate her volume helped to create: one in which the burden of proof rests on whoever would suppress a substantive early text, and in which a play's performance life counts as evidence about its texts rather than as decoration upon them. Levenson coined no slogan and founded no school; the concept spread because it described the documents better than the alternatives did, and because her edition demonstrated, in the hardest case the canon offered, that an editor could act on the description without dissolving the reading text into chaos. The main text remained a main text. The witnesses were printed, the procedures stated, the choices owned.

The edition's passage into the classroom completed the circuit. Graduate seminars in textual studies set her procedures beside the Greg tradition she had revised; undergraduate courses that could not assign the whole volume assigned the mobile text essay as the orientation for any encounter with the play's two quartos; and the paperback's price and format carried the two text idea to readers who would never open a bibliographical journal. An idea about the ontology of play scripts became, through one well made book, part of the common knowledge of the profession, which is the rarest afterlife an editorial theory can have.

One measure of the settlement is bibliographical and can be checked in any research library. Companion volumes and handbooks to the play that appeared after the edition take its texts as their reference points, quote its introduction for the performance history, and send readers to its second text for the 1597 version as a matter of course; the pre-2000 scholarship had cited the short quarto, when it cited the book at all, through the variorum or through facsimiles that few libraries held open on the shelf. Levenson moved a rare book document into the working library of every student of the play, in a modernized, annotated, affordable form, and that act of distribution is inseparable from the edition's intellectual effect: arguments about the short quarto after the edition are arguments among readers who have all read it, which is a different and better scholarly world than the one in which the text's character was debated from report.

## The Verdict on Levenson's Contribution

The verdict can now be drawn with the evidence assembled. Levenson's contribution to the editing of Romeo and Juliet is threefold, and each fold survives the criticisms recorded above. First, she settled the standing of the 1597 quarto by the only settlement the evidence supports: not acquittal, not conviction, but full publication, with its status as the secondary witness marked by the economy of its annotation. The bad quarto verdict, which had governed the play's editing for most of a century, cannot be reimposed after her volume, because her volume put the supposedly corrupt text into every reader's hands in readable form, and readers who have read it do not consent to its suppression. Second, she made performance history an editorial instrument rather than an ornament, building an edition whose claims about staging rest on a public, auditable archive of promptbook profiles, and whose commentary carries that archive into the reading of scene after scene. Third, she modelled an editorial ethics for a two text play: print the witnesses, state the procedures, debate the alternatives in the open, and decline to manufacture a composite that flatters the editor's taste at the evidence's expense. The contrast with the conflated Arden Third text, and with the reservations its method drew from reviewers, is the measure of the model's timeliness.

A comparison with the play's most popular classroom text sharpens the verdict by contrast of purpose. The Folger Shakespeare Library edition, edited by Barbara A. Mowat and Paul Werstine, serves the general reader and the school student with facing page glosses, illustrations, and essays pitched to first encounters; it is an admirable instrument for its audience, and its text, founded on the second quarto with the Folio consulted, makes its peace with the tradition quietly. Levenson's volume addresses a different reader, the one who asks why the text reads as it does and what the alternatives are, and it answers at a depth the Folger format does not attempt. The two editions are not rivals so much as successive instruments, the Folger for the first reading, the Oxford for the second and all the readings after, when the play's doubleness has become the reader's own question. That a single play sustains both instruments, and rewards the passage from one to the other, is itself a testimony to the mobility her edition describes.

The edition's limits should be recorded with the same plainness. Its agnosticism about the short quarto's origin leaves the volume's central question theoretically open, and readers who regard the draft hypothesis as proved will find the introduction cautious to a fault. Its performance emphasis, for all the commentary's literary richness, gives less weight than Erne's scholarship does to the possibility that the long text's amplitude was designed for readers, a possibility the edition acknowledges without pursuing. Its sheer fullness, the quality its first reviewers praised, makes it a demanding first text for a new student, who may be better served initially by a slimmer volume and brought to Levenson when the questions her book answers have begun to press. None of these limits touches the settlement itself. Among the modern editions compared by anyone choosing how to read, teach, or stage the play, hers remains the fullest record of what the play has been: two early texts, four centuries of performance, and an editor who insisted that an edition should contain the evidence for its own conclusions. The edition's deepest claim on the future is methodological, and it can be stated as the lesson her career teaches editors of the other two text plays. Evidence kept public outlasts theory. Levenson's agnosticism may be overturned by a future proof of the short quarto's origin, her performance history will certainly be supplemented, and her reading of the play's forms will be argued with by critics unborn; but the promptbook profiles, the full printing of the second text, and the procedures stated in the open will remain usable by whoever overturns her, because she built the volume so that its evidence could survive its conclusions. That is the sense in which the edition deserves the word settlement: not that it ended argument, but that it fixed the ground on which argument about this play must now proceed, the two quartos in every reader's hands and four centuries of performance on the record. Editors are usually remembered, if at all, as obstacles or as conduits. Levenson arranged to be remembered as neither: the edition effaces its maker into its method, and the method, print the witnesses, count the evidence, follow the documents, is the monument.

The cliche about scholarly editing holds that editors bury plays under learning. Levenson's edition is the standing refutation for this play: the learning is the means by which the tragedy, in both its early states and its long stage life, is handed back to its readers entire.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Levenson profile, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch Levenson profile is the table in this article that sets out Jill Levenson's Oxford edition feature by feature: its two modernized texts, its mobile text theory, its promptbook archive of some 170 profiles, its commentary, its direction policy, and the database its research produced. The pattern it exposes is a refusal of forced choices. At every point where the editorial tradition demanded a choice between the 1597 and 1599 quartos, between the study and the stage, between gloss and performance record, Levenson built the structure that holds both terms, and the profile arranges her choices so that each can be checked against the argument of the article.

### Q: Who is Jill Levenson, and where did she teach?

Jill L. Levenson is a professor of English at Trinity College in the University of Toronto and the editor of the Oxford Shakespeare Romeo and Juliet of 2000. Her scholarship joined textual editing to performance history: she wrote the play's volume in the Manchester University Press series Shakespeare in Performance in 1987, co-edited Shakespeare and the Twentieth Century, and published the essay "Show Business: The Editor in the Theater" in 1999. The Toronto base mattered to the edition's making, since the promptbook profiles she assembled were later entered into a searchable database under the care of the university's Centre for Renaissance and Reformation Studies.

### Q: When was the Oxford edition of Romeo and Juliet published, and who published it?

The Oxford edition appeared in 2000 from Oxford University Press, in the series called The Oxford Shakespeare. It presented William Shakespeare's play in two modernized texts, the 1599 second quarto as the main text and the complete 1597 first quarto after it, with an introduction covering the story's narrative history, the play itself, its performance history, its date, its mobile text, and the editor's procedures. The volume succeeded the Oxford Complete Works text of the 1980s as the press's scholarly statement on the play, and it has remained the Oxford single play edition against which the Arden, Cambridge, and Folger volumes are compared.

### Q: Does the Oxford edition print the whole of Q1 or only selected passages?

The edition prints the whole of the 1597 first quarto, as a complete modernized text under its own title, An Excellent Conceited Tragedy of Romeo and Juliet, following the main text based on the 1599 quarto. This was the edition's most remarked decision: earlier editors had printed the short quarto only in fragments, quoted in collations and notes, while Levenson gave it continuous form, numbered lines, and annotation at the points where it differs significantly from the later text. The Review of English Studies called the inclusion of the whole first quarto an excellent decision. A reader of the volume can therefore read the short text as a play and compare the two states of the work directly.

### Q: Why does Levenson call the play's text mobile?

Levenson calls the text mobile because the play changed at every stage of its existence: in composition, as Shakespeare wrote and augmented it; in the playhouse, as his company shaped it for performance; and across four centuries of revivals, adaptations, films, operas, and ballets. Her introduction's section "The Mobile Text" argues that the 1597 and 1599 quartos are two surviving stations of that movement rather than an original and its corruption. The term replaces the older bibliographical picture of a fixed text degenerating in transmission. On her account, mobility is the normal condition of a play script, and an edition should exhibit the movement rather than freeze one moment of it and suppress the rest.

### Q: What did Levenson write about the play before she edited it?

Before the edition, Levenson wrote Romeo and Juliet for the Shakespeare in Performance series, published by Manchester University Press in 1987. The book reconstructed the Elizabethan production and then examined five decisive revivals: David Garrick's of 1748, Charlotte Cushman's of 1845 and 1846, John Gielgud's of 1935, Peter Brook's of 1947, and Franco Zeffirelli's of 1960 and 1968. Its first study rebuilds the Elizabethan production, the play at the Theatre or the Curtain in the mid-1590s, a company of sharers and hired men doubling the citizens of Verona, the boys playing Juliet and the Nurse, the open stage absorbing the street, the orchard, and the tomb without scenery, assembled candidly from the texts' directions and the period's theatrical documents. Its argument, that later versions of the play had differed in tone and effect from the Elizabethan original while drawing their power from it, formed the outlook of the Oxford edition: the 1987 study taught its editor the play's stage shape, and the edition's performance history grew from the same research.

### Q: What is "Show Business: The Editor in the Theater" about?

"Show Business: The Editor in the Theater" is Levenson's methodological essay of 1999, published in Shakespeare: Text and Theater, a collection honouring Jay L. Halio. It describes how she used promptbooks as evidence in preparing the Oxford edition: the marked copies from which productions were staged, profiled for their cuts and staging notes, and read as documents of the play's theatrical life. The essay explains her two phase procedure, probing the early quartos for staging clues and studying later theatrical texts for the adjustments performance generated. It is the fullest published account of the method behind the edition, and the Oxford volume's performance history and commentary are its application.

### Q: Which earlier editor's view of Q1 does Levenson's edition break with?

Levenson's edition breaks most directly with the settlement represented by Brian Gibbons's Arden Second Series edition of 1980, the standard scholarly text of the preceding twenty years. Gibbons based his text on the 1599 quarto and treated the 1597 quarto as a reported text of derivative authority, admitting its readings only where the preferred text seemed deficient, so that a reader met the short quarto only in fragments. Levenson rejected that hierarchy's consequence, though not the 1599 quarto's primacy as the reading text: her volume prints the short quarto entire, treats it as an independent witness, and reverses the burden of proof that had kept it in the apparatus.

### Q: What does Lukas Erne argue about Q1 that differs from Levenson's account?

Lukas Erne argues that the 1597 quarto was set in print from a manuscript close to Shakespeare's own early papers, making it a document of the play's composition rather than a playhouse record. His Cambridge edition of the first quarto develops that case from the book's bibliographical character. Levenson's account differs in method rather than in allegiance: her introduction weighs the rival genealogies of the short quarto, memorial reconstruction, early draft, and performance adaptation, and declines to stake the edition on any one of them. Where Erne offers a genealogy, Levenson offers a frame, printing both early texts entire so that readers can test each theory against the evidence.

### Q: How does Levenson's edition differ from the Arden editions in its handling of the two quartos?

Levenson's edition differs from both Arden editions by printing the two quartos separately and entire instead of blending them into one text. Brian Gibbons's Arden Second Series edition of 1980 rested on the 1599 quarto and admitted the 1597 quarto's readings selectively, chiefly in the apparatus. René Weis's Arden Third Series edition of 2012 also rested on the 1599 quarto but patched it liberally with the shorter text's readings and directions, producing a composite that exists in no early book. Levenson's Oxford volume gives the reader the 1599 text modernized under stated rules and the 1597 text complete behind it, with the alternatives debated in commentary rather than merged in silence.

### Q: What happened to the promptbook archive Levenson built for the edition?

The archive became a public research instrument: Levenson's profiles of approximately 170 promptbooks were entered into databases that remain searchable through the Centre for Renaissance and Reformation Studies at the University of Toronto. Working with a colleague and graduate students under a grant from the Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council of Canada, she methodized the profiles she had accumulated since the mid-1980s, recording each promptbook's cuts and staging notes so that the staging of any speech or scene can be traced across productions from the seventeenth century to the end of the twentieth. The database is open ended, designed to absorb further promptbook evidence as it surfaces.

### Q: What does Levenson's introduction make of the play's ending?

Levenson's introduction reads the ending as a reconciliation that restores order without restoring value. The fathers who could not keep their children alive compete to memorialize them in gold, Montague promising a statue for Juliet and Capulet matching it for Romeo, while the Prince's verdict, "All are punished" (5.3.295), distributes blame across the whole city. She dwells on the Prince's phrase "A glooming peace" (5.3.305) as the ending's exact register: peace with its light reduced. On her reading the flatness is written, not failed writing, and the statues mark the city's admission that it can honour the lovers only as monuments.

### Q: Which parts make up Levenson's introduction?

Levenson's introduction falls into six parts: a history of the Romeo and Juliet narrative before Shakespeare, tracing the story from mythic analogues through the Italian novella and its French and English transmitters; a critical account of the play itself, its language, rhetoric, and dramatic forms; a performance history built from her promptbook research; a section on the play's date, settling its composition in the mid-1590s; "The Mobile Text," her account of the two early quartos as independent witnesses; and "Editorial Procedures," the statement of the rules her texts follow. The architecture is itself an argument: story, play, stage, and text are given equal standing before the edited texts begin.

### Q: Why does the edition annotate Q1 only where it differs significantly from Q2?

The edition annotates the 1597 text selectively because its function in the volume is to stand as the play's second state, and its differences from the 1599 text are the evidence that matters. Full annotation would duplicate the main commentary and bury the comparison the volume exists to enable; annotation at the significant differences keeps the reader's eye on exactly the passages where the two states of the work part company, where the short text compresses, reassigns, reorders, or preserves a staging solution the long text lacks. The economy also marks the short quarto's standing honestly: it is printed entire, as a witness, but the volume does not pretend it needs the same density of commentary as the reading text.

### Q: What do the edition's notes on the Capulet feast draw from promptbooks?

The notes draw on the promptbook record of how companies have staged the feast's transitions. The early texts leave the movement fluid: the masquers arrive, the servants clear and grumble, and the ball assembles around them. Levenson's commentary reports that many promptbooks omit the servants' dialogue at this point, and that most have the masquers exit and re-enter for the ball, regularizing the scene's geography. The note neither prescribes nor forbids the regularization; it documents the theatrical tradition so that a reader or director can see which staging choices descend from the early texts and which were invented by the play's long life in performance.

### Q: What did Levenson mean by probing the quartos for staging clues?

Levenson meant the first phase of her editorial method, in which the early quartos are read as evidence of how the play was staged in its own time. The 1597 quarto in particular is rich in such clues: its fuller stage directions, its actable ordering of the tomb scene, and its direction naming the clown Will Kemp among the musicians all preserve traces of playhouse practice that a literary manuscript would not contain. The second phase of the method studies later theatrical texts, promptbooks above all, for the adjustments performance generated afterward. Together the phases treat the text and its stagings as one continuous record, which the edition's commentary then reports scene by scene.

### Q: Does the Oxford edition modernize Shakespeare's spelling and punctuation?

The Oxford edition modernizes the spelling and punctuation of both early texts it prints. Modernization is the stated policy of Levenson's "Editorial Procedures": the 1599 quarto's accidentals are brought to modern forms, its chaotic speech headings are regularized so that speakers are identified consistently, and the 1597 quarto is presented by the same conventions, so that the two states of the play can be compared without the friction of old spelling. Emendation of the words themselves is conservative and recorded in the collations. The policy serves the edition's comparative purpose: readers are meant to weigh the quartos against each other as plays, not to decipher them as documents.

### Q: What is the strongest objection to printing Q1 entire in a scholarly edition?

The strongest objection is that a complete text confers a dignity the document has not earned: if the 1597 quarto is a memorial reconstruction, a derivative report of the play as others remembered it, then printing it entire, in modern spelling, beside the authoritative 1599 text invites readers to treat a corruption as a version and doubles the bulk and cost of the volume in the process. The objection has force wherever the short quarto's inferiority is manifest, in its compressed rhetoric and garbled passages. Levenson's answer is architectural rather than polemical: the short text's selective annotation marks its secondary standing, and suppression, she holds, is a stronger claim than presentation, one the evidence does not justify.

### Q: How does the Oxford text open the play, and which early text supplies its first direction?

The Oxford main text opens with the 1599 quarto's direction, which names the speakers and arms them: Sampson and Gregory enter with swords and bucklers, of the house of Capulet. Levenson's editing regularizes the second quarto's inconsistent speech headings into consistent designations for the two serving-men throughout the scene. The 1597 quarto printed later in the volume opens the same scene differently, with a direction for two unnamed serving-men of the Capulets, a functional playhouse designation that her presentation preserves. The pair of openings, two pages of the volume apart in conception, exhibits the edition's whole argument: the long text reads as literature in preparation, the short text as a show in progress.

The choice of the 1599 quarto as the reading text interprets the work as the author's augmented design; the printing of the 1597 quarto entire interprets it as a mobile work with more than one legitimate state; the promptbook commentary interprets performance as evidence rather than decoration.
