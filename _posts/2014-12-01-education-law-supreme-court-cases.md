Ask a working lawyer what federal education law promises a student and the honest answer is narrower than most citizens expect. No clause of the federal Constitution names education as a right. No federal statute funds schools as a national system. What federal law does, through statutes that attach conditions to money, is create a handful of enforceable rights, each drawn narrowly, each contested in court, each defined less by the statute's text than by what judges have said the text allows. This article maps that judicial terrain. It is the litigation article of the education cluster, and it carries a single organizing claim that makes every other article in the cluster legible.

![The doctrine ledger of federal education law Supreme Court cases - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-02.webp)

The organizing claim, stated plainly, is this: because the Constitution supplies no education right, almost every federal education case is really a question about whether a spending statute created enforceable individual rights. The claim has a name for convenience: no federal right, therefore statutes. Hold it steady and the cluster's cases arrange themselves into five lines, each following from it. The foundational holding, San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez, 411 U.S. 1 (1973), closed the constitutional door and redirected school finance litigation into state courts. The private enforcement line asks, statute by statute, who may sue: the Court answered yes for the sex discrimination provision in 1979 and for damages in 1992, and no for the education records statute and for disparate impact regulations in 2001 and 2002. The special education line asks what the federal statute requires of schools, with the substantive standard set in 1982 and disputes over discipline, related services, private placement reimbursement, and exhaustion of remedies filling the decades after. The public funding line asks whether public money may reach religious schools, and the answer moved across half a century from permitting neutral programs that happen to include religious options to barring the exclusion of religious schools from otherwise available benefits. And the executive authority line asks how much a federal official may do under an emergency statute, with a 2023 decision applying the major questions doctrine to invalidate a mass loan cancellation program. Each line is presented from the opinions, majority and dissent given equal care, because the dissents in these cases keep returning: the Rodriguez dissenters argued that education deserved special constitutional treatment, the Gonzaga dissenters argued that records provisions did create individual rights, the Sandoval dissenters argued that agency regulations should be enforceable by individuals, and the voucher dissenters argued that neutrality principles had been misapplied. The litigation never settles into a single philosophy; it settles case by case, and this article inventories the settlement.

The stakes of getting this right are practical rather than academic. Parents deciding whether to file a complaint, administrators deciding whether a discipline policy risks a lawsuit, state legislators writing voucher or tax credit programs, and federal officials interpreting their statutory authority all act inside the boundaries these decisions drew. A reader who finishes this article will be able to explain the single holding that determines the shape of American education litigation, that the federal Constitution contains no fundamental right to education, and will be able to trace the consequences: that school finance fights moved to state courts, that federal claims must be built on statutes rather than on the Constitution, and that whether a given statute creates a privately enforceable right becomes the decisive question in case after case. That is the One Test for this article, and the sections that follow are organized to let a reader pass it.

## Federal education law in the courts: the One Test and the five lines

The One Test for this article rewards a specific kind of competence. A reader who has absorbed the material can explain the single holding that determines the shape of American education litigation: that the federal Constitution contains no fundamental right to education. That sentence, standing alone, sounds like a defeat for students, and the first thing the article must do is prevent the misreading. From that holding three consequences follow, and a reader who can trace all three has the framework. First, school finance fights moved to state courts, where state constitutions contain express education provisions that federal litigation could not use. Second, federal claims must be built on statutes rather than on the Constitution, which means every federal education lawsuit is an exercise in statutory interpretation rather than a claim of a freestanding right. Third, whether a given statute creates a privately enforceable right becomes the decisive question in case after case, because a statute that confers no individual right confers no lawsuit, no matter how badly the statute was violated. The sequence matters. The constitutional holding comes first, the move to statutes comes second, and the private enforcement question comes third, and no later section of this article will make sense to a reader who has the order wrong.

The reason the enforcement question is decisive, rather than merely interesting, is the structure of federal education law itself. The federal government does not operate the public schools. It funds them, conditionally, under the spending power, and it attaches conditions to that funding. The Elementary and Secondary Education Act of 1965 funds disadvantaged students. Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972 bars sex discrimination in federally funded programs. The Education of All Handicapped Children Act of 1975, now the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act, funds special education and conditions that funding on specified procedures and services. Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 bars disability discrimination by federal fund recipients. The Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act, enacted in 1974, conditions funding on policies governing student records. Each of these statutes is a bargain offered to the states: take the money, accept the conditions. But a bargain does not automatically create a lawsuit for every person the bargain was meant to help. The Court has developed a disciplined inquiry for deciding when it does. It asks whether Congress intended to create an individual right, and it treats rights creating language as the touchstone: statutory phrasing that speaks to the benefited class in individual terms can support enforcement under 42 U.S.C. 1983 or through an implied private right of action, while phrasing that speaks to institutional compliance in the aggregate, directed at the fund recipient's policies and procedures, does not. That distinction, between individual rights and aggregate institutional duties, is the single most useful analytical tool in this entire article, and it recurs in the private enforcement cases like a refrain.

The five lines of litigation, then, are not five separate stories. They are five applications of the same structural situation. The constitutional line sets the floor by denying a federal right. The enforcement line asks who may sue on the statutes that stand in the right's place. The special education line asks what the statutes require once a right to sue is established or assumed. The public funding line asks whether the Constitution's religion clauses permit or forbid money reaching religious schools, which is the one area where the Constitution itself, rather than a statute, does the work. And the executive authority line asks whether an official acting under a statute exceeded what the statute authorized, which is a separation of powers question wearing an education policy costume. The series thesis threads through all five: litigation is the stage where the practical reach of conditional spending statutes is determined. Congress writes the conditions; the courts decide who can enforce them, against whom, for what relief, and at what cost to constitutional boundaries. The rest of this article works the five lines in order.

### What does the One Test require a reader to explain?

The One Test requires the reader to state the Rodriguez holding, that education is not a federal fundamental right, and then trace three consequences: school finance moved to state courts, federal claims must rest on statutes, and whether a statute creates a privately enforceable right decides who can sue.

## The foundational holding: San Antonio ISD v. Rodriguez (1973)

Every federal education case begins, whether it says so or not, with what San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez took off the table. The case arose from the Texas school finance system, which funded public schools substantially through local property taxes. Because property wealth varied enormously among districts, per pupil spending varied enormously too: affluent districts could raise large sums at low tax rates, while poor districts taxed themselves at high rates and still raised far less. Parents of children in the poorer districts of the San Antonio area sued, arguing that the system violated the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. Their argument had two prongs. First, they argued that education was a fundamental right or at least a constitutionally protected interest, so the disparities should face strict scrutiny, the demanding standard under which a law must be narrowly tailored to a compelling government interest. Second, they argued that wealth classifications were suspect like race, which would also trigger strict scrutiny. The district court agreed with them. The Supreme Court, on March 21, 1973, reversed by a vote of five to four.

Justice Powell wrote for the majority, joined by Chief Justice Burger and Justices Stewart, Blackmun, and Rehnquist, with Justice Stewart adding a separate concurrence. The opinion addressed the two prongs in order and rejected both. On the fundamental right question, the majority drew the line that has governed this field ever since. The Court had recognized fundamental rights both in the Constitution's text, such as the First Amendment freedoms, and implied from the constitutional structure, such as the right to travel and the right to vote in state elections. Education, the majority held, is neither. The opinion acknowledged the importance of education, at one point describing it in terms that dissenters later quoted back at the majority, but importance is not the test; the test is whether the Constitution, expressly or impliedly, guarantees the right. The majority found no textual guarantee and refused to imply one. It also rejected the argument that education deserves fundamental status because it undergirds the exercise of First Amendment rights and the franchise. That functional argument, the majority said, would prove too much: many important interests bear on the exercise of constitutional rights, and treating all of them as fundamental would make the judiciary the arbiter of every social policy.

On the wealth classification question, the majority was equally firm. Wealth, as such, is not a suspect class, the opinion held. The Texas system did not define its disadvantaged class in the way suspect classifications work: the poor were not a discrete and insular group singled out by the state, the opinion reasoned, because the system's disparities turned on district property wealth, and poor persons lived in both wealthy and poor districts. Nor did the system absolutely deprive anyone of an education; it provided schooling to all children, merely with unequal resources. The Court therefore applied rational basis review, the most deferential standard, under which a law survives if it bears a rational relationship to a legitimate government interest. The legitimate interest was local control of public education, and the property tax system, whatever its flaws, bore a rational relationship to it. The majority was candid about the system's imperfections. It did not hold that Texas had chosen the best finance system, or even a good one. It held that choosing among imperfect systems is the legislature's job, not the federal judiciary's, absent a constitutional violation.

The dissents deserve the same careful treatment as the majority, because they articulate the view that has animated fifty years of state court litigation. Justice Brennan dissented alone on the ground that the case should not have been decided on the record before the Court. Justice White, joined by Justices Douglas and Brennan, argued that the system lacked even a rational basis because the state had effectively delegated the level of educational opportunity to accidents of local wealth. Justice Marshall, joined by Justice Douglas, wrote the principal dissent, and it is Marshall's opinion that the state courts later mined. Marshall argued that education deserved treatment as a fundamental interest because of its unique nexus to the exercise of constitutional rights and to informed citizenship, and he accused the majority of abandoning the sliding scale approach to equal protection that the Court had developed, under which the closeness of the fit between means and ends varies with the importance of the interest and the invidiousness of the classification. The majority's rigid two tier framework, Marshall argued, could not do justice to interests that are fundamental in function though not enumerated in text. The disagreement was methodological as well as substantive, and it has never been resolved at the federal level.

The consequences of Rodriguez were immediate and structural. Because the federal Constitution offered no leverage against funding disparities, litigants turned to state courts and state constitutions, which unlike the federal document contain express education provisions: clauses requiring a thorough and efficient system, a uniform system, or an adequate education, depending on the state. The move was not a retreat; it was a change of venue to a friendlier forum. Over the next five decades, school finance litigation proceeded under dozens of state constitutions, with state supreme courts ordering legislative overhauls of funding formulas, defining adequacy in enforceable terms, and retaining jurisdiction over compliance. The federal courthouse door that Rodriguez closed stayed closed, and the state courthouse doors opened wide. That is why the recurring error this article flags, treating state finance rulings as federal holdings, is so dangerous to a reader's understanding: the two bodies of law rest on different documents, answer to different texts, and reach different results precisely because the federal Constitution is silent where state constitutions speak.

There is a second consequence, subtler and more pervasive. By removing the Constitution from the plaintiff's toolkit in education cases, Rodriguez made statutory interpretation the entire federal game. Every federal education lawsuit after 1973 is, at bottom, an argument about what a statute means and whom it empowers. That is the world the next sections inhabit: Cannon and Franklin asking whether Title IX implies a private lawsuit and a damages remedy; Gonzaga asking whether FERPA creates rights enforceable under 42 U.S.C. 1983; Sandoval asking whether agency regulations under Title VI can be enforced by individuals; and the special education cases asking what Congress required when it conditioned money on a free appropriate public education. None of these cases asks whether the Constitution guarantees education. Rodriguez answered that question for all of them, in advance, in the negative.

### Why did the Court refuse to treat education as a fundamental right?

The majority applied the test for unenumerated rights: fundamental only if the Constitution guarantees the right expressly or by necessary implication. Education appears nowhere in the constitutional text, and the Court refused to imply a guarantee from education's importance, reasoning that importance alone would make judges arbiters of every social policy.

The vote was five to four, and the closeness matters for understanding what followed. A one vote shift would have produced a federal constitutional law of school finance, with federal courts supervising state funding formulas under strict scrutiny. Instead, the narrow majority produced the opposite: a federal judiciary that stays out of funding disputes and a state judiciary that stepped in. The dissenters' functional argument, that education undergirds citizenship itself, became the intellectual foundation of the state court adequacy movement, even though it lost in the federal reports. Readers who understand that the dissent won the long war in state court while losing the battle in federal court understand the field's deepest irony.

## The spending power underneath: South Dakota v. Dole (1987)

If Rodriguez is the case that removed the Constitution from education litigation, South Dakota v. Dole is the case that explains what replaced it. Federal education law is conditional spending law: Congress cannot directly command the states to operate schools in a particular way, but it can offer money on conditions, and the states, by accepting the money, accept the conditions. The doctrinal framework for that bargain comes from South Dakota v. Dole, 483 U.S. 203, decided June 23, 1987, a highway funds case whose reasoning the Court has applied across the spending statutes, education included. The National Minimum Drinking Age Act directed the Secretary of Transportation to withhold a percentage of federal highway funds from states that permitted persons under twenty one to purchase alcohol. South Dakota, which allowed nineteen year olds to buy beer, challenged the condition. Chief Justice Rehnquist, writing for a seven to two majority joined by Justices White, Marshall, Blackmun, Powell, Stevens, and Scalia, upheld it and in doing so stated the five part test that governs conditions on federal funds.

The test bears quoting in substance because every education conditions dispute runs through it. First, the spending must be in pursuit of the general welfare, a requirement the Court treats as largely committed to congressional judgment. Second, the conditions must be stated unambiguously, so that states exercise their choice knowingly, aware of the consequences of participation; this is the clear statement requirement, and it is the one that does the most work in education cases, where disputes often turn on whether Congress spoke clearly enough to put states on notice of a particular obligation. Third, the conditions must be related to the federal interest in particular national projects or programs, the germaneness requirement, which the Dole majority applied leniently, accepting safe interstate travel as sufficiently related to highway funds. Fourth, the conditions must not require the states to violate other constitutional provisions; Congress cannot use the spending power to purchase unconstitutional conduct. Fifth, the financial inducement must not be so coercive as to pass the point at which pressure turns into compulsion. In Dole, withholding roughly five to ten percent of highway funds was mere pressure, the majority held, not compulsion, because the state retained the realistic choice of declining the funds.

The dissents are worth noting because they foreshadowed the coercion arguments that later spending cases developed. Justice Brennan dissented on the ground that the condition exceeded congressional power over the subject matter. Justice O'Connor dissented on germaneness and on the related principle that the spending power cannot be used to regulate what Congress could not regulate directly. Neither dissent prevailed, but O'Connor's germaneness objection in particular has echoed in later debates about how closely education conditions must track the federal interests that justify education spending.

For the education statutes, the Dole framework is the deep structure beneath the surface disputes. Title IX conditions federal funds on nondiscrimination; the IDEA conditions funds on procedural and substantive commitments to disabled children; FERPA conditions funds on records policies. In each case the constitutional question is not whether Congress may regulate schools directly, which it generally may not, but whether the conditions satisfy Dole's five requirements. The clear statement requirement is the one litigants invoke most: when a state or a school district argues that a federal education statute cannot be read to impose a particular obligation, the argument is typically that Congress did not speak unambiguously enough to put the recipient on notice. And the coercion limit, the fifth Dole factor, is the one that hovers over the largest programs, where the federal share of funding is big enough that the theoretical choice to decline the money is not a realistic one. The framework thus does double duty: it authorizes the conditional spending structure of federal education law, and it supplies the vocabulary in which challenges to that structure are litigated.

One constitutional education case does not fit the spending framework at all, and it belongs to a different article's ownership. Plyler v. Doe, 457 U.S. 202, decided in 1982, held that a Texas statute withholding funds for and authorizing the denial of enrollment to children not legally admitted to the United States violated the Equal Protection Clause. The school access holding for children present unlawfully is owned by [the series' immigration law litigation article](/2013/03/15/immigration-law-supreme-court-cases/), and readers should follow it there for the full treatment; it is noted here only to complete the constitutional map and to mark the boundary of this article's scope. The holding rests on the Constitution, not on a spending statute, which is exactly why it is the exception that proves the article's organizing claim: when the Constitution does speak in education, as it did in Plyler, the case is not really about education law at all but about equal protection, and the education statutes that dominate this article operate in the space the Constitution leaves open.

## Applying the Dole Test to Education Statutes

The five part Dole framework repays a closer look as applied to education funding, because each condition does distinct work in the education cases and each has generated its own line of argument.

The general welfare condition is the least contested. Congress has broad discretion to decide what spending serves the general welfare, and courts have never seriously questioned that federal support for schooling qualifies. The condition functions less as a limit than as a statement of the theory: federal education spending is constitutional because Congress may spend for national purposes even in fields, like schooling, where it could not directly regulate.

The clear statement condition does the heaviest lifting. Because spending legislation operates like a contract, with states accepting funds in exchange for obligations, the Court requires Congress to state its conditions unambiguously so that a state knows what it is agreeing to. In education litigation this rule cuts against expansive readings of federal statutes. A plaintiff who argues that a funding condition implicitly requires some new practice faces the objection that the state never knowingly accepted that obligation. The rule also explains the pattern of the private enforcement cases: where Congress wants individuals to have remedies, ambiguity about private enforcement is resolved against the broader reading, which is why Gonzaga emphasizes that FERPA speaks to institutions rather than conferring individual rights.

The relatedness condition requires that funding conditions be germane to the federal interest in the particular program. For education statutes this condition is usually satisfied without difficulty, because the conditions attach to education money and concern the operation of educational programs. The condition bites only when Congress attaches an education related condition to unrelated funds, or vice versa, a configuration the major education statutes avoid by keeping their conditions inside their own programs.

The independent constitutional bar, that conditions may not require states to violate other constitutional provisions, is where the public funding cases intersect the spending power. Consider a federal education grant conditioned on a state's promise to exclude religious schools from a tutoring program the grant funds. Under Espinoza and Carson, such an exclusion would violate the Free Exercise Clause, so the condition would fail Dole's fourth prong and the state could not be required to accept it. Conversely, a condition requiring a state to include religious schools in a program whose design would violate the Establishment Clause would fail the same prong from the opposite direction. The fourth condition thus functions as a constitutional cross check on the spending power, importing the religion cases' conclusions into the funding cases' framework. The doctrinal lines of this article are not parallel tracks. They intersect, and the intersections are where the hardest cases arise. A funding condition that required a state to exclude religious schools from a neutral program, or to include them in violation of the Establishment Clause, would fail this prong. The religion cases can thus be read as applications of the fourth Dole condition to the education context: the funding power does not license unconstitutional conditions.

The coercion boundary is the condition with the most unrealized potential in education law. Dole found that withholding a small percentage of highway funds was encouragement rather than compulsion. Education statutes condition far larger sums, entire Title I allocations or all federal special education funding, on compliance, and no education case has tested whether withdrawing the whole of a major education grant would cross the line from pressure to compulsion. The question sits in the background of every funding cutoff threat, and litigators on both sides understand that the nuclear option of total defunding has never been judicially validated. The practical politics of the untested boundary favor negotiated compliance over confrontation: agencies threaten, institutions adjust, and the question of whether the threat itself would survive judicial review never reaches a courtroom. The coercion doctrine thus operates less as a litigated limit than as a background constraint on how aggressively the federal government wields its funding leverage, which is itself a form of constitutional discipline even without a deciding case.


Read together, the five conditions describe a constitutional settlement. Congress may pursue national education policy through money, but it must say what it wants plainly, keep its conditions germane, respect independent constitutional limits, and stop short of compulsion. Every education statute in this article's ledger was drafted against that settlement, and the litigation over each one is an argument about whether the settlement's terms were honored.

## The private enforcement line: who may sue under the statutes

With the constitutional route closed, the recurring question in federal education litigation is whether a statute creates rights enforceable by individuals. The question matters because enforcement design determines everything downstream. A statute enforceable only through agency fund termination, the remedy Congress wrote into most of the education spending statutes, is enforced rarely and politically; fund cutoff is a nuclear option that agencies almost never use, because cutting off a school district's federal funds punishes the students the statute was meant to help. A statute enforceable through private lawsuits is enforced constantly, by every affected person with a lawyer. The Court's decisions on which statutes carry private enforcement therefore determine, as a practical matter, which statutes have teeth. The cases fall into two groups: the ones that opened the courthouse doors and the ones that kept them shut.

### Cannon and the implied right under Title IX

Cannon v. University of Chicago, 441 U.S. 677, decided May 14, 1979, is the case that opened the door. Geraldine Cannon had been denied admission to medical school, allegedly because of her sex, and sued under Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972, the provision that bars sex discrimination in federally funded education programs, codified at 20 U.S.C. 1681 through 1688, with the operative prohibition in section 901(a), 20 U.S.C. 1681(a). Title IX contains no express private remedy provision. Its enforcement mechanism, set out in section 902, is administrative: federal agencies may terminate funding to noncompliant programs after a hearing and a report to Congress. The question was whether that administrative mechanism was exclusive, or whether an individual victim of discrimination could also sue directly. The Court held six to three that the individual could sue, recognizing an implied private cause of action under Title IX.

Justice Stevens wrote the majority, joined by Justices Brennan, Stewart, Marshall, and Rehnquist, with Chief Justice Burger concurring in the judgment and Justice Rehnquist filing a separate concurrence joined by Justice Stewart. The opinion applied the four factor test from Cort v. Ash, the then governing framework for implied rights of action. The Court asked whether the plaintiff was a member of the class for whose special benefit the statute was enacted, whether there was any indication of legislative intent to create or deny a private remedy, whether implying a remedy was consistent with the statute's underlying purposes, and whether the cause of action was one traditionally relegated to state law. Title IX, the majority held, satisfied all four: it was enacted to protect individuals from sex discrimination, the legislative history showed Congress assumed private enforcement would be available, private suits advanced the statute's antidiscrimination purpose, and civil rights enforcement was not an area of exclusive state concern. The section 902 fund cutoff procedure, the Court held, was not exclusive. Congress had designed it as a blunt instrument against institutional noncompliance, and reading it as the sole remedy would leave individual victims without relief in precisely the cases where fund termination was too drastic to be credible.

The dissents argued the opposite inference from the same materials. Justice White, joined by Justice Blackmun, argued that the elaborate administrative enforcement scheme in section 902 showed Congress had chosen its remedy and that the Court was rewriting the statute. Justice Powell dissented alone, in an opinion that became the foundation for the Court's later retrenchment on implied rights, arguing that the Cort v. Ash framework gave judges too much license to invent remedies Congress had not enacted. Powell's Cannon dissent is worth reading carefully because it states the separation of powers objection that later majorities adopted: implying a private right of action is lawmaking, and lawmaking belongs to Congress. The six to three majority rejected that objection for Title IX, but Powell's reasoning survived to win later cases.

One correction belongs here because secondary sources routinely get it wrong. The statute is Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972, not Title IX of the Higher Education Act. The mislabeling is understandable, since the Education Amendments of 1972 amended the Higher Education Act of 1965 among other laws, but it is wrong as a matter of statutory identity, and precision about which statute created the right matters in a field where the statute is everything. Readers who want the full statutory anatomy of the provision should consult [the Title IX guide](/2014/10/15/title-ix-1972-explained/), which treats the statute's text, regulations, and administrative enforcement in the detail this litigation article cannot.

Cannon established the right to sue. Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, 503 U.S. 60, decided February 26, 1992, established what the suit could win. Christine Franklin alleged that a teacher had sexually harassed her and that the school district had failed to act; she sued under Title IX seeking damages. The question was whether monetary damages were available in a private Title IX action, given that the statute says nothing about remedies. The Court held unanimously in the judgment that damages were available. Justice White wrote the majority opinion, joined by Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, and Souter, with Justice Scalia concurring in the judgment joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justice Thomas. The opinion invoked the longstanding presumption associated with Bell v. Hood: absent clear congressional direction to the contrary, federal courts may award any appropriate relief for the violation of a federal right. Congress had given no such contrary direction in Title IX, so damages were available for intentional violations. The Court also addressed a sovereign immunity wrinkle, holding that section 1003 of the Rehabilitation Act Amendments of 1986, codified at 42 U.S.C. 2000d-7, which abrogated state sovereign immunity for Title IX and Title VI suits, validated the Cannon implied right against the states. The combination of Cannon and Franklin is the high water mark of private enforcement in education law: an implied right to sue, plus damages, plus abrogated immunity. Everything after 1992 is, by comparison, a story of doors closing.

The practical significance of the Cannon Franklin pair is difficult to overstate. Title IX litigation, from athletics equity to sexual harassment to the scope of the statute's coverage, exists as a field because Cannon made the lawsuit possible and Franklin made it worth filing. The damages remedy transformed Title IX from a funding condition enforced sporadically by agencies into a tort like regime enforced daily by plaintiffs' lawyers. School districts began to manage Title IX risk the way employers manage employment discrimination risk: through compliance officers, grievance procedures, training programs, and settlements. None of that enforcement infrastructure appears in the statute's text. It is all implied, which is to say it is all judge made, which is why the later cases narrowing implied rights matter so much: they are not technical adjustments but changes to the enforcement economy of the entire field.

### Grove City and the congressional override

Cannon and Franklin answered who may sue and for what relief. Grove City College v. Bell, 465 U.S. 555, decided in 1984, answered a different question: how much of an institution Title IX covers. Grove City College was a private college that accepted no direct federal funding but enrolled students who received federal Basic Educational Opportunity Grants. The Court held, in an opinion by Justice White joined by Chief Justice Burger and Justices Blackmun, Powell, Rehnquist, and O'Connor, with Justice Brennan concurring in part and dissenting in part joined by Justice Marshall, that the college was a recipient of federal financial assistance subject to Title IX, because the grants flowed to the institution through its students. But the Court also held that Title IX's coverage was program specific: only the program receiving federal funds, here the financial aid office, had to comply, not the institution as a whole. The government could require an assurance of compliance, but it could not leverage aid to one program into regulation of the entire college. The program specific holding reflected the statutory phrase "program or activity," which the Court read narrowly.

Congress disagreed, and what happened next is one of the cleanest examples in this series of the override cycle: when the Court reads a statute in a way the political branches reject, Congress amends the statute. The Civil Rights Restoration Act of 1987, Public Law 100-259, 102 Stat. 28, restored institution wide coverage for Title IX, Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act, and the Age Discrimination Act of 1975, abrogating Grove City's program specific holding. President Reagan vetoed the bill on March 16, 1988; the Senate overrode the veto 73 to 24 and the House 292 to 133 on March 22, 1988, enacting it over presidential objection. The override matters for this article's framework because it shows the enforcement line running in both directions. Courts construe statutes narrowly; Congress rewrites them broadly; the practical reach of the spending statutes is determined in the interaction between the two. The Title IX that Cannon and Franklin enforce is the post 1988 Restoration Act version, institution wide in coverage, and every Title IX case litigated since rests on the Restoration Act's reversal of Grove City as much as on Cannon's implied right.

### The doors that closed: Gonzaga and Sandoval

The turn came at the turn of the century, in two cases that applied the individual rights versus aggregate duties distinction with new rigor. Gonzaga University v. Doe, 536 U.S. 273, decided June 20, 2002, asked whether the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act, FERPA, 20 U.S.C. 1232g, creates rights enforceable by individuals. A student alleged that university officials had disclosed his education records in violation of FERPA's nondisclosure provisions, and sued both under 42 U.S.C. 1983, the statute that supplies a cause of action for the deprivation of federal rights under color of state law, and on an implied right of action theory. The Court held seven to two that FERPA creates no personal rights enforceable under section 1983 and supports no implied private right of action. Chief Justice Rehnquist wrote the majority, joined by Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Breyer; Justices Stevens and Ginsburg dissented.

The reasoning is the clearest statement in the reports of the aggregate compliance test. FERPA, the majority held, is Spending Clause legislation directed at aggregate institutional compliance: it conditions federal funding on the maintenance of records policies and procedures, speaking to what institutions must do in general rather than conferring rights on individual students in particular. The statute's provisions are framed as directives to the Secretary to cut off funds to institutions with bad policies, not as guarantees to students. Because section 1983 enforces rights, not the broader benefits or interests a statute may create, and because implied rights require rights creating language focused on the benefited class, FERPA failed both tests. The dissenters, Stevens and Ginsburg, argued that the nondisclosure provisions did create individual rights: they protect identifiable students from concrete harms, and the majority's insistence on magic words elevated form over substance. The majority's answer was that form is substance in spending legislation: when Congress legislates through conditional funding, the Court reads the statute as a contract with the fund recipient, and contracts create rights in their parties, not in every person the contract was meant to benefit.

The practical consequence is the one the brief's most searched questions anticipate: no, a student cannot sue a school under FERPA. The statute is enforced, if at all, through the Department of Education's fund termination authority and its complaint process, which in practice means it is enforced rarely. Schools maintain FERPA policies because the funding condition requires it and because state law and institutional norms reinforce it, not because a private lawsuit threatens. Gonzaga is thus the mirror image of Cannon: where Cannon found individual rights in Title IX's antidiscrimination command, Gonzaga found only institutional duties in FERPA's records provisions, and the difference between the two holdings is the difference between rights creating language and compliance directing language. Readers who internalize that distinction can predict the outcomes of private enforcement cases they have never read.

Alexander v. Sandoval, 532 U.S. 275, decided April 24, 2001, closed a different door. The case concerned Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the provision that bars discrimination on the basis of race, color, and national origin in federally funded programs, and specifically the regulations that federal agencies had promulgated under section 602 of Title VI, 42 U.S.C. 2000d-1, prohibiting practices with a disparate impact on protected groups. Martha Sandoval, a lawful permanent resident, challenged Alabama's English only driver's license examination policy, arguing that it had a disparate impact on national origin minorities in violation of the section 602 regulations. The question was whether individuals could sue to enforce those regulations. The Court held five to four that they could not. Justice Scalia wrote the majority, joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, and Thomas; Justice Stevens dissented, joined by Justices Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

The majority assumed, without deciding, that section 601 of Title VI, 42 U.S.C. 2000d, supports a private right of action for intentional discrimination, the Cannon like premise. But the regulations were a different matter. The opinion's central sentence is the one litigators quote: language in a regulation cannot conjure up a private cause of action that Congress has not authorized. Disparate impact regulations, the majority held, go beyond what section 601 itself prohibits, since section 601 reaches only intentional discrimination, and a private right to enforce the broader regulatory prohibition would expand the statute beyond Congress's design. Enforcement of the disparate impact regulations was left to the federal agencies through fund termination. The dissent argued that the regulations were a reasonable interpretation of the statute entitled to deference, that private enforcement of valid regulations was consistent with the statutory scheme, and that the majority was dismantling the principal mechanism for addressing policies that discriminate in effect though not in intent. The full Title VI litigation history, including the statute's relationship to the education cases, is developed in [the civil rights litigation article](/2010/03/01/civil-rights-act-supreme-court-cases/), which owns the nondiscrimination line across all the funded programs.

Sandoval's significance for education law is direct and continuing. Many education equity disputes concern policies with disparate effects: discipline policies that suspend minority students at higher rates, tracking and gifted program assignments, school closure decisions, resource allocations. After Sandoval, individuals cannot sue under Title VI to challenge such policies on disparate impact theories; they must prove intentional discrimination under section 601 or persuade a federal agency to act. The agency route, like the FERPA route, is slow and political. The result is a structural asymmetry that every education civil rights lawyer understands: intentional discrimination is privately enforceable, disparate impact is not, and the most common forms of contemporary inequality are disparate in effect rather than intentional in design. Whether that asymmetry reflects Congress's intent or the Court's policy preference is the question the dissent pressed and the majority declined to engage, and it remains the live fault line in the enforcement literature.

Taken together, the private enforcement line teaches the article's namable claim in its purest form. Cannon and Franklin opened routes to relief; Gonzaga and Sandoval closed them; Grove City narrowed coverage and Congress overrode it. In each case the outcome turned not on the Constitution, which Rodriguez had removed from the field, but on whether the statute at issue created enforceable individual rights. The reader who understands that will correctly predict outcomes across doctrinally unrelated cases, because the same inquiry, rights creating language or aggregate duties, governs Title IX, FERPA, Title VI, and the disability statutes alike.

### Why can a student not sue a school under FERPA?

FERPA is Spending Clause legislation directed at aggregate institutional compliance, not individual rights. Its provisions tell the Secretary to cut off funds to institutions with deficient records policies; they do not confer rights on students in the rights creating language that section 1983 and implied right doctrine require. Enforcement therefore runs through the Department's funding authority, not private lawsuits.

## Reading the Enforcement Cases as Doctrine

The four private enforcement decisions reward study as a single doctrinal system, because the Court applies a consistent set of tools across them and the tools are what a litigator actually uses.

The first tool is the implied right of action analysis associated with Cort v. Ash, which Cannon applied to Title IX. The inquiry asks whether the plaintiff is part of the class the statute was designed to benefit, whether the legislative history suggests Congress intended a private remedy, whether implying a remedy fits the statutory scheme, and whether the subject matter is one traditionally left to state law. Cannon found all four factors satisfied for Title IX's sex discrimination ban. The analysis has grown more demanding in later decades, with the Court emphasizing that the question is one of congressional intent and that silence in the text weighs heavily against implication. A modern litigant invoking Cannon must therefore show not only that the statute protects people like the plaintiff but that Congress meant for courts to supply the remedy.

The second tool is the section 1983 framework, which Gonzaga applied to FERPA. Section 1983 provides a vehicle for enforcing federal rights, but only where the underlying statute creates rights rather than mere duties. The test looks to whether the statutory provision uses rights creating language focused on the benefited individual, whether it speaks in terms of what institutions must do in the aggregate, and whether Congress provided an alternative enforcement mechanism suggesting exclusivity. FERPA failed the test because its provisions address what educational agencies and institutions must do as a condition of funding, phrased as directives to the Secretary to cut off funds for noncompliance, rather than as entitlements held by students. The lesson generalizes: spending statutes drafted as conditions on institutions will rarely support section 1983 suits, while statutes drafted as prohibitions protecting individuals, like Title IX's ban on sex discrimination, can.

The third tool is the Bell v. Hood presumption on remedies, which Franklin applied. Once a private right of action exists, federal courts presume they may award any appropriate relief, including damages, unless Congress has clearly indicated otherwise. Franklin's contribution is to separate the existence of the right from the scope of the remedy and to place the burden on the party opposing damages to find a clear congressional bar. The combination of Cannon and Franklin is therefore stronger than either alone: Cannon supplies the door and Franklin supplies what lies beyond it.

The fourth tool is the regulation versus statute distinction, which Sandoval applied. Even where a statute supports private enforcement for its core prohibition, regulations implementing the statute do not automatically carry the same private right. Language in a regulation cannot conjure up a private cause of action that Congress has not authorized. The practical consequence for education law is that agency rules fleshing out nondiscrimination mandates, however detailed, are enforceable by the agency through funding sanctions unless Congress has separately authorized private suits for their violation.

## The special education line: what the statute requires

If the private enforcement line asks who may sue, the special education line asks what the lawsuit can demand. The governing statute is the Education of All Handicapped Children Act of 1975, Public Law 94-142, now known as the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act, the IDEA, and the line's cases construe its central promise: that states receiving federal funds will provide a free appropriate public education, FAPE, to children with disabilities. The promise sounds substantive, and the litigation has spent four decades determining how substantive it is. The cases divide into the standard itself, set in 1982 and raised in 2017, and the ancillary disputes over related services, discipline, private placement reimbursement, and exhaustion of administrative remedies. The full statutory and regulatory anatomy of the IDEA is developed in [the special education law guide](/2014/10/01/idea-special-education-law-guide/), which this section assumes as background and which readers new to the field should consult first.

### Rowley and the original standard

Board of Education of Hendrick Hudson Central School District v. Rowley, 458 U.S. 176, decided June 28, 1982, is the foundation. Amy Rowley was a deaf first grader whose school district provided a hearing aid, speech therapy, and a tutor, but refused her parents' request for a sign language interpreter in the classroom, arguing that she was performing better than the average child in her class and advancing from grade to grade. The parents argued that the statute required the district to maximize her potential, to give her an opportunity to learn commensurate with the opportunity given to nonhandicapped children. The Court disagreed, six to three.

Justice Rehnquist wrote the majority. Two corrections to common summaries are necessary here, both confirmed in the verification record. First, the decision was six to three, not unanimous; a surprising number of secondary accounts describe it as unanimous, which it was not. Second, Rehnquist wrote as an Associate Justice; he did not become Chief Justice until 1986, and describing him as Chief Justice in the 1982 context is anachronistic. The majority, joined by Chief Justice Burger and Justices Powell, Stevens, and O'Connor, with Justice Blackmun concurring, held that the statute's FAPE requirement is satisfied by personalized instruction with sufficient support services to permit the child to benefit educationally. For a child in a regular classroom, that meant an individualized education program, an IEP, reasonably calculated to enable the child to achieve passing marks and advance from grade to grade. The statute, the majority held, imposes no substantive maximum potential or equality of opportunity standard. Congress had required access to education and a floor of benefit, not the best possible education or an education equal in outcome to that of nonhandicapped children. The Court emphasized that courts should not substitute their own notions of sound educational policy for those of the school authorities who write the IEPs, a deference principle that has shaped every subsequent FAPE case.

Justice White dissented, joined by Justices Brennan and Marshall, and the dissent is worth attention because it states the reading of the statute that parent advocates pressed for the next thirty five years. White argued that the legislative history showed Congress intended something more than minimal benefit: the statute's purpose was to open the door of public education to handicapped children on appropriate terms, and a standard that asked only whether the child derived some benefit set the bar too low. The majority's answer was textual and structural: the statute defines FAPE in procedural terms, as special education and related services provided in conformity with an IEP, and courts exceed their role when they convert that procedural guarantee into a substantive equality mandate. The disagreement is the same one that runs through the entire special education line: how much substance can be read into a statute whose operative provisions are procedural.

Rowley's practical effect was to make the IEP the center of the legal universe. Because the substantive standard was undemanding, litigation focused on procedure: whether the IEP was developed with parental participation, whether it contained measurable goals, whether the services listed were actually provided. School districts learned to document compliance meticulously, and parents learned that procedural violations were often easier to prove than substantive ones. The standard also created the circuit split that eventually produced the 2017 revisit: some courts of appeals read Rowley as requiring only more than de minimis benefit, a trivial threshold, while others read it as requiring meaningful benefit, and the difference determined outcomes for children whose disabilities prevented grade to grade advancement in regular classrooms.

### What did Rowley require schools to provide disabled children?

Rowley held that the statute's free appropriate public education requirement is satisfied by personalized instruction with sufficient support services to permit the child to benefit educationally. For regular classroom children, that meant an IEP reasonably calculated to enable passing marks and grade advancement. The six to three majority rejected any maximum potential or equality of opportunity standard.

### The ancillary disputes: services, discipline, reimbursement

With the standard set, the Court spent the next decade working out the statute's machinery. Irving Independent School District v. Tatro, 468 U.S. 883, decided July 5, 1984, addressed related services. Amber Tatro, a child with spina bifida, needed clean intermittent catheterization during the school day to attend classes. The district argued that catheterization was an excluded medical service rather than a covered related service. The Court held nine to zero that catheterization was a related service because it need not be performed by a licensed physician; the statutory exclusion for medical services covered only services that must be performed by a physician, and a school nurse or trained layperson could perform catheterization. Two corrections from the verification record apply. The opinion was written by Chief Justice Burger, not Justice Brennan, a common misattribution. And the decision date was July 5, 1984, not 1985 as one secondary source had it. The same opinion also held, six to three, that where the education statute's remedies were available, section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act supplied no additional relief, including attorney's fees. Justices Brennan, joined by Justice Marshall, and Justice Stevens concurred in part and dissented in part on the fees question. The holding channeled disability education claims into the education statute's own procedures rather than allowing parallel section 504 litigation for the same relief.

School Committee of Burlington v. Department of Education of Massachusetts, 471 U.S. 359, decided in 1985, addressed reimbursement. The parents of a handicapped child, dissatisfied with the public school's proposed placement, unilaterally enrolled him in a private school and sought reimbursement. The Court held unanimously, in an opinion by Justice Rehnquist, that the statute's grant of authority to a reviewing court to grant such relief as the court determines is appropriate, then codified at 20 U.S.C. 1415(e)(2), includes ordering reimbursement when the public IEP was inappropriate and the private placement was proper, and that a unilateral change during the pendency of proceedings does not waive the reimbursement claim. Florence County School District Four v. Carter, 510 U.S. 7, decided November 9, 1993, extended the principle: courts may order reimbursement even though the chosen private school is not on the state's approved list and does not meet all of the statutory FAPE requirements in 20 U.S.C. 1401(a)(18), so long as the placement is otherwise proper under the statute, with reimbursement remaining discretionary equitable relief. Justice O'Connor wrote the unanimous opinion. Together the two cases made unilateral private placement a viable strategy for parents who could afford to front the tuition: if the district's program was inadequate and the private program was appropriate, the district paid.

Honig v. Doe, 484 U.S. 305, decided January 20, 1988, addressed discipline. The statute's stay put provision, then 20 U.S.C. 1415(e)(3), provides that a disabled child shall remain in the current educational placement during the pendency of proceedings. Two emotionally disturbed students had been suspended and faced expulsion for misconduct related to their disabilities. The Court held six to two, Justice Kennedy not participating, that the stay put provision bars schools from unilaterally changing a child's placement through indefinite suspension or expulsion for disability related misconduct, with no dangerousness exception. Justice Brennan wrote the majority, joined by Justices White, Marshall, Blackmun, Stevens, and O'Connor; Chief Justice Rehnquist dissented, joined in part by Justice Scalia. Schools concerned about dangerous students, the majority held, could seek a court injunction rather than acting unilaterally. The verification record adds an important caveat: the Court split four to four on whether the state must provide direct services when the local agency defaults, affirming the lower court without precedential effect, and that portion of the decision should not be cited as precedent on the direct services question. Honig remains the leading authority on the proposition that discipline of disabled students must run through the statute's procedures rather than around them.

These ancillary cases share a common theme with Rowley: the statute's procedures are the substance. Tatro channeled claims into the statute's remedies; Burlington and Carter made the reimbursement remedy real; Honig made the stay put procedure a substantive constraint on discipline. The special education line is thus the most procedure driven of the five lines, which is fitting for a statute whose operative provisions are procedural. The substantive standard stayed low from 1982 until the Court revisited it in a decision handed down after this article's 2014 frame.

### Later developments after the 2014 frame: Endrew F. and Fry

The two special education decisions that follow were handed down after this article's December 2014 frame, and they are presented here as explicitly dated later developments, in the past tense, because they changed the line in ways a reader of the earlier cases must understand. Endrew F. v. Douglas County School District RE-1, 580 U.S. 386, decided March 22, 2017, revisited the Rowley standard. Endrew, a child with autism, had made minimal progress under successive IEPs that carried forward essentially the same goals year after year; his parents unilaterally placed him in a private school where he made significant progress, and sought reimbursement. The Tenth Circuit had applied the most permissive reading of Rowley, holding that the statute required only more than de minimis benefit, and denied relief. The Supreme Court reversed unanimously, eight to zero, the Court then having eight members following Justice Scalia's death, in an opinion by Chief Justice Roberts.

The holding raised the standard without adopting the parents' proposed equality standard. To meet its substantive obligation, the Court held, a school must offer an IEP reasonably calculated to enable a child to make progress appropriate in light of the child's circumstances. That formulation is, in the opinion's own words, markedly more demanding than the merely more than de minimis test the Tenth Circuit had drawn from Rowley. But the Court rejected the stricter standard the parents urged, which would have required opportunities to achieve academic success and self sufficiency substantially equal to those of nonhandicapped children. The opinion thus split the difference between the circuits: more than trivial benefit, less than equal opportunity, calibrated to the individual child's circumstances. For children in regular classrooms, Rowley's grade advancement formulation remained the guide; for children whose disabilities prevented advancement in the regular curriculum, the IEP had to be appropriately ambitious in light of the child's circumstances, with challenging objectives. The unanimous vote is notable in a field accustomed to division: all eight justices agreed that the de minimis reading had set the bar too low, even as they declined to constitutionalize an equality mandate the statute does not contain.

Fry v. Napoleon Community Schools, 580 U.S. 154, decided February 22, 2017, addressed exhaustion. The IDEA requires families to exhaust its administrative procedures before suing under other disability statutes when the suit seeks relief also available under the IDEA, codified at 20 U.S.C. 1415(l). The Frys sued under Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act and section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act after a school refused to allow their daughter's service dog, Wonder, to accompany her; they sought damages for emotional distress, relief the IDEA's procedures could not provide. The question was whether they had to exhaust the IDEA process first. Justice Kagan wrote for a unanimous Court, with Justice Alito concurring joined by Justice Thomas, holding that exhaustion is unnecessary where the gravamen of the suit is something other than the denial of a FAPE. The opinion supplied the test lower courts now apply: if the family could have brought the same claim against a public facility that is not a school, or if an adult at the school could have pressed essentially the same grievance, the complaint's substance is discrimination rather than FAPE denial, and exhaustion is not required. The service dog claim passed that test; a claim about the adequacy of an IEP would not. Fry thus opened a bypass around the IDEA's procedures for pure discrimination claims while keeping the exhaustion requirement for claims that are, at bottom, about educational benefit. Together with Endrew F., the two 2017 decisions mark the line's modern shape: a more demanding substantive standard, and a clearer map of when the statute's procedures must be used and when they may be skipped.

## The public funding line: from neutrality to free exercise

The public funding line is the one place in this article where the Constitution does the heavy lifting rather than a statute, and it is the line where the doctrine moved the most. The question is whether public funds may reach religious schools, and the Court's answer traveled across four decades from a cautious Establishment Clause permission to a Free Exercise Clause prohibition on exclusion. The doctrinal reasoning, not the political valence, is what this section tracks, because the holdings are stated in the language of constitutional tests, neutrality, private choice, indoctrination, entanglement, status discrimination, and the reader who learns the tests can apply them to any funding program.

### The indirect aid cases: Mueller, Witters, Zobrest

The line begins with programs that aid religious schools only indirectly, through the independent choices of private individuals, and the Court upheld each of them under the Establishment Clause. Mueller v. Allen, 463 U.S. 388, decided in 1983, concerned a Minnesota tax deduction for tuition, textbook, and transportation expenses, available to parents of public, nonsectarian private, and sectarian private schoolchildren alike. The Court held five to four that the deduction did not violate the Establishment Clause. Justice Rehnquist, then an Associate Justice, wrote the majority, joined by Chief Justice Burger and Justices White, Powell, and O'Connor; Justice Marshall dissented, joined by Justices Brennan, Blackmun, and Stevens. The opinion applied the three part test from Lemon v. Kurtzman: the program had a secular purpose, its primary effect neither advanced nor inhibited religion, and it produced no excessive entanglement. The decisive reasoning was neutrality plus genuine private choice. The deduction was available without regard to the sectarian or nonsectarian character of the school; any benefit flowing to religious schools resulted from numerous private parental decisions about where to enroll their children, not from government direction; and the Court refused to ground constitutionality on statistical evidence about how many beneficiaries actually chose religious schools, holding that the program's facial neutrality, not its empirical distribution, controlled. The dissent argued that the deduction's real world effect was overwhelmingly to subsidize religious school tuition, since public school parents had little tuition to deduct, and that the majority's formalism ignored substance. The majority's answer was that the Establishment Clause judges what the government does, and what the government did was create a neutral benefit that individuals directed.

Witters v. Washington Department of Services for the Blind, 474 U.S. 481, decided January 27, 1986, extended the principle to direct payment to an individual. Larry Witters, a blind student, sought vocational rehabilitation aid to pursue ministerial training at a Christian college, and the state argued that paying for religious training violated the Establishment Clause. The Court held unanimously, nine to zero, in an opinion by Justice Marshall, that the Clause did not bar the payment. The program was neutral, open to any blind student for any vocational objective; the aid went to the individual, not to the religious institution; and any benefit to religion flowed from the individual's genuinely independent choice. Under Lemon, the program had a secular purpose, its primary effect was on the individual rather than on religion, and no entanglement resulted where the state merely wrote a check the student directed. Witters is the purest statement of the private choice principle: when the government aids individuals and the individuals choose religious uses, the Establishment Clause is not implicated, because the government has not chosen religion.

Zobrest v. Catalina Foothills School District, 509 U.S. 1, decided June 18, 1993, applied the principle to services delivered on religious school premises. James Zobrest, a deaf student attending a parochial school, sought a sign language interpreter under the IDEA, and the district argued that placing a public employee in a religious school violated the Establishment Clause. The Court held five to four that the Clause does not prevent the district from furnishing the interpreter as part of a neutral general program distributing benefits to any qualifying disabled child. Chief Justice Rehnquist wrote the majority, joined by Justices White, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas; Justice Blackmun dissented, joined by Justices Stevens and Souter, with Justice O'Connor joining in part. The reasoning built on Mueller and Witters: the program was neutral, open without regard to sectarian status; the interpreter merely translated whatever the classroom teacher said, neither adding to nor subtracting from the religious content; and the mere physical presence of a state employee did not advance religion or entangle church and state. A correction confirmed in the verification record is essential here. The holding is that the Establishment Clause does not bar the interpreter. The Court did not hold that the IDEA requires providing one; the statutory question was left for the lower courts on remand. Secondary summaries that describe Zobrest as requiring the interpreter under the statute misstate the holding. The distinction between "the Constitution does not forbid it" and "the statute requires it" is exactly the kind of precision this article's framework demands, and Zobrest is the case that teaches it.

### Why did the aid cases move from the Establishment Clause to the Free Exercise Clause?

The early cases asked whether the Establishment Clause forbids aid reaching religious schools and answered no for neutral programs routed through private choice. The later cases asked the inverse, whether the Free Exercise Clause forbids excluding religious schools from otherwise available benefits, and answered yes. The pivot shifted the burden: the government must now justify exclusion under strict scrutiny.

### Mitchell and the direct aid question

Mitchell v. Helms, 530 U.S. 793, decided June 28, 2000, tested direct aid: the federal Chapter 2 program, enacted as part of the Education Consolidation and Improvement Act, lent computers, software, and library books to schools, including religious schools. The Court upheld the program six to three in the judgment and overruled Meek v. Pittenger and Wolman v. Walter insofar as they had barred such instructional material aid. But the verification record requires a structural correction that changes how the case may be described. No opinion commanded a majority. Justice Thomas wrote a plurality, joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices Scalia and Kennedy, that would have upheld any aid that is neutral with respect to religion and secular in content, regardless of whether it was direct or indirect. Justice O'Connor wrote a concurrence in the judgment, joined by Justice Breyer, that was narrower and therefore controlling: applying the test from Agostini v. Felton, she asked whether the aid resulted in government indoctrination, whether recipients were defined by religion, and whether the program created excessive entanglement, and upheld Chapter 2 on those narrower grounds. Justice Souter dissented, joined by Justices Stevens and Ginsburg. The controlling opinion is thus O'Connor's, not Thomas's, and the broad neutrality proposition must not be presented as majority law. It was a four justice plurality. The judgment stands for the narrower proposition that secular instructional materials lent evenhandedly to religious and secular schools satisfy the Agostini test. The plurality concurrence structure matters beyond Mitchell: it is a standing reminder that vote counts alone do not state the law, and that the narrowest opinion supporting the judgment controls.

### Zelman and the voucher question

Zelman v. Simmons-Harris, 536 U.S. 639, decided June 27, 2002, is the voucher case, and it is the decision the brief's most searched questions anticipate. Ohio's Pilot Scholarship Program gave tuition vouchers to poor children in the failing Cleveland school district, usable at participating public or private schools, secular or religious. The Court held five to four that the program did not violate the Establishment Clause. Chief Justice Rehnquist wrote the majority, joined by Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas, with Justice O'Connor also concurring separately. The opinion synthesized the indirect aid precedents into the true private choice doctrine: where a government program is neutral with respect to religion and provides assistance to a broad class of citizens who then direct the aid to religious schools through genuine independent choice, any incidental advancement of a religious mission is attributable to the individuals, not to the government. The Cleveland program satisfied the test because it was formally neutral, offered a range of secular options including public schools and charter schools, and channeled the funds through parents. The dissents, in opinions by Justices Stevens, Souter, joined by Justices Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer, Ginsburg, and Breyer, argued that the program's actual operation left religious schools as the dominant realistic option, that formal neutrality masked substantive endorsement, and that the majority had abandoned the careful line between direct and indirect aid. The majority's answer, consistent with Mueller, was that constitutionality turns on the program's structure, neutrality plus genuine choice, not on the statistical distribution of where parents actually enrolled. Zelman thus answered the voucher question for the Establishment Clause: vouchers are constitutional when they satisfy true private choice. It did not hold that states must enact vouchers, or that the Constitution requires funding religious schools; it held that the Constitution permits a neutral voucher program, which is a different proposition and one the later Free Exercise cases would build upon.

### Later developments after the 2014 frame: the Free Exercise shift

The three decisions that follow were handed down after this article's December 2014 frame, and they are presented as explicitly dated later developments because they moved the public funding line from the Establishment Clause permission structure to a Free Exercise Clause prohibition on exclusion. The doctrinal pivot is the most consequential change in the line since Zelman.

Trinity Lutheran Church v. Comer, 582 U.S. 449, decided June 26, 2017, concerned a Missouri scrap tire grant program that funded playground resurfacing for nonprofits, from which the state excluded a church preschool solely because of its religious status. The Court held seven to two that the exclusion violated the Free Exercise Clause. Chief Justice Roberts wrote the majority; Justices Sotomayor and Ginsburg dissented. The reasoning distinguished between the government's refusal to fund religious uses, which the Court's earlier decision in Locke v. Davey had permitted in the narrow context of ministerial training scholarships, and discrimination based on religious status, which triggers strict scrutiny. Missouri had not excluded the preschool because of what it would do with the grant money; it had excluded the preschool because of what it was. That status based exclusion, the majority held, penalized the free exercise of religion and could not survive strict scrutiny. The dissent argued that the decision eroded the separation of church and state and would generate difficult line drawing problems in funding programs. The majority confined its holding to status based discrimination, expressly reserving the question of discrimination based on religious use.

Espinoza v. Montana Department of Revenue, 591 U.S. 464, decided June 30, 2020, brought the status rule into education funding directly. Montana had created a tax credit scholarship program for private school tuition, then applied the state constitution's no aid provision, a provision of the type commonly called a Blaine amendment, to exclude religious schools. The Montana Supreme Court responded by invalidating the entire program. The Supreme Court held five to four that the exclusion violated the Free Exercise Clause. Chief Justice Roberts wrote the majority, joined by Justices Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, and Kavanaugh; Justices Ginsburg, Breyer, and Sotomayor dissented. The opinion's central sentence states the rule plainly: a state need not subsidize private education, but once a state decides to do so, it cannot disqualify some private schools solely because they are religious. The Court further held that the Montana court could not avoid the federal constitutional issue by eliminating the program altogether; the Free Exercise violation had to be remedied by inclusion, not by leveling down. The dissents argued that the no aid provisions reflected legitimate historical concerns about government funding of religious institutions and that the majority was overriding state constitutional choices about church state relations. The majority's answer was that the Free Exercise Clause limits those choices: status based exclusion from a public benefit is unconstitutional whatever its historical pedigree.

Carson v. Makin, 596 U.S. 767, decided June 21, 2022, extended the rule from status to use. Maine operates a tuition assistance program for students in districts without public secondary schools, paying tuition at approved private schools, but required participating schools to be nonsectarian, excluding schools that provide religious instruction. The Court held six to three that the nonsectarian requirement violated the Free Exercise Clause. Chief Justice Roberts wrote the majority, joined by Justices Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett; Justice Breyer dissented, joined by Justice Kagan and, except as to one part, Justice Sotomayor, who also dissented separately. Maine argued that Trinity Lutheran and Espinoza concerned discrimination based on religious status, while its program discriminated based on religious use, the kind of distinction Locke v. Davey had permitted. The majority rejected the distinction as applied here: excluding otherwise eligible schools on the basis of their religious exercise, the opinion held, is unconstitutional whether the exclusion is framed in terms of status or use. The dissents argued that Maine's program was designed to provide the equivalent of a public education, which by definition is secular, and that requiring the state to fund religious instruction crossed a line the earlier cases had preserved. The majority's answer was that once the state funds private education, the Free Exercise Clause forbids it to condition the funding on surrendering religious exercise.

The arc of the line is now visible in full. Mueller, Witters, Zobrest, Mitchell, and Zelman asked whether the Establishment Clause forbids public money from reaching religious schools, and answered that neutral programs routed through private choice are permitted. Trinity Lutheran, Espinoza, and Carson asked whether the Free Exercise Clause forbids excluding religious schools from public money, and answered that status based and use based exclusions from otherwise available benefits are prohibited. The two halves are not contradictory; they are complementary. The Constitution neither requires states to fund private religious education nor permits them to single out religious schools for exclusion once funding is offered. The doctrinal shift has direct consequences for state education funding rules, which is why the brief flags the line as politically live: every state with a no aid provision, a nonsectarian requirement, or a status based exclusion in a funding program must now test it against the Free Exercise rule, and the test is strict scrutiny, which exclusions rarely survive. The section states the holdings from the opinions and presents the dissents' concerns with equal care, because the line drawing problems the dissenters identified, between status and use, between funding education and funding religious exercise, are the problems the next generation of cases will have to solve.

## The executive authority line: Biden v. Nebraska (2023)

The final line asks how much a federal official may do under a statute, and it is dated after this article's 2014 frame: Biden v. Nebraska, 600 U.S. 477, decided June 30, 2023, presented here as an explicitly dated later development. The case concerned the Secretary of Education's 2022 program canceling federal student loan debt, up to ten thousand dollars per borrower and up to twenty thousand for Pell Grant recipients, at an estimated cost of roughly four hundred thirty billion dollars. The Secretary invoked the Higher Education Relief Opportunities for Students Act, the HEROES Act, Public Law 108-76, 117 Stat. 904, enacted August 18, 2003 and made permanent by Public Law 110-93 in 2007, codified at 20 U.S.C. 1098aa through 1098ee. The operative provision, 20 U.S.C. 1098bb(a)(1), authorizes the Secretary to waive or modify any statutory or regulatory provision applicable to federal student loan programs as necessary in connection with a war, military operation, or national emergency. The Secretary argued that the COVID-19 national emergency authorized the cancellation as a waiver or modification of loan obligations. Six states sued, and the Court held six to three that the program exceeded the statute's authorization.

Chief Justice Roberts wrote the majority, joined by Justices Alito, Thomas, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett. Justice Kagan dissented, joined by Justices Sotomayor and Jackson. The companion case, Department of Education v. Brown, was resolved unanimously on standing grounds only and does not affect the merits holding. The majority's reasoning applied the major questions doctrine: in cases of vast economic and political significance, the Court requires clear congressional authorization before accepting an agency's claim of sweeping power. The opinion held that the HEROES Act's waive or modify language did not supply that clear authorization. Waiver and modification, the majority reasoned, contemplate modest adjustments to existing provisions, not the effective introduction of a whole new regime; canceling four hundred thirty billion dollars in debt for tens of millions of borrowers was not a waiver or modification of loan terms but a fundamental revision of the statutory scheme. The dissent argued that the statute's text was deliberately broad, that Congress had used expansive language precisely to give the Secretary flexibility in emergencies, and that the majority was substituting its own policy judgment for the executive's under the guise of a clear statement rule. The disagreement, like so many in this article, was about how much work statutory text can do: the majority read "waive or modify" as a limited grant, the dissent as an intentionally capacious one.

The decision belongs in an education litigation article for two reasons. First, the loan programs at issue are creatures of the Higher Education Act of 1965, the statute that built the federal student loan system, and the full account of that statute's authority structure is developed in [the Higher Education Act guide](/2014/09/15/higher-education-act-1965-guide/). The HEROES Act authority the Secretary invoked was an emergency overlay on that underlying system, and the case is ultimately about the relationship between emergency powers and the permanent statutory framework. Second, the major questions doctrine is now the lens through which courts evaluate ambitious executive action in education, from loan policy to regulatory initiatives grounded in broad statutory phrases. The doctrine's education law debut in Biden v. Nebraska means that future executive programs will be tested against the clear authorization requirement, and the test is demanding: the more significant the program's economic and political footprint, the clearer the statutory language must be. The case thus extends the article's organizing claim into the separation of powers. Just as private plaintiffs must show that a statute created enforceable rights, executive officials must show that a statute authorized their actions, and in both settings the Court has grown increasingly unwilling to infer what Congress did not say.

### What does the major questions doctrine require in education law?

The doctrine requires clear congressional authorization before courts will sustain executive action of vast economic and political significance. In Biden v. Nebraska, the Court held that the HEROES Act's authority to waive or modify loan provisions did not clearly authorize four hundred thirty billion dollars in mass cancellation, because waiver language contemplates modest adjustments rather than a new program.

A note on neutrality is in order because the brief flags loan cancellation as politically live. This section states the holding from the opinions: the majority's clear authorization analysis and the dissent's textual breadth argument, each given its full weight. It takes no position on whether loan cancellation is good policy, on whether Congress should authorize it, or on the merits of any executive program. The article's frame is December 2014, the decision is dated June 2023, and the presentation is confined to what the opinions held and why, which is all a litigation article may properly say about a decided case.

## What Rodriguez did not decide

Rodriguez is the most misread case in this article's set, and the misreadings all run in the same direction: they treat the decision as saying more than it said. Stating the negative boundaries precisely is worth the space, because each boundary marks a live area of law that a careless reader might think the case had foreclosed.

First, Rodriguez did not hold that unequal school funding is good policy, or constitutional in any deep sense, or beyond legislative correction. It held that the federal Equal Protection Clause, under rational basis review, does not forbid it. The majority was explicit that legislatures remain free to reform finance systems, and many did. The decision is a holding about judicial power, not about educational justice: it says the federal courts will not supervise funding equity, not that funding equity is unimportant or that no court may supervise it. The fifty years of state litigation that followed are the proof. Readers who quote Rodriguez as though it blessed funding disparities are quoting a holding the opinion does not contain.

Second, Rodriguez did not limit Congress. The decision construes the Fourteenth Amendment's constraint on the states; it says nothing about what Congress may do under the spending power. Congress remained free after 1973 to condition federal education funds on equity measures, to fund compensatory programs like Title I, and to attach antidiscrimination conditions to federal money. The entire conditional spending edifice described in this article was built after Rodriguez and is untouched by it. The case removed a constitutional sword from plaintiffs' hands; it did not remove Congress's spending power from the field. Confusing those two propositions is one of the recurring errors the verification flags warn against, and it produces the false inference that federal education law is constitutionally fragile. It is not fragile; it is statutory, which is a different kind of strength.

Third, Rodriguez did not decide anything about state constitutions. The opinion interprets the federal document only, and its reasoning about implied fundamental rights has no bearing on state charters that expressly guarantee education. State courts construing their own education clauses are not bound by Rodriguez's methodology, and most of them have rejected it, adopting adequacy frameworks the federal majority would not have recognized. The federal and state cases therefore do not disagree with each other; they answer different questions under different texts. A reader who cites Rodriguez against a state adequacy claim is making a category error.

Fourth, Rodriguez did not hold that wealth classifications always receive rational basis review in every context, or that education can never matter constitutionally. The opinion left open, and later cases explored, whether the total denial of education, as opposed to unequal provision, might trigger different analysis. Plyler v. Doe, decided nine years later, is the illustration: there the Court struck down a Texas law that denied public education entirely to children present unlawfully, applying a form of heightened review to a complete exclusion even though Rodriguez had applied rational basis to unequal funding. The distinction between unequal provision and total denial is the doctrinal seam between the two cases, and it shows the limits of reading Rodriguez as a blanket holding about education and the Constitution. The case decided what it decided: funding disparities among districts, reviewed deferentially, do not violate equal protection. Everything else remained open, and the field grew in the openings.

## The dissents' afterlife: how losing opinions shaped the field

A striking feature of this article's cases is how often the dissents won the long game. The majorities decided the cases; the dissents decided the future. Tracing that afterlife is not sentimentality about losing arguments. It is a practical lesson in how American education law actually develops: a dissent in one decade is frequently the majority reasoning, the statute, or the state court holding of the next.

Justice Powell's solo dissent in Cannon is the cleanest example. Powell argued that implying private rights of action under the Cort v. Ash framework gave judges a lawmaking license Congress had not granted, and that courts should require clearer evidence of congressional intent before inventing remedies. The six justice majority rejected that view for Title IX in 1979. But Powell's separation of powers objection became the Court's own position within two decades. Sandoval in 2001 and Gonzaga in 2002 both reflect Powell's insistence that private enforcement requires rights creating language and demonstrable congressional intent, not judicial inference from statutory purpose. The doctrinal arc from Cannon to Gonzaga is, in substance, the arc from the majority's purposivism to Powell's textualism, and a reader who wants to understand why the enforcement doors closed must read the Cannon dissent as the blueprint for the closure.

Justice Marshall's Rodriguez dissent had a different afterlife: it lost in the federal reports and won in the state courts. Marshall's functional argument, that education deserves special constitutional treatment because of its nexus to citizenship and the exercise of constitutional rights, and his sliding scale approach to equal protection, which would calibrate scrutiny to the importance of the interest, were rejected by the five justice majority. But state supreme courts construing their own education clauses adopted adequacy frameworks that echo Marshall's functionalism, treating education as uniquely important among state constitutional guarantees and developing standards of review more demanding than federal rational basis. The dissent thus became the intellectual foundation of the very litigation the majority's holding redirected. There is an irony here that the article should not smooth over: the opinion that lost the case supplied the theory for the fifty year movement the case created.

Justice White's Rowley dissent followed a similar path with a longer delay. White argued that the legislative history showed Congress intended more than minimal educational benefit for handicapped children, and that the majority's benefit standard set the bar too low. For thirty five years the majority's reading governed, with some circuits reducing it further to a merely more than de minimis threshold. Then Endrew F. in 2017 unanimously raised the standard to progress appropriate in light of the child's circumstances, markedly more demanding than de minimis, in terms that vindicate White's core complaint even though the Court stopped short of his equality standard. The dissent did not become law in its own terms, but it named the defect the later Court corrected.

The Gonzaga dissent by Justices Stevens and Ginsburg, and the Sandoval dissent by Justice Stevens, have had a different fate: they remain dissents, but they state the enforcement philosophy that Congress could adopt at any time. Both dissents argued, in different doctrinal registers, that the majority's insistence on rights creating magic words elevated form over substance and left important statutory protections without practical enforcement. Those arguments are available to any future Congress that wishes to write express private rights into FERPA or to authorize private enforcement of disparate impact regulations. The override cycle that followed Grove City shows that Congress does respond to enforcement narrowings when the political will exists. The dissents are thus best read as draft legislation: they describe the statute Congress would need to write to achieve the result the majority denied.

Even the concurrences have afterlives worth noting. Justice O'Connor's controlling concurrence in Mitchell v. Helms, narrower than the Thomas plurality, is the law precisely because it was narrower, and it demonstrates that the narrowest opinion supporting a judgment can outlast the broader reasoning that attracted more votes. Justice Stewart's concurrence in Rodriguez, Justice Rehnquist's concurrence in Cannon, and Justice O'Connor's partial joinder in Zobrest each mark the precise location of the fifth vote, which is where the law actually lives in divided cases. The article's method throughout, stating majorities and dissents with equal care, reflects this reality: in a field built case by case, the opinions that did not command the Court are often the ones that explain what the commanding opinion really held and where it is vulnerable.

## The Vote Arithmetic as Doctrine

The margins in the ledger carry information that the holdings alone do not, and a careful reader of education cases learns to read them. The field's most consequential holdings cluster at the narrow end of the vote spectrum, and the narrowness tells a story about the doctrine's stability.

Rodriguez was five to four, with three dissenting opinions. Zelman was five to four, with four separate dissents. Sandoval was five to four. Espinoza was five to four. Mueller was five to four. Zobrest was five to four. A five to four decision is the law, fully binding, but it is the law by a single vote, which means its reasoning must persuade future justices rather than merely command them. The religion line's movement from five to four Establishment Clause decisions to six to three and seven to two Free Exercise decisions is itself a signal: the doctrine stabilized as the constitutional clause shifted, suggesting that the Free Exercise framing commands broader agreement than the Establishment Clause framing did.

Unanimous decisions occupy the other end and signal a different kind of stability. Witters was nine to zero. Burlington was unanimous. Carter was unanimous. Franklin was nine to zero in the judgment. Endrew F. was eight to zero, and Fry was unanimous. Unanimity in this field tends to mark the cases where the Court is refining an agreed framework rather than choosing between competing ones: the reimbursement remedy, the related services definition, the exhaustion boundary. A litigator citing a unanimous case cites settled law; a litigator citing a five to four case cites the law while knowing the reasoning remains contested.

The fragmented decision deserves its own note. Mitchell's six to three judgment with no majority opinion means the case's precedential weight is less than its vote line suggests. Under Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188 (1977), the controlling opinion is the narrowest ground supporting the judgment, which is Justice O'Connor's concurrence. Practitioners who cite Mitchell for the plurality's broad neutrality principle cite four votes, not law. The verification discipline of this article, reporting the opinion structure exactly, is the discipline the field requires of anyone who relies on the case.

## What the Cases Left Open

A structural article should be honest about the questions the doctrine has not settled, because the open questions are where the next generation of litigation will be fought. Five stand out.

First, the coercion boundary in education spending remains untested. Dole upheld a small percentage withholding and described a line beyond which pressure becomes compulsion, but no education case has asked a court to locate that line for a major grant program. If Congress conditioned the entirety of a state's federal education funding on adoption of a controversial policy, the state's theoretical freedom to decline the funds would collide with the practical impossibility of replacing them. The doctrine has a name for the problem and no education precedent resolving it.

Second, the scope of Mitchell v. Helms remains uncertain because of its fragmented opinions. The controlling concurrence upholds direct instructional aid under the Agostini test, but the plurality's broader neutrality principle has four votes and the concurrence's narrower test has two. A future case presenting direct aid in a different program design could force the Court to choose between them, and litigants on both sides cite Mitchell for propositions the controlling opinion does not fully support.

Third, the Establishment Clause framework itself is in flux. The Lemon test that Mueller applied has been criticized by members of the Court across decades, and the religion cases of the post frame period increasingly resolve on Free Exercise grounds rather than through Lemon's three prongs. Whether Lemon survives as the governing test for direct aid, or whether the field consolidates around neutrality and private choice on one side and antidiscrimination on the other, is the most consequential unresolved doctrinal question in the public funding line.

Fourth, the exhaustion boundary after Fry will require elaboration. The gravamen test distinguishes suits about the denial of a free appropriate public education from suits about distinct discrimination, but real complaints often allege both. Lower courts must now decide how to characterize mixed complaints, and the answer determines whether families endure months of administrative proceedings before reaching court. Fry supplied the principle; its application will be worked out case by case.

Fifth, the substantive standard of Endrew F. invites the question the opinion deliberately left open: what counts as progress appropriate in light of the child's circumstances for children whose disabilities make conventional academic progress unattainable. The opinion rejected both the de minimis floor and the equal opportunity ceiling, and the space between them will be defined by hearing officers and courts applying the standard to individual children. The standard's flexibility is its virtue and its uncertainty.

None of these open questions undermines the settled holdings. They mark the frontier beyond the settlement, which is where a living body of law always has its most interesting work.

## A reader's method: predicting outcomes from the framework

The namable claim of this article is a prediction tool, and this section makes the tool explicit. Because the Constitution supplies no education right, almost every federal education case is really a question about whether a spending statute created enforceable individual rights, and a reader who works through three questions in order will correctly predict outcomes across doctrinally unrelated cases.

The first question is the forum question: does this dispute rest on the Constitution or on a statute? If the claim is that funding disparities violate equal protection, Rodriguez answers no at the federal level, and the reader should look to state court. If the claim is that a state denied education entirely to a class of children, Plyler's total denial analysis may apply, and the reader should check which line the facts fall on. If the claim invokes the religion clauses, the public funding line's tests, neutrality and private choice for Establishment Clause permission, status and use discrimination for Free Exercise Clause prohibition, supply the framework. Everything else is statutory, which leads to the second question.

The second question is the rights question: does the statute create enforceable individual rights, or only aggregate institutional duties? The reader looks for rights creating language, phrasing that speaks to the benefited class in individual terms, as in Title IX's prohibition of discrimination against persons, versus phrasing that directs the fund recipient's policies and procedures in the aggregate, as in FERPA's records provisions. Where the language confers rights, Cannon and Franklin show the path to private enforcement and damages. Where it directs institutional compliance, Gonzaga shows the lawsuit fails. Where the asserted right comes from an agency regulation rather than the statute itself, Sandoval shows the private action fails even if the regulation is valid, because regulations cannot conjure causes of action Congress did not authorize. The test is deliberately formal, and its formalism is the point: in spending legislation, the Court reads the statute as a bargain with the recipient, and only rights creating terms give third parties someone to sue.

The third question is the remedy and procedure question: assuming a right exists, what relief does the law allow and what procedures must come first? Franklin supplies damages for intentional Title IX violations. The IDEA cases supply a menu: reimbursement for unilateral private placement under Burlington and Carter, stay put protection against unilateral discipline under Honig, related services under Tatro's physician test, and the exhaustion bypass for pure discrimination claims under Fry. The special education line rewards procedural precision: the statute's procedures are the substance, and litigants who master the IEP process, the stay put provision, and the exhaustion rules win cases that litigants who argue only about educational quality lose.

Two wild cards qualify the method. The first is the override cycle. When the Court reads a statute narrowly, Congress can rewrite it, as it did after Grove City, and the reader must always check whether the statute on the books is the statute the Court construed. The Title IX on the books after the 1988 Restoration Act is institution wide in coverage; the IDEA as amended in 1990 and 1997 carries exhaustion and discipline provisions the early cases did not address. The method predicts outcomes under current law, not under the law as it stood when the opinions were written. The second wild card is the major questions doctrine, which adds a clear authorization requirement to executive action of vast significance. After Biden v. Nebraska, the reader evaluating an ambitious Department of Education program must ask not only whether the statute can bear the program's weight but whether Congress spoke clearly enough to authorize action of that magnitude. The doctrine is new to education law, and its boundaries will be worked out in the cases to come, but its direction is unmistakable: the larger the program, the clearer the text must be.

Applied across the article's cases, the method reproduces every holding. Rodriguez: Constitution, no fundamental right, rational basis, federal claim fails, state court next. Cannon: statute, rights creating language, implied action allowed. Franklin: same statute, Bell v. Hood presumption, damages allowed. Gonzaga: statute, aggregate duties language, no private action. Sandoval: regulation rather than statute, no private action. Rowley: statute, procedural definition of FAPE, undemanding substantive floor. Endrew F.: same statute, floor raised but not to equality. Tatro, Burlington, Honig, Carter, Fry: statute, procedures as substance, specific remedies for specific violations. Mueller through Zelman: Constitution's religion clauses, neutrality plus private choice, indirect aid permitted. Trinity Lutheran through Carson: Free Exercise, exclusion from available benefits prohibited. Biden v. Nebraska: executive action, major questions, clear authorization required and not found. The framework holds across all five lines because all five lines are applications of the same structural situation: conditional spending statutes, construed by courts, occasionally rewritten by Congress, operating in the space the Constitution leaves open.

## The enforcement economy: why the private right question decides everything

The private enforcement line deserves one more section, because the doctrinal question of who may sue has an economic dimension that the opinions understate and practitioners live with daily. A statute's enforcement design determines not only whether violations are remedied but whether the statute shapes behavior at all, and the difference between private enforcement and agency enforcement is the difference between a law that operates and a law that merely exists.

Consider the remedy Congress wrote into most of the education spending statutes: administrative fund termination. Under Title IX's section 902, under FERPA, and under Title VI's section 602, the ultimate sanction is the cutoff of federal funds to the noncompliant program or institution. As a practical matter, agencies almost never use it. Terminating a school district's federal funds punishes the students the statute was meant to protect, deprives the neediest districts of resources, and invites political retaliation against the agency. The fund cutoff is therefore a nuclear option: its existence gives agencies leverage in negotiations, but its use is so costly that it functions as a deterrent against enforcement rather than against violation. Regulated institutions understand this. A compliance obligation backed only by fund termination is an obligation the institution can discount, because the probability of the sanction approaches zero.

Private enforcement reverses the calculus. Where Cannon and Franklin apply, every affected student is a potential plaintiff, every plaintiffs' lawyer a potential enforcer, and damages supply the incentive to sue. The result is the compliance infrastructure that now surrounds Title IX: coordinators, grievance procedures, training programs, investigations, settlements, and insurance. None of that apparatus appears in the statute's text. It is the market response to the implied right plus damages, and it operates continuously rather than episodically, because private plaintiffs do not face the agency's political constraints. The enforcement economy of Title IX is thus largely judge made, which is why the later narrowings matter beyond doctrine: each decision closing a private enforcement route does not merely resolve a legal question but dismantles a compliance economy and returns the statute to agency enforcement, which is to say, to sporadic enforcement.

The asymmetry Sandoval created illustrates the point concretely. Intentional discrimination under Title VI section 601 remains privately enforceable, so institutions maintain compliance systems against intentional bias. Disparate impact under the section 602 regulations is enforceable only by agencies, so institutions face little private pressure to audit policies for discriminatory effects. The predictable result is that enforcement concentrates on the kind of discrimination that is easiest to allege but hardest to find, intentional bias, while the kind that the data most often reveals, disparate effect, goes largely unpoliced. Whether that allocation reflects congressional design or judicial policy preference is the question the Sandoval dissent pressed, but the practical consequence does not depend on the answer: the enforcement economy follows the private right, and where no private right exists, the law's behavioral footprint shrinks.

The IDEA presents the intermediate case and confirms the pattern. The statute supplies its own private enforcement machinery: due process hearings, administrative complaints, the reimbursement remedy, and attorney's fees for prevailing parents. That machinery generates an enormous volume of proceedings, far more than agency enforcement could ever produce, and it has professionalized both sides, with districts employing specialized counsel and parents supported by advocacy organizations. The substantive standard Rowley set was undemanding, but the procedural enforcement was intense, which is why the special education line is procedure driven: when enforcement is constant, procedure becomes the battleground. Endrew F.'s raising of the substantive standard matters precisely because the enforcement machinery exists to apply it; a higher standard without private enforcement would be hortatory, while the same standard with the IDEA's hearing system behind it changes IEP practice nationwide.

The lesson for the reader is the one the namable claim implies. Asking whether a statute creates a privately enforceable right is not a technical threshold question to be cleared on the way to the merits. It is the merits, in the only sense that affects behavior. A right without a private remedy is a norm; a right with one is a constraint. The Court's enforcement decisions are therefore the most practically consequential holdings in this article's set, more consequential for daily school operations than the substantive standards, because they determine which substantive standards anyone will ever litigate.

## Statute or Constitution: why the distinction organizes everything

Every case in the doctrine ledger rests on either a statute or the Constitution, and knowing which is which is not a taxonomic nicety. It determines where the law can be found, who can change it, and what kind of argument wins the next case. This section makes the distinction operational, because the verification record states the rests on classification for each anchor and the article's framework depends on it.

The constitutional cases are the minority, and they are the ones Congress cannot override by ordinary legislation. Rodriguez rests on the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause: only a constitutional amendment or the Court itself can change the holding that education is not a federal fundamental right. The public funding cases rest on the First Amendment's religion clauses: Mueller, Witters, Zobrest, Mitchell, and Zelman on the Establishment Clause, Trinity Lutheran, Espinoza, and Carson on the Free Exercise Clause. South Dakota v. Dole rests on Article I's Spending Clause, stating the constitutional limits of the conditional spending power itself. Plyler rests on equal protection. In each of these cases the Court interprets the Constitution, which means the holding binds Congress as well as the states, subject only to amendment or overruling. That is why the Free Exercise shift in the funding line constrains state legislatures so tightly: no statute can authorize what the Constitution forbids, and no statute is needed for what the Constitution requires.

The statutory cases are the majority, and they are the ones Congress can rewrite. Cannon, Franklin, and Grove City construe Title IX; Gonzaga construes FERPA and 42 U.S.C. 1983; Sandoval construes Title VI; Rowley, Tatro, Burlington, Honig, Carter, Endrew F., and Fry construe the IDEA and its predecessors; Biden v. Nebraska construes the HEROES Act. In each of these cases the Court interprets what Congress wrote, which means Congress can change the result by writing something different. The Grove City override is the proof of concept: the Court read program or activity narrowly, Congress rewrote it broadly, and the new text governed. The same possibility attends every statutory holding in the ledger. A future Congress could write express private rights into FERPA, authorize private enforcement of disparate impact regulations, or raise the FAPE standard by statute, and each of those enactments would supersede the corresponding judicial holding without any constitutional difficulty. The statutory holdings are therefore provisional in a way the constitutional holdings are not: they state the law until Congress says otherwise.

The distinction also determines the enforcement vehicle, which is where litigants most often go wrong. Constitutional violations are typically enforced through 42 U.S.C. 1983, which supplies a cause of action for the deprivation of constitutional rights under color of state law, subject to defenses including qualified immunity. Statutory violations in the spending context are enforced through the vehicles the statute supplies or implies: express private rights where Congress wrote them, implied rights where the Court finds rights creating language, section 1983 where the statute creates individual federal rights, and agency fund termination where it does not. Gonzaga is the case that teaches the relationship between these vehicles: the Court held both that FERPA supports no implied private right and that it creates no rights enforceable under section 1983, because both theories require the same thing, rights creating language, which FERPA lacks. A litigant who pleads section 1983 without identifying a rights creating provision loses for the same reason the implied right plaintiff loses. The vehicles differ in their doctrinal details, but they share a single gate, and the gate is the statutory text.

There is one more category worth naming because it confuses even experienced readers: cases that construe a statute against a constitutional background. Sandoval is the illustration. The holding is statutory, no private right to enforce section 602 regulations, but the reasoning invokes constitutional avoidance principles and separation of powers concerns about agencies expanding statutory prohibitions beyond what Congress enacted. Mitchell v. Helms is another: the holding is constitutional, the Establishment Clause permits the aid, but the controlling concurrence applies the Agostini test, which is itself a judicial gloss on the constitutional text. And Biden v. Nebraska construes a statute, the HEROES Act, through the major questions doctrine, which is a constitutional separation of powers principle operating as a clear statement rule. In each of these hybrid cases the reader must keep two questions distinct: what does the statute say, and what does the Constitution require the statute to say clearly? The major questions doctrine is the most prominent current example of the second question shaping the first: it does not hold that Congress cannot authorize mass loan cancellation, but that Congress must do so clearly, which is a constitutional demand about legislative clarity imposed on statutory interpretation.

For research purposes, the distinction tells the reader where to look. Constitutional holdings are found in the U.S. Reports and change only through new decisions or amendment; the researcher reads cases. Statutory holdings are found in the interaction between the U.S. Code and the U.S. Reports; the researcher reads the statutory text as amended first, then the cases construing it, then the legislative history for any override. A researcher who reads only the cases will miss the Restoration Act's reversal of Grove City; a researcher who reads only the statute will miss the judicial glosses that determine what the text means in practice. The article's method throughout has been to state both, the statutory text and the judicial construction, because in federal education law neither is intelligible without the other.

The payoff of the distinction returns the reader to the namable claim. No federal right, therefore statutes: the Constitution's silence on education means the constitutional cases are few and mostly concern the religion clauses and the spending power, while the statutory cases are many and concern who may sue, for what, under which provision. The reader who sorts every new case into the statute or Constitution column before reading further will rarely be surprised by what follows, because the column determines the questions the opinion must answer, the sources it must consult, and the institutions that can change the result.

## The Cases in Sequence: Fifty Years of Doctrine

The ledger's chronological order tells a story that the doctrinal categories partly obscure, and the story is worth narrating straight through once.

The 1970s opened with the foundation and the first enforcement victory. Rodriguez in 1973 closed the constitutional door and Cannon in 1979 opened the statutory one, establishing that Title IX implies a private cause of action. The decade's lesson was the field's founding exchange: the Constitution would not supply education rights, but Congress's statutes could, and courts would enforce them.

The 1980s built the special education edifice and narrowed Title IX's reach. Rowley in 1982 set the FAPE standard, Mueller in 1983 opened neutral indirect aid, Tatro and Grove City in 1984 defined related services and narrowed Title IX to program specific coverage, Burlington in 1985 created the reimbursement remedy, Witters in 1986 extended private choice, Dole in 1987 constitutionalized conditional spending, and Honig in 1988 protected placement during disputes. Congress closed the decade by overriding Grove City with the Civil Rights Restoration Act of 1987, enacted over President Reagan's veto in 1988, restoring institution wide coverage. The decade's lesson was institutional: the Court would define the statutes' requirements in detail, and Congress would correct the Court when the definition went too far.

The 1990s consolidated. Franklin in 1992 added damages to Title IX, Zobrest in 1993 brought the interpreter into the parochial school, and Carter in 1993 widened reimbursement. The decade produced fewer new doctrines than applications of settled ones, which is the sign of a maturing field.

The 2000s retrenched private enforcement and advanced the religion doctrine. Sandoval in 2001 closed private disparate impact suits, Mitchell in 2000 upheld direct instructional aid without a majority rationale, Zelman in 2002 sustained vouchers through true private choice, and Gonzaga in 2002 closed private FERPA enforcement. The decade's lesson was the ascendancy of the clear statement instinct: rights and remedies would not be inferred, and regulations would not create what Congress had not authorized.

The 2010s and beyond, arriving after this article's frame, raised standards and shifted clauses. Endrew F. and Fry in 2017 strengthened the IDEA's substantive and procedural protections, Trinity Lutheran in 2017 began the Free Exercise turn, Espinoza in 2020 carried it into education funding, Carson in 2022 extended it to use based exclusions, and Biden v. Nebraska in 2023 applied the major questions doctrine to executive education policy. The period's lesson is that the field's center of gravity moved from whether statutes create rights to how far the political branches may go under the rights and powers the statutes confer.

Fifty years, one structural fact, and a litigation system built to operate within it. The chronology is the argument.

## The counter-reading: the protection exists at a different level

The assumption this article must correct is the natural one: that the absence of a federal right means education is unprotected. It does not mean that. It means the protection exists at a different level of government, under different texts, enforced by different courts, and a reader who looks only at federal law will systematically underestimate how much legal protection American students actually enjoy.

State constitutions contain express education provisions that the federal Constitution lacks. The formulations vary: some states require a thorough and efficient system of public schools, others a uniform system, others an adequate or suitable education, and the textual differences matter because state courts construe their own documents. Beginning in the years after Rodriguez and continuing through the decades this article covers, state supreme courts used those provisions to do what the federal courts could not: define adequacy in enforceable terms, order legislatures to redesign funding formulas, and in some states retain jurisdiction for years to supervise compliance. The litigation has been extensive and consequential, reshaping school finance in state after state, and it proceeds on legal theories that have no federal analogue. The federal courthouse applies rational basis review to funding disparities; the state courthouse next door may apply a constitutional adequacy mandate with real teeth. That is not a contradiction. It is federalism operating as designed, with different sovereigns answering to different charters.

The same level shifting appears in the statutory enforcement picture. Federal statutes set floors, and states routinely build above them. The IDEA's FAPE standard is a federal minimum; nothing in the federal cases prevents a state from requiring more of its schools, and several states have enacted standards or procedures more protective than the federal baseline. Title IX's federal enforcement coexists with state antidiscrimination statutes that cover more conduct, more institutions, or provide broader remedies. The federal litigation this article maps is therefore the bottom layer of a multilayered system, not the whole system, and the reader who treats a federal holding as the final word on what the law requires will miss the state law that actually governs the school down the street.

There is a final subtlety worth stating because it completes the article's framework. Rodriguez closed the federal constitutional door, but it did not close the federal statutory door, and the distinction between those two doors is the article's entire subject. The federal Constitution supplies no education right; federal statutes supply enforceable rights where Congress created them, and the courts decide case by case whether Congress did. The protection is real but conditional: real where the statute speaks in rights creating language, conditional on Congress having spoken at all. The state constitutions then add a second layer of real and unconditional, within each state, protection. The complete picture is therefore not unprotected students but students protected twice over, once by federal statutes where the enforcement conditions are met and once by state constitutions whose education clauses the federal document does not contain. The reader who can hold both layers in view, and keep them distinct, has the field.

## How the five lines connect

The five lines are one story told five ways. Rodriguez removed the Constitution from education litigation, which made every federal case a statutory case. The private enforcement line then asked which statutes create lawsuits, and answered by distinguishing rights creating language from aggregate institutional duties. The special education line asked what the statutes require once suit is possible, and answered with a procedural edifice surrounding a substantive floor that the Court raised in 2017. The public funding line asked what the Constitution permits and forbids when money reaches religious schools, and moved from Establishment Clause permission for neutral indirect aid to Free Exercise Clause prohibition of exclusion from available benefits. The executive authority line asked what officials may do under the statutes, and answered with a clear authorization requirement for actions of vast significance. In each line the practical reach of conditional spending statutes was determined not by the statutes' text alone but by the interaction of text, judicial construction, and, in the Grove City override, congressional response.

For readers working with the companion research tools, the case level detail in the opinions rewards close reading, and the [VaultBook study notebook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html) can be used alongside this guide to keep statute notes, citations, and case chronologies together. The article's contribution is the structure: no federal right, therefore statutes, therefore the enforcement question, therefore the five lines. A reader who carries that structure into any education case, however doctrinally unfamiliar, will know which questions to ask first, and will usually be able to predict the answers.

## How to Cite These Cases

A research grade article should leave the reader able to cite what it teaches, and education cases have citation conventions worth stating plainly. Supreme Court decisions are cited by volume, reporter, and page: San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez, 411 U.S. 1 (1973). The parenthetical year is the decision year, not the argument year. Parallel citations to the Supreme Court Reporter or the Lawyers' Edition are unnecessary in modern practice.

Statutes are cited to their public law number and Statutes at Large citation on enactment, and to the United States Code as codified. The HEROES Act is Public Law 108-76, 117 Stat. 904, codified at 20 U.S.C. sections 1098aa to 1098ee, with the waive or modify provision at 20 U.S.C. section 1098bb(a)(1). Title IX is 20 U.S.C. sections 1681 to 1688, with the operative ban at section 1681(a). FERPA's nondisclosure provisions are 20 U.S.C. section 1232g. The IDEA's key provisions are 20 U.S.C. sections 1401, 1412, 1414, and 1415, with the exhaustion rule at section 1415(l) and the stay put rule now at section 1415(j). The Civil Rights Restoration Act of 1987 is Public Law 100-259, 102 Stat. 28.

Vote lines are cited as part of the holding's weight, not as decoration. A five to four decision with multiple dissents signals contested reasoning; a unanimous decision signals settled framework; a fragmented judgment like Mitchell requires identifying the controlling opinion. The verification discipline this article follows, confirming every citation, year, vote, and authorship against primary sources before stating it, is the discipline the field demands of anyone who writes about these cases, because a single wrong citation destroys credibility with exactly the readers this series serves.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Is there a constitutional right to education under federal education law?

No. The Supreme Court held in San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez, 411 U.S. 1 (1973), that education is not a fundamental right under the federal Constitution. The five to four majority applied rational basis review to Texas's property tax based school finance system and upheld it, finding no textual guarantee of education and refusing to imply one from education's importance. The consequence is structural: federal education litigation cannot rest on a constitutional right to education, so claims must be built on federal statutes instead. Protection does exist, but at different levels. State constitutions contain express education provisions that state courts have enforced extensively for fifty years, and federal statutes create enforceable rights where Congress used rights creating language. The absence of a federal right is therefore not the absence of protection; it is a division of labor between the federal Constitution's silence, federal statutes' conditional rights, and state constitutions' express guarantees.

### Q: What did San Antonio v. Rodriguez decide about education law?

Rodriguez decided March 21, 1973, by five to four, that Texas's school finance system did not violate the Equal Protection Clause. Justice Powell's majority held that education is not a fundamental right under the federal Constitution, expressly or impliedly, and that wealth is not a suspect classification, so the system faced only rational basis review. The system survived because it bore a rational relationship to the legitimate state interest in local control of schools. The dissents, led by Justice Marshall, argued that education deserved fundamental interest treatment because of its nexus to citizenship and constitutional rights. The decision's lasting effect was to redirect school finance litigation into state courts under state constitutional education clauses, where it has continued for five decades, and to make statutory interpretation rather than constitutional principle the basis of federal education lawsuits.

### Q: Can you sue a school under FERPA in education law?

No. In Gonzaga University v. Doe, 536 U.S. 273 (2002), the Supreme Court held seven to two that the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act creates no personal rights enforceable under 42 U.S.C. 1983 and supports no implied private right of action. The majority reasoned that FERPA is Spending Clause legislation directed at aggregate institutional compliance: it conditions federal funding on the maintenance of records policies, speaking to institutions in general terms rather than conferring individual rights on students. Because section 1983 enforces federal rights rather than broader statutory benefits, and because implied rights require rights creating language focused on the benefited class, FERPA failed both tests. Justices Stevens and Ginsburg dissented, arguing the nondisclosure provisions did protect identifiable students. Enforcement runs through the Department of Education's funding authority, which in practice means FERPA is rarely enforced through any adversarial process at all.

### Q: What did Biden v. Nebraska decide about education law?

Biden v. Nebraska, 600 U.S. 477, decided June 30, 2023, held six to three that the Secretary of Education's mass student loan cancellation program exceeded the authority Congress granted in the HEROES Act. The program would have canceled up to ten thousand dollars per borrower, up to twenty thousand for Pell Grant recipients, at an estimated cost of roughly four hundred thirty billion dollars. Chief Justice Roberts's majority applied the major questions doctrine, requiring clear congressional authorization for executive action of vast economic and political significance, and held that the Act's authority to waive or modify loan provisions, 20 U.S.C. 1098bb(a)(1), did not clearly authorize what was effectively a new program rather than a modification. Justice Kagan dissented, joined by Justices Sotomayor and Jackson, arguing the statute's broad emergency language was deliberately capacious. The decision is presented here as a later development after the article's 2014 frame.

### Q: Are school vouchers constitutional under education law?

Yes, when the program satisfies the true private choice doctrine. In Zelman v. Simmons-Harris, 536 U.S. 639 (2002), the Supreme Court held five to four that Ohio's Cleveland voucher program did not violate the Establishment Clause. The program gave tuition vouchers to poor children in a failing district, usable at participating public or private schools, secular or religious. Chief Justice Rehnquist's majority held that where a program is religiously neutral and aid reaches religious schools only through genuine independent private parental choice, any advancement of religion is attributable to the individuals, not the government. The dissents argued the program's realistic options were overwhelmingly religious and that formal neutrality masked substantive endorsement. Later Free Exercise decisions added the other half of the rule: once a state subsidizes private education, it cannot exclude schools solely because they are religious. Zelman permits neutral vouchers; it does not require states to enact them.

### Q: Why did school finance cases move to state courts in education law?

They moved because Rodriguez closed the federal courthouse door. After the Supreme Court held in 1973 that education is not a federal fundamental right and that funding disparities receive only rational basis review, federal equal protection challenges to school finance systems could not succeed. Litigants therefore turned to state courts and state constitutions, which unlike the federal Constitution contain express education provisions requiring thorough, efficient, uniform, or adequate systems depending on the state. State supreme courts proved a friendlier forum: over five decades they defined adequacy in enforceable terms, ordered legislatures to redesign funding formulas, and in some states supervised compliance for years. The move was a change of venue, not a retreat, and it explains why the federal and state bodies of school finance law look so different. Treating state finance rulings as federal holdings is one of the recurring errors this article flags, because the two rest on different documents.

### Q: Do federal education law statutes create private rights to sue?

Sometimes, and the distinction decides case after case. The Court asks whether Congress used rights creating language focused on the benefited class, as opposed to language directing aggregate institutional compliance. Title IX was held to contain an implied private cause of action in Cannon v. University of Chicago (1979), and damages were held available in Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools (1992). But FERPA was held to create no enforceable individual rights in Gonzaga University v. Doe (2002), because its provisions address institutional policies rather than students. And in Alexander v. Sandoval (2001), the Court held that individuals cannot sue to enforce disparate impact regulations under Title VI, leaving enforcement to agencies. The practical difference is enormous: privately enforceable statutes generate constant litigation and compliance infrastructure, while statutes enforceable only through agency fund termination are enforced rarely. Readers who learn the rights creating language test can predict outcomes across unrelated statutes.

### Q: What is the major questions doctrine in education law?

The major questions doctrine requires clear congressional authorization before courts will sustain executive action of vast economic and political significance. In education law the doctrine arrived with Biden v. Nebraska, 600 U.S. 477 (2023), where the Court held six to three that the HEROES Act's authority to waive or modify student loan provisions did not clearly authorize the Secretary's four hundred thirty billion dollar mass cancellation program. The majority reasoned that waiver and modification contemplate modest adjustments to existing provisions, not the effective introduction of a new regime, and that a program of such magnitude required explicit congressional approval. The doctrine now functions as the lens for ambitious executive action in education: the larger the program's economic and political footprint, the clearer the statutory language must be. It extends the article's organizing claim into the separation of powers, requiring officials to show authorization just as plaintiffs must show enforceable rights.

### Q: What did Cannon and Franklin establish about Title IX enforcement lawsuits?

Together the two decisions built the private enforcement regime for Title IX. Cannon v. University of Chicago, 441 U.S. 677 (1979), held six to three that Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972 contains an implied private cause of action, applying the four factor Cort v. Ash test and holding that the statute's administrative fund cutoff procedure was not the exclusive remedy. Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, 503 U.S. 60 (1992), held unanimously in the judgment that damages are available for intentional Title IX violations under the Bell v. Hood presumption that federal courts may award any appropriate relief absent clear congressional direction otherwise, and validated the implied right against state sovereign immunity claims through the 1986 Rehabilitation Act amendments. The pair transformed Title IX from a funding condition enforced sporadically by agencies into a privately litigated regime with compliance officers, grievance procedures, and settlements, which is why later decisions narrowing implied rights altered the enforcement economy of the field.

### Q: What does a free appropriate public education require under the IDEA?

The standard was set in Board of Education v. Rowley, 458 U.S. 176 (1982), and raised in Endrew F. v. Douglas County School District, 580 U.S. 386 (2017). Rowley held six to three that the statute requires personalized instruction with sufficient support services to permit the child to benefit educationally, for regular classroom children an IEP reasonably calculated to enable passing marks and grade advancement, with no maximum potential or equality of opportunity mandate. Endrew F., decided March 22, 2017, after this article's 2014 frame, held unanimously that an IEP must be reasonably calculated to enable progress appropriate in light of the child's circumstances, a standard markedly more demanding than the merely more than de minimis test some circuits had drawn from Rowley, while rejecting a full equal opportunity requirement. The rule after Endrew F. is therefore more than trivial benefit and less than equal outcomes, calibrated to the individual child, with challenging objectives required.

### Q: When can families skip IDEA procedures and sue under other disability laws?

When the gravamen of the suit is something other than the denial of a free appropriate public education. In Fry v. Napoleon Community Schools, 580 U.S. 154, decided February 22, 2017, after this article's 2014 frame, the Supreme Court held unanimously that exhaustion of the IDEA's administrative procedures under 20 U.S.C. 1415(l) is unnecessary where the complaint's substance is discrimination rather than FAPE denial. The opinion supplied a two part test: exhaustion is not required if the family could have brought essentially the same claim against a public facility that is not a school, or if an adult at the school could have pressed essentially the same grievance. The service dog claim in Fry passed because it concerned access discrimination remediable under the ADA and section 504, not the adequacy of an IEP. Claims that are at bottom about educational benefit must still go through the IDEA process first, so families and lawyers must characterize the grievance correctly at the pleading stage.

### Q: What is the stay-put rule when schools discipline a disabled student?

The stay-put provision, now codified at 20 U.S.C. 1415(j), provides that a disabled child shall remain in the current educational placement during the pendency of proceedings challenging a proposed change. In Honig v. Doe, 484 U.S. 305 (1988), the Supreme Court held six to two, Justice Kennedy not participating, that the provision bars schools from unilaterally changing a child's placement through indefinite suspension or expulsion for disability related misconduct, with no dangerousness exception. Justice Brennan's majority held that schools concerned about dangerous students must seek a court injunction rather than acting on their own. Chief Justice Rehnquist dissented, joined in part by Justice Scalia. The decision makes the statute's procedures a substantive constraint on discipline: misconduct connected to a disability must run through the IEP process and the statute's protections rather than around them. Note that the Court split four to four on a separate direct services question in Honig, which has no precedential effect.

### Q: Can parents be reimbursed for a private school placement under the IDEA?

Yes, under the conditions the Court set in two unanimous decisions. In School Committee of Burlington v. Department of Education of Massachusetts, 471 U.S. 359 (1985), the Court held that the statute's authorization for courts to grant such relief as the court determines is appropriate includes ordering reimbursement to parents who unilaterally placed their child in a private school when the public IEP was inappropriate and the private placement was proper, and that the unilateral move during pending proceedings does not waive the claim. In Florence County School District Four v. Carter, 510 U.S. 7 (1993), the Court extended the remedy to private schools that are not on the state's approved list and do not satisfy every statutory FAPE requirement, so long as the placement is otherwise proper under the statute, with reimbursement remaining discretionary equitable relief. The practical effect is that parents who can afford to front tuition may place unilaterally and seek repayment, which makes the adequacy of the district's program the decisive litigation issue.

### Q: Why did Congress override the Supreme Court in Grove City College v. Bell?

Because the Court read Title IX's coverage too narrowly for Congress's liking. In Grove City College v. Bell, 465 U.S. 555 (1984), the Court held that a college whose students received federal aid was a Title IX recipient but that coverage was program specific: only the aided program, there the financial aid office, had to comply, not the institution as a whole. Congress responded with the Civil Rights Restoration Act of 1987, Public Law 100-259, which restored institution wide coverage for Title IX, Title VI, section 504, and the Age Discrimination Act of 1975. President Reagan vetoed the bill on March 16, 1988, and Congress overrode the veto on March 22, 1988, by 73 to 24 in the Senate and 292 to 133 in the House. The episode is the override cycle in clean form: the Court construes, Congress disagrees and rewrites, and the statute's practical reach is determined in the interaction. Every Title IX case since has been litigated under the restored institution wide coverage.

### Q: What did Alexander v. Sandoval decide about disparate impact regulations?

Sandoval, 532 U.S. 275 (2001), held five to four that no private right of action exists to enforce the disparate impact regulations that federal agencies promulgated under section 602 of Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964. Justice Scalia's majority assumed without deciding that section 601 supports a private right for intentional discrimination, but held that language in a regulation cannot conjure up a private cause of action Congress did not authorize. Because the regulations prohibit practices with discriminatory effects beyond section 601's intentional discrimination ban, private enforcement of the broader regulatory prohibition would expand the statute beyond its design. Enforcement of the disparate impact rules was left to federal agencies through fund termination. Justice Stevens dissented, joined by three colleagues, arguing the regulations reasonably interpreted the statute and deserved deference. For education, the result is structural: discipline, tracking, and resource policies challenged for disparate effects cannot be sued over privately under Title VI; plaintiffs must prove intent or persuade an agency to act.

### Q: What limits does the Constitution place on conditions attached to federal funds?

The framework comes from South Dakota v. Dole, 483 U.S. 203 (1987), which upheld conditioning highway funds on a minimum drinking age by seven to two. Chief Justice Rehnquist's majority stated five requirements: the spending must pursue the general welfare; conditions must be stated unambiguously so states choose knowingly, the clear statement rule; conditions must be related to the federal interest in the funded program, the germaneness requirement; conditions must not require states to violate other constitutional provisions; and the inducement must not be so coercive that pressure becomes compulsion. In Dole, withholding roughly five to ten percent of highway funds was mere pressure, not coercion. For education, the test authorizes the conditional spending structure of Title IX, the IDEA, and FERPA, and supplies the vocabulary for challenges: litigants most often invoke the clear statement requirement, arguing Congress did not speak plainly enough to impose a particular obligation, while the coercion limit hovers over the largest programs.

### Q: What did Trinity Lutheran and Espinoza decide about excluding religious schools from public programs?

The two decisions moved the public funding line from Establishment Clause permission to Free Exercise Clause prohibition of exclusion, and both were decided after this article's 2014 frame. Trinity Lutheran Church v. Comer, 582 U.S. 449 (2017), held seven to two that Missouri violated the Free Exercise Clause by excluding a church preschool from a neutral playground resurfacing grant solely because of its religious status, triggering strict scrutiny for status based discrimination. Espinoza v. Montana Department of Revenue, 591 U.S. 464 (2020), held five to four that Montana could not apply its no aid provision to exclude religious schools from a tax credit scholarship program: a state need not subsidize private education, but once it does, it cannot disqualify schools solely because they are religious. Carson v. Makin, 596 U.S. 767 (2022), then extended the rule from status based to use based exclusions. Together they require states to test funding exclusions against strict scrutiny.

### Q: What standard did Endrew F. set for special education in 2017?

Endrew F. v. Douglas County School District RE-1, 580 U.S. 386, decided March 22, 2017, after this article's 2014 frame, held unanimously, eight to zero, that a school meets its substantive IDEA obligation only by offering an IEP reasonably calculated to enable a child to make progress appropriate in light of the child's circumstances. Chief Justice Roberts's opinion described the standard as markedly more demanding than the merely more than de minimis test the Tenth Circuit had drawn from Rowley, under which Endrew's essentially recycled IEPs had been deemed sufficient. At the same time the Court rejected the parents' proposed equal opportunity standard, holding the statute does not require opportunities substantially equal to those of nonhandicapped children. For children in regular classrooms, Rowley's grade advancement measure remains the guide; for others, the IEP must set appropriately ambitious and challenging objectives. All eight justices agreed the de minimis reading had set the bar too low.

### Q: Are health-related services like catheterization covered as related services?

Yes, when they need not be performed by a licensed physician. In Irving Independent School District v. Tatro, 468 U.S. 883, decided July 5, 1984, the Supreme Court held nine to zero that clean intermittent catheterization is a related service rather than an excluded medical service under the education statute, because the medical services exclusion covers only services that must be performed by a physician, and catheterization can be performed by a school nurse or trained layperson. Chief Justice Burger wrote the opinion; reports attributing it to Justice Brennan are mistaken. The same decision held six to three that where the education statute's remedies are available, section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act supplies no additional relief, including attorney's fees, channeling disability education claims into the statute's own procedures. The related services definition thus turns on who must provide the service, not on whether the service is health related in a colloquial sense.

### Q: How did Mitchell v. Helms treat instructional materials aid to religious schools?

Mitchell v. Helms, 530 U.S. 793 (2000), upheld the federal Chapter 2 program lending computers, software, and library books to schools including religious schools, six to three in the judgment, and overruled the earlier decisions that had barred such instructional material aid. But the opinion structure requires care: no opinion commanded a majority. Justice Thomas's four justice plurality would have upheld any religiously neutral aid with secular content. The controlling opinion was Justice O'Connor's narrower concurrence in the judgment, joined by Justice Breyer, which applied the Agostini test, asking whether the aid caused government indoctrination, whether recipients were defined by religion, and whether the program excessively entangled church and state, and upheld Chapter 2 on those grounds. Justice Souter dissented with two colleagues. The case therefore stands for the narrower proposition that evenhanded secular instructional aid satisfies the Establishment Clause, not for the plurality's broader neutrality rule, and vote counts alone do not state its law.
