A New Historicist reading of Gatsby begins with a refusal. It refuses to treat the novel as a sealed aesthetic object that history merely surrounds, and it refuses just as firmly to treat history as a stable backdrop the novel passively reflects. Both refusals matter, and most student essays that claim to use the lens abandon one of them within a paragraph. What replaces the two refusals is a harder and more interesting proposition: that Fitzgerald's book and the culture it came out of were made of the same material, that the anxieties circulating in American public life between 1919 and 1925 pass through the novel's sentences the way current passes through wire, and that the novel does not simply record those anxieties but participates in their circulation. This is the claim the lens exists to test, and it changes what counts as evidence.

![New Historicist reading of The Great Gatsby and 1920s discourses of class race and gender - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The change is easy to underestimate. A reader who has been trained on themes will ask what the valley of ashes means. A reader working the New Historicist lens will ask a different question: what did the phrase "valley of ashes" do in 1925, what vocabulary was already available for talking about industrial waste and the human beings who lived beside it, and how does the novel's version of that vocabulary line up with, or push against, the versions circulating in newspapers, political speeches, popular science, and advertising copy. The first question produces an interpretation. The second produces a map of the interpretation's conditions. The lens is worth learning because the second map explains things the first cannot.

## What New Historicism Actually Claims

New Historicism emerged in American literary study during the early 1980s, largely out of Renaissance scholarship, and the critic most associated with naming it is Stephen Greenblatt, who used the phrase in a 1982 introduction to a special issue of the journal Genre on Renaissance writing. The movement drew heavily on Michel Foucault's account of discourse and power, in which the ways a culture is permitted to speak about a subject are themselves an exercise of power rather than a neutral medium, and on the anthropologist Clifford Geertz's practice of thick description, in which a small cultural act is read at length for the whole web of meaning it assumes. Those two debts explain almost everything distinctive about how New Historicists behave in front of a text.

From Foucault the lens takes the conviction that power is not a substance held by a ruler and applied downward but a set of relations distributed through institutions, vocabularies, habits, and forms of knowledge. Under that conviction, a novel is not outside power looking in. It is one of the places where the era's permitted ways of speaking get rehearsed, tested, and occasionally strained. From Geertz the lens takes a method: begin with something small and apparently trivial, a gesture, an object, an offhand remark, and read it until the whole cultural system it presumes becomes visible. Put the two together and you get the characteristic New Historicist move, which is to set a literary passage beside a non-literary document from the same moment and read them as two expressions of one cultural grammar rather than as text and background.

### What is a New Historicist reading of a novel?

A New Historicist reading places a literary work inside the discourses and power relations of its historical moment and treats the two as mutually constituting. The novel does not merely reflect its period; it circulates, tests, and helps shape the period's ways of speaking about class, race, gender, and value.

That definition contains a word doing enormous work, and it is worth slowing down on. Discourse, in this tradition, does not mean conversation. It means the organized set of statements, categories, assumptions, and permissions that govern how a subject can be talked about at a given time. There was a 1920s discourse of racial hierarchy, with its own vocabulary of stocks and strains and submersion, its own claim to scientific authority, its own publishing apparatus and its own political outlet in immigration legislation. There was a 1920s discourse of class mobility, with its own vocabulary of self-making and its own quiet countervocabulary of breeding and background. There was a 1920s discourse of the modern woman, with its own contested terms for freedom, indiscretion, and sophistication. A New Historicist asks where the novel's sentences sit inside those systems.

The second load-bearing word is circulation. Greenblatt and the critics around him were interested in how cultural energy moves between spheres that a tidy account keeps separate: how a theatrical convention borrows from a court ritual, how a religious controversy shows up in a comedy's joke structure. Applied to Fitzgerald, the question is not whether he read a particular pamphlet but how the language of that pamphlet ends up in a character's mouth at a dinner party, what happens to it there, and what the novel's framing does to its authority. Circulation is a two-way idea. The pamphlet's language enters the novel; the novel's rendering of that language then re-enters the culture with a different charge.

The third commitment, and the one students most often drop, is that the historian's own position is not neutral either. New Historicism was born partly out of dissatisfaction with the older historicism that presented a period as a settled unity, a coherent worldview a reader could simply be told about and then apply. The newer version insists that any account of a period is itself constructed, partial, and shaped by present concerns. That is why New Historicist essays so often begin with an anecdote rather than a thesis: the anecdote signals that the critic is entering the material from somewhere particular rather than surveying it from above.

## How a New Historicist Reading of Gatsby Works

Start with the questions the lens actually asks, because they are specific enough to organize an essay. What discourses were available in the novel's moment for speaking about the subjects the novel raises? Where in the text do those discourses surface, in whose mouth, and with what framing? What does the novel do to a discourse when it renders it: amplify it, expose it, hollow it out, leave it standing? Whose interests does each discourse serve, and how does the novel position its reader in relation to those interests? What does the text make unsayable, and what does that silence tell you about the limits of the period's permitted speech?

Those five questions can be run against almost any passage, and they will produce different results depending on the passage's density. Some scenes in the novel are saturated with period discourse; the first dinner at the Buchanan house is one. Others are almost inert, and the lens will say less about them, which is useful information rather than a failure. A good New Historicist essay chooses its passages for discourse density the way a good close reading chooses its passages for figurative density.

### How do you apply New Historicism to a specific passage?

Identify the period discourse the passage draws on, locate its non-literary sources in the same decade, note who speaks it and how the narration frames them, then argue what the novel does to that discourse rather than only what the passage means. The argument lives in the transformation, not the identification.

The second half of that instruction is where student essays usually stop too early. Identifying that Tom Buchanan is repeating popular racial pseudoscience is the beginning of the analysis, not its conclusion, and an essay that ends there has produced a source note rather than a reading. The New Historicist question is what the novel does with the material: it gives the vocabulary to the least intelligent adult at the table, has him misremember his source, lets his wife mock him mid-sentence, and then makes the reader watch him use the same vocabulary later when he is genuinely frightened. The discourse is neither endorsed nor refuted. It is placed, and the placing is the argument.

Notice how much this differs from the older habit of gathering background. Background says: here is what people believed about race in 1925, and Tom believes it. Circulation says: here is a body of language that carried enormous political weight in 1925, here is what happens to that language when a novel routes it through a character the reader has already been taught to distrust, and here is why the routing does not neutralize the language so much as make its emotional function visible. The first account explains the character. The second explains the culture and the novel at once.

A further methodological point deserves emphasis because it separates a real application of the lens from a decorative one. New Historicism does not require that Fitzgerald consciously intended any of this. Intention is not the unit of analysis. The unit is the text as a site where period languages meet, and a novelist's ear can capture a discourse precisely without the novelist holding a settled position on it. This is liberating for the essay writer, because it removes the need to prove what Fitzgerald thought, and it is also a discipline, because it removes the option of resolving every difficulty by appeal to the author's supposed opinion.

## The Class Discourse: Self-Making, Breeding, and the Ash Heaps

Class is the discourse the novel handles most self-consciously, and it is where a New Historicist reading has the easiest work and therefore the greatest risk of becoming trivial. Every reader notices that East Egg and West Egg encode a distinction between inherited and acquired money. The lens asks what vocabulary the 1920s had for that distinction and how the novel deploys it.

The period's dominant public language about wealth was the language of self-making, and it was not a nineteenth-century leftover. It was actively renewed through the decade in advertising, in the enormous popular market for success literature, in the celebration of the self-taught executive, and in a stock market whose broadening participation made ordinary earners feel like proprietors. Against that language ran a quieter and older one, the vocabulary of family, breeding, and background, which had no popular publishing apparatus because it did not need one. The novel puts both languages on the page and lets them collide.

Nick opens with the second vocabulary and does not notice he is speaking it. He tells the reader that his family have been "prominent, well-to-do people in this Middle Western city for three generations," attaches to that a claim of descent from the Dukes of Buccleuch, and then, in the same breath, names an ancestor who "sent a substitute to the Civil War" and started a hardware business. The paragraph is doing precisely the thing that the discourse of breeding always does: it converts commercial money into inherited standing by adding time and a genealogy. Three generations is exactly long enough. The substitute sent to the war is the detail that gives the whole away, because purchasing exemption from military service is a transaction, and a transaction is what the vocabulary of breeding exists to make invisible.

Gatsby's side of the collision is the discourse of self-making run past its breaking point. He does not merely improve his position; he replaces his person. The novel is careful about the mechanics: the name changes, the biography is assembled from prestige components, the mansion is a copy of a European original, and the wealth is illegal. Read as character, that is a portrait of ambition. Read as discourse, it is the success narrative taken at face value and thereby exposed. The period's self-making language promised that a man could become anyone. Gatsby does exactly what the language says, and the novel's plot is the demonstration that the promise contained a limit nobody had bothered to state, which is that the receiving class reserves the right to refuse the arrival.

### Why does class function as a discourse rather than a fact in the novel?

Because the novel never presents class as a simple economic position. It presents it as a set of competing vocabularies, self-making against breeding, that characters use to claim or deny standing. Tom wins arguments by controlling which vocabulary applies, and that control is the power the lens is designed to make visible.

Tom's exercise of that control is worth watching closely, because it is where class discourse and raw power stop being distinguishable. At the Plaza he does not out-argue Gatsby on the merits. He changes the terms, calls him "Mr. Nobody from Nowhere," and in that phrase revokes the self-making vocabulary altogether. The phrase works because it denies Gatsby the two things the breeding vocabulary requires, a name with standing and a place of origin with standing, and because Tom has the social position to make the denial stick. The novel stages the moment as a defeat rather than a debate, which is the New Historicist point: discourses are not equal contestants, and the one attached to institutional power wins by controlling the frame.

The valley of ashes belongs in this section rather than in a purely symbolic reading, and this is one of the places where the lens produces a genuinely different result. The passage describes a "valley of ashes" where "ash-grey men" move through the dust, and the phrasing turns the workers into a product of the landscape rather than agents in it. The 1920s had a well-developed public language for industrial waste and the populations living beside it, drawn from sanitary reform, from the era's version of urban planning, and from a nativist strand that habitually described immigrant labor in terms of dirt and contagion. Fitzgerald's ash-grey men sit inside that language. The novel does not invent the equation of poverty with dust; it inherits it. Where the novel does something is in the geography: the ash heaps lie between the eggs and the city, so every journey between wealth and pleasure passes through the waste that produced both. That routing is the novel's contribution to the discourse, and it is closer to an argument than the description alone would be.

A New Historicist would also insist on noticing whom the ash-heap language does not extend to. George Wilson is described in terms of dust and fading, but Myrtle is described in terms of vitality and flesh, and the difference tracks the era's separate discourses for laboring men and for women who are being sexually appraised. The novel switches vocabularies within a single household, and the switch is not a slip. It is evidence that period discourse is not one language but several, sorted by the category of the person being described.

## The Race Discourse: Nordicism, Nativism, and the Color Line

The novel's racial material is where the New Historicist lens earns its keep, because it is the material that a purely formal or thematic reading has the most trouble placing. A theme-based approach tends to file Tom's outburst under characterization and move on. The lens cannot do that, because the language Tom uses was not eccentric in 1925. It was mainstream, institutionally backed, and about to be written into federal law.

The scene is the first dinner in Chapter 1. Tom announces that "Civilization's going to pieces" and asks whether Nick has read "The Rise of the Coloured Empires by this man Goddard." The book is Fitzgerald's lightly disguised version of a real publication, Lothrop Stoddard's 1920 volume on the rising tide of color, which sold heavily and was widely reviewed as serious argument rather than as fringe agitation. Tom's confidence that "it's all scientific stuff; it's been proved" is not a novelist's invention of a fool's opinion. It is an accurate reproduction of how that body of writing presented itself and how a large reading public received it. The claim to scientific authority was the discourse's central mechanism, because it converted a political preference into a fact and thereby removed it from argument.

Tom then supplies the two operative moves. He identifies the in-group by a scientific-sounding category, "This idea is that we're Nordics," and he states the political imperative that follows: "It's up to us, who are the dominant race, to watch out or these other races will have control of things." Those two sentences compress the entire structure of 1920s racial nativism, which classified European populations into ranked stocks, treated the ranking as biology, and drew from it an urgent policy conclusion. The policy conclusion was not hypothetical. The decade's immigration restriction, culminating in the 1924 act with its national-origins quota system, was argued in exactly this vocabulary, and the quotas were designed to freeze the country's ethnic composition at an earlier profile. When Fitzgerald put the vocabulary in Tom's mouth, he was quoting the room he was standing in.

### How does New Historicism read Tom Buchanan's racial theories?

Not as personal eccentricity but as period discourse in circulation. The vocabulary of ranked stocks and submersion was mainstream, scientifically credentialed, and legislatively active in the 1920s. The novel's contribution is what it does with the language: it attaches it to a frightened man defending an inheritance, exposing the discourse's emotional function.

That exposure is worth tracing carefully, because it is subtle and easily overstated. The novel does not refute Stoddard. No character presents a counterargument, no narrator explains why the science is bad, and Nick's response is a mild social embarrassment rather than an objection. What the novel does instead is structural. It makes Daisy mock the performance while it is happening, noting that Tom "reads deep books with long words in them," and it lets her ironize the imperative with a whispered "We've got to beat them down" while she winks at Nick. It gives Tom's certainty a visible source in insecurity, since he is a man whose athletic peak is behind him and whose social position is the only thing he has left that cannot be beaten. And then, decisively, it brings the vocabulary back at the moment of maximum threat.

That return is the passage in Chapter 7 where Tom, losing control of both his wife and his mistress on the same afternoon, moves from a complaint about his marriage to a warning that people who sneer at "family life and family institutions" will "next they'll throw everything overboard and have intermarriage between black and white." The leap is absurd on its face, and Nick registers the absurdity, describing the outburst as "impassioned gibberish" and noting that Tom "saw himself standing alone on the last barrier of civilization." A character-based reading calls this hypocrisy, since Tom's own adultery is the family institution actually under assault. The New Historicist reading goes one step further and identifies what the discourse is for. Racial panic here is not a belief Tom holds separately from his marital crisis. It is the vocabulary available to him for converting a private humiliation into a defense of civilization, and its availability is a fact about the period, not about him.

Jordan's murmured "We're all white here" is the line that seals the analysis, and it repays attention out of proportion to its length. It is spoken as a joke, deflating Tom's rhetoric by pointing out that his audience contains no one for the warning to be about. But it also states the room's actual condition, and in stating it the joke concedes the premise: the category is real, everyone present belongs to it, and the belonging is what makes the joke possible. That is exactly how a dominant discourse survives mockery. Being laughed at from inside costs it nothing, because the laughter confirms the boundary it draws.

The novel's other major racial moment is the Chapter 4 drive into the city. Crossing Blackwell's Island, Nick sees a limousine "driven by a white chauffeur" carrying three well-dressed Black passengers, and the sight prompts him to think, "Anything can happen now that we've slid over this bridge." The narration reaches for a demeaning description of the passengers, which is itself part of the evidence, since Nick's language belongs to the same period vocabulary he is elsewhere too fastidious to speak aloud. What matters for the lens is the logic of the moment: the inversion of the expected racial order in the seating arrangement registers to Nick as a sign that the ordinary rules have been suspended, and the very next thought is that Gatsby becomes possible in such a place. The novel has just told the reader that its central story of class reinvention belongs to the same category of disorder as a Black passenger with a white employee. That is a claim about how the period's discourses of race and class were braided together, and it is the novel making the claim, not the critic imposing it.

The treatment of Meyer Wolfshiem sits in the same territory and should not be softened. He is introduced through a physical description that leads with his ethnicity and dwells on his nose, and his speech is rendered in a phonetic tic. The 1920s produced a large volume of this material, in fiction, in cartoons, and in the era's most widely circulated antisemitic publishing, and Fitzgerald's portrait draws on the available conventions rather than departing from them. A New Historicist reading does not have to decide whether Fitzgerald was personally antisemitic in order to say something precise: the novel's account of criminal money is routed through a figure the period's discourse had already prepared, and the routing lets the story of American corruption be told without implicating the American families who are actually corrupt. Wolfshiem takes the moral weight; Tom, who has been careless with lives all summer, walks into a jewelry store at the end. The discourse does the sorting.

### Does the novel endorse the racial views it depicts?

The novel does not argue for them, and it frames Tom's version as frightened and self-serving. But it also lets the era's racial vocabulary shape its own narration and its portrait of Wolfshiem, so the honest answer is that the book both exposes the discourse and participates in it, which is what circulation means.

That double answer is not a hedge, and it is worth reading alongside the series' thematic treatment of [race and whiteness in the novel](/2024/11/18/race-whiteness-great-gatsby/), which traces the same material without the theoretical apparatus. It is the specific advantage of the lens over the two positions students usually take, which are that the novel is a critique of racism and that the novel is a racist artifact. Both positions assume the text has a settled relation to the discourse, either outside and judging or inside and complicit. Circulation describes a text that is inside the discourse and doing something with it, which is a more accurate account of how any book relates to the language it is written in.

## The Gender Discourse: The Modern Woman and the Beautiful Little Fool

The 1920s discourse about women was in visible flux, and the novel is written across the fault line. The vote had arrived in 1920, the terms flapper and modern woman were in constant circulation, hemlines and hair and cigarettes had become public arguments, and a large body of popular writing was devoted to explaining what the new freedoms meant and how much they should be feared. Alongside all of it ran the durable older language of feminine value, in which a woman's worth is her charm, her marriageability, and her decorative effect. The novel gives its three principal women three different positions inside that fault line.

Daisy's most quoted line is the one that states the discourse outright. Recounting her daughter's birth, she says she hoped the child would be a fool, and adds, "that's the best thing a girl can be in this world, a beautiful little fool." Read as psychology, the line is bitter maternal realism. Read as discourse, it is a woman correctly describing the terms on which her class assigns female value and choosing to teach them rather than fight them. The scene surrounds the line with the vocabulary of the modern woman, since Daisy immediately claims to have "been everywhere and seen everything and done everything" and calls herself sophisticated with an irony she cannot sustain. She is fluent in the new language and governed by the old one, and the novel makes that combination the source of her particular unhappiness.

### What does a New Historicist reading say about Daisy?

That she is positioned between two competing period vocabularies for female worth, the emerging language of the sophisticated modern woman and the older language of decorative value, and that her fluency in the first does not release her from the second. Her carelessness is what the contradiction produces, not a personal flaw the novel simply reports.

Jordan Baker occupies the other position. She is a professional athlete in a decade when women's competitive sport was newly visible, she is financially independent enough to be casual, she drives, she lies, and she is described in terms of hardness, coolness, and an athletic self-sufficiency that the narration finds attractive and faintly disreputable in the same breath. The period had a name for this figure and a whole apparatus of anxiety around her. The novel's contribution is the golf scandal, which attaches dishonesty to her independence in a way that reproduces the era's suspicion exactly, and then complicates it by making Nick, who prides himself on honesty, the man who wants her anyway and the man who leaves.

Myrtle Wilson shows what the discourse looks like when class is subtracted. Her attempt to enter the modern-woman vocabulary is made entirely through purchase: the dress, the apartment, the dog, the magazines, the affected accent she adopts along with the clothes. The novel is not gentle about the effort, and the ungentleness is itself period evidence, since the era's mockery of the shopgirl imitating her betters was a stock genre. Myrtle's fate delivers the discourse's harshest verdict. She is the only woman in the book who acts on desire in public, and she is killed for it by a car driven by the woman who never has to.

Set the three side by side and the pattern is not three characters but one system with three positions. The system rewards the woman who accepts decorative value and stays married, tolerates with suspicion the woman who is independent and childless, and destroys the woman who wants mobility and lacks the money to buy safety. Fitzgerald did not design that system. He recorded its operation with unusual precision, and a New Historicist reading is the tool that lets a student say so without reducing the novel to a moral scorecard.

### How does the novel treat the vote and the new legal status of women?

It never mentions them, and the silence is data. Suffrage arrived five years before publication, yet no character refers to it. The absence shows the novel working inside the older discourse of feminine value, where political rights are simply not part of how women's lives are described.

Silences of that kind are a standard New Historicist target, and they need to be handled with discipline. Not every absence is meaningful; a novel cannot mention everything. An absence becomes evidence when the topic is unmistakably live in the period, when the text has obvious occasions to raise it, and when the silence is consistent. Suffrage meets all three conditions here, since the novel contains political conversation, a woman who claims to have seen everything, and a decade-defining change to women's legal standing that no one finds worth a sentence.

## The Discourse of Illicit Money and the Timing of Respectability

Prohibition gave the 1920s a discourse without a precedent: a nationwide legal prohibition that a large share of the population declined to obey, producing an economy that was criminal by statute and ordinary by practice. The public language around it was correspondingly split. Enforcement rhetoric described bootlegging as an assault on the republic. Newspaper coverage of organized crime treated the same activity as a spectacle with recognizable stars. Advertising and social reporting simply assumed the availability of drink at any gathering worth describing. Three incompatible ways of speaking about the same conduct ran simultaneously, and a New Historicist reading looks for what a novel does when it has three to choose from.

Fitzgerald's answer is to use all three and let the reader feel the gap. Gatsby's parties present illicit liquor in the register of social reporting, as abundance and glamour, with nobody at any point remarking that the entire event is a crime. The source of the money is presented in the register of crime coverage, through Wolfshiem and the drugstore chain and the muttered telephone calls about towns and bonds. And the moral verdict arrives, when it arrives at all, in the register of enforcement rhetoric, through Tom, who is the only character to state that Gatsby is a criminal and who states it as an accusation of unfitness rather than of illegality.

### Why does the novel treat criminal money as ordinary rather than shocking?

Because Prohibition had made illegality the normal condition of new fortune rather than its exception. The novel reproduces that condition faithfully: nobody at the parties finds the liquor remarkable, and Gatsby's crime becomes damning only when Tom needs a weapon, which shows the charge functioning socially rather than morally.

The most useful thing the lens extracts here is the observation about timing. Nick's own family fortune begins with a great-uncle who bought his way out of military service and started a wholesale business; the money has been respectable for three generations. Tom's inheritance has no stated origin at all, which is itself a mark of how thoroughly time launders wealth, since a fortune old enough stops requiring an explanation. Gatsby's money is three years old and criminal, and the novel makes clear that the difference between his position and theirs is duration rather than character. That is a claim about the period's actual mechanics of respectability, and it is a claim the novel makes structurally by giving the reader all three origins and withholding any comment connecting them.

The fixed World's Series of 1919 belongs in the same analysis and is handled with characteristic economy. Wolfshiem is presented as the man who arranged it, and Nick's reaction is astonishment that a single person could tamper with something so many people believed in. The scandal was recent and enormous when Fitzgerald wrote, and the era's discussion of it had already settled into a shape: an emblem of how thoroughly money could reach into things that were supposed to be beyond it. The novel does not explain the emblem. It assumes the reader carries it and uses the assumption to establish the character of the world Gatsby's fortune comes from in a single exchange, which is exactly the efficiency a discourse makes available to a writer.

## The Closing Page and the Discourse of the Frontier

The final page is where a New Historicist reading has to be most careful, because it is the passage most likely to be treated as pure lyric and therefore exempt from historical analysis. It is not exempt. The vision of the "fresh, green breast of the new world" that greets Dutch sailors, and the sequence that runs from that vision to Gatsby's green light to the closing image of boats and current, is assembled from one of the period's most heavily worked discourses: the frontier as the source of American character and the anxiety that its closing had ended something.

That discourse had a formal academic origin in the frontier thesis advanced by Frederick Jackson Turner in the 1890s, and by the 1920s it had spread far beyond the seminar into popular history, political speech, advertising, and the enormous cultural production around westerns. Its structure was consistent: American possibility was spatial, the space had been used up, and what replaced it was uncertain. Fitzgerald's closing takes that structure and performs one substitution. The frontier that has closed is not geographic but temporal, and the direction of longing is not westward but backward. The novel's last sentences describe a striving that runs against a current toward a past, which converts a spatial discourse about national possibility into a temporal one about personal loss.

### What does the frontier discourse have to do with Gatsby's ending?

The closing vision of the new world greeting Dutch sailors draws on the period's frontier discourse, in which American possibility was spatial and had been used up. Fitzgerald converts the geography into chronology, so the lost frontier becomes the lost past and the westward dream becomes a backward one.

The conversion matters politically as well as formally. A frontier discourse locates the loss in history and implies that the country as a whole has been diminished. Fitzgerald's version locates it in an individual's relation to his own earlier life, which makes the loss universal and simultaneously drains it of political content. This is precisely the sort of transformation the lens is designed to catch: a public vocabulary about national decline enters the novel and leaves as a private vocabulary about personal irrecoverability. Millions of readers have absorbed the second without ever encountering the first, which is one more demonstration that a novel does not merely receive a discourse but reissues it in a form that can outlive the original.

Read the last column downward and a single proposition emerges, which is the article's namable claim: the novel is a node in its period's circulation of power rather than a mirror held up to it. Call it the circulation reading. A mirror reflects and adds nothing. A node receives the era's languages, redistributes their charge, and passes them on altered. Every row above describes a redistribution: a promise taken at face value, a weapon made visible, a panic given a source, a corruption relocated, a waste placed on the route. That is what the novel does to its moment, and it is a claim a student can defend in a paragraph and a scholar can cite.

## The Lens Applied: Three Passages Read Closely

A methodological description is worth less than a demonstration, so here are three passages worked at length. Each is chosen for discourse density, and each shows the lens producing something a thematic reading would not.

### The Buchanan dinner in Chapter 1

The scene is usually taught as characterization: Tom is a bully, Daisy is charming and hollow, Jordan is cool, Nick is out of his depth. Under the lens it becomes a compressed anthology of period speech. Within a few pages the reader gets the racial-science discourse in Tom's Goddard summary, the vocabulary of breeding in Nick's framing of the Buchanans' wealth, the modern-woman idiom in Daisy's performance of sophistication, and the athletic-woman figure in Jordan's posture and silence. The passage is doing what a New Historicist calls cultural work: it assembles the languages a reader would have recognized from the street and the magazine rack and stages them in a single room.

The staging has a shape. Tom speaks the discourse, Daisy mocks the speaking, Jordan declines to engage, Nick records without objecting, and the telephone interrupts. The interruption is Myrtle, which means the scene's high talk about the defense of civilization is broken off by the affair that will end in a death. New Historicism does not usually go looking for irony, but it will happily note structure, and the structure here connects the language of racial defense to the sexual conduct it coexists with. The connection is not that Tom is a hypocrite. It is that the discourse of civilizational defense and the practice of taking what one wants from a lower-class woman occupied the same social position without friction, and the novel's arrangement of the scene shows the absence of friction.

Notice too what Nick does not do. He is the novel's moral instrument, the man who reserves judgments, and he offers no objection at all. His discomfort is aesthetic; the phrasing embarrasses him more than the content. A New Historicist reads that as the period's actual condition among educated Northerners of Nick's class, for whom explicit racial theory was slightly vulgar rather than wrong. The novel's honesty about this is one of the reasons it survives as evidence.

### The Blackwell's Island crossing in Chapter 4

The passage lasts four sentences and contains an entire social logic. Nick and Gatsby cross the bridge into the city, a limousine passes them, and the seating order is inverted from the expected one. Nick's reaction is not moral outrage or approval but a sense of suspended rules, and the thought he forms is that "Anything can happen now that we've slid over this bridge," followed immediately by the conclusion that even Gatsby is possible here.

The lens asks what makes that sequence feel natural to the narrator, and the answer is that the period's discourses of racial order and social order were versions of one another. Both organized people into ranked categories, both treated the ranking as natural, and both were under visible strain in the postwar city. Nick's mind moves from one to the other without a step in between because the culture had already fused them. The novel does not comment. It records the fusion in the shape of a thought, and the recording is more valuable than a comment would be, because it captures the discourse operating below the level of stated belief.

The narration's demeaning phrasing about the passengers belongs to the evidence rather than being an embarrassment to be skipped. It shows that the vocabulary Nick finds vulgar in Tom's mouth is available in his own head. The lens is not interested in scoring Nick; it is interested in the demonstration that a discourse's reach exceeds the set of people who would defend it.

### The Plaza confrontation in Chapter 7

The confrontation is where every discourse in the novel converges and where power stops being polite. Gatsby's claim rests on self-making and on the idea that a sufficiently complete transformation is real. Tom's counterattack does three things in quick succession. He researches and exposes the source of the money, which converts Gatsby's fortune from evidence of achievement into evidence of criminality. He deploys the breeding vocabulary with "Mr. Nobody from Nowhere," which denies the transformation any standing. And he escalates to the racial discourse, tying the defense of his marriage to the defense of the color line.

The escalation is the tell. Tom reaches for racial panic at the moment his class position is most threatened, and the reach is not a change of subject but a consolidation. All three discourses defend the same thing, which is an inheritance, and the New Historicist point is that they were designed to be interchangeable in exactly this way. A social order that must justify itself will reach for whichever vocabulary is closest to hand, and a period supplies several so that no single defeat is final.

Watch what happens to Gatsby's language under the pressure. He has no counter-discourse. The vocabulary of self-making has no answer to the charge that the self was made from crime, because the vocabulary never contained a way to distinguish a fortune's sources; that was its appeal. Gatsby's defeat at the Plaza is therefore not a defeat of character. It is the failure of a period language to hold against a period language with institutions behind it. Nick's later verdict that Gatsby was worth "the whole damn bunch put together" is a moral judgment the novel earns, and it is precisely the judgment that has no purchase inside the discourse the scene is conducted in.

## What the Reading Produces

Run the lens across the whole novel and a picture assembles that differs from the standard account in one respect that matters. The standard account says the novel critiques the American Dream. The New Historicist account says the novel dramatizes a collision between two period vocabularies for social value, and that the collision is resolved by institutional power rather than by argument or merit. The second account explains something the first cannot, which is why the novel's ending feels less like a moral than like a demonstration.

It also explains the book's peculiar afterlife. A novel that argued a thesis about the American Dream would date, because theses date. A novel that captured the working grammar of a moment stays useful, because every later period can read its own arrangements against the recorded one. That is part of why the book keeps being assigned in decades whose politics have nothing in common with the 1920s: what it preserves is not a position but a system in operation.

The reading produces a third result, which is a corrective to a common student instinct. Once you can see the discourses, the temptation is to conclude that the characters are merely mouthpieces and the novel merely a specimen. New Historicism does not require that conclusion and is weaker when it draws it. The characters speak the available languages, but the novel's arrangement of who speaks what, in whose hearing, at which moment, and with what result is a construction, and the construction is where Fitzgerald's intelligence is located. The lens finds the languages. The reader still has to account for the design.

## New Historicism Versus Historical Background

The single most common failure in student work using this lens is that the essay produces background wearing a theoretical name. The two look similar on the page and are structurally different, so the distinction is worth stating with some precision.

Historical background treats history as prior and stable. It establishes what was true in the period, then shows the novel reflecting it. The logical shape is: the 1920s were like this, and here is a passage confirming it. History supplies the facts, the novel supplies the illustration, and the relationship runs one way. Background is genuinely useful, and the series' [survey of why the 1920s still explain the novel](/2027/07/12/1920s-explain-gatsby/) is built to do that job well. It is not New Historicism.

New Historicism treats history as a set of contested discourses rather than a settled condition, and it treats the literary text as one of the places where those discourses are produced rather than merely recorded. The logical shape is: here is a body of period language, here is what it was doing politically, here is the novel routing it through a character and a scene, and here is the alteration. The relationship runs both ways, because the novel's rendering of a discourse becomes part of how later readers encounter it. Millions of people have met 1920s racial pseudoscience for the first time through Tom Buchanan, which means the novel is now one of that discourse's principal transmission points.

### What is the practical difference between background and New Historicism?

Background says the period explains the novel. New Historicism says the novel and the period explain each other. The practical test is directional: if your evidence only flows from history into the text, you are writing background. If you also show the text acting on the discourse, you are working the lens.

There is a second practical difference in the kind of source each wants. Background research goes to the general historical account, the survey of the decade, the summary of the era's politics. New Historicist research goes to the specific artifact: a particular book that was actually selling, a specific piece of legislation, an advertisement, a court record, a magazine feature. Specificity is not a stylistic preference here. The whole argument depends on being able to say that a named body of language existed in a form the novel could touch, and a general statement about the mood of the decade cannot support that.

This is also why the [context and biography material on immigration and nativism](/2027/02/08/immigration-nativism-gatsby/) and on [class among the gilded elite](/2027/02/01/gilded-elite-1920s-gatsby/) is a resource for this lens rather than a substitute for it. Those articles establish what was circulating. The lens then asks what the novel did with it. The division of labor is clean, and an essay that borrows the first without performing the second has done half the work.

## The Limits of the Lens and the Strongest Objection

Every critical method has a characteristic failure, and honest use of a lens includes naming it. New Historicism has at least four, and a student who names them in an essay looks considerably more competent than one who does not.

The strongest objection is that the lens flattens literature. If a novel is a site where period discourses meet, then any sufficiently rich document from the same decade would serve equally well, and the reasons a book is worth reading twice become invisible. A sales catalogue also circulates the era's discourses of value. Why read Fitzgerald? The objection has force, and the answer has to be earned rather than asserted: the novel is worth the attention because its arrangement of the discourses is more intricate than any catalogue's, because it stages collisions rather than simply exhibiting a vocabulary, and because its formal decisions, the retrospective narration, the withheld biography, the placement of the confrontation two chapters before the end, control how the discourses meet. A New Historicist reading that ignores form has conceded the objection.

The second limit is a tendency toward totalization. If power is everywhere and every text circulates it, then nothing escapes, and criticism becomes the endless demonstration that a book is inside its culture. Critics noticed this early, and the standard response is to look for friction: the moments where a text handles a discourse awkwardly, where its own devices work against the language it is reproducing, where a minor character says something the framework cannot absorb. Jordan's "We're all white here" is such a moment. So is Nick's final verdict on Gatsby, which asserts a value the novel's social system does not recognize.

The third limit is the risk of anachronism running the other way. Because New Historicism insists that the critic's position is not neutral, it can slide into reading the past exclusively through present categories, at which point the discourses discovered in the text turn out to be the ones the critic brought. The discipline against this is documentary: name the period source, quote the period language, show that the category existed in the period's own terms.

The fourth limit is that the lens has comparatively little to say about beauty, pleasure, and the reasons readers return to particular sentences. Fitzgerald's prose is one of the reasons the book survives, and a New Historicist account of the final page can explain the era's investment in westward myth and frontier nostalgia without explaining why the sentences move people who have never heard of either. This is not a fatal weakness, but it is a real one, and it is why the lens is best used alongside others rather than as a complete method.

### Is New Historicism still used by literary critics?

Yes, though rarely under a banner. Its core assumptions, that texts participate in period discourse and that history is constructed rather than given, were absorbed into mainstream literary study during the 1990s and now underpin much cultural, postcolonial, and book-history scholarship without being named.

Worth adding for context is where the lens sits among its neighbors. Cultural materialism, the British parallel associated with Jonathan Dollimore and Alan Sinfield, shares the interest in text and power but is more explicitly political and more interested in present-day intervention. A [Marxist reading of the novel](/2027/07/26/marxist-reading-great-gatsby/) shares the attention to class but organizes everything around economic base and ideological superstructure, whereas New Historicism refuses that ranking and treats discourses as mutually constituting. The series' account of [how critics have read the novel over time](/2028/02/28/gatsby-critical-history/) places all of these in sequence, which is useful when an essay needs to situate its own method.

## How to Build an Essay Around the New Historicist Lens

The lens is unusually easy to gesture at and unusually hard to execute, so a structure helps. What follows is a working sequence rather than a formula, and it produces essays that read as arguments rather than as surveys.

Begin with a specific textual moment, not with a definition of the theory. The anecdotal opening is a genuine convention of New Historicist writing, and it is also good essay craft: a marker will forgive a great deal if the first paragraph puts them inside a scene. Open on Tom's book recommendation, on Jordan's murmur, on the seating in the limousine, on Daisy's line about her daughter. Then move outward to the discourse the moment belongs to.

Establish the discourse with a named source. Vague reference to attitudes at the time is what separates a weak essay from a strong one, and the fix is cheap: name the actual book that was selling, the actual legislation, the actual scandal. Stoddard for the racial material, the 1924 restriction act for the political consequence, the 1919 World's Series fix for the criminal money, the era's success literature for the self-making vocabulary. One specific source outperforms three paragraphs of atmosphere.

State the transformation. This is the sentence the whole essay exists to produce, and it should be visible in the thesis. The novel does something to the discourse it carries: gives it to a frightened man, tests its promise to destruction, relocates its blame, places its waste on the route between its pleasures. Name the verb.

Then close the loop by returning to form. Show how a narrative decision makes the transformation possible: the retrospective narration that lets Nick's discomfort stand in for a judgment he never makes, the withheld biography that keeps the self-making vocabulary credible for four chapters, the placement of the Plaza scene where institutional power can win with two chapters left to show what winning costs. An essay that ends on form has answered the flattening objection without having to state it.

### How do you write a thesis for a New Historicist essay?

Name a period discourse, name where the novel carries it, and name the transformation. For example: the novel routes 1920s racial pseudoscience through a man defending an inheritance, so that the discourse's claim to science is displaced by its evident function as a defense of privilege.

Two mistakes are worth pre-empting because markers see them constantly. The first is treating the lens as a license to write about history with occasional quotations, which produces an essay a history department would grade generously and a literature department would not. The remedy is the transformation sentence: if you cannot write it, you are not doing the reading. The second is using the lens to reach a verdict on Fitzgerald's personal politics. The lens does not deliver that verdict, does not need it, and is weakened by the attempt. Say what the text does, and leave the man out of it unless a biographical claim is genuinely load-bearing.

For evidence gathering, the practical step is to read the novel with the discourses in mind and mark every passage where period language surfaces, which is a different pass from reading for symbol or character. You can [read and annotate The Great Gatsby free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.org/tools/great-gatsby-annotated-text.html), where the full annotated text, the searchable quotation bank, and the theme and motif trackers make it straightforward to collect every appearance of a vocabulary across all nine chapters and keep the passages organized by discourse as your argument develops. The library keeps expanding, so the same workflow carries over to whatever you read next.

## The Verdict

The New Historicist lens is the right tool for The Great Gatsby more often than its reputation suggests, and for a reason particular to this book. Fitzgerald wrote a novel whose surface is almost entirely made of other people's language: the success story, the society page, the racial pamphlet, the advertisement, the gossip, the police report. The prose is famous for beauty, but the beauty is largely assembled from period idiom held at an angle. A lens designed to read texts as nodes in a circulating system is therefore not being imposed on this novel. It is being handed the exact material the novel is made of.

The verdict this article defends is the circulation reading. The book is neither an autonomous artifact that history happens to surround nor a mirror that reflects its decade back without alteration. It is a node: period languages of class, race, gender, waste, and illicit money enter it, get redistributed through character, scene, and narration, and leave altered, with their emotional functions exposed and their promises tested past the point the promises could bear. That is a claim about what novels do, and this one does it with unusual density.

The practical payoff is that a student who learns the lens here can carry it anywhere. The five questions, which discourses are available, where do they surface, in whose mouth, what does the text do to them, and what cannot be said, will work on any novel from any period. Gatsby is simply an unusually generous place to practice, because its moment was loud and its author was listening.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is a New Historicist reading of The Great Gatsby?

It is a reading that places the novel inside the discourses and power relations of the 1920s and treats the two as mutually constituting rather than as text and background. Instead of asking what the green light means or whether Gatsby is admirable, the approach asks which period vocabularies the novel is built from, where each one surfaces, in whose mouth, and what the book does to it. The vocabularies at issue include self-making and breeding, racial hierarchy and nativism, the modern woman and decorative feminine value, industrial waste, and illicit money. The reading's payoff is a picture of the novel as a participant in its moment's circulation of power rather than a detached observer of it, which explains why the book still reads as evidence about its decade rather than as a period piece.

### Q: How does New Historicism read the novel against 1920s discourses?

By treating each stretch of period language in the text as a live political instrument rather than as flavor. When Tom summarizes a book about the rising tide of color, the lens does not stop at noting that he is unpleasant. It identifies the actual body of writing that language came from, establishes that it carried scientific credentials and legislative consequences in the early 1920s, and then asks what changes when a novel puts it in the mouth of a man who is losing an argument about his marriage. The same procedure runs on the success vocabulary behind Gatsby's reinvention, the breeding vocabulary behind Nick's opening, and the competing languages of feminine value that Daisy and Jordan and Myrtle occupy. Each discourse is traced to its non-literary circulation and then followed into the text.

### Q: How does the lens treat text and history as mutually constituting?

By denying that either one is prior. History does not sit behind the novel as a stable set of facts the book illustrates, and the novel does not sit outside history as an autonomous design. Both are made of discourse, and discourse moves between them. Period racial pseudoscience enters the novel through Tom; the novel then becomes, for millions of later readers, the primary place they encounter that pseudoscience, which means the book is now part of how the discourse survives and is understood. The traffic runs in both directions and does not stop at publication. Practically, this means an essay using the lens must show the text acting on the period language, not only the period language explaining the text, and the second half is where most student work stops.

### Q: How does the novel circulate the era's anxieties?

By giving each anxiety a speaker, a scene, and a consequence rather than a statement. The fear that inherited social position was becoming purchasable circulates through Gatsby's arrival and Tom's counterattack. The fear of racial displacement circulates through the Chapter 1 dinner and returns, tellingly, at the moment Tom's private authority collapses. The fear that women's new freedoms would dissolve the family circulates through Jordan's independence, Daisy's claimed sophistication, and Myrtle's fatal mobility. The fear that new money was inseparable from crime circulates through Wolfshiem, the drugstores, and the free liquor. In each case the novel does not argue about the anxiety. It stages the conditions under which people reach for it, which is a more durable kind of evidence than an opinion would have been.

### Q: How does New Historicism differ from historical background?

Background treats the period as prior and settled and uses the novel to illustrate it, so the evidence flows one way. New Historicism treats the period as a field of contested discourses and treats the novel as one place those discourses are produced, so the evidence flows both ways. There is also a difference in sources. Background wants the general survey of the decade. The lens wants the specific artifact: a named book that was actually selling, a named piece of legislation, a named scandal. The directional test is the quickest diagnostic available. If every claim in your essay runs from history into the text, you have written background with theoretical vocabulary attached. If you can also name what the text does to the discourse, you are working the lens.

### Q: Is the text autonomous, a mirror, or a node in power relations?

A node, and the distinction is the article's central claim. An autonomous text would be a self-contained design that history merely surrounds, which is the Formalist position and which cannot account for how much of Fitzgerald's surface is assembled from period idiom. A mirror would reflect the decade back unaltered, which cannot account for the fact that the novel's version of 1920s racial theory is now more widely known than its actual sources. A node receives the era's languages, redistributes their charge through character, scene, and narration, and passes them on changed. That is what the discourse map in this article documents row by row, and it is the reading a New Historicist essay should be defending.

### Q: Who founded New Historicism and what are its main principles?

The critic most associated with naming it is Stephen Greenblatt, who used the phrase in a 1982 introduction to a special issue of the journal Genre devoted to Renaissance writing, and the approach grew primarily out of Renaissance studies in American universities during that decade. Its principles are compact. Literary and non-literary texts are read together as expressions of one cultural system rather than as art and context. Power is understood after Michel Foucault as distributed through discourse rather than held by rulers. Small cultural details are read at length for the whole system they assume, a practice borrowed from the anthropologist Clifford Geertz and often called thick description. And the critic's own historical position is acknowledged as shaping the account, which is why the essays so often open with an anecdote rather than a survey.

### Q: What questions does a New Historicist ask when approaching a literary work?

Five, and they are portable enough to organize an essay on any novel. Which discourses were available in this moment for speaking about the subjects the work raises? Where in the text do they surface, in whose mouth, and with what framing from the narration? What does the work do to a discourse when it renders it, meaning does it amplify, expose, hollow out, or leave standing? Whose interests does each discourse serve, and where does the text position its reader in relation to those interests? And what does the text make unsayable, since a consistent silence about a live period topic is evidence about the limits of permitted speech. The fifth question is the one most often skipped and frequently the most productive.

### Q: How does a New Historicist reading handle Tom Buchanan's Nordicism speech?

Not as personal eccentricity, which is how a character-based reading files it, but as mainstream period discourse in circulation. The book Tom recommends stands in for a real publication that sold heavily and was reviewed as serious argument, its claim to scientific proof was the mechanism that converted political preference into fact, and the same vocabulary was actively shaping immigration policy in the years around the novel's composition. Having established that, the lens asks what the novel does with the material. It gives the language to the least intelligent adult present, lets his wife mock the performance mid-sentence, supplies a visible source for his certainty in his own decline, and then brings the vocabulary back in Chapter 7 at the precise moment his authority collapses. The discourse is not refuted. Its function is exposed.

### Q: What does the Blackwell's Island crossing look like under a New Historicist lens?

It looks like four sentences containing an entire social logic. A limousine passes with a white chauffeur driving Black passengers, the expected order is inverted, and Nick's response is not judgment but a sense that ordinary rules have been suspended, followed immediately by the thought that Gatsby becomes possible in such a place. The lens asks why that sequence feels natural to the narrator, and the answer is that the period's discourses of racial order and social order were built on the same logic of natural ranking and were under the same postwar strain. Nick's mind moves between them without a step because the culture had fused them. The narration's demeaning phrasing about the passengers is part of the evidence, since it shows the vocabulary Nick finds vulgar in Tom's mouth operating inside his own head.

### Q: How does New Historicism treat the valley of ashes differently from a symbol reading?

A symbol reading asks what the ashes mean and produces answers about moral waste, spiritual desolation, and the cost of the boom. The lens instead asks where that language came from and what it was doing in 1925. The 1920s had a developed public vocabulary for industrial waste and for the populations living beside it, drawn from sanitary reform, early urban planning, and a nativist habit of describing immigrant labor in terms of dirt and contagion. Fitzgerald's ash-grey men sit inside that vocabulary rather than inventing it. Where the novel acts on the discourse is geographical: the ash heaps are placed on the road between the eggs and the city, so every journey between wealth and pleasure crosses the waste that produced both. The placement is the argument.

### Q: What is the difference between New Historicism and cultural materialism?

They are close relatives with different accents and different national origins. New Historicism developed in American universities, is associated above all with Stephen Greenblatt, and tends to present itself as descriptive, tracing how cultural energy circulates without pressing a political program. Cultural materialism developed in Britain, is associated with Jonathan Dollimore and Alan Sinfield, and is explicitly political, more interested in dissidence and in what the past can be made to do in present arguments. Both reject the older separation of text and context, both read literary and non-literary material together, and both owe debts to Foucault. In practice the American version asks how power circulates and the British version asks where it can be resisted, and an essay is stronger for knowing which of those two questions it is actually asking.

### Q: Can a New Historicist reading say anything about gender in the novel?

It can say a great deal, and the material is unusually clean because the 1920s discourse about women was visibly in flux. The vote had arrived in 1920, the modern woman was a constant media subject, and an older vocabulary of decorative feminine value ran underneath both. The novel places its three principal women at three positions in that fault line: Daisy fluent in the new language and governed by the old one, Jordan independent and dishonest in a way that reproduces the era's suspicion of the athletic woman, Myrtle attempting the modern-woman vocabulary through purchase and destroyed for it. Read together they form one system with three outcomes, and the system's silence about suffrage, never mentioned despite obvious occasions, is part of the evidence.

### Q: What are the main weaknesses of a New Historicist approach?

Four are worth naming in an essay. It can flatten literature, since if every text circulates period discourse then a sales catalogue would serve as well, and the only answer is to show that the novel's arrangement of the discourses is more intricate and more deliberate than a catalogue's. It can totalize, making every text merely another proof that nothing escapes its culture, which is why looking for friction and awkwardness matters. It can smuggle in present categories under historical language, which is why naming period sources in period terms is a discipline rather than a formality. And it has comparatively little to say about beauty and pleasure, which is a real gap when the novel in question is admired chiefly for its sentences. Naming the limits strengthens an essay considerably.

### Q: How do you structure an essay using a New Historicist approach?

Open on a specific textual moment rather than a definition of the theory, since the anecdotal opening is both a genuine convention of the school and good essay craft. Move outward to the discourse that moment belongs to, and establish it with a named source: an actual book that was selling, an actual law, an actual scandal, never a vague reference to attitudes of the era. State the transformation, meaning what the novel does to the discourse it carries, and make that the thesis rather than a late observation. Then close by returning to a formal decision that makes the transformation possible, such as the retrospective narration or the placement of the confrontation two chapters before the end. Ending on form answers the flattening objection without having to raise it.

### Q: How does a New Historicist reading differ from a Marxist one?

Both attend to class and both refuse to treat literature as sealed off from material conditions, so they overlap heavily on this novel. The difference is architectural. A Marxist reading organizes the analysis around economic relations as the determining level, with ideology and culture understood as arising from them, and it moves toward a verdict about the system the novel exposes. New Historicism refuses that ranking and treats economic, racial, sexual, and religious discourses as mutually constituting, with none of them foundational. On the Plaza scene, a Marxist reading finds class power asserting itself through ideology; the New Historicist reading finds three interchangeable vocabularies defending one inheritance and notes that the interchangeability is itself the period's design. The second is less conclusive and more precise about mechanism.

### Q: How does a New Historicist read Gatsby's self-invention?

As the period's success vocabulary taken at face value and thereby exposed. The 1920s renewed the language of self-making through advertising, an enormous market in success literature, the celebration of the self-taught executive, and a stock market that made ordinary earners feel like proprietors. That language promised that a man could become anyone. Gatsby does exactly what it says: he changes the name, assembles the biography from prestige components, imports the architecture, and funds all of it illegally, because the vocabulary never contained a means of distinguishing between sources of money. The plot then demonstrates the limit the promise had left unstated, which is that the receiving class reserves the right to refuse the arrival. His defeat is not a failure of character but the failure of one period language against another with institutions behind it.

### Q: How does New Historicism handle an author's intentions?

It sets them aside, and this is a relief rather than an evasion. The unit of analysis is the text as a site where period languages meet, not the mind that produced it, and a novelist's ear can reproduce a discourse with great precision without the novelist holding a settled position on it. This means an essay never has to prove what Fitzgerald believed about race, class, or women in order to say something exact about what the novel does with the era's language on all three. It also imposes a discipline, since the easy move of resolving a difficulty by appealing to the author's supposed opinion is no longer available. Say what the text does, and leave the biography out unless a specific biographical claim is genuinely load-bearing.
