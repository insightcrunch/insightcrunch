The debt ceiling decides whether the United States pays obligations that Congress has already enacted into law. The mechanism is a statutory limit at 31 U.S.C. 3101 on the face amount of obligations issued by the Treasury, and the outcome governed by that mechanism is payment itself. When enacted spending exceeds enacted receipts, borrowing fills the gap between the two totals. A ceiling that blocks borrowing therefore blocks the means of payment without removing a single obligation. The ceiling is best understood as a permission slip for bills already run up, since it controls whether the government may pay for spending Congress has already approved rather than controlling the spending itself.

![The United States Capitol and the statutory debt ceiling that governs federal borrowing to pay enacted obligations - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-45.webp)

That separation organizes every episode in the record. Appropriations acts supply authority for agencies to incur obligations for stated purposes. Mandatory spending laws create obligations by formula and by eligibility rule. Tax laws determine the receipts that reach the Treasury through rates, bases, deductions, and credits. None of those decisions originates in section 3101. Section 3101 enters after the other decisions have combined to produce a financing need, and section 3101 answers a narrower question, whether the Treasury may issue obligations in an amount sufficient to meet the need. Confusion about the instrument almost always begins with a collapse of that sequence into a single decision.

History reinforces the same reading. Before 1917, Congress authorized specific loans and instruments individually, specifying amount, maturity, and purpose for each issue. The Second Liberty Bond Act of 1917 replaced that transaction by transaction method with aggregate constraints on categories of securities and gave the Treasury flexibility to choose instruments and respond to conditions. In 1939, Congress eliminated the separate category amounts and created the first aggregate cap covering nearly all public borrowing, set at 45 billion dollars. A device created to make borrowing workable inside one legislated total became, over later decades, a recurring point of negotiation, delay, and procedural invention.

The breadth of the cap gives the sequence its force. Section 3101 reaches over 99 percent of federal obligations of the relevant kind, including obligations held by the public and securities held by federal trust funds and other intragovernmental accounts. Almost no corner of federal borrowing sits outside the boundary. Payment for benefits, salaries, contracts, and interest therefore depends on the same financing capacity, and a Treasury at the boundary cannot isolate a single program from the constraint. The breadth also explains why internal measures focus on fund holdings. Redeeming or pausing reinvestment in large accounts creates the only substantial headroom available without new issuance to outside investors. That structure turns an abstract figure into an operating deadline for the whole government.

Practice after the cap binds follows a recognizable sequence. Cash on hand and auction timing provide a brief transitional stage. The Secretary then declares that the cap has been reached, and a defined set of extraordinary measures creates headroom measured in weeks to months. Forecasts converge on an exhaustion date, called the X date, and Congress acts through an increase, a suspension, or a deeming device, or a debate turns to untested theories for acting without legislation. Each stage, each device, and each theory tests the same proposition in a different form, that enacted obligations remain payable obligations even when financing authority has run out.

## Where the Debt Ceiling Came From

The statutory ceiling has a precise legal identity, and that identity matters for every later argument about what an increase does or fails to do. The Congressional Research Service describes the arrangement as a limitation on the face amount of obligations issued by the Treasury, codified at 31 U.S.C. 3101, with the dollar limitation itself placed at subsection (b). The measure is therefore not a general instruction to spend less, a target for a future budget, or a statement of fiscal aspiration. The measure is a cap on the face amount of obligations that the Treasury may have outstanding under the authority that Congress granted. According to the Congressional Research Service, the cap reaches over 99 percent of federal obligations of the relevant kind, which means that nearly the whole stock of borrowing carried on the books of the United States sits beneath a single statutory number. A change to that number changes the room available for issuance. A change to that number does not, by itself, rewrite any program, repeal any benefit formula, alter any tax schedule, or direct any agency to undertake a new activity.

The roots of the arrangement reach back to the Borrowing Clause of Article I of the Constitution. That clause assigns the power to borrow money on the credit of the United States to Congress. The power belongs to the legislature, and the Treasury possesses borrowing authority only because Congress has conferred that authority by statute. Section 3101 represents one form of that conferral, framed as a maximum face amount rather than as permission for a single named loan. The distinction between a legislative power and an executive administration of that power runs through the entire history. Congress decides how much authority exists. The Treasury, acting within authority that Congress supplied, decides how to issue securities, when to bring an offering to market, and how to manage maturities and cash balances from day to day. Confusion enters public debate when administrative discretion is mistaken for independent authority, or when a statutory maximum is mistaken for a decision about the spending that made borrowing necessary in the first place.

Before 1917, Congress exercised the borrowing power in a manner far closer to the individual transaction. A borrowing proposal came to Congress as a specific loan or a specific instrument, and Congress authorized that loan or instrument on stated terms. The authorization specified the amount to be raised, the maturity or term of the obligation, and the purpose that the proceeds were to serve. Each of those elements formed part of the legislative decision. Congress did not hand the Treasury a broad pool of authority and leave the composition of borrowing to administrative judgment. Congress instead approved borrowing piece by piece, with the size, duration, and object of a given issue fixed in advance by law. Such a system gave the legislature close control over each act of borrowing, at the cost of repeated legislative action whenever financing needs changed in scale, in timing, or in the form of security that investors were prepared to buy on favorable terms.

The Panama Canal loan offers the example that the Congressional Research Service uses to illustrate the older practice. Under that practice, financing for a great public work did not rest on a general authority that the Treasury could draw upon for any lawful purpose. Financing rested on an authorization tied to the undertaking itself. Congress identified the project, fixed the borrowing connected with the project, and set the terms on which obligations could be issued for that object. A reader accustomed to a single aggregate cap may find that arrangement natural, because a canal is a discrete undertaking with an identifiable cost. The older system, however, applied the same transaction by transaction logic as a general method of federal finance. Each new need required a return to Congress. Each new instrument required legislative specification. The method suited a federal establishment with narrower and more episodic borrowing demands, and the method broke down when war finance demanded borrowing on a scale and at a speed that piece by piece authorization could not comfortably sustain.

The break came with the First World War. War financing created an emergency of scale, because the federal government had to raise very large sums, raise those sums repeatedly, and adjust offerings as conditions in financial markets and demands on the Treasury changed. The First Liberty Loan Act of 1917 and the Second Liberty Bond Act of 1917 belonged to that emergency response. The significance of the Liberty Bond legislation lies less in any single offering than in the structural change that the legislation introduced. Rather than returning to Congress for authority over each separate loan on fully specified terms, Congress began to legislate in terms of aggregate amounts attached to categories of securities. The Treasury received room to act within legislated aggregates. That room represented a deliberate transfer of practical discretion over the composition and timing of issuance, while Congress retained control over the outer amounts. War supplied the occasion, but the device that emerged from the occasion outlasted the emergency and reshaped federal borrowing for the following century.

The Second Liberty Bond Act, cited as P.L. 65-43 and enacted on September 24, 1917, carried the new method into law in the form that later development built upon. According to the Congressional Research Service, the act imposed aggregate constraints on categories of instruments, including bonds and certificates of indebtedness, while separate limitations for distinct categories remained in place. The Treasury gained flexibility to choose among instruments and to respond to conditions rather than to execute a single loan exactly as Congress had drawn the loan in advance. Flexibility was the point of the design. A Treasury able to select the mix of securities could match an offering to investor demand, spread maturities with greater care, and avoid forcing the whole of a financing requirement through one instrument at one moment. Congress, for its part, preserved a legislative check through the category aggregates. The act therefore balanced two objectives that the pre-1917 system had kept in a different relation: legislative control over the total scale of borrowing within each class, and executive discretion over the practical construction of the borrowing program.

For roughly two decades after 1917, that categorical structure persisted. Separate limitations governed distinct forms of federal obligations, with bills, certificates, and bonds treated under distinct ceilings rather than under one consolidated figure. Administration of federal borrowing under such a structure required attention not only to the total amount outstanding but also to the amount outstanding within each category. A category could approach a category maximum while room remained elsewhere, and the composition of new issuance had to respect the distribution of authority across categories. Such a structure preserved a finer degree of legislative specification than a single aggregate figure permits. Congress could influence the shape of federal borrowing by adjusting one category without adjusting another. The same structure, however, limited the very discretion that the Liberty Bond legislation had introduced, because the Treasury had to fit financing choices inside several compartments rather than inside one broad boundary drawn around public borrowing as a whole.

Consolidation arrived in 1939. In July of that year, Congress eliminated the separate limitations on bonds and other forms of federal borrowing and created the first single aggregate limitation covering nearly all public obligations of the United States, through P.L. 76-201. The new aggregate figure stood at 45 billion dollars. The Congressional Research Service treats the modern form of the ceiling as dating from that 1939 consolidation, and the reasoning behind that treatment is structural rather than merely chronological. After 1939, the governing question for the Treasury became whether total outstanding obligations remained within one overall statutory figure, rather than whether each class of security remained within a class figure of its own. One number replaced several numbers. One boundary replaced several boundaries. The change simplified the legal test that issuance had to satisfy and widened the discretion that the Treasury could exercise within the boundary, because authority unused in one category no longer sat locked inside that category while another category pressed against a separate maximum.

The analytical consequence of that history deserves emphasis, because later rhetoric often reverses the direction of the original reform. The aggregate ceiling was created to give the Treasury more discretion, not less. The movement from 1917 to 1939 ran away from loan by loan specification and toward broader administrative freedom inside a legislated total. The movement replaced repeated congressional design of individual borrowings with a framework in which Congress set an outer amount and the Treasury managed the borrowing program beneath that amount. To read the ceiling as an instrument originally devised to restrain the Treasury, in the sense of forcing the executive back toward transaction by transaction legislative control, inverts the purpose that produced the instrument. Restraint of a kind exists in any maximum, since a maximum marks a point beyond which issuance may not proceed without further legislation. The historical function, however, was enabling. The ceiling consolidated authority, made authority usable across categories, and allowed financing to proceed without a fresh statute for each security or each loan.

## The Limit as a Permission to Pay

The scope of the statutory cap explains why a binding cap reaches deeply into the operations of the federal government. The ceiling covers obligations held by the public and obligations held within the government itself, including securities held by federal trust funds and similar intragovernmental accounts. Debt held by the public arises when the Treasury sells securities to investors outside the federal government, a class that spans individuals, financial institutions, and other purchasers of Treasury securities. Intragovernmental holdings arise when a federal account with receipts dedicated to a program invests balances in Treasury securities under law, so that one part of the government holds a claim against another part. Both forms count within the same overall framework described by the Congressional Research Service, under which the limitation at 31 U.S.C. 3101 reaches over 99 percent of the relevant total. A framework of that breadth leaves almost no corner of federal borrowing outside the boundary, and a Treasury operating at the boundary therefore confronts a constraint on the central financing mechanism of the government rather than on a marginal or specialized program.

Trust fund securities illustrate why intragovernmental holdings belong in any serious account of scope. A trust fund balance invested in Treasury securities represents money that a program collected, or that law credited to the program, and that the program did not spend at once. The investment converts a balance into an interest bearing claim on the Treasury, redeemable when the program requires cash to pay benefits or meet other lawful outlays. Redemption of such a security reduces intragovernmental holdings, while new issuance to a purchasing investor increases holdings by the public, and both movements pass through the same aggregate accounting that the ceiling governs. The arrangement can appear circular to a casual observer, because the government appears on both sides of the transaction. Appearance, however, does not remove the legal reality. The security is an obligation issued under authority that Congress granted, the holding account possesses a lawful claim, and payment when the claim falls due depends on the same Treasury cash and borrowing capacity that support every other federal payment.

Obligations that require payment do not originate in section 3101. Obligations originate in the laws that direct federal spending and in the commitments that those laws create. Appropriations supply authority for agencies to incur obligations for the purposes and in the amounts that appropriations acts specify, while mandatory spending laws create obligations by formula, by eligibility rule, or by other standing legal direction without a fresh appropriations decision for each payment. The budget process that frames those decisions received a modern statutory structure through the Congressional Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974, examined in [a full guide to the Congressional Budget Act of 1974](/2020/01/01/congressional-budget-act-1974-guide/), and the obligations that emerge from that process rest on enacted law rather than on any later adjustment of borrowing authority. Tax law occupies the other side of the ledger. Statutes governing taxation determine the receipts that flow to the Treasury, through rates, bases, deductions, credits, and the other elements that tax legislation establishes. Borrowing finances the gap that remains when enacted outlays exceed enacted receipts in a given period. The gap is an arithmetic consequence of decisions taken in spending law and tax law. Borrowing does not create the gap, and a refusal to permit borrowing does not remove the gap from the accounts.

### Does raising the debt ceiling authorize new spending?

No. An increase in borrowing authority permits the Treasury to issue obligations and use the proceeds to pay commitments that enacted law has already created. The increase creates no appropriation, establishes no program, expands no eligibility rule, and directs no agency to spend. Spending authority comes from separate statutes that Congress enacted through appropriations acts and mandatory spending laws.

The precision of that answer matters, because loose language about the ceiling converts a financing act into a spending act and thereby misdescribes both. Raising the statutory cap authorizes borrowing to pay obligations already incurred. Raising the statutory cap authorizes no new spending. Refusing to raise the statutory cap cancels no spending. Each clause states a distinct proposition, and each proposition follows from the separation between the laws that create obligations and the statute that permits the Treasury to finance payment of obligations. An appropriations act that funds an agency program remains in force whether or not borrowing authority expands. A mandatory spending statute that promises benefits to persons who satisfy eligibility conditions remains in force whether or not borrowing authority expands. A tax statute that yields receipts below enacted outlays remains in force whether or not borrowing authority expands. The Treasury confronts the combined result of those statutes. Adjustment of the ceiling changes only whether the Treasury may borrow to meet the result, not the result itself.

The mechanical consequence of a binding cap follows directly from the statutory language, and the Treasury has described operations at the boundary in those mechanical terms. When the ceiling binds, the Treasury cannot issue net new obligations above the face amount that the statute permits. Issuance that merely replaces maturing securities may proceed within the boundary, because replacement does not increase the total face amount outstanding, but issuance that would push the total beyond the permitted figure may not proceed. The Treasury must then live on cash on hand and on receipts as receipts arrive. Cash on hand fluctuates with the rhythm of tax collections, benefit payments, agency outlays, and maturing securities, so a government confined to cash and receipts operates with a margin that changes from day to day and that no amount of careful cash management can convert into new borrowing authority. Every payment competes, in practical terms, against every other payment for a finite pool of available cash, even though the legal validity of the underlying obligations remains intact.

A binding ceiling must also be distinguished from a government shutdown, because conflation of the two conditions ranks among the most common errors in public discussion of federal finance. A shutdown arises when appropriations lapse. When appropriations for an agency or a set of activities expire without a successor appropriations act or a continuing resolution, authority to incur new obligations for those activities generally ceases, and the affected operations curtail activity for want of spending authority. A binding ceiling presents the opposite configuration. In a ceiling impasse, money has been appropriated and obligations have been incurred under valid law, but the Treasury lacks authority to borrow the cash required to pay those obligations in full and on time. Spending authority exists, while financing authority falls short. In a shutdown, financing arrangements are not the central defect, while spending authority has lapsed. The two conditions can produce superficially similar images of a government unable to function, but the legal causes differ, the statutes involved differ, and the remedies differ. Restoration of appropriations cures a shutdown. Enactment of appropriations alone does not cure a binding ceiling, because appropriated money that cannot be borrowed in a period when receipts fall short of outlays remains beyond the reach of the paying officer.

That separation, understood clearly, returns discussion to the origin with which the history began. Congress designed the aggregate ceiling as a workable grant of borrowing authority for a government whose financing needs had outgrown loan by loan legislation. Congress preserved the power to set and to revise the maximum, and every revision poses a genuine legislative decision about whether the Treasury may continue to finance obligations that other laws created. The decision is genuine, and the consequences of refusal are genuine, but the object of the decision remains financing rather than spending. The Congressional Research Service, in describing the ceiling as a limitation on the face amount of obligations issued under 31 U.S.C. 3101, captures the object exactly. A permission to pay is not a trivial permission. Payment sustains the credit of the United States, honors claims that law created, and allows appropriated programs to function as Congress directed. Denial of that permission leaves the claims standing and removes the means of satisfying the claims through borrowing. No clearer statement of the instrument exists, and no account of later controversies over the ceiling can proceed soundly without that statement fixed firmly in place.

## What Happens When the Limit Binds

The sequence begins well before any payment is missed, and the order of the stages matters because each stage buys time without changing the underlying obligations. Appropriations and mandatory spending laws create the obligations that the federal government must meet. The statutory ceiling codified at 31 U.S.C. 3101 governs a different question. It governs whether the Treasury may borrow to pay obligations that Congress has already enacted. That separation, drawn in Congressional Research Service analysis of the ceiling, explains why a refusal to raise the borrowing maximum does not cancel enacted spending. The spending remains law. The borrowing authority to finance it is the element that runs out, and the threat that follows is a threat to payment of obligations already incurred rather than a repeal of the programs, benefits, contracts, or interest commitments that produced those obligations in the first place.

In the first stage, the Treasury manages what it holds and what it schedules. Cash on hand becomes the immediate resource, and the timing of auctions becomes a tool for staying beneath the maximum without issuing net new borrowing beyond it. When the outstanding amount approaches the ceiling, ordinary issuance cannot proceed in the ordinary way, because each new obligation issued by the Treasury counts against the face amount covered by section 3101, which the Congressional Research Service described as covering over 99 percent of federal obligations of the relevant kind. Auction timing can be adjusted, and the cash balance can be drawn upon to meet outlays that fall due while receipts arrive on their own schedule. None of those steps creates new authority. Cash management stretches an existing balance. Auction management controls the pace at which new borrowing would otherwise press against the cap. Together they form a brief transitional stage, useful for smoothing uneven flows, but incapable of sustaining a government whose outlays exceed its receipts over any extended period without borrowing.

The second stage is formal notice. The Secretary of the Treasury declares that the ceiling has been reached and notifies Congress by letter. Treasury letters of that kind, cited by the Congressional Research Service in its accounts of past episodes, serve several functions at once. A letter fixes a date and signals that the transitional stage of cash and auction management has ended and that the next stage, the use of extraordinary measures, has begun or is about to begin. The declaration does not raise the maximum, does not appropriate money, and does not authorize any payment that Congress has not already authorized through law. It announces a condition created by the interaction of two bodies of law that Congress controls: the laws that created obligations to pay, and the law that restricts borrowing to finance those payments.

Default is not automatic on the day the ceiling is reached. That point corrects a common error, and the structure of the sequence shows why the error is wrong. Between the date of reaching the maximum and any failure to pay in full and on time, an interval intervenes in which cash remains available and extraordinary measures create additional headroom. Payments continue during that interval because the Treasury continues to receive revenue and continues to hold balances and freed capacity that can be applied to obligations as they fall due. The danger builds inside the interval rather than arriving at its opening. Each day consumes part of a finite stock of resources, and the end of the interval depends on flows that vary, which is why attention shifts quickly from the date the ceiling was reached to a different and later date, the projected date of exhaustion.

Extraordinary measures form the third stage, and they are described in detail in the section that follows. In sequence terms, their role is simple. Each measure either prevents an addition to the amount counted against the ceiling or redeems or suspends reinvestment of an existing holding in a way that creates room beneath the maximum, and that room can then be used for borrowing needed to meet other obligations. The measures are extraordinary in the sense that they depart from normal investment and issuance practice for designated federal funds and securities programs. They are not extraordinary in the sense of being invented in the moment. Treasury letters and Congressional Research Service accounts describe a recognized set of steps, taken in past episodes in a recognizable order, under authorities that exist in law before any particular confrontation begins. Their availability explains the interval between reaching the cap and facing exhaustion, and their finite size explains why that interval ends.

Once the measures are in place, the fourth stage is a joint rundown. Measures and cash are consumed together against the daily pattern of federal receipts and outlays. Receipts do not arrive evenly. Tax payments cluster on particular dates, other collections arrive in smaller streams, and outlays also cluster, with benefit payments, salaries, vendor payments, and interest falling due on schedules set by separate laws and agreements. On a day of heavy receipts, the available balance can rise even inside the rundown. On a day of heavy outlays, the balance can fall sharply. The overall direction, when borrowing is constrained and enacted outlays exceed receipts, is downward, but the path is jagged rather than smooth. That jagged path matters for forecasting. A projection made during a strong receipts period can look more comfortable than the position a few weeks later, after a large benefit cycle or interest payment has passed through the accounts.

The fifth stage is the approach of the projected exhaustion date, known as the X date, a term used by the Bipartisan Policy Center and adopted in press coverage. As the rundown proceeds, the Treasury, the Congressional Budget Office, and the Bipartisan Policy Center publish estimates of when measures and cash will be exhausted. The estimates can move. Stronger tax receipts can push the projected date later. Weaker receipts, or heavier outlays than anticipated, can pull the projected date earlier. The movement reflects new information about flows that no forecaster controls and that arrive day by day. For Congress, the movement creates a practical problem of timing, because legislation to raise or suspend the ceiling must be enacted before exhaustion if the payment threat is to be removed entirely, yet the forecasts that frame the deadline carry ranges rather than a single fixed day.

At exhaustion, the sixth stage begins, and the choices narrow sharply. With measures consumed and cash exhausted, the Treasury can pay only from incoming receipts. Money that arrives on a given day can be applied to obligations, but obligations falling due on that day can exceed the amount that has arrived, and no borrowing above the ceiling is available to close the gap. In that condition, obligations are paid late or in part, not because the underlying laws creating them have been repealed, and not because the amounts owed have been recalculated, but because the cash available at the moment of payment is insufficient and the legal authority to borrow the difference is absent. A late payment and a partial payment are different experiences for a recipient, but in sequence terms they arise from the same cause. The payment system has become a receipts system, forced to match a schedule of obligations set by enacted law to a stream of revenue that was never designed to match that schedule day by day.

The boundaries on Treasury action run through every stage and become decisive at exhaustion. At each stage the Treasury may manage its cash balance, may adjust the timing of auctions, may declare that the ceiling has been reached and notify Congress, and may use the extraordinary measures that law makes available. At no stage may the Treasury borrow above the statutory maximum, expressed at section 3101 as a limitation on the face amount of obligations that may be outstanding. At no stage may the Treasury spend money that Congress has not appropriated. The ceiling restricts borrowing. It does not create spending authority, and a confrontation over borrowing does not transfer the appropriations power to the Secretary. At exhaustion, a further boundary appears. The Treasury may not select which enacted obligations the law itself ranks, because no statute gives the Treasury a priority order among those obligations. Without such a ranking, delay and partial payment follow from cash insufficiency rather than from an authorized scheme of preference.

## Extraordinary Measures and How Long They Last

Extraordinary measures are the set of steps the Treasury takes, after the ceiling has been reached, to create headroom beneath the statutory maximum and to keep meeting obligations from cash and freed capacity for a period of weeks to months. Congressional Research Service accounts and Treasury letters describe four canonical measures, and the order in which they are presented in those accounts reflects the order in which they are ordinarily deployed. Each measure affects a different program or fund. Each operates by preventing an increase in the amount counted against the ceiling or by reducing an existing counted holding so that room becomes available for other borrowing. None of the measures appropriates money, cancels an obligation, or raises revenue. Their combined effect is to substitute internal adjustments, made inside accounts that the Treasury administers or issues, for the external borrowing that the ceiling forbids, and that substitution can work only as long as the accounts involved hold balances large enough to matter.

The first measure is the suspension of sales of State and Local Government Series Treasury securities. Suspending sales is the simplest step in the set because it requires no redemption and no change to an existing holding. It works by stopping an addition. Each sale that does not occur is borrowing that does not press against the maximum, and the capacity that would have been absorbed by new sales remains available for the borrowing the Treasury needs to finance obligations falling due across the government. The step also illustrates a wider feature of the measures as a group. Their value is measured in headroom rather than in cash received. A suspended sale produces no payment to a beneficiary and funds no program. It preserves room, and room, once the ordinary borrowing channel is closed, becomes the scarce resource that the remaining measures are designed to enlarge.

The second measure operates through the Civil Service Retirement and Disability Fund under a formal legal declaration. The Secretary declares a debt issuance suspension period under 5 U.S.C. 8348, and that declaration permits two linked actions affecting the fund. Existing investments held by the fund may be redeemed, and new investment of the fund may be suspended. Redemption of an existing holding reduces the amount counted against the ceiling and thereby creates room for other borrowing. Suspension of new investment prevents the fund from adding holdings that would consume room again. A related authority handles the Postal Service retiree health benefits fund, but the central retirement measure in the canonical set is the action taken for the Civil Service Retirement and Disability Fund under section 8348. The formality of the declaration matters. The suspension period is a defined legal condition, invoked by the Secretary under a cited section of Title 5, rather than an informal decision to delay investment, and the defined condition is what supports both the redemption and the suspension that follow from it.

The third measure concerns the Government Securities Investment Fund, known as the G Fund, of the Thrift Savings Plan. Under the authority at 5 U.S.C. 8438, reinvestment of the G Fund is suspended. In normal operation, maturing holdings are reinvested, and reinvestment maintains the counted holdings at their established level. Suspension interrupts that cycle. As holdings mature without replacement, room beneath the ceiling becomes available, and that room can support borrowing required for payments elsewhere in the government. The mechanics differ from the retirement fund measure in an instructive way. The retirement fund measure combines redemption of existing investments with suspension of new investment after a declared suspension period. The G Fund measure centers on reinvestment, on the decision not to roll maturing amounts into new holdings while the ceiling binds. Both measures convert balances held inside a federal savings or retirement structure into temporary borrowing capacity for the government as a whole, without changing the amounts that participants and beneficiaries are owed under the programs themselves.

The fourth measure is the suspension of reinvestment of the Exchange Stabilization Fund. The pattern matches the G Fund step in its basic form. Reinvestment that would ordinarily maintain the fund holdings is suspended, maturing amounts are not replaced with new holdings counted against the maximum, and capacity is freed for other purposes while the constraint lasts. With that step, the canonical set described in Treasury letters and Congressional Research Service accounts is complete. A securities sales program has been paused, a large retirement fund has been placed under a declared suspension period with redemption and suspended investment, and reinvestment has been suspended in two further funds. The set draws headroom from issuance, from redemption, and from interrupted reinvestment, because no single account is large enough, on its own, to sustain federal payments for long once ordinary borrowing has stopped.

A statutory make whole rule governs the retirement funds at the center of the set. Federal law requires both the Civil Service Retirement and Disability Fund and the G Fund to be made whole, including lost interest, once an increase in the ceiling is enacted. The requirement is written into law rather than left to administrative discretion, and its effect is to hold federal employees and retirees harmless from the use of the measures. Balances that were redeemed are restored, investment that was suspended is corrected for in the restoration, and interest that would have been earned during the suspension is included, so the funds are returned to the position they would have occupied if the measures had not been taken. The measures borrow time from internal accounts, but by statute they do not permanently transfer losses to the employees and retirees whose savings and benefits those accounts support.

### How long can extraordinary measures last?

Extraordinary measures can last for weeks to months rather than for days or for years. Episodes in 2011, 2013, and 2015 each produced an interval of roughly two to more than seven months between the date the ceiling was reached or a suspension expired and the date legislation resolved the confrontation.

The evidence for that range comes from dated episodes that the Congressional Research Service recorded and that Treasury letters documented at the time. In 2011, the ceiling was reached on May 16, and extraordinary measures held to August 2, when legislation raising the maximum was signed, an interval of close to three months. In 2013, a suspension expired in May, measures ran from May to October 17, and legislation enacted on October 17 suspended the ceiling again. That interval was longer, and it ended on the date the new suspension took effect rather than on a date set by the measures themselves. In 2015, the ceiling was reached on March 16, and measures lasted to November 2, when a new suspension was enacted. That interval, from March to November, was the longest of the three, and it shows how far the combination of fund balances, arriving receipts, and the calendar of payments can stretch when the starting date falls at a favorable point in the revenue year.

What drives duration is the interaction of stock and calendar. The stock element is the size of the funds and accounts the measures can reach. Larger balances in the retirement funds and other affected accounts mean more holdings that can be redeemed or left unreinvested, and therefore more headroom beneath the ceiling. Smaller balances mean less headroom and a shorter interval. The calendar element is the timing of tax receipts and benefit payment dates. A confrontation that begins shortly before a major receipts period gains time from the revenue that flows in, because incoming cash reduces the borrowing that the headroom must support. A confrontation that begins ahead of heavy benefit payment dates or large interest payments loses time, because outlays draw down cash and capacity on a schedule that the Treasury cannot move. Duration is therefore a joint product of the balances available at the start, the revenue that arrives during the interval, and the obligations that fall due before legislation resolves the standoff.

The end of the interval has a precise name and a precise definition. The X date is the projected date when extraordinary measures and cash are exhausted. Each element of the definition carries weight. The date is projected, because it rests on forecasts of receipts and outlays that arrive unevenly and can differ from expectations. The exhaustion covers measures and cash together, because the two resources are consumed jointly during the rundown. The date is an estimate rather than a statutory deadline. No law fixes the X date in advance, and no legal consequence is triggered by the arrival of a forecast date in the way that a consequence is triggered by an enacted effective date. The forecast can move earlier or later as receipts are reported, and a published estimate from the Treasury, the Congressional Budget Office, or the Bipartisan Policy Center is best treated as the strongest projection available at its moment of publication, subject to revision as the flows that determine it become known.

Duration evidence also carries a warning against reading any single episode as a guarantee. The 2011 interval does not promise that a confrontation beginning on a similar date would last the same number of weeks, because the balances in the affected funds, the level of cash on hand at the start, and the receipts and outlays of the intervening months would each differ. The 2015 interval, the longest of the three recorded episodes, arose from a particular alignment of starting balances and calendar. A confrontation beginning with smaller fund balances, lower cash, or an approaching cluster of large payments could exhaust the same four measures far more quickly. The measures are finite by design. They rearrange and pause activity inside accounts that exist for other purposes, and the accounts hold a fixed stock at the moment the ceiling begins to bind. Once that stock has been converted into headroom and spent down against daily obligations, no further measure in the canonical set remains to extend the interval, and the sequence returns to the condition described at exhaustion, in which payment depends on revenue as revenue arrives.

## The Sequence From Binding Limit to Exhaustion Date

The sequence moves through six stages, and each stage changes only the resources available for payment rather than the obligations themselves. Cash management comes first, extraordinary measures follow after the Secretary declares that the cap has been reached, and both resources run down together against receipts and outlays that arrive on separate schedules. The exhaustion date is a projection built from those flows, and exhaustion itself arrives when measures and cash together no longer cover obligations falling due.

| Stage | What happens | What the Treasury may do | What the Treasury may not do |
|---|---|---|---|
| The limit is reached | Enacted obligations remain due while outstanding obligations reach the statutory maximum | Declare the limit reached and notify Congress | Issue net new obligations above the maximum |
| Cash management | Cash on hand and receipts cover outlays for a brief interval | Draw on cash balances and adjust auction timing | Create new borrowing authority |
| Extraordinary measures begin | The Secretary declares a debt issuance suspension period and the defined measures take effect | Suspend specified sales and reinvestment and redeem specified fund investments | Appropriate money or cancel any obligation |
| Measures and cash run down | Measures and cash are consumed together against uneven receipts and outlays | Apply freed headroom to obligations falling due | Borrow above the maximum |
| The exhaustion date approaches | Forecasts place exhaustion within a narrowing range as flows arrive | Publish updated estimates of the exhaustion date | Fix the exhaustion date by law |
| Measures are exhausted | Measures and cash no longer cover obligations falling due | Pay obligations from incoming receipts | Pay amounts beyond cash and receipts on hand |

## How Congress Has Acted on the Limit in Practice

The practice that developed around the statutory ceiling bore little resemblance to a barrier that held borrowing to a fixed total and then stopped the government at that line. Across the decades after the modern aggregate form took shape in 1939, Congress returned to the question again and again, and each return ended in action that allowed the Treasury to keep financing obligations that other laws had already created. The ceiling was raised to a higher figure, extended in its operation, or, in the pattern that came to dominate after 2013, suspended for a defined period. Those outcomes repeated under administrations of both parties, and the repetition itself became the central fact of the practice. A vote on borrowing authority was never a vote on whether the underlying obligations existed. Appropriations laws and mandatory spending laws created the obligations. The question before Congress on each occasion was narrower and, in mechanical terms, unavoidable once spending and revenue laws had produced a gap between outlays and receipts. Either borrowing authority expanded to cover the gap, or the Treasury faced a date on which cash on hand and internal accounting measures would no longer suffice to pay amounts already owed.

That sequence explains why action on the ceiling acquired the character of routine necessity even when the debates surrounding it were extended and contested. Practice therefore settled into a recurring cycle with a predictable shape. The Treasury approached the operative figure. The department announced that the figure had been reached or would be reached on a stated date. A period followed in which cash balances and extraordinary measures supplied headroom. Congress debated, negotiated, and attached conditions or companion measures in some episodes and acted without them in others. The cycle then closed with an enactment that raised, extended, or suspended the ceiling, and the government continued paying obligations that had never ceased to be lawful obligations during the interval.

The routine character of the outcome did not mean that each episode was brief or that each enactment was simple. Some adjustments passed as freestanding measures concerned only with the figure itself. Others traveled as part of larger statutes that carried budget rules, spending provisions, or entirely separate programs, and the 1985 episode described below showed how readily the ceiling could serve as the vehicle for a major fiscal law. From the middle of the twentieth century through the second decade of the twenty-first century, no episode in the practice ended with a permanent refusal that left enacted obligations permanently unfinanced. Each standoff, delay, and negotiation closed with authority sufficient for the Treasury to proceed, and the next occasion was set by arithmetic rather than by any change in the underlying structure, because enacted spending and revenue law continued to produce borrowing needs that the existing figure could accommodate for only a finite period.

### Why does Congress suspend the limit instead of raising it?

A suspension avoids a vote on a specific dollar figure while allowing the Treasury to finance obligations already enacted. During the suspension the statutory cap does not apply, and afterward the cap resets at the level of obligations then outstanding, accommodating borrowing undertaken during the period.

The distinction between a dated suspension and a numbered raise shaped the legislative practice of the period from 2013 to 2019, and the mechanics repay careful statement. When Congress raised the ceiling in the older manner, the statute named a figure, the figure appeared in the text that members approved, and debate could attach itself to the size of that figure as though the figure represented a decision about how much the government ought to owe. In one sense the figure was real enough, since the Treasury could not lawfully pass it once borrowing reached it. In another sense the figure decided nothing about policy, because the borrowing it permitted was borrowing made necessary by spending and revenue decisions already taken. A suspension separated those two elements. The statute named dates instead of a total. From the first date to the second, the ceiling simply did not operate, and the Treasury issued obligations as enacted law required without reference to a statutory maximum. No member voted on a number, because the enacted text contained no number to vote on. The political point of the device, as the Congressional Research Service described the suspension practice, lay precisely in that absence.

Reinstatement completed the mechanism. When a suspension ended, the ceiling did not return at the figure that had applied before the suspension began, since that figure would have been below the amount of obligations already outstanding and would have placed the Treasury in breach of its own governing statute at the moment of restoration. Instead, the ceiling returned at a higher level, reset to accommodate the obligations issued while the suspension ran. The reset was automatic in the sense that it followed from the terms of the suspending statute rather than from fresh legislative action. Congress therefore acted once, at the start of the period, and the consequences at the end of the period unfolded by operation of law. The government gained an interval of ordinary financing measured by the calendar, and the question of borrowing authority returned only when the reset figure was itself approached. That design explains why suspensions could run for a few months or for close to two years with equal mechanical ease. The length of the interval changed the duration of the respite. It changed nothing about how the device worked.

The episode of 1953 to 1954 stands at the head of the modern practice because the elements that recurred in later decades appeared in it in recognizable form. The Eisenhower administration sought an increase in borrowing authority, presenting the request as a requirement of financing the government under laws already in force. The request went to Congress, and in the Senate it met resistance centered in the Finance Committee, where Harry F. Byrd of Virginia served as chairman. In 1953 the committee declined to approve the increase the administration had sought, and the year closed without the enactment the administration had requested. The refusal, however, did not alter the obligations the government carried or the borrowing those obligations required, and the question returned. In 1954 an increase was enacted. The sequence therefore ran from request, through refusal in committee, to eventual enactment, and that arc from initial resistance to closing action became the template against which later episodes can be measured. The first modern standoff demonstrated that even a refusal grounded in a powerful committee chairmanship operated as a delay within the practice rather than as a termination of it.

The years that followed the 1954 enactment settled the pattern into routine. Congress acted on borrowing authority repeatedly, and the actions accumulated across administrations without regard to which party held the presidency or which party organized either chamber. Each action responded to the same underlying condition, namely that enacted law had committed the government to spending in excess of receipts across the relevant period and that the Treasury required authority to finance the difference. The direction was set elsewhere, in appropriations acts, in authorizing statutes, in revenue law, and in the mandatory spending programs whose costs ran by formula rather than by annual decision. The ceiling votes trailed behind those decisions and ratified their financing.

The 1985 episode added a new dimension to the practice by showing that a measure adjusting borrowing authority could carry major fiscal legislation into law. In that year the Gramm Rudman Hollings legislation, a statute addressed to deficit reduction through a structured framework, was attached to an increase in borrowing authority, and the combined measure was enacted as P.L. 99-177. The episode is treated in full in [the guide to the Gramm-Rudman-Hollings Act](/2020/01/15/gramm-rudman-hollings-1985-guide/), and its significance for the history of the ceiling lies in the pairing itself. A bill that the Treasury needed in order to keep financing enacted obligations became the vehicle for a fiscal statute that Congress wished to enact, and the necessity attaching to the vehicle lent momentum to the cargo. The practice thereby acquired a second function alongside its financing function. Adjustment measures were must pass measures in a practical sense, since failure to enact them placed payments at risk once headroom expired, and must pass measures attract amendments, riders, and companion provisions that might struggle to move on their own. From 1985 onward, any account of how Congress acted on borrowing authority had to reckon with the possibility that the operative question in a given episode would not be the figure or the dates at all, but the content of the larger statute in which the adjustment traveled.

The episode of 2011 carried the recurring cycle closer to its deadline than the practice had ordinarily run, and its chronology can be stated entirely in dates and outcomes. The ceiling was reached on May 16, 2011. From that date the Treasury relied on extraordinary measures, the set of internal accounting steps available under existing law, including suspension of certain investment and reinvestment activity in federal retirement and related funds, with statutory assurance that affected funds would be made whole after an increase was enacted. Those measures supplied headroom for a period measured in weeks and months rather than days, and the period ran to August 2, 2011. On that date the Budget Control Act of 2011 was signed into law as P.L. 112-25, and the episode closed. The full treatment of that statute appears in [the guide to the Budget Control Act of 2011](/2020/02/15/budget-control-act-2011-guide/). For the history of borrowing authority, the structure of the 2011 enactment mattered as much as its timing. The statute did not accomplish the entire adjustment in a single step. It structured the increase in tranches, in stages that unfolded under conditions the statute itself specified, and it paired the staged increase with a mechanism of congressional disapproval, under which a stage could be made the subject of a disapproval process within Congress.

Three days after the signing, on August 5, 2011, Standard and Poor's downgraded the sovereign rating of the United States. The agency stated governance reasons for its decision, and the downgrade entered the record of the episode as an outcome dated after the enactment that closed the financing question rather than before it. The sequence deserves emphasis because it fixed the order of events in a way that later argument sometimes blurred. The measures ran out on the schedule the Treasury had projected. The enactment arrived on August 2, 2011. The rating action followed on August 5, 2011, accompanied by the governance reasons the agency stated. The episode as a whole demonstrated both halves of the modern practice at once. The cycle of deadline, measures, negotiation, and enactment held, in that payments continued and authority was restored. The cycle also ran long enough, and close enough to exhaustion of headroom, to produce consequences in credit markets and in the stated assessment of a rating agency that no earlier episode in the practice had produced on the same scale.

The episode of 2013 then introduced the instrument that would dominate the remainder of the decade. A suspension of the ceiling, enacted earlier in 2013, expired on May 18, 2013, and on the expiration the operative figure returned and the Treasury again faced the constraint that the suspension had held in abeyance. Extraordinary measures again supplied the interval, and the interval ran from May 2013 to October 17, 2013. On October 17, 2013, the Continuing Appropriations Act, 2014 was enacted, and that statute ended the episode by suspending the ceiling again rather than by raising it to a new figure. The choice of instrument in October 2013 confirmed the direction the practice had taken in February of the same year, when the first suspension of the period had been enacted. Congress had discovered, or rediscovered, that a dated suspension accomplished everything a numbered raise accomplished for financing purposes while changing the terms on which members voted. The vote concerned a period. The borrowing during the period followed from enacted law. The figure at the end of the period set itself. The 2013 episode therefore closed one standoff and simultaneously supplied the model for the six suspensions that followed it through 2019.

The Congressional Research Service recorded the suspensions in a table that permits the era to be described with precision, act by act and date by date, and the sequence shows a practice converging on a single instrument. The first suspension of the era came in the No Budget, No Pay Act of 2013, P.L. 113-3, enacted on February 4, 2013, which suspended the ceiling through May 18, 2013. The second came in the Continuing Appropriations Act, 2014, P.L. 113-46, enacted on October 17, 2013, which suspended the ceiling through February 7, 2014, and which also closed the 2013 episode described above. The third came in the Temporary Debt Limit Extension Act, P.L. 113-83, enacted on February 15, 2014, which suspended the ceiling through March 15, 2015, an interval of more than a year that illustrated how a dated suspension could push the next confrontation well beyond a single session's ordinary horizon. 

The middle suspensions of the era lengthened the intervals and attached the device to the recurring budget agreements that structured fiscal legislation across the decade. The Bipartisan Budget Act of 2015, P.L. 114-74, enacted on November 2, 2015, suspended the ceiling through March 15, 2017. The enactment date itself marked the end of another interval of extraordinary measures, which had run in 2015 from March 16 to November 2, so that the suspension arrived on the day the preceding headroom expired and continued the financing of enacted obligations without interruption. The Continuing Appropriations Act, 2018, P.L. 115-56, enacted on September 8, 2017, suspended the ceiling through December 8, 2017, a shorter interval of three months that showed the device working at the opposite end of the durational range, as a bridge across a single season rather than as a settlement for a full budget cycle. The Bipartisan Budget Act of 2018, P.L. 115-123, enacted on February 9, 2018, suspended the ceiling through March 1, 2019. Across these enactments the names and public law numbers changed and the intervals varied, but a reader comparing the texts would find the same operative idea repeated, because Congress had settled on a form that answered the recurring necessity while asking the least specific question a financing vote can ask, namely how long, rather than how much.

The final suspension of the period covered by this article brought the era to its longest dated horizon. The Bipartisan Budget Act of 2019, P.L. 116-37, enacted on August 2, 2019, suspended the ceiling through July 31, 2021. That end date was an enacted schedule, written into the law of August 2019 and operative as the governing timetable from the date of enactment forward, and it carried the suspension across the remainder of 2019, across 2020, and into the middle of 2021 by the statute's own terms. On reinstatement after July 31, 2021, the ceiling would reset under the mechanism the suspension statutes employed, returning at the level of obligations then outstanding so as to accommodate borrowing issued during the suspended period. The 2019 enactment thus closed the sequence that the Congressional Research Service tabulated as seven suspensions between February 2013 and August 2019, and it left the practice in a settled posture. Congress had acted on borrowing authority seven times in six and a half years without once voting a new dollar figure into the statute as the operative maximum for the interval ahead, and each of the seven actions had ended, as every earlier episode in the practice had ended, with the Treasury able to finance the obligations that enacted law imposed. Each enactment also reset the calendar of the practice, because the date or figure chosen determined when Congress would face the same financing question again under the same underlying constraint that spending and revenue law, rather than the ceiling itself, created.

Read as a whole, the practice from 1953 to 2019 describes a single institution working under a single constraint and producing a single outcome through instruments that changed over time. The constraint was that enacted spending and revenue law generated borrowing needs independent of the ceiling that governed the borrowing. The outcome was that Congress always, in the end, supplied the authority the Treasury required, whether through the increase enacted in 1954 after the Finance Committee had declined the administration request in 1953, through the increase of 1985 that carried the Gramm Rudman Hollings framework into law as P.L. 99-177, through the staged increase and disapproval mechanism of the Budget Control Act of 2011, or through the seven dated suspensions that ran from the No Budget, No Pay Act of 2013 to the Bipartisan Budget Act of 2019. A history of the ceiling written from its practice rather than from its text is therefore a history of repeated adjustment presented as recurring crisis and completed as routine, and the suspension era represented not a departure from that history but its most economical expression, in which Congress dispensed with the number altogether and voted only on the time during which the question would not be asked again.

## The Gephardt Rule and the Deeming Devices

A separate vote on borrowing occupies an unusual place in congressional procedure because the decision it appears to make has, in substance, already been made elsewhere. Appropriations laws and mandatory spending laws create the obligations of the United States. Tax laws determine the revenue available to meet those obligations. When revenue falls short of enacted spending, the Treasury must borrow the difference if the government is to pay what it owes, and the statutory ceiling codified at 31 U.S.C. 3101 governs only whether that borrowing may occur. Refusing to adjust the ceiling does not cancel a single enacted program, reduce a single benefit fixed by law, or repeal a single tax provision. It threatens payment of obligations already incurred. A member voting on borrowing authority therefore confronts a vote that changes nothing about the fiscal choices that produced the need to borrow, while carrying severe consequences if the vote fails and considerable political exposure if it succeeds. Opponents can portray an affirmative vote as a vote for more borrowing, even though the borrowing finances commitments Congress itself enacted.

That combination explains why both parties, across very different fiscal eras, have searched for procedural devices that let the necessary adjustment happen without a freestanding recorded vote devoted to it alone. Each one folds the borrowing decision into another decision that Congress must make in any event, or reverses the default so that borrowing authority expands unless Congress affirmatively stops it, or supplies a substitute procedure for the day when authority runs out. Each one therefore concedes, by its structure, a point that floor debate often obscures. The question under such a device is rarely whether the United States will pay its obligations. The question is who must be seen voting to permit payment, in what form that vote appears in the record, and how much separate political cost attaches to it. The history of the best known of these devices, the House procedure associated with Representative Richard Gephardt of Missouri, illustrates the appeal of avoidance and the limits of a rule that one chamber can adopt and discard at the opening of each Congress. According to the Congressional Research Service, that history runs from adoption in 1979 through repeated suspension, repeal, and restoration, to a substantially modified version adopted in January 2019.

### What was the Gephardt rule?

The Gephardt rule was a House procedure under which adoption of a budget resolution carried a borrowing adjustment with it, automatically engrossing a joint resolution matched to the resolution level and sending that measure to the Senate without a separate House vote on borrowing authority alone.

The rule took its name from Representative Richard Gephardt of Missouri. According to the Congressional Research Service, the procedure was first added to the House standing rules as Rule XLIX by P.L. 96-78 in the 96th Congress, and it applied from the fiscal 1981 budget resolution. The rule was renumbered Rule XXVIII in the 106th Congress recodification. The choice of the budget resolution as the vehicle was deliberate and, within the logic of the congressional budget process, exact. A budget resolution is the one regular measure in which Congress states, in aggregate terms, the levels of spending, revenue, and borrowing that its separate taxing and spending decisions are expected to produce. If that aggregate statement already sets the level to which federal obligations are expected to rise, then a second vote restating the same figure in a borrowing measure adds a procedural step without adding a substantive decision. Attaching the consequence to the resolution therefore located the borrowing decision in the single vote where the fiscal totals were actually debated and approved. What disappeared was the intermediate moment, after the plan had been approved, at which the same member could be asked to vote again on one unpopular component of a plan already adopted, isolated from the spending, revenue, and program choices that gave the plan whatever political defense it possessed.

The original mechanism, as the Congressional Research Service describes it, was precise in its sequencing. When the House and Senate agreed to the conference report on a budget resolution, a joint resolution adjusting the ceiling to the level in the resolution was automatically engrossed and deemed passed by the House, and the measure was then sent to the Senate. Every element of that sequence mattered. The trigger was agreement on the conference report, the product of both chambers, so the figure carried into the joint resolution was the figure in the final congressional budget plan rather than a figure from a House proposal that the Senate might later change. Engrossment, the formal preparation of the measure as passed, occurred automatically, without a motion, a debate, or a roll call directed to borrowing. The deeming language did the central work. The House treated the joint resolution as passed because the rule said to treat it that way, and the legal fiction converted the conference report vote into the operative House action on borrowing as well. The device removed the separate House vote without removing Senate consideration or any other step required for a joint resolution to become law.

That design also reveals what the device did not do. It did not repeal the statutory ceiling or transfer borrowing authority to the executive branch. A joint resolution had to become law before the Treasury gained additional room, and its amount was tied to the level stated in the budget resolution rather than left to administrative discretion. Nor did the procedure guarantee an outcome in the Senate. The avoidance was specific: it spared House members a redundant recorded vote, on the theory that the conference report vote was the real decision. Critics could answer that accountability diluted across a large fiscal vote is weaker than accountability attached to a single unmistakable question, and that the political cost proponents sought to escape was the discipline the separate vote was supposed to impose.

The interrupted life of the procedure shows how contingent that judgment was. According to the Congressional Research Service, the House suspended the rule for particular years, namely 1988, from 1990 to 1991, from 1994 to 1997, and from 1999 to 2000. Suspension for specified years differs from repeal. A suspension leaves the architecture in the rules while disabling it for a defined period, a choice that fits a majority that finds the device inconvenient for a particular budget cycle without wishing to renounce it permanently. The pattern of suspensions across stretches governed by different majorities and different fiscal politics underlines that discomfort with a standalone borrowing vote never belonged to one party. Majorities of both parties found reasons, in particular years, to set the automatic mechanism aside, and majorities also found reasons to let it operate.

The House repealed the procedure at the start of the 107th Congress in 2001, restored it at the start of the 108th Congress in 2003, and repealed it again at the start of the 112th Congress in 2011, as the Congressional Research Service recounts. Because House rules are adopted anew at the beginning of each Congress, a procedure of this kind lives only as long as successive majorities choose to carry it forward, and each repeal or restoration was itself a statement about how the incoming majority wished borrowing votes to be handled. Repeal restored the default world in which additional authority required its own measure and its own House vote, with the exposure that entailed. Restoration announced that the majority preferred the borrowing consequence to travel with the budget resolution instead. The oscillation therefore measured the procedural preference of House majorities confronting the same recurring dilemma, some willing to absorb the separate vote as a cost of governing or as a source of leverage, others preferring to fold the vote into the budget process.

The version adopted in the 116th Congress carried the idea further and changed its mechanics in two respects that altered both its politics and its operation. The House reinstated the procedure in modified form in January 2019. Under the first modification, as described by the Congressional Research Service, the deemed joint resolution would suspend the ceiling rather than raise it to a number. Suspension had become, in the years after 2013, a familiar technique in enacted borrowing measures that set the ceiling aside for a defined period. Importing that technique into the deemed joint resolution removed the most quotable element of the traditional measure, a specific new dollar figure that an opponent could repeat in isolation. A suspension states a period rather than a total, and the instrument itself carries no headline number. That absence is the political point of the device. Under the second modification, House adoption of the budget resolution alone triggered engrossment of the deemed joint resolution, with no conference report required. That change loosened the connection that the original design had prized. The 1979 mechanism waited for a figure agreed by both chambers in conference, so the joint resolution embodied a completed congressional budget decision. The 2019 mechanism allowed the House to generate the measure from its own adoption of a resolution, whether or not the Senate agreed to the same plan or to any plan at all.

The consequences of that second change run in both directions. On one side, the modified trigger made the device more reliable for a House majority, because budget resolutions frequently fail to reach a conference agreement with the Senate, and a House that wanted the avoidance benefit could obtain it through its own action alone. On the other side, the change weakened the original justification. If the deemed measure rests on a House resolution that the Senate never adopted, the claim that the borrowing figure simply restates a decision Congress as a whole already made loses much of its force. The joint resolution had to pass the Senate under its regular process and become law, so the Senate retained its full opportunity to reject or amend a figure it had never endorsed in a budget plan. The Senate, as the Congressional Research Service notes, has never had a comparable rule. That asymmetry is structural. One chamber arranged to deem a measure passed on the strength of a budget vote, while the other considered the same measure as ordinary legislation. The device therefore never eliminated the legislative process for borrowing authority; it redistributed the burden of visibility within that process.

An operational footnote from the first year of the modified procedure shows how completely such a device depends on the budget process that feeds it. The House adopted no budget resolution for fiscal 2020, so the modified rule produced no engrossed joint resolution in 2019. A trigger keyed to adoption of a resolution cannot fire in a year without a resolution. A deeming rule is therefore not a self executing solution. It is a supplement to a functioning budget process, and when that process stalls, majorities return to freestanding measures and suspensions enacted as ordinary law, with the votes and bargaining that the rule was designed to avoid. A device built to spare members one hard vote presupposed willingness to take another hard vote, on the resolution itself, and in 2019 that presupposition was not met.

The Gephardt procedure belongs to a wider family of deeming and avoidance devices. Some devices deem a vote to have occurred, as the House rule did. Some deem approval granted unless a blocking action succeeds. Some change the form of the adjustment from a number to a period, as the suspension technique did both in enacted statutes and in the 2019 version of the rule. Others accept the ceiling as binding and attempt instead to manage the consequences of reaching it. Each design allocates visibility, leverage, and risk differently. A majority confident in its budget plan may prefer the resolution based device. A majority negotiating under pressure may prefer a mechanism that forces opponents to assemble an overriding coalition. A majority unwilling to authorize more borrowing at all may turn to ordering payments instead.

The Budget Control Act of 2011 supplied the leading example of the second design, a deeming device built into a statute rather than into the rules of one chamber. That act, P.L. 112-25, signed on August 2, 2011, raised borrowing authority in stages and paired the staged increases with a presidential certification and congressional disapproval mechanism. Under that arrangement, the increase took effect on the strength of the presidential certification unless a resolution of disapproval survived the full legislative process, including the prospect of a veto that opponents would have to override. The inversion of the ordinary default was the heart of the design. Ordinary borrowing legislation requires supporters of an increase to assemble a majority in both chambers and a presidential signature, or two thirds of both chambers over a veto, before authority expands. The 2011 mechanism required opponents of an increase to assemble that larger coalition to prevent an expansion already set in motion by the statute and the certification. The political allocation followed from the arithmetic. A member could vote for disapproval, record opposition to additional borrowing, and campaign on that vote, while knowing that the disapproval would not stop the increase unless it commanded a veto proof majority. Supporters of continued payment of federal obligations obtained the authority without requiring every member of the governing coalition to cast an affirmative vote for it. The device changed which side bore the burden of assembling a decisive coalition, and in a closely divided Congress that burden is often the whole contest.

Comparing that statutory device with the House rule exposes the distinct problems each was built to solve. The rule addressed one chamber and one procedural embarrassment, the redundant House vote that duplicated a budget decision, and it operated only when the budget process produced the necessary trigger. The 2011 mechanism addressed a government wide impasse, and it was written into the statute that resolved that impasse, so its operation did not depend on a future budget resolution. The rule deemed a measure passed by the House. The statute deemed an increase approved unless Congress could enact a prohibition over executive resistance. Both devices allowed authority to expand while reducing the number of members who had to vote affirmatively for expansion in a form opponents could isolate, and both diffused accountability into procedure in exchange for a workable result.

Prioritization proposals represent the opposite philosophy within the same family of responses, because they begin by accepting the ceiling as a hard boundary and then attempt to rank the payments the Treasury should make when cash falls short of obligations. Rather than deem an increase to have passed or allow an increase unless Congress blocks it, a prioritization statute would direct the order in which obligations are honored, placing some categories ahead of others that would wait for cash to accumulate. The House passed a measure of this kind, the Full Faith and Credit Act, in May 2013, and that measure was not enacted. Prioritization treats the recurring crisis as a cash management problem governed by a statutory queue, instead of a voting problem to be engineered away. Its proponents could claim fidelity to the ceiling as enacted, while promising that the obligations most closely tied to the credit of the United States would be protected from delay. Its opponents could answer that a queue does not create cash, that ranking some obligations first necessarily ranks others later, and that a government selecting which lawful obligations to honor on time has already departed from the premise that enacted commitments will be paid in full and on schedule. The proposal and the deeming devices therefore disagree about means while sharing a revealing premise: each side built a procedure around the collision between spending law, revenue law, and the borrowing ceiling.

Read together, the devices in this family support a single diagnostic conclusion. Every device concedes the core fact that the obligations will be paid. The House rule conceded it by tying the adjustment to a budget plan that assumed payment. The suspension form conceded it by setting the ceiling aside for a period and allowing it to return at whatever higher level payment required. The 2011 certification mechanism conceded it by making the increase the default outcome that only a veto proof coalition could prevent. Even prioritization conceded a version of it, by singling out the obligations whose payment was treated as too important to leave to chance. The sustained argument, across four decades of procedural invention, was not about whether the United States would honor commitments made under its laws. The argument was about who must cast the visible vote, in which chamber, attached to which larger measure, under which default rule, and with what opportunity for opponents to force a separate confrontation. That is why majorities of both parties have adopted, suspended, repealed, and restored such devices, and why none has settled the question permanently. The fiscal arithmetic that makes borrowing necessary is produced by tax and spending statutes that these procedures do not touch, so each new approach to the ceiling revives the same search for a way to let payment proceed while assigning the political cost of permitting it to someone else, to another vote, or to no identifiable vote at all.

## The Untested Theories for Acting Without an Increase

When the statutory cap binds, the executive branch confronts a conflict that ordinary administration cannot dissolve. Congress has enacted appropriations and mandatory spending laws that create obligations. Congress has enacted tax laws that determine the revenue available to meet those obligations. Congress has also enacted a ceiling, codified at 31 United States Code 3101, that restricts the face amount of obligations the Treasury may issue. When cash on hand proves insufficient to pay every obligation in full and on time, obedience to all three commands at once becomes impossible, because payment requires borrowing and the available borrowing authority has reached the boundary Congress set.

Congressional Research Service Report R45011, Clearing the Air on the Debt Limit, catalogued the principal theories advanced for acting without an increase and catalogued the principal objections to each theory with equal care. Report R45011 treated each proposal as a legal argument with a text, a mechanism, and a set of consequences. No court had resolved the central questions by April 1, 2020, and no administration had acted on any of the theories. The theories therefore occupied a distinctive position, developed in law reviews and memoranda, tested in argument, and untested in a courtroom judgment or in an actual issuance carried through the financial system.

The trilemma deserves precise statement. The first command directs the executive branch to spend what Congress appropriated and to pay the benefits and obligations that mandatory law creates. Appropriations and mandatory spending laws create the obligations, and the ceiling governs only whether the Treasury may borrow to pay obligations already incurred. Refusing to raise the ceiling does not cancel enacted spending. The second command directs the executive branch to tax only what Congress levied, in the manner Congress prescribed, so revenue arrives in amounts determined by tax law and economic conditions rather than by the payment calendar of the Treasury. The third command directs the executive branch to borrow only up to the boundary in section 3101. When receipts fall short of obligations coming due, any course of action disappoints at least one instruction that Congress enacted.

Each theory resolves the trilemma by assigning priority to one command over another, and the theories differ chiefly in the source of law invoked to justify that priority. The Fourteenth Amendment theory assigns priority to payment, on the ground that a constitutional provision protecting the validity of public obligations outranks a statutory restriction on borrowing. The platinum coin theory assigns priority to a different statute, on the ground that a separate grant of coinage authority in section 5112(k) supplies payment capacity outside section 3101. The premium bond theory assigns priority to the text of section 3101 itself, on the ground that the statute counts face amount rather than cash raised, so a financing structure that raises more cash than face amount complies with the statutory measure while meeting the payment command.

### Could the Fourteenth Amendment override the debt ceiling?

No court had decided the question by April 1, 2020, and Congressional Research Service Report R45011 presented the clause as an untested argument rather than settled authority. Proponents read Section 4 as forbidding default. Opponents read the clause as protecting validity without transferring borrowing authority from Congress to the President.

Section 4 of the Fourteenth Amendment, ratified in 1868, provides that the validity of the public debt of the United States, authorized by law, shall not be questioned. Report R45011 catalogued the argument built on that sentence in the following form. A missed payment on an obligation authorized by law would place the validity of that obligation in question in the most concrete sense available, because a government that declines to pay an obligation as promised invites every holder to doubt whether the obligation means what the obligation says. On that reading, the clause imposes a duty that forbids a course of conduct culminating in repudiation, and a statutory ceiling that compels repudiation when receipts run short must yield to the constitutional command. A prohibition without a remedy would leave the President able to identify a constitutional violation and unable to prevent the violation, so the clause, on the proponent account, authorizes or compels borrowing past the statutory boundary as necessary to keep payment whole.

Proponents also emphasized the breadth of the operative language and the hierarchy between a constitutional provision and a statute, since ordinary principles give a constitutional command precedence over a conflicting statute. The clause speaks of validity, a concept that reaches the standing of the obligation in law and in the expectations of holders, and states the protection in absolute terms without an exception for fiscal inconvenience or legislative inaction. On the proponent account, a payment failure caused by a borrowing restriction differs in cause from a declaration of repudiation, but the holder who receives no payment experiences the same result, and the clause protects the holder against the result rather than against a particular legislative motive.

Report R45011 catalogued the counterargument at corresponding strength, beginning with the distinction between validity and authority. The clause addresses whether an obligation, once authorized by law and incurred, may be questioned. The clause does not, in its terms, address who may create an obligation, in what amount, or by what procedure. Borrowing authority presents a separate question, and Article I assigns the power to borrow money on the credit of the United States to Congress. On the counterargument account, Section 4 protects obligations that Congress authorized, while Article I determines the channel through which authorization occurs, and a provision protecting the product of the legislative process cannot supply a substitute for the legislative process itself. A President who borrowed without statutory authority would be creating a new obligation and then invoking the new obligation as proof that the borrowing was required, a circular sequence that the counterargument treats as a decisive weakness.

The historical counterargument reinforces the textual counterargument on the account Report R45011 catalogued. Section 4 emerged from the Civil War and addressed the obligations of that conflict. The provision protected Union obligations against repudiation and denied validity to obligations incurred in aid of the Confederate cause. On that account, the clause answered a specific historical danger, a future Congress disavowing wartime borrowing or honoring the obligations of a defeated rebellion, rather than a general problem of executive borrowing authority during a legislative impasse. Proponents answered that a clause written in broad language may govern circumstances beyond the occasion that produced the clause. The counterargument responds that the controversy of 1868 supplies evidence about the kind of questioning the clause forbids, namely legislative repudiation of acknowledged obligations, rather than executive payment management under a borrowing restriction that Congress retained power to lift by ordinary legislation.

A further counterargument concerns the actor bound by the clause and the remedy that follows from a violation. Even on an account that treats a missed payment as a questioning of validity, the clause does not specify which officer bears the duty to act or what action the duty requires. Congress could satisfy the clause by raising the ceiling, by reducing obligations, or by raising revenue, and each remedy lies within legislative power. Unilateral borrowing represents one response among several, and the counterargument maintains that converting a clause addressed to the government generally into a specific grant of borrowing power to one branch sits uneasily beside an express assignment of that power to Congress in Article I.

The only Supreme Court authority bearing on the clause, Perry v. United States, 294 United States 330, decided on February 18, 1935, supplies material for both sides and a holding for neither side on the borrowing question. Perry formed one of the Gold Clause Cases. The case concerned a government bond that promised payment in gold coin, and a congressional enactment abrogating gold clauses in obligations, including the obligations of the government itself. Chief Justice Hughes wrote for a Court divided five to four. The Court held that Congress exceeded its power by abrogating the gold clause in the government bonds, reasoning that the power to borrow and to fix the terms of borrowing did not include a power to repudiate the terms after holders had relied on those terms. The decision discussed the Public Debt Clause as confirmatory of a broader principle protecting the integrity of public obligations.

Precision about Perry matters because advocacy on both sides pressed the decision beyond its holding. The bondholder in Perry recovered nothing, because the bondholder showed no actual loss from the abrogation, and the judgment therefore denied relief even while accepting an important part of the bondholder argument about congressional power. The case involved no statutory borrowing ceiling. No party asked whether the President could borrow beyond a boundary Congress had set, and the Court decided nothing about a conflict between section 3101 and Section 4. Proponents drew from Perry the language of integrity and the holding that Congress lacks a general power to rewrite the terms of federal obligations after issuance. Opponents drew from Perry the structure of the reasoning, in which the clause confirmed a principle grounded elsewhere rather than supplying a freestanding source of authority, and the remedial outcome, in which an established constitutional defect produced no recovery absent proven loss.

The institutional record after Perry added caution rather than clarification. During the episodes of 2011 and 2013, when extraordinary measures supplied weeks and months of headroom while Congress debated an increase, administration officials publicly declined to treat the clause as authority for unilateral borrowing. The White House and the Treasury stated that the clause was not a basis for unilateral borrowing. An administration confronting an impending payment failure held every incentive to preserve a lawful escape route, and a public refusal to invoke the clause signaled doubt about the argument, doubt about the market reception of obligations issued under the argument, or doubt about both propositions at once. The statements did not decide the legal question, because executive restraint binds no court. The statements did decide the practical question for those episodes, because a theory that the sitting administration refuses to invoke supplies no payment capacity.

The platinum coin theory proceeds from statute rather than from the Constitution. Section 5112(k) of title 31 authorizes the Secretary of the Treasury to mint platinum coins. Report R45011 catalogued the theory as a reading of that authorization in which the denomination of a platinum coin carries the entire fiscal effect. On the proponent reading, the statute grants the Secretary discretion over denomination without an express numerical cap, so the Secretary could mint a platinum coin bearing a very high denomination, deposit the coin at the Federal Reserve, and receive credit in the government account against the deposited coin. The credited funds would supply payment capacity for obligations coming due, and no obligation counted under section 3101 would increase, because coinage under section 5112(k) operates outside the borrowing framework that section 3101 measures.

The statutory purpose objection maintains that Congress wrote section 5112(k) for bullion and proof purposes, as a provision governing platinum bullion coins and proof coins for collectors and investors, rather than as a fiscal instrument for financing the government at a denomination detached from metal value. On that account, denomination discretion in a coinage statute presupposes denominations connected to a coin program, and reading the discretion to authorize a financing device of enormous size attributes to Congress a decision of major fiscal significance hidden inside a provision addressed to mint products. Proponents answered that statutory text, rather than presumed purpose, governs the scope of a grant, and that Congress wrote denomination language without the numerical restriction that Congress placed elsewhere when Congress intended a restriction.

The acceptance objection operates independently of statutory interpretation. The Federal Reserve would need to accept the deposit and extend the credit, and acceptance was not guaranteed. A refusal, a delay for legal review, or a conditional acceptance that markets treated as provisional would leave the Treasury with a minted coin and no additional payment capacity at the moment capacity was required. Proponents noted that coin deposits move through established arrangements between the Treasury and the Federal Reserve, and that a platinum coin differs in denomination rather than in legal character from other coins. Opponents responded that routine handling of circulation and collector coinage supplies a weak precedent for a deposit intended to finance government operations during a borrowing impasse, and that the central bank would face questions of first impression under time pressure.

The institutional cost objection reaches beyond legality. A financing device perceived by markets as an artifice could raise questions about the regularity of federal payment practices even when the device succeeded in meeting a payment date. Holders price federal obligations on the expectation of predictable procedures administered through recognizable channels, and a sudden reliance on a high denomination coin could signal that ordinary channels had failed. Proponents answered that a missed payment would damage credibility more severely than an unconventional payment method, and that a government choosing between two damaging signals should choose the signal accompanied by payment in full. Opponents answered that a failed or contested coin operation could produce both the credibility damage and the payment disruption, and that the availability of the device in future debates could alter legislative incentives by reducing pressure on Congress to act. In January 2013, the Treasury publicly dismissed the idea, removing executive endorsement at the moment when endorsement would have mattered. The dismissal settled the practical status of the theory for that episode without settling the statutory question, which no court addressed.

Premium bonds present the most technical of the three theories and the theory most closely confined to the accounting structure of section 3101. The statute restricts the face amount of obligations issued. A bond carries a face amount payable at maturity and a coupon rate that determines interest payments during the life of the bond. When a bond bears a coupon rate above the market rate for comparable obligations, investors pay more than face amount to acquire the bond, because the above market coupon delivers a stream of payments worth more than the price of a bond issued at the market rate. The excess of the purchase price over face amount constitutes the premium. A Treasury offering structured with above market coupons could therefore raise cash greater than the face amount added under section 3101, and the difference would supply payment capacity when the face amount boundary otherwise blocked new issuance.

The accounting logic follows from the statutory measure Congress chose. Section 3101 measures obligations by face amount rather than by proceeds, and the premium proposal treats that choice as a deliberate legislative decision with consequences Congress accepted when Congress selected the measure. A bond sold at a premium counts once, at face amount, even though the Treasury receives more than face amount at issuance and pays more than a market coupon during the term. Over the full life of the bond, the premium received at issuance and the excess coupon paid across the term stand in an offsetting economic relationship, because investors pay in advance for the elevated payments the bond promises. The government gains time and immediate cash rather than a permanent fiscal advantage, and proponents presented the structure as compliance with the statute as written.

The objections to premium bonds concern cost, market reception, and administrative fit rather than constitutional power. Elevated coupons commit the government to interest payments above market rates for the life of each bond, and the premium received at issuance compensates investors for precisely those payments, so the device purchases headroom at a price that a full accounting must recognize. Repeated use could complicate the composition of outstanding obligations, since bonds with widely varying coupons issued for financing structure rather than for investor demand depart from the regular pattern of issuance that supports predictable auctions. An offering designed around the statutory measure could also attract scrutiny about purpose even when the terms offered investors full economic value, and a premium structure introduced during an impasse would require design, announcement, and settlement under time pressure. No administration adopted the proposal, which remained a law review proposal rather than a practice of the Treasury.

Comparison among the three theories clarifies why a debate presenting a single question, whether the President may act alone, in fact presents three different questions. The Fourteenth Amendment theory asks a court, or an executive officer bound by oath to the Constitution, to rank a constitutional clause above a statute and to locate a borrowing remedy inside a clause addressed to validity. The platinum coin theory asks a reader to construe a coinage grant at the full extent of denomination language and asks an independent central bank to convert that construction into spendable balances. The premium bond theory asks the Treasury to administer a financing structure that complies with a statutory measure while altering the economics of an issuance. Each theory relocates the difficulty rather than eliminating the difficulty.

Untested therefore functions as the operative word in any accurate description of the three theories. Untested means that the Fourteenth Amendment argument never received a judicial construction addressed to a borrowing ceiling, that the denomination reading of section 5112(k) never produced a minted coin presented to the Federal Reserve, and that the premium structure never appeared in a Treasury offering. Untested also means that the counterarguments never received the testing that operative use would supply, so defenders of congressional control over borrowing could not point to a judgment sustaining their position on the precise questions any more than proponents could point to a judgment sustaining executive action. The record through April 1, 2020, consisted of a constitutional clause from 1868, a single Supreme Court decision from 1935 addressed to a different controversy, two statutes whose interaction no court had construed in the relevant setting, executive statements in 2011 and 2013 declining the constitutional theory, an executive dismissal of the coin in January 2013, and continued nonuse of the premium structure. Secretaries and administrations associated with both political parties left each theory unused. The pattern of nonuse reflects the trade at the center of each proposal, in which a legislative problem, the refusal or inability of Congress to raise the ceiling before the payment date, converts into a constitutional problem about the location of borrowing power, a statutory problem about the purpose of a coinage grant, or a credibility problem about the regularity of federal finance.

## The Comparative Picture and the Case for the Limit

The American arrangement stands apart less for the existence of a fiscal rule than for the kind of rule Congress chose. Almost no other country, however, separates the decision to incur obligations from the decision to permit payment of those obligations through a freestanding statutory ceiling expressed as an absolute amount of face value. In the United States, appropriations and mandatory spending laws create the obligations, and the ceiling codified at Title 31, Section 3101 of the United States Code governs only whether the Treasury may borrow to pay them. The Congressional Research Service has described the cap as covering over 99 percent of federal obligations of the relevant kind, which means the separation reaches nearly the whole of public finance rather than a narrow class of securities.

### Do any other countries have a debt ceiling?

Almost no other country uses a comparable instrument. Denmark is the only other country with a statutory ceiling set as an absolute amount. Most countries have no such ceiling at all, and other fiscal rules expressed as shares of economic output operate through procedures rather than through a threat to payment.

The Danish ceiling dates from 1993, and the Congressional Research Service account treats the date and the institutional setting as essential to its meaning. The ceiling was adopted as a formality when management of Danish public borrowing moved to the Danish central bank. A legal amount had to exist in the new arrangement, and legislators supplied one, but the purpose described in the comparison is administrative rather than disciplinary. The amount was set far above actual borrowing by broad consensus, a level chosen so that ordinary management would not approach it. A ceiling placed beyond reach by agreement among the parties that set it does not create a recurring occasion on which payment itself becomes the subject of negotiation. It records an institutional transfer.

The single Danish increase confirms that reading. The ceiling was raised once, in 2010, when borrowing reached about two thirds of the amount then in law, and on that occasion the amount was more than doubled. Action came well before exhaustion, at a comfortable distance from any point at which payments might have been interrupted, and the size of the increase restored a wide margin rather than a narrow one. No episode in the Danish record turned the ceiling into a constraint on management, and the Congressional Research Service summary states that conclusion directly, that the Danish ceiling has never constrained management of public borrowing. A comparison that stopped at the shared label would therefore mislead. The United States and Denmark share an absolute amount written into law. The two amounts occupy different places in public life, one as a recurring point of legislative leverage in Washington and the other as a distant marker placed, by consensus, beyond the range of ordinary operations in Copenhagen.

Most countries have no such ceiling at all, and that absence deserves the same plain statement as the Danish exception. Governments without an absolute cap borrow, tax, appropriate, and argue about deficits, but the legal authority to issue obligations follows from the budgetary decisions themselves rather than from a separate vote on a cumulative total. Other countries express fiscal rules as percentages of gross domestic product. The European Union reference value of 60 percent of gross domestic product offers the standard illustration. A rule of that form measures a stock against the size of an economy and judges a fiscal position against a benchmark. Breach of such a benchmark triggers a fiscal procedure, with assessment, recommendation, and correction directed at future policy. The difference is one of kind rather than degree. A percentage rule speaks to whether a government ought to adjust its plans. The American ceiling, once reached, speaks to whether the Treasury may obtain the cash to honor plans already enacted into law.

The case for retaining the American ceiling, stated at full strength, begins from that same distinctiveness and reads it as a virtue. The argument from accountability holds that the ceiling is the one point in the federal process where Congress must vote on the accumulated total rather than on increments. Appropriations arrive bill by bill, program by program, and year by year. Tax measures, entitlement formulas, and emergency measures each present a partial question, and a legislator may support or oppose each increment without ever confronting the sum that the increments produce over time. A vote on the ceiling presents the sum. Defenders describe that confrontation as forcing periodic accounting and public debate about the size of the total, a debate that the ordinary calendar of spending and revenue decisions tends to disperse. The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget has argued, as a documented position recorded by the Congressional Research Service, that the ceiling forces periodic fiscal debate of exactly that character. On this account, the awkwardness of the vote is the point. A total that no single bill displays becomes visible, attributable, and debatable in one place.

The negotiation record gives the accountability argument its historical exhibits, and the strongest version of the case rests on outcomes rather than on symbolism alone. The ceiling has repeatedly been the occasion for negotiated fiscal restraint, because a measure that the Treasury needs and that must pass becomes a vehicle to which broader agreements attach. The Gramm-Rudman-Hollings Act of 1985 reached enactment attached to an increase, enacted as Public Law 99-177, and the episode established the pattern in modern form, a borrowing measure carrying a framework aimed at deficit reduction. The Budget Control Act of 2011, Public Law 112-25, signed on August 2, 2011, followed the same pattern on a larger stage, raising the ceiling in stages under a congressional disapproval mechanism as part of a negotiated settlement reached after the ceiling had been reached on May 16, 2011 and after extraordinary measures had carried the Treasury to the date of enactment. Supporters of the ceiling treat those two episodes, both central to the budget statutes examined in this series, as proof that the device produces agreements that the regular process had not produced on its own timetable.

A related argument reaches deeper than tactics and appeals to democratic accountability for the total. A legislature that votes the spending, defenders ask, ought also to have to vote the borrowing that the spending requires, rather than treating borrowing as an automatic administrative consequence that no elected body ever approves in aggregate. Taxation and appropriation both receive votes, and each vote carries a public record. Borrowing on the scale practiced by the United States finances a large share of enacted government, and a process in which that financing never faced its own vote would, on this view, hide a central fact of public choice from the electorate. Members who vote for programs and for tax levels that together imply a rising total must periodically vote to accommodate that total, and members who oppose the accommodation must explain which enacted obligations they would leave unpaid or which earlier decisions they would reverse. The vote thus connects cause and consequence across sessions of Congress, binding together choices that the legislative calendar otherwise separates by months or years.

The risk arguments deserve statement with equal care, beginning with the limit that the device imposes on its own usefulness. Raising or suspending the ceiling does not reduce any obligation by a single dollar, and refusing to raise it does not cancel enacted spending either. The obligations arise from appropriations and mandatory spending laws, and those laws remain in force whatever happens to borrowing authority. The ceiling governs only the ability to pay. A standoff therefore does not present a choice between borrowing and restraint in the abstract. The choice presented is between borrowing to meet commitments already made and impairing the payment of those commitments while they remain legally due. Critics regard that structure as an inversion of sound procedure, because the moment of maximum danger arrives after the decisions that created the obligations have closed. The accountability vote praised by defenders occurs, on this reading, at the one moment when accountability can no longer change the obligations being counted.

The costs of delay have a documented measure. The Government Accountability Office examined the 2011 episode and reported, in Government Accountability Office report GAO-12-701, that the delay in raising the ceiling increased Treasury borrowing costs by about 1.3 billion dollars for fiscal 2011. That figure carries its attribution and its limits with it, an estimate by the Government Accountability Office of the added cost in one fiscal year from one episode, rather than a general price for every confrontation. Investors who lend to the Treasury price uncertainty about payment, and a government that approaches the boundary of its borrowing authority while debating whether to extend that authority pays more for funds during the interval of doubt. The expense buys no reduction in obligations, no reform of any program, and no change in any revenue law. Delay alone produced it.

A broader form of the same argument concerns the repricing of public credit through repeated proximity to nonpayment. Each episode teaches markets to treat a previously unquestioned promise as a subject of political timing, and the lesson does not expire when an agreement restores borrowing authority. Standard and Poor's downgraded the sovereign rating of the United States on August 5, 2011, three days after the Budget Control Act of 2011 became law, an outcome that belonged to the aftermath of the 2011 episode rather than to any missed payment. No payment had been missed, yet the credit judgment moved. Critics draw from that sequence a conclusion about cumulative effect, that periodic confrontation converts the full faith and credit of the United States from a background condition of world finance into a variable that traders, rating analysts, and foreign holders must reassess at intervals set by domestic legislative politics. Defenders answer that debate about a growing total is a legitimate price of democratic control. The record supports elements of both readings, agreements on restraint in 1985 and 2011 alongside a measurable added cost in 2011 and a downgrade in the same year.

## The Misunderstandings That Survive Every Episode

Public argument about the ceiling repeats a small set of errors with remarkable fidelity, and each error survives because it offers a simpler story than the mechanics allow. The errors concern what an increase authorizes, what arrival at the ceiling means, what the courts have decided, what the Treasury may do on its own authority, what foreign practice shows, and where the device came from. Taken together, the corrections describe a single underlying reality, a borrowing cap that operates late in the sequence of public decisions, after obligations exist, and that therefore behaves unlike the spending decisions with which debate habitually confuses it.

The most durable error holds that an increase in the ceiling authorizes new spending. The sequence of federal law runs the other way. Congress authorizes and appropriates spending through legislation, agencies incur obligations under that legislation, and the Treasury then requires borrowing authority sufficient to pay the resulting bills when cash on hand falls short. An increase supplies payment capacity for obligations already enacted. The increase neither appropriates money nor creates a program nor enlarges any agency authority to commit the government. Conversely, refusal to increase the ceiling repeals nothing. A program funded by enacted law remains funded in law even when the Treasury lacks borrowing authority to disburse on schedule, which is why a standoff produces arrears and uncertainty rather than savings that any budget document can record. Treating a vote for an increase as a vote for the underlying spending misassigns responsibility, crediting or blaming members for decisions taken in earlier appropriations and authorizations while missing the narrower question actually presented, whether obligations that Congress has already imposed on the government will be paid from borrowed cash.

A second error treats arrival at the ceiling as immediate default, as if a calendar date in a statute switched off payment at a stroke. No such cliff edge appears in the law. When the ceiling binds, the Treasury cannot issue net new obligations, but payment continues from cash on hand, and a set of extraordinary measures then extends the interval before exhaustion. Those measures include suspension of sales of State and Local Government Series securities, declaration of a debt issuance suspension period under Title 5, Section 8348 of the United States Code affecting the Civil Service Retirement and Disability Fund, suspension of reinvestment of the Government Securities Investment Fund of the Thrift Savings Plan under Title 5, Section 8438, and suspension of reinvestment of the Exchange Stabilization Fund. Federal law requires the Civil Service Retirement and Disability Fund and the Government Securities Investment Fund to be made whole, including lost interest, once an increase is enacted. The measures have historically created weeks to months of headroom. In 2011 the ceiling was reached on May 16 and measures ran to August 2. In 2013 a suspension expired on May 18 and measures ran to October 17, and in 2015 measures ran from March 16 to November 2. The projected exhaustion point carries the name X date, a term used by the Bipartisan Policy Center and adopted in press coverage. An X date is an estimate built from projected receipts and outlays, moving as cash flows move, rather than a deadline written into the statute itself.

A third error asserts that the courts have settled the constitutional escape route, usually described through Section 4 of the Fourteenth Amendment. That provision, ratified in 1868, declares that the validity of the public debt of the United States, authorized by law, shall not be questioned. The theory built on the language holds that the clause forbids default and therefore permits or requires presidential borrowing despite the ceiling. The theory has never received a judicial ruling. The only treatment by the Supreme Court of the United States came in Perry v. United States, 294 U.S. 330, decided on February 18, 1935 by a vote of five to four, with Chief Justice Hughes writing for the Court, and Perry decided a different question. Perry was one of the Gold Clause Cases. The Court held that Congress had exceeded its power by abrogating the gold clause in the bonds of the government itself, discussed the Public Debt Clause as confirmatory of a broader principle protecting the integrity of public obligations, and denied recovery because the bondholder had shown no actual loss. No borrowing ceiling was at issue, no presidential action was before the Court, and no holding addressed whether Section 4 displaces a statutory cap. The Congressional Research Service report R45011, titled Clearing the Air on the Debt Limit, catalogues the theory among untested proposals, and administration officials in 2011 and 2013 publicly declined to rely on the clause, with the White House and the Treasury stating that the clause was not a basis for unilateral borrowing.

A fourth error assumes that the Treasury, facing exhaustion, can simply rank payments under existing law, paying bondholders and other urgent claimants first and deferring the rest in an orderly queue. No statute supplies a priority order of that kind. The existing body of appropriations law directs payment of obligations as they come due under enacted programs, and no enacted provision instructs the Secretary of the Treasury which lawful claims to honor and which to postpone when borrowing authority runs out. Prioritization has existed as a proposal rather than as law. The House of Representatives passed the Full Faith and Credit Act in May 2013, a measure directed at paying bondholders first, and that measure was not enacted. The legislative history of the proposal proves the gap it sought to fill, because a power already held under existing law would have required no new statute. Without such a statute, a decision by the Treasury to favor one class of claimant over another would rest on improvisation rather than on authority granted by Congress. Debate that presents prioritization as an available administrative option skips the missing step, enactment, which no Congress had completed by April 1, 2020.

A fifth error imagines that other countries manage the same device routinely, so that American difficulty reflects a failure of political maturity rather than a peculiarity of design. The comparative record offers no such reassurance. Denmark is the only other country with a statutory ceiling set as an absolute amount, and the Danish ceiling never binds. Adopted in 1993 as a formality when management moved to the Danish central bank, set far above actual borrowing by broad consensus, and raised once in 2010 when borrowing reached about two thirds of the amount, at which point the amount was more than doubled, the Danish arrangement was constructed to stay out of the way of management and has done so. Most countries have no ceiling of the kind at all. Other fiscal rules, such as the European Union reference value of 60 percent of gross domestic product, operate through fiscal procedures aimed at future policy and do not threaten payment of obligations already incurred. No foreign example therefore demonstrates a working routine for governing by repeated confrontation over payment authority.

A sixth error, quieter than the others, recasts the origin of the device as an act of fiscal restraint, as if Congress in 1917 had imposed the ceiling to hold down borrowing that wartime finance threatened to expand. The Second Liberty Bond Act of 1917, Public Law 65-43, 40 Stat. 288, enacted on September 24, 1917, did the opposite. Before 1917, Congress authorized specific loans and instruments individually, specifying amount, maturity, and purpose, as in the loan for the Panama Canal. The 1917 statute imposed aggregate constraints on categories, bonds in one category and certificates of indebtedness in another, while retaining separate amounts, and the point of aggregation was flexibility, permission for the Treasury to choose instruments and respond to conditions within a total rather than return to Congress for authority over each issue. In 1939 Congress eliminated the separate amounts for bonds and other obligations and created the first aggregate ceiling covering nearly all public borrowing, set at 45 billion dollars by Public Law 76-201 in July 1939, and the modern form dates from that consolidation. A grant of flexibility later acquired uses its authors did not design, including use as leverage for restraint in 1985 and 2011, but origin and later use remain different facts.

## A Diagnostic for Reading Any Debt Limit Episode

A diagnostic for any episode begins with the obligations rather than with the rhetoric surrounding the vote. The first task is identification of the enacted laws that created the borrowing need. Appropriations acts, mandatory spending laws, and tax laws supply the components, and the financing gap is the arithmetic result of those components. Placement of responsibility follows from that identification. A refusal to adjust the cap leaves every component in force, and the diagnostic therefore treats refusal as a decision about payment rather than as a decision about the size or direction of any program.

The second task is location of the sequence. Cash on hand, auction timing, and the four extraordinary measures define the early stages, and the projected exhaustion date defines the practical deadline. A reader who knows whether an episode stands at the declaration stage, inside the rundown of measures and cash, or near exhaustion can judge competing claims about urgency with precision. Duration in the dated episodes ran from weeks to months, with the 2011 interval running from May 16 to August 2 and the 2015 interval running from March 16 to November 2.

The third task is identification of the device Congress is reaching for and of the negotiation the episode is actually carrying. An increase names a new figure. A suspension names dates, leaves the cap inoperative during the period, and resets the cap afterward to accommodate obligations issued during the period. A deeming rule folds the adjustment into adoption of a budget resolution, in the form associated with Representative Richard Gephardt of Missouri and in the modified suspension form adopted in January 2019. Episodes also carry companion bargains, and [the record of reform proposals attached to limit episodes](/2020/04/15/budget-process-reform-impact/) traces how often a financing measure became the vehicle for a broader fiscal settlement, from the Gramm Rudman Hollings framework in 1985 to the Budget Control Act of 2011.

The fourth task is testing any legal theory against the judicial record. The Fourteenth Amendment argument rests on Section 4, ratified in 1868, and on language protecting the validity of public obligations authorized by law. The platinum coin argument rests on section 5112(k) and on denomination language in a coinage statute. The premium bond argument rests on the face amount measure inside section 3101 itself. Each argument requires a check against Perry v. United States, decided on February 18, 1935, in which the Supreme Court held that Congress exceeded its power by abrogating the gold clause in government bonds, treated the Public Debt Clause as confirmatory of a broader principle, and denied recovery because the bondholder showed no actual loss. Perry involved no borrowing cap and decided no question about presidential borrowing beyond a statutory boundary, and a theory presented as settled law fails that check on the record alone.

Application of the four tasks together separates mechanism from theater in a disciplined order. Enacted obligations explain the need. Sequence position explains the timing. One further point anchors the whole diagnostic, since [the mechanics of ordinary lawmaking for a limit change](/2022/02/01/how-a-bill-becomes-law-in-practice/) govern the reliable route in every episode, and changing the cap requires an ordinary enacted law passed through the regular legislative process.

Read through that diagnostic, the episodes from 1953 to 2019 form a single pattern with changing instruments. An administration sought an increase in 1953, a Senate committee declined, and an increase followed in 1954. Suspensions replaced numbered increases as the dominant instrument between 2013 and 2019, with seven suspensions enacted from the No Budget, No Pay Act of 2013 through the Bipartisan Budget Act of 2019 and a final period running through July 31, 2021 under the 2019 statute. Denmark provides the lone foreign comparison, with a ceiling dating from 1993, set far above actual borrowing, raised once in 2010, and never constraining management.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does raising the debt ceiling actually authorize?

Raising the ceiling authorizes the Treasury to issue obligations and apply the proceeds to obligations that enacted law has already created. The adjustment creates no appropriation, establishes no program, and expands no eligibility rule. Spending authority comes from appropriations acts and mandatory spending laws enacted through separate statutes. Tax law determines receipts, and borrowing finances the gap between enacted outlays and enacted receipts. A refusal to raise the ceiling cancels no spending and repeals no benefit formula. The refusal removes borrowing capacity while the obligations remain in force. The vote therefore concerns payment of commitments already incurred rather than authorization of new commitments.

### Q: Where did the debt ceiling come from?

The ceiling originated in the Second Liberty Bond Act of 1917, enacted on September 24, 1917 as Public Law 65-43. Before 1917, Congress authorized specific loans and instruments individually, specifying amount, maturity, and purpose, as in the Panama Canal loan. The 1917 statute imposed aggregate constraints on categories of securities, including bonds and certificates of indebtedness, while separate amounts for distinct categories remained in place. In 1939, Congress eliminated the separate amounts through Public Law 76-201 and created the first aggregate cap covering nearly all public borrowing, set at 45 billion dollars. The modern form dates from that 1939 consolidation.

### Q: Was the debt ceiling originally a restriction?

The original design functioned as a grant of flexibility rather than as a restraint on the Treasury. Before 1917, each loan required individual legislative specification of amount, maturity, and purpose. The Second Liberty Bond Act of 1917 allowed the Treasury to choose among instruments and respond to market conditions inside aggregate category totals. The 1939 consolidation widened that discretion by replacing several category amounts with one overall figure, so authority unused in one category no longer sat locked inside that category. A maximum does mark a point beyond which issuance requires further legislation. The historical movement, however, ran toward broader administrative freedom inside a legislated total.

### Q: What are extraordinary measures under the debt ceiling?

Extraordinary measures are the defined steps the Treasury takes after the cap has been reached to create headroom beneath the statutory maximum. The canonical set contains four measures. Sales of State and Local Government Series securities are suspended. A debt issuance suspension period is declared for the Civil Service Retirement and Disability Fund, permitting redemption of existing investments and suspension of new investments. Reinvestment of the Government Securities Investment Fund of the Thrift Savings Plan is suspended. Reinvestment of the Exchange Stabilization Fund is suspended. The measures appropriate no money, cancel no obligation, and raise no revenue. The combined effect extends payment capacity for weeks to months.

### Q: What is the X date in a debt ceiling standoff?

The X date is the projected date when extraordinary measures and cash are exhausted. The term originated with the Bipartisan Policy Center and entered press coverage as a shorthand for the exhaustion point. The date is a projection rather than a statutory deadline, because the forecast rests on receipts and outlays that arrive unevenly and can differ from expectations. Stronger receipts can push the projected date later, and heavier outlays can pull the projected date earlier. The Treasury, the Congressional Budget Office, and the Bipartisan Policy Center publish estimates during a standoff. No legal consequence attaches to a forecast date itself, and each published estimate reflects the strongest projection available at publication.

### Q: What was the Gephardt rule for the debt ceiling?

The Gephardt rule was a House procedure named for Representative Richard Gephardt of Missouri. The procedure entered the House standing rules as Rule XLIX through Public Law 96-78 in 1979 and applied from the fiscal 1981 budget resolution. Under the original mechanism, agreement by the House and Senate to the conference report on a budget resolution automatically engrossed a joint resolution adjusting the cap to the level in the resolution and deemed that joint resolution passed by the House. The deemed measure then went to the Senate, which had no comparable rule and acted under regular process. The House suspended, repealed, and restored the procedure across later Congresses.

### Q: Can the Fourteenth Amendment solve the debt ceiling?

No court had decided the question by April 1, 2020, and Congressional Research Service Report R45011 presented the argument as untested. Section 4 of the Fourteenth Amendment, ratified in 1868, provides that the validity of the public debt of the United States, authorized by law, shall not be questioned. Proponents read the clause as forbidding default and therefore as authorizing presidential borrowing beyond the statutory cap. Opponents read the clause as protecting the validity of obligations Congress authorized without transferring borrowing authority from Congress to the President. Administration officials in 2011 and 2013 publicly declined to rely on the clause as a basis for unilateral borrowing.

### Q: Do other countries have a debt ceiling?

Almost no other country uses a comparable instrument. Denmark is the only other country with a statutory ceiling set as an absolute amount. The Danish ceiling dates from 1993, was adopted as a formality when debt management moved to the Danish central bank, and was set far above actual borrowing by broad consensus. The Danish ceiling was raised once, in 2010, when borrowing reached about two thirds of the amount, and the amount was then more than doubled. The Danish ceiling has never constrained debt management. Most countries have no such ceiling, and rules expressed as a percentage of gross domestic product operate through fiscal procedures rather than through a threat to payment.

### Q: What is the statutory citation for the debt ceiling?

The statutory citation is 31 U.S.C. 3101, with the dollar limitation placed at subsection (b). The provision states a limitation on the face amount of obligations issued by the Treasury. The Congressional Research Service describes the cap as covering over 99 percent of federal obligations of the relevant kind, including obligations held by the public and obligations held within the government, such as securities held by federal trust funds. Section 3101 confers borrowing authority in the form of a maximum face amount rather than as permission for a single named loan. A change to the figure changes the room available for issuance without rewriting any spending or tax statute.

### Q: What is the difference between suspending the debt ceiling and raising it?

A raise names a new dollar figure as the operative maximum, and debate can attach to the size of that figure. A suspension names dates instead of a total. During a suspension the cap does not apply, and the Treasury issues obligations as enacted law requires without reference to a statutory maximum. No vote occurs on a number, because the suspending statute contains no number. Seven suspensions were enacted between February 2013 and August 2019, from the No Budget, No Pay Act of 2013 through the Bipartisan Budget Act of 2019. The suspension device accomplished the same financing result as a raise while changing the question members answered from how much to how long.

### Q: What happens to the retirement funds used in extraordinary measures?

Federal law requires the Civil Service Retirement and Disability Fund and the Government Securities Investment Fund to be made whole, including lost interest, once an increase in the cap is enacted. Balances redeemed during a suspension period are restored, investment suspended during the period is corrected for in the restoration, and interest that would have accrued is included. The funds return to the position each fund would have occupied if the measures had not been taken. The statutory assurance holds federal employees and retirees harmless from the use of the measures. The measures borrow time from internal accounts without permanently transferring losses to the participants and beneficiaries those accounts support.

### Q: What happens when extraordinary measures run out?

At exhaustion, cash and measures together no longer cover obligations falling due, and the Treasury can pay only from incoming receipts. Money arriving on a given day can be applied to obligations, but obligations falling due on that day can exceed the amount that has arrived, and no borrowing above the cap is available to close the gap. Obligations are then paid late or in part. The underlying laws creating the obligations remain in force, and the amounts owed remain unchanged. Delay and partial payment follow from cash insufficiency combined with absent borrowing authority, rather than from any repeal or recalculation of the commitments themselves.

### Q: What is a debt issuance suspension period?

A debt issuance suspension period is the formal legal condition the Secretary of the Treasury declares under 5 U.S.C. 8348 in connection with the Civil Service Retirement and Disability Fund. The declaration permits two linked actions. Existing investments held by the fund may be redeemed, which reduces the amount counted against the cap and creates room for other borrowing. New investment of the fund may be suspended, which prevents the fund from adding holdings that would consume room again. The declaration is a defined condition invoked under a cited section of Title 5 rather than an informal decision to delay investment, and the defined condition supports both actions that follow from the declaration.

### Q: What is the platinum coin proposal?

The platinum coin proposal rests on 31 U.S.C. 5112(k), which authorizes the Secretary of the Treasury to mint platinum coins. Congress wrote the provision for bullion and proof coins. The proposal reads the denomination language in the provision as permitting a coin of very high denomination, deposited at the Federal Reserve to create credit in the government account and payment capacity without borrowing under section 3101. No administration adopted the proposal, and no coin of that kind was minted. The Treasury publicly dismissed the idea in January 2013. Acceptance by the Federal Reserve was not guaranteed, and no court addressed the statutory question.

### Q: What are premium bonds in the debt ceiling debate?

Premium bonds are bonds carrying coupon rates above market rates, sold to investors at a price above face amount. Section 3101 counts obligations by face amount rather than by cash proceeds. A bond sold at a premium therefore raises cash greater than the face amount added under the cap, and the difference supplies payment capacity when the face amount boundary otherwise blocks new issuance. The premium received at issuance and the excess coupon paid across the term of the bond stand in an offsetting economic relationship, because investors pay in advance for the elevated payments. No administration adopted the structure, which remained a proposal rather than a Treasury practice.

### Q: Does the debt ceiling control how much Congress can spend?

The ceiling controls borrowing rather than spending. Appropriations acts and mandatory spending laws determine how much the government spends, through agency authority, benefit formulas, and eligibility rules. Tax laws determine receipts. Borrowing finances the gap between enacted outlays and enacted receipts. An increase in the cap creates no appropriation and directs no agency to spend, and a refusal to increase the cap cancels no program and reduces no benefit fixed by law. The ceiling governs whether the Treasury may borrow to pay obligations after spending and revenue decisions have produced a financing need. Control over spending levels sits in the appropriations, authorization, and tax statutes themselves.

### Q: What share of federal debt does the debt ceiling cover?

The Congressional Research Service describes the cap at 31 U.S.C. 3101 as covering over 99 percent of federal obligations of the relevant kind. The covered total includes obligations held by the public, sold to investors outside the federal government, and obligations held within the government itself, including securities held by federal trust funds and similar intragovernmental accounts. Both forms count within the same aggregate accounting. A framework of that breadth leaves almost no corner of federal borrowing outside the boundary. A Treasury operating at the boundary therefore confronts a constraint on the central financing mechanism of the government rather than on a marginal or specialized program.

### Q: What did Perry v. United States decide?

Perry v. United States, 294 U.S. 330, decided on February 18, 1935, was one of the Gold Clause Cases. Chief Justice Hughes wrote for a Court divided five to four. The Court held that Congress exceeded its power by abrogating the gold clause in the bonds of the government itself, reasoning that the power to borrow and to fix the terms of borrowing did not include a power to repudiate terms after holders had relied on those terms. The decision discussed the Public Debt Clause as confirmatory of a broader principle protecting the integrity of public obligations. The bondholder recovered nothing, because the bondholder showed no actual loss. The case involved no borrowing cap.

### Q: What is the difference between a debt ceiling standoff and a government shutdown?

A shutdown arises when appropriations lapse. Authority to incur new obligations for affected activities generally ceases, and affected operations curtail activity for want of spending authority. A ceiling standoff presents the opposite configuration. Money has been appropriated and obligations have been incurred under valid law, but the Treasury lacks authority to borrow the cash required to pay those obligations in full and on time. Spending authority exists while financing authority falls short. Restoration of appropriations cures a shutdown. Enactment of appropriations alone does not cure a binding cap, because appropriated money that cannot be borrowed during a period when receipts fall short of outlays remains beyond the reach of the paying officer.

### Q: How does a suspension of the debt ceiling end?

A suspension ends on the date named in the suspending statute. At the end of the period, the cap does not return at the figure that applied before the suspension began, because that figure would sit below the amount of obligations already outstanding and would place the Treasury in breach of the governing statute at the moment of restoration. The cap instead returns at a higher level, reset to accommodate the obligations issued while the suspension ran. The reset follows from the terms of the suspending statute rather than from fresh legislative action. The Bipartisan Budget Act of 2019, enacted on August 2, 2019, suspended the cap through July 31, 2021 under an enacted schedule.
