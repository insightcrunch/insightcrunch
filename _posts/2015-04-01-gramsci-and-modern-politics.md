Gramsci wrote his strategic theory in a prison cell, in notebooks smuggled past a censor, for a movement that had been crushed. Few bodies of political writing have travelled further from their author's intentions for their audience. The vocabulary he built to analyse how consent is organised is quoted by strategists who share his aims, by scholars who share only his questions, and by intellectual currents organised against everything he worked for, including currents that describe their own project, without embarrassment, as a Gramscianism of the right. A reader meeting the word hegemony in a newspaper column, a party memorandum, a management text, or a rival movement's founding programme is entitled to ask two questions. What analytical work, if any, is the word doing in that place? And how did a communist prisoner's categories become common property?

This article answers both questions with a procedure and a claim. The procedure is a Gramscian analysis reduced to five questions: which alliance rules, what its consent costs and who pays, where opinion is being manufactured and by whom, what ground a challenger would have to take and hold, and who performs the organising work on each side. A reader who can run those five questions, and can say what evidence each one demands, owns the method rather than its vocabulary. The claim, named here as the availability problem, explains the migration. Hegemony in Gramsci is a description of how consent is organised, not a partisan doctrine with an allegiance built in, and a description of that kind is available to any project that wants to organise consent. Describing a strategy as Gramscian therefore identifies a method, never a lineage. The pages that follow build the procedure, test it on closed historical cases, trace the adoptions across the spectrum without deploring or celebrating any of them, state the strongest objection to the whole framework at full strength, and answer it with the discipline the five questions impose.

## The first correction: hegemony is not dominance

The dominant misreading of Gramsci in strategic writing treats hegemony as a grand word for dominance, the condition of being on top, secured by whatever mixture of pressure and persuasion the top happens to use. Journalism made the usage habitual, and international relations theory gave it a scholarly home, where a hegemon is simply the strongest power in a system and hegemonic stability is the order its preponderance buys. Nothing in that usage is Gramscian beyond the syllables. In the Prison Notebooks, hegemony names a specific relationship with conditions attached: a group leads when it secures the active consent of subordinate groups, organises that consent through the institutions of civil society, and pays for leadership with real concessions from its own immediate advantage, while coercion stands in reserve for those who refuse the settlement. Where those conditions fail, Gramsci's vocabulary has other words, domination among them, and the distinction between the two is the concept's cutting edge. The concept itself is reconstructed in full in [the complete guide to hegemony](/2015/01/15/gramsci-hegemony-complete-guide/); the task here is narrower and more practical, to show what happens to the concept when it is carried into strategy, and what discipline keeps the carrying honest.

The misreading matters because it reverses the analyst's task. If hegemony means dominance, then identifying the dominant party finishes the analysis, and the word becomes a label pasted onto a result already known. If hegemony means organised consent under conditions, then identifying who rules is where the analysis starts: the interesting questions concern the alliance that rules, the price it pays, the sites where opinion is being manufactured, and the functions it has staffed. The first usage flatters the writer, who sounds theoretical while saying that the strong are strong. The second usage burdens the writer, who must produce evidence about concessions and institutions before the word is earned. Strategic writing prefers the first usage for the same reason editorial writing does, and the preference explains most of what has happened to Gramsci's vocabulary in transit. His terms circulate as prestige synonyms, detached from the conditions that made them discriminating, and the detachment is so complete that a minister, a columnist, and a revolutionary can all describe the same arrangement as hegemonic while meaning three different things, none of them checkable.

### Does naming an arrangement hegemonic explain it?

No. Naming classifies, and classification is only the claim that an arrangement belongs to a kind. Explanation begins afterwards, when the analyst shows the machinery the name implies: the alliance, the concessions, the institutions forming opinion, the organisers at work. A label offered as a conclusion is the misreading at work.

A second misreading follows from the first and is corrected by the same distinction. Because the vocabulary of cultural struggle is vivid, it is often treated as a replacement for the analysis of production, as if Gramsci had announced that politics had migrated from the factory to the studio and the seminar room. The notebooks refuse that reading on nearly every page where industry appears. Hegemony, for Gramsci, must be economic as well as cultural: the leading group is decisive in the central activity of the economy, and its leadership in the state and in civil society rests on that nucleus rather than floating free of it. The cultural terrain matters in his theory because consent is organised there, not because the economy stopped mattering, and the factory councils of his Turin years remained his model of an institution in which a subordinate group learns to govern. A strategy article that quotes the war of position while forgetting the economic nucleus has kept the metaphor and dropped the theory. The correction generalises into a rule the rest of this article follows: every Gramscian term used here travels with its conditions, or it does not travel at all.

## The availability problem: why Gramsci travels

The migration of the vocabulary is usually explained by fashion, by the prestige of difficulty, or by the academy acting as a wholesaler of continental theory. Those explanations are not wrong, but they miss the structural reason, which lies in the kind of thing hegemony is. Some political concepts are doctrines: they specify ends, name friends and enemies, and carry their allegiance inside them, so that borrowing them means joining something. Hegemony is not a doctrine of that kind. It is a description of a mechanism, an account of how durable rule is assembled out of alliances, concessions, institutions, and organised opinion, and of how that assembly can fail. Descriptions of mechanisms behave like maps and unlike flags. A map of a coastline serves the navy that drew it and the smuggler who buys a copy, and its accuracy is the same in both hands. The availability problem is the name this article gives to that property and to its consequences: because a theory of that kind, an anatomy of consent rather than a creed, lies open to any project in the consent business, which is how the vocabulary of cultural preparation before political victory came to be spoken fluently by currents organised against its author's own, and why no custodianship can recall the loan.

The property is not an accident of reception. It was built into the theory at the point of construction, because Gramsci built the concept by studying his enemies as carefully as his allies. The notebooks analyse the Catholic Church as the most accomplished organising machine in Italian life, the liberal order of the Risorgimento as a case of leadership achieved and then wasted, and the fascist regime's own consent work with a cold attention to its techniques. A concept hammered out by examining how other people's rule holds together cannot be fenced for one camp's use. Its author intended it as a weapon, certainly, and the intention is part of the historical record; but the instrument he forged was an analysis, and analyses outrun intentions in a way that programmes do not. The strategist who reads the notebooks for the anatomy of consent and ignores the anatomy of exploitation is reading selectively, and the selectivity can be criticised; what cannot be done is to make the anatomy unreadable to him. The availability problem is therefore also a discipline for Gramsci's admirers. It obliges them to stop treating adoption by opponents as theft or as compliment, and to start grading applications by the only standard the theory itself supplies: whether the conditions travel with the words.

### Why do people across the political spectrum cite Gramsci?

Because his theory describes how consent is organised rather than prescribing a politics. Any project seeking to build consent can use the analysis, which is why currents opposed to his own politics have adopted the vocabulary of cultural struggle preceding political victory.

What follows from availability is a grading problem, and grading is the skill this article exists to teach. If anyone can cite Gramsci, citation proves nothing, and the interesting work shifts to the borrower's cargo. Some borrowings carry the whole machine: the concessions condition, the bloc analysis, the attention to where common sense is manufactured, the patience of the war of position. Others carry a single term, usually hegemony itself, stripped to a synonym for influence. Between those poles lie the serious partial borrowings, the ones that take the terrain analysis and leave the economic nucleus, or take the intellectuals and leave the class anchorage, and each partial borrowing is a different theory wearing the same citation. The five questions of the next sections double as the grading instrument, since an application that can answer them has imported the method, and an application that cannot has imported the letterhead. Availability also cuts the other way, against a lazy defence. It is sometimes said that hostile adoption refutes the theory, or that adoption by everyone proves it empty. Neither follows. A description used by opposed camps may be general rather than vacuous, as a grammar is general, and the test of generality is whether the description still discriminates, still returns negative verdicts, still forbids some arrangements the name. Sections below return to that objection at full strength. Here the point is only that availability is a fact about the theory's design, and that strategy writing becomes literate when it treats adoption as the beginning of a grading exercise rather than as a scandal or a triumph.

### What exactly travels when a concept is borrowed?

Three things can travel, separately. The word travels cheapest, as a prestige label for influence. The picture travels next, the image of trenches, terrain, and slow cultural siege. The conditions travel last and dearest, the demands the concept makes on evidence. Grading a borrowing means asking which of the three arrived.

One further consequence deserves emphasis before the procedure is set out, because it governs the case material of this article. Availability means that the history of Gramsci's reception cannot be written as a history of discipleship. The postwar Italian leadership that presented itself as his heir, the British cultural theorists who rebuilt his framework for a different island's crisis, the Argentine and French theorists who unmoored hegemony from class, the French right that studied him as an instructor, and the international relations scholars who repackaged him for world order studies were not members of a school. They were successive borrowers, each solving a local problem with a portable instrument, and the instrument changed a little in each pair of hands. The chapters of adoption below are therefore organised by borrower and by problem, not by fidelity. Fidelity is a question for textual scholarship, and it matters; but for strategy the prior question is what each borrower needed the concept to do, what they kept, what they discarded, and what the discards cost the analysis. That is the form in which the availability problem becomes usable knowledge rather than a curiosity of intellectual history.

## Analysis as procedure, not vocabulary

A Gramscian analysis of a political situation, in the canonical form this article teaches, asks what alliance constitutes the ruling bloc, what concessions sustain its consent, where common sense is being formed, what terrain a counter-hegemonic project would have to contest, and which organising functions each side has secured. Five commitments are packed into that sentence, and each one excludes a habit of ordinary commentary. The bloc commitment excludes the habit of treating the ruling party as the ruling power, since a bloc is an alliance of groups held together by a programme and a settlement, wider than any party and often wider than the state. The concessions commitment excludes the habit of reading consent off applause, since consent in this theory is invoiced, and the invoice is found in budgets, statutes, and settlements rather than in speeches. The formation commitment excludes the habit of treating public opinion as weather, since common sense has addresses, schools, pulpits, editorial offices, professional bodies, and the analyst is expected to name them. The terrain commitment excludes the habit of treating an election as the whole of politics, since the war of position is fought in the institutions between elections. The functions commitment excludes the habit of counting famous names, since organic intellectuals are identified by the organising work they do for a group, not by their visibility.

Reduced to a slogan, all of that evaporates, which is why this article insists on the procedural form. A procedure can be taught, checked, and graded; a vocabulary can only be displayed. The distinction also explains the strange shape of the literature. Bookshops and search results overflow with writing that invokes Gramsci, and very little of it performs an analysis, because invocation is cheap and procedure is laborious. The analyst who runs the five questions on a case must handle fiscal records for the concessions question, institutional histories for the formation question, and organisational detail for the functions question, none of which fits the rhythm of a column. The reward for the labour is that the procedure can fail, and can be seen to fail, which is the property that separates analysis from incantation. Each of the five questions admits a negative answer: no bloc, only a clique; no concessions, only extraction; no formation, only noise; no contested terrain, only administration; no intellectuals, only spokesmen. A case that returns several negatives is not a hegemonic order at all, and the framework that says so has earned its keep by refusing a compliment the analyst may have wanted to pay.

### What happens when an analysis skips one of the five questions?

It inherits a characteristic error. Skip the bloc and the ruling party is mistaken for the ruling power. Skip the concessions and applause is mistaken for consent. Skip formation and opinion becomes weather. Skip terrain and an election becomes the whole of politics. Skip functions and fame is mistaken for organisation.

The procedure also disciplines the order of work, and the order is part of the method rather than a presentational choice. The bloc comes first because every later answer depends on knowing whose consent is being organised and by whom: a concession is a payment from a leading group to its allies, and it cannot be priced until the alliance is drawn. Consent comes second because it defines what the terrain is for: the institutions of civil society matter strategically as the places where the settlement is renewed or allowed to rot. Formation comes third because common sense is the material the terrain holds, the sedimented assumptions through which the settlement is experienced as natural. Terrain comes fourth because the map of contestable institutions is drawn from the first three answers, not from the constitution. Functions come last because the organic intellectuals of each side are identified by the work the previous answers specify: whoever organises the bloc, prices the concessions, staffs the formation sites, and holds the terrain is performing the function, whatever their job title says. An analyst who begins with personalities, as commentary does, has reversed the procedure and will end with a dramatis personae instead of an analysis. Extra explanations of how the ordering grades a borrowed concept, and of what a borrower must check before applying the framework to a fresh case, are collected in the questions at the end of this article; the procedure itself is now ready to be taken apart.

## What the notebooks actually prescribe

A question that shadows the whole adoption story deserves a direct answer before the procedure is itemised: does the source itself offer a strategy, or only the materials for other people's strategies? The notebooks are fragments, written under censorship, across eight years, without a final arrangement by their author, and any honest account begins there. They contain no campaign plan, no timetable, and no organisational chart, and the borrower who opens them for instructions of that kind will close them empty handed, which is one source of the disappointment recorded in the errors section below. What they contain is something rarer in the literature of strategy: an account of what kind of object a political order is, such that strategies can be built for situations the author never saw. The prescriptions that do exist are at the level of orientation and prohibition. Build leadership before power, since a group that has not learned to lead its allies in civil society will rule by the armour alone and call the result by the wrong name. Price every alliance, since consent unbought is consent unexamined. Study the enemy's formation machinery with respect, since the Church's centuries are data and not an embarrassment. Distinguish the organic from the conjunctural, the movement of the structure from the noise of the occasion, and never mistake a favourable conjuncture for a transformation of the structure. Expect the transformation, when it comes, to be molecular and slow in the West, a war of position whose advances are measured in institutions staffed and assumptions shifted.

Two of the notebooks' constructions carry the prescription load and repay the strategist's attention in their original form. The first is the Modern Prince, Gramsci's name for the political party reconceived: not a bureaucracy of functionaries and not a charismatic following, but a collective intellectual, an organism in which a social group's self-understanding is elaborated and its organisers are formed. The prescription hidden in the metaphor is about staffing and education at once. A party worthy of the name manufactures intellectuals from its own ranks continuously, promotes the worker and the peasant into the work of connection and elaboration, and treats its internal life as the first formation site it controls. The second construction is the analysis of the relations of force, the notebooks' most nearly formal passage, which grades a situation in three moments: the objective balance given by the structure, which no will can alter by wishing; the political balance, the degrees of organisation and consciousness the groups have achieved; and the military balance, decisive only in the instant and ruinous when mistaken for the whole. The five questions of this article are, in effect, the second moment unfolded into an audit, since the relations of force at the political level are exactly a bloc, its settlement, its common sense, its terrain, and its organisers, stated as a state of affairs. A borrower who keeps the three moments distinct has imported more of the method than a borrower who memorises the vocabulary, because the distinction is what prevents the commonest strategic hallucination, the reading of a favourable headline, which belongs to the third moment's weather, as a shift in the first moment's geology.

The notebooks also prescribe by refusal, and the refusals mark the boundary between the method and several of its descendants. Gramsci refuses economism, the doctrine that the structure delivers its political conclusions automatically and that strategy is a waiting room for the crisis; the whole architecture of consent exists in his writing because the delivery does not happen. He refuses the opposite voluntarism as well, the cult of the decisive act by the enlightened few, which his analysis of nineteen twenty convicts of ignoring every question in this article's procedure. He refuses, finally, the treatment of intellectuals as a decorative class, insisting that the function is universal and graded, performed in every group and decisive in aggregate, so that a movement's contempt for its own organisers is a strategic fact about it and not a stylistic preference. A strategy assembled from the notebooks is therefore an orientation with prohibitions, a terrain map with a staffing table, and a refusal of two shortcuts. That this disappoints the reader who wanted a manual is the manual's last lesson: the theory's author believed the situation must be analysed before it can be changed, and he left the analysis, deliberately, as the transferable part.

## The economic nucleus at work: the Fordism notes

The correction issued early in this article, that cultural struggle in the notebooks never substitutes for economic struggle, rests on a body of evidence the strategist should see at first hand, because it is the author's own application of his method to a hegemonic project inside production. In the notebook devoted to Americanism and Fordism, Gramsci analysed the new industrial order of the United States as a formation attempting exactly what his categories describe: a leading group reorganising not only the factory but the worker, and paying for the reorganisation in the coin the second question audits. The famous high wage of the Ford system is read, in the notes, neither as generosity nor as a simple bribe but as the price of a new kind of labour power, a worker adapted to the line's rhythm and intensity, whose reproduction the wage had to fund at a higher level than the old craft economy required. The concession was real, priced, and conditional, and the conditionality is the notebooks' point: the same corporation that paid the wage policed the life the wage bought, through investigators of domestic conduct, campaigns for temperance and sexual regularity, and an interest in the worker's family as an organ of the production system. Formation, in the Fordist case, did not happen in a school or a pulpit. It happened in the personnel department, and the common sense being manufactured was a morality fitted to the assembly line.

The notes are the method's existence proof that its author never mistook the cultural for the whole. Hegemony in the Ford analysis begins in the relations of production: the leading group is decisive in the central economic activity, its leadership is exercised through a transformation of the labour process itself, and the cultural and moral machinery is the superstructure of that transformation, not its replacement. Gramsci even entertained, with his usual refusal of comfort, the possibility that the new industrialism contained rational elements a future order would inherit, since the discipline of the line had dissolved older, parasitic strata and habits, a judgement his own side found scandalous and which the analyst should file as further evidence of the audit's symmetry. For the borrower of the vocabulary, the Fordism notes set the entrance examination. An application of Gramsci that cannot say where the case's economic nucleus lies, which group is decisive in the central activity and what its leadership costs there, has skipped the foundation the notebooks build on, whatever its fluency about the superstructure. The strategists who read him whole have always known this, and the five questions encode it: the bloc of the first question is an alliance of groups with material interests, the concessions of the second are sacrifices of those interests, and the culture of the third is the medium in which interests are experienced as a world. Culture is where hegemony is felt. The factory, the farm, and the office are where it is paid for.

## Alliance doctrine: the united front and the Lyons Theses

The procedure did not descend from theory alone; it was distilled from a decade of argument about alliances inside the workers' movement, and the argument's terms still organise how the five questions are answered in practice. The young Gramsci's party was formed in the split at Livorno in nineteen twenty-one, when the communists walked out of the socialist congress rather than remain in a formation they judged incapable of discipline, and the secession's cost, a divided movement before a rising enemy, was the first datum of his strategic education. The remedy debated across the international movement in the following years was the united front: joint action with the very organisations the split had separated from, proposed by some as a manoeuvre to expose rival leaders before their followers, and understood by Gramsci, increasingly, as something more substantial, a school in which a leading group learns to lead, by fighting for demands the allied strata already feel and by proving, in action, whose programme answers them. The distinction is the concessions condition in its party form. A united front conducted as a trap teaches the allies that the leading group's word is an instrument; a united front conducted as the embryo of a bloc, paying real costs for common victories, is hegemony being rehearsed before power, and the notebooks' later doctrine of leadership before power is the reflection of that rehearsal remembered in a cell.

The doctrine reached its fullest pre-prison statement in the Lyons Theses of nineteen twenty-six, the programme Gramsci's leadership carried at the party's third congress, and in the Southern Question essay already examined as the procedure's prototype. Read together, the two documents show the alliance doctrine at its most complete: an analysis of Italian society as a set of blocs and fractures, the southern alliance as the decisive construction, the party's own organisation to be rebuilt as the instrument of that construction, intellectuals to be formed, a press to be made national, a programme priced in the allies' demands. The documents also record, by their fate, the doctrine's limit in its own moment. Within the year the leadership was arrested, the party driven underground, and the alliance unbuilt, and the analyst of the catastrophe cannot but notice that the doctrine diagnosed the situation faster than the organisation could execute the diagnosis, a gap between the first question's answer and the fifth's that the Italian case in this article audits in full. The lesson the later borrowers drew, each in their idiom, is written into the procedure's order: the bloc question is first because it is the strategic question, and the functions question is last because its answer decides whether the first was a plan or a wish. Alliance doctrine, in the tradition this article inherits, is not the art of agreeing with more people. It is the discipline of becoming, measurably and in advance, the kind of formation that other groups can afford to follow, and its price list is the one the second question keeps.

## The five questions, taken one at a time

### The first question: which alliance rules?

The first question asks what alliance constitutes the ruling bloc, and it is the question commentary most persistently refuses to ask, preferring the simpler drama of a leader, a party, or a class acting alone. Gramsci's term for the answer is the historic bloc: a durable alliance of social groups, held together not by a shared label but by a settlement in which the leading group represents the allies' interests inside its own programme and pays for their allegiance in the currency the second question audits. A bloc is therefore wider than a coalition of parties, which can be assembled in a weekend and dissolved in an afternoon. It binds groups with different and partly opposed interests, industrialists and small proprietors, urban workers and southern landowners, into a formation stable enough to reproduce itself through changes of government. The analyst's evidence for a bloc is accordingly structural: the flow of fiscal advantage among the allied groups, the recruitment patterns that move personnel between them, the programme's ability to phrase each ally's demand as a national interest, and the formation's survival across electoral accidents. A formation that collapses when one ministry falls was a government, not a bloc, and the distinction matters for everything downstream, because concessions are priced, and consent is tested, at the level of the bloc.

Gramsci's own laboratory for the question was the Risorgimento, the Italian unification he dissected in the notebooks as a hegemonic success permanently shadowed by a failure. The Piedmontese Moderates, the liberal landowners and administrators around Cavour, achieved leadership of the national movement by absorbing even their republican rivals into a subordinate role: the Action Party of Mazzini and Garibaldi supplied the volunteers and the romance, while the Moderates supplied the state, the diplomacy, and the programme, and the settlement was sealed by a monarchy and a parliament that represented the alliance rather than the nation. Gramsci's verdict was that the Moderates exercised hegemony over their allies and domination over the southern peasantry, on whose land hunger the whole construction silently rested, and that the missing agrarian alliance was the bloc's congenital defect. The case teaches the question's discipline in both directions. It shows a bloc identified by its settlements and recruitments rather than by its rhetoric, and it shows the analyst obliged to record the exclusion on which the bloc rested, since a bloc is defined as much by the group it cannot afford to admit as by the groups it enrols. Any application that draws the alliance only from the victors' self-description has failed the first question while appearing to answer it, and the failure propagates: concessions will be mispriced, because the payer and the payees have been misidentified.

The commonest error at this step is the substitution of visibility for structure. The loudest group in a formation, the one with the newspapers and the rallies, is routinely named the ruling power, when the bloc analysis would show it to be a junior ally hired for its noise, or a traditional stratum administering an order whose leading group works elsewhere. The second error is the mirror image: treating the bloc as a conspiracy, a single will distributed among plotters, when the notebooks insist that a bloc is held together by interests and institutions, and can be riven by genuine conflicts among its members without ceasing to be a bloc. A ruling alliance is neither a masquerade nor a committee of the whole class. It is a settlement with terms, and the first question is answered only when the terms are on the table.

### The second question: what does consent cost?

The second question asks what concessions sustain the bloc's consent, and it is the question that separates the method from impressionism, because it sends the analyst to records that can be checked. A concession, in the strict sense the concept requires, is a sacrifice of the leading group's immediate and corporate interest made to secure an ally's allegiance: a tax forgone, a protection granted, a wage settlement allowed to stand, a service built, a franchise extended, a monopoly surrendered. The sacrifice must be real, which means it must be priced, and the pricing is what makes the question answerable. Speeches cost nothing and are therefore evidence of nothing at this step; budgets, statutes, land registers, and arbitration awards are the archive of consent. The analyst also dates the concessions, because a settlement is a living account. Concessions granted in one decade and quietly withdrawn in the next describe a bloc consuming its own reserves, and the withdrawal schedule is often the earliest reliable symptom of the organic crisis that the fourth question maps. The concessions condition, named and defended as the centre of the concept in [the hegemony guide](/2015/01/15/gramsci-hegemony-complete-guide/), functions here as a working instruction: until the analyst can itemise what leadership costs the leaders, the word consent in the write up is decorative.

The worked illustration is Bismarck's Germany, chosen because it displays a concession strategy pursued with open eyes and recorded in statute. The imperial chancellor who carried the Anti-Socialist Laws against the workers' movement simultaneously built the first modern social insurance state: sickness insurance in 1883, accident insurance in 1884, old age and invalidity provision in 1889. The pairing was deliberate. Coercion would harass the movement's organisation while insurance detached its constituency, offering the industrial worker a statutory stake in the imperial order that no party programme could match for reliability. Read through the second question, the case is more instructive than a success would be, because the purchase failed on its own terms. The insured workers accepted the entitlements and continued to vote for the harassed party in growing numbers, treating the concessions as rights extracted rather than as gifts binding them to the giver. The lesson is written into the question's design. A concession sustains consent only when the recipients experience it as part of a settlement they have a hand in, administered through institutions they partly inhabit; a concession delivered as a sedative, from above and against the recipients' own organisations, buys compliance at best and can be banked as evidence of the giver's fear. Gramsci's framework grades the Bismarckian settlement as domination equipped with concessions, a hybrid the second question exists to detect, and the grading is exactly the kind of negative precision that vocabulary borrowing never achieves.

The commonest error at this step is counting rhetoric as concession, the announcement audited as if it were the appropriation. The second error is subtler and more common among sympathetic analysts: treating every benefit received by subordinate groups as a concession, when some benefits are the byproducts of the leading group's own accumulation, conceded to no one and revocable without political cost. The test is sacrifice and irrevocability in combination. What did the leading group give up that it would have preferred to keep, and what would it now cost to take the gift back? Where the honest answer to both parts is nothing, the arrangement's stability must be explained by other means, and the analyst who has done the pricing is free to say so. That freedom, purchased with bookkeeping, is the method's whole advantage over the vocabulary it replaces.

### The third question: where is common sense formed?

The third question asks where common sense is being formed and by whom, and it relocates the analyst's attention from what people say in surveys to the institutions that teach them what goes without saying. Common sense, in Gramsci's usage, is the sedimented philosophy of an epoch as ordinary people carry it: a layered deposit of religious teaching, proverbial wisdom, schoolroom fragments, half-digested science, and the practical lore of a trade, held incoherently and defended as obvious. It is formed slowly, in places with addresses. The analyst inventories those places for the case in hand: the parish and the pulpit, the elementary school and its textbooks, the newspaper's habits of selection, the professional association's code, the barracks, the union hall, the broadcasting studio of the period, and in every period the family's table, where the deposit is first laid down. The question is never whether a formation machine exists, since one always does, but whose it is, how often it convenes its audience, and what it would cost a rival to build an alternative that meets as weekly and lasts as long. This is the point at which the analysis of consent becomes an analysis of institutions rather than of messages, and it is also the point at which most commentary quietly gives up, because institutions have archives and messages have only clippings. The companion treatment of how such machinery appears in the press is developed in [ideology and news media](/2012/04/15/ideology-and-news-media/); here the formation question is asked of every site, journalistic and otherwise, with the same demand for names and schedules.

Gramsci's standing illustration was the Catholic Church in Italy, which he studied with an admiration for its organisational intelligence that scandalised readers who expected a materialist to sneer. The Church, as the notebooks present it, operated the deepest formation machine in the country: a parish in every village, a priest formed by a centuries-old educational system, confraternities and orders organising lay life by trade and by devotion, a press, schools, and a calendar that structured the year of people who never opened a political pamphlet. Crucially, Gramsci attended to the machine's internal discipline, its long management of the gap between the intellectuals of the faith and the simple believers, a gap the Church worked to keep bridged by ritual and repetition rather than allowing it to split into two religions. The strategic moral he drew was unsentimental. A rival formation that met its audience at election time, with leaflets, was competing against an institution that met its audience at baptism, marriage, and burial, and the war of position would be measured in the building of comparable depth. Analysts who invoke his name for media criticism alone have usually skipped this question's real content, which is not that messages matter but that formation is an institutional achievement with running costs, staffing, and time horizons that dwarf a campaign.

The characteristic failure at this step is the editorial fallacy: reading the newspapers of a period and reporting their line as the common sense of the population. Editorials are evidence about the intellectuals who write them; common sense is evidenced by what sermons assume, what textbooks repeat without argument, what proverbs settle, what juries and foremen and school inspectors take for granted in the conduct of ordinary business. The second error is treating formation as a one-way injection, when the notebooks insist on contradictory consciousness: the same worker carries the deposit of the formation machine and a practical knowledge, learned in activity, that contradicts it, and political change begins in the friction between the two. The analyst's task is to map both layers and their points of friction, since a formation site that merely repeats the official deposit while daily experience contradicts it is an institution in quiet decay, however imposing its façade. The third question, answered with that care, tells the analyst where the settlement's real defences are dug, which is the information the fourth question needs.

### The fourth question: what terrain must a challenger contest?

The fourth question asks what terrain a counter-hegemonic project would have to contest, and it translates the first three answers into a map. The terrain is civil society itself: the ensemble of institutions standing between the family and the state, in which consent is organised and in which, on Gramsci's account of the West, the decisive struggle is positional rather than frontal. The strategic vocabulary of siege and assault, of trenches held and trenches to be dug, belongs to [the companion treatment of the two wars](/2015/02/15/war-of-position-and-manoeuvre/), and to [the analysis of civil society as a terrain](/2015/03/15/gramsci-on-civil-society/); in the procedure, the fourth question performs a narrower service. It requires the analyst to state, for the case in hand, which institutions a challenger must enter, build, or neutralise before a change of government could become a change of order, and which it may leave alone. A new ministry inherits the schools, the courts' habits, the professional bodies, the press structure, and the common sense those institutions keep in repair; the fourth question asks whether the inheritance would be an asset or a garrison of the old bloc. Where the answer is garrison, the analysis predicts the fate of purely electoral victories with a precision commentary rarely achieves: the government changes, the order persists, and the victors discover that they administer a settlement priced by their predecessors.

The classical illustration is the associational world of German Social Democracy before the First World War, the most complete counter-society a subordinate movement ever built inside a hostile order. Around the party and its unions grew a dense fabric of singing societies, cycling clubs, gymnastic associations, lending libraries, consumer cooperatives, women's and youth organisations, a daily press, and a calendar of festivals, until a worker could pass from cradle to grave inside institutions of the movement's own making. Whatever verdict history passes on that movement's ends, the fourth question reads its means as the war of position rendered visible: trenches dug in civil society, staffed weekly, forming a common sense alternative to the imperial deposit. The case also teaches the question's necessary second half, because terrain held is not terrain used. The associational fortress became, in the judgement of many of its own historians, an end in itself, a world so complete that its defence replaced the contest for the wider society, and the trenches sheltered an administration rather than an advance. The analyst must therefore grade not only the depth of a challenger's institutions but their orientation, whether they face outward toward the unorganised or inward toward the comfort of the already convinced. A counter-society that talks only to itself has answered the fourth question in the negative while appearing to ace it, and the distinction between a fortress and a waiting room is one the method exists to draw.

What goes wrong most reliably at this step is constitutionalism of the imagination: assuming that the terrain is defined by the formal distribution of powers, so that winning the offices named in the constitution wins the order. The second error is its romantic opposite, treating every institution as equally contestable and every contest as equally urgent, when the first three answers usually identify two or three sites where the settlement is actually renewed, a school system, a church, a press structure, a profession's licensing body, and where a challenger's resources purchase disproportionate effect. The fourth question, done properly, is an exercise in strategic accounting. It prices the entry costs of the terrain, distinguishes the load-bearing institutions from the decorative ones, and records the uncomfortable finding, when it comes, that the challenger has been contesting the decorative sites for a generation because they were the sites that admitted it cheaply.

### The fifth question: who does the organising?

The fifth question asks which organising functions each side has secured, and it returns the analysis from institutions to the people who make institutions cohere. Gramsci's category for the answer is the organic intellectual: not the celebrated thinker but the stratum every social group creates to organise its activity, elaborate its self-understanding, and connect it to allies, the managers and technicians of a rising industry, the clergy of a church, the permanent officials of a union, the teachers of a school system, the journalists of a movement press. The function is identified by work, not by fame or by doctrine. Who recruits, who trains, who writes the circulars and keeps the minutes, who translates the leading group's programme into the idiom of each ally, who maintains the formation sites the third question inventoried? The analyst grades both sides on the same schedule, and the grading is comparative by design: a bloc whose intellectuals are numerous, rooted, and rising from the groups they organise confronts, in the favourable case for the challenger, a stratum of traditional intellectuals administering an inherited order with diminishing conviction. The fuller classification of the function, and its test, is the business of [the guide to organic intellectuals](/2015/03/01/organic-intellectuals-explained/); the procedure needs only the working rule that organisation is a staffed activity and that the staffing can be counted.

The illustration is Turin in the years of the factory councils, the episode from which Gramsci's whole strategic vocabulary grew. In the engineering works of the city, the internal commissions of the war years were transformed, under the pressure of the shop floor and the theorising of the young journalists around the weekly L'Ordine Nuovo, into councils that aspired to organise production itself, and the transformation was carried by a new kind of functionary: the commissar elected by the work team, the technician who sided with the council, the journalist who served as the movement's collective memory. Against them stood the industrialists' own organic intellectuals, the engineers, works managers, and organisers of the employers' federation, who understood the contest in the same functional terms and fought it over the allegiance of the intermediate strata of the factory. The socialist party of the day, by contrast, disposed of celebrated orators and a parliamentary fraction while leaving the council movement's organisers undertrained, unconnected, and finally unsupported, a distribution of functions that the fifth question records as a verdict in advance. When the occupation of the factories came in the autumn of nineteen twenty, the councils possessed the factories and lacked almost everything the five questions enumerate: no national bloc, no priced concessions to offer the peasantry or the middle strata, formation sites confined to one city's industry, and intellectuals whose authority stopped at the factory gate. The defeat that followed was, in Gramsci's later analysis, legible in the staffing table before it was enacted in the streets.

The standing temptation at this step is the celebrity substitution, counting a side's famous sympathisers as its intellectuals when the function is performed, or left unperformed, by organisers nobody interviews. The second error is asymmetry of curiosity: cataloguing the opponent's machinery in detail while treating one's own side's staffing as a mystery of enthusiasm. The method permits neither. It also forbids a comforting inference, that securing the functions guarantees the outcome, since the councils' commissars performed their function superbly inside a formation that failed the other four questions. The five questions are a set, and their joint verdict is the analysis. A side can win the fifth and lose the rest, and the analyst's duty is to record the whole score, which is the last discipline the procedure imposes before it is applied to a case entire.

## The five-question analysis

The procedure is gathered here into the artifact this article is built around, in a form a reader can lift onto a card or into a notebook. The five-question analysis sets each question beside what the analyst looks for, the evidence that would answer it, the commonest error at that step, and a worked historical illustration drawn from the sections above. Its purpose is portability with the conditions attached: a table is the most borrowable of formats, and this one is designed so that borrowing it means borrowing the discipline, since every row carries its own defeater in the error column and its own standard of proof in the evidence column.

| Question | What to look for | Evidence that answers it | The commonest error | Worked historical illustration |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Which alliance rules? | The composition of the historic bloc and the terms of its settlement | Fiscal flows among allies, recruitment between groups, a programme phrasing each ally's demand as national | Naming the loudest group as the ruling power | The Piedmontese Moderates, who led their rivals and excluded the southern peasantry |
| What does consent cost? | Concessions the leading group sacrifices real interest to maintain | Budgets, statutes, settlements, and the dates of their withdrawal | Auditing announcements as if they were appropriations | Bismarck's insurance statutes, offered against the Social Democrats and banked as rights |
| Where is common sense formed? | The institutions that teach what goes without saying, and their schedules | Sermons, textbooks, proverbs, professional codes, the assumptions of ordinary business | Reading editorials and reporting them as a population's mind | The Italian parish network, meeting its audience at baptism, marriage, and burial |
| What terrain must be contested? | The load-bearing institutions of civil society and their entry costs | Institutional histories, staffing, the fate of governments that changed without changing the order | Treating the constitution's offices as the whole field | The Social Democratic counter-society, a fortress that settled into a waiting room |
| Who does the organising? | The strata performing the organising function for each side | Career paths, circulars, training systems, the personnel of formation sites | Counting famous sympathisers instead of working organisers | The Turin council commissars, authoritative at the gate and unconnected beyond it |

The rows repay reading as a single argument rather than as five separate tips. The sequence embodies the method's causal order: the bloc defines whose consent is at stake, the concessions define what that consent costs, the formation sites define where the settlement is renewed, the terrain defines where a challenge must be built, and the functions define whether anyone is available to build it. Break the sequence and the errors in the fourth column arrive on schedule, because each error is precisely the mistake an analyst makes when working without the previous rows: the loudest group is mistaken for the bloc when fiscal flows go unread, editorials are mistaken for common sense when formation sites go unvisited, and celebrities are mistaken for organisers when functions go uncounted. The illustrations column does quieter work. Each illustration is a case in which the question's answer surprised the participants, a bloc resting on an exclusion, a concession banked against its giver, a fortress that became a waiting room, organisers whose authority stopped at a gate, and the surprises are the point: a procedure that never surprises its user is a mirror, and the five questions are designed to be a window.

Two cautions govern the artifact's use. The first is that the table grades analyses, not outcomes: a formation can pass all five questions and still be defeated by war, famine, or its own errors of timing, because the procedure audits how consent is organised, and causation has other provinces besides. The second is that the table is symmetrical in application and must stay so. An analyst who runs the rows over opponents and exempts allies has converted a method into a weapon of description, and the conversion shows in the grades, which inflate exactly where sympathy begins. The strongest internal check the procedure possesses is the habit of answering each question for the analyst's own side first, on the record, before turning the instrument outward. The cases that follow, an Italian failure analysed entire and the most influential English application of the framework, are both written under that rule: the grading is the same whether the formation examined is one the century admired or one it buried.

A word about the artifact's physical form, since portability is its argument. The table is designed to be copied entire, onto a card, into a notebook, or into the front of a case file, and to survive the copying with its discipline intact, which is why each row carries its defeater in the fourth column rather than leaving the warnings to a preface that copying would shed. An analyst who keeps the card in use develops a reflex the vocabulary alone never teaches: confronted with a claim about an order, a movement, or a ministry, the hand goes to the rows in order, and the claim is either furnished with its evidence or marked with the row at which it stops. The reflex matters more than any single finding, because the literature this article has surveyed is, at bottom, a record of claims that stopped at different rows and declined to say so. The textbook case stopped at the first, the journalistic case usually stops before the second, the strategist's memorandum is precious because it does not stop. Grading, performed with the card, is not a ceremony of approval or disapproval. It is the simple, repeatable act of recording where the evidence ran out, and the record, kept over years and across cases, becomes a map of the borrower's seriousness that no citation count can counterfeit.

## The integral state: the strategist's map of the whole

Underlying the procedure is a picture of the state that the strategist must hold whole, because each question surveys one face of it. The liberal imagination of Gramsci's century divided the world into the state, an apparatus of coercion and administration, and society, the free remainder where opinion formed itself. The notebooks dissolve the division. In the formula he returned to, the state in its integral sense is political society and civil society together, dictatorship and hegemony in one construction: the ministries and the police, certainly, and equally the schools that certify the settlement's common sense, the churches that bless it, the press that narrates it, the associations that rehearse it, protected by the armour of coercion that stands behind the whole arrangement for those who refuse it. The strategic consequences are immediate, and they explain why the procedure's questions range where they do. If the state were only the apparatus, strategy would be a science of seizing or staffing the apparatus, and every election or coup would be a change of order. In the integral picture, the apparatus is the order's smallest and most replaceable part, and a change of ministry that leaves the civil half of the state untouched has changed the guard on a fortress whose garrison, stores, and maps belong to the old settlement. Hence the fourth question's insistence on pricing the inheritance, and the second's insistence that the settlement, not the cabinet, is the unit that pays and receives consent.

The integral picture also supplies the method's account of the state as educator, the aspect of the construction that purely coercive theories of rule cannot see. Every state, in the notebooks' analysis, conducts a vast pedagogy: its laws teach what may be done and what will be punished into habit, its schools and courts and licensing bodies distribute prestige among ways of life, its ceremonies rehearse the citizen in the settlement's story, and its silences mark the questions that are not to be asked in public. The pedagogy is not propaganda in the crude sense, since most of it is not believed the less for being compulsory and most of its teachers are unaware of teaching; it is the formation of common sense by the arrangement of incentives and dignities across a lifetime. A strategist who grasps the pedagogy reads a school curriculum, a honours list, a planning inquiry, and a tax schedule as documents of the same order of fact as a budget of the ministry of war, and the third question's directory, sermons and textbooks and professional codes, is simply the pedagogy's staff list. The challenger, correspondingly, is in the business of a counter-pedagogy long before it is in the business of government, which is why the notebooks treat the building of schools, journals, and associations as strategy rather than as culture, and why the metapolitical borrowers, who took that single insight and built a doctrine on it, were reading a real page of the source even as they left the ledger behind.

One caution completes the map. The integral state is a construction of the analysis, not a conspiracy of the world: its two halves are not coordinated by a single director, its teachers contradict each other, its concessions are fought over by the allied groups themselves, and its common sense is a deposit of centuries rather than a script of the season. The analyst who forgets this turns the method into the thing the objection accuses it of being, a story in which everything fits. The fortresses, to return to the founding image, were built at different times by different engineers for different wars, and their alignment into a system is the analyst's finding, to be evidenced fortress by fortress, not the assumption with which the audit begins. The five questions enforce the caution mechanically, since each demands its own evidence and returns its own verdict, and a map drawn from five separate surveys can be wrong in one region without being fantasy in all. That is the map's final recommendation to the strategist who inherits it: the whole is real, and it is knowable only in parts, and the patience to survey the parts is the difference between the integral state's analyst and its mythologist.

## The incumbent's replies: passive revolution and trasformismo

A procedure for challengers is only half a strategy literature, and the notebooks are equally exact about the incumbent's counter-moves, the techniques by which a ruling bloc answers the five questions defensively. Two of them belong in every application, because any analysis that omits them will mistake a managed transformation for a spontaneous one. The first is trasformismo, the absorption of the opposition's personnel into the ruling bloc's own ranks, decapitating a challenger by promotion. Gramsci anatomised it in the Risorgimento's aftermath, where the historic parties dissolved into a single ministerial continuum by the steady recruitment of opposition deputies into office, and he extended the analysis to the molecular form it takes in mass politics: the co-optation of a subordinate movement's intellectuals, offered careers, honours, and administrative posts at the price of their organising function. The fifth question is the detector. A challenger whose ablest organisers keep disappearing into the incumbent's apparatus is not being persecuted, which would strengthen it, but digested, and the audit records the digestion in the staffing table of both sides at once, the incumbent's fifth answer improving exactly as the challenger's decays. The technique's elegance is that it presents as generosity and operates as disarmament, and its tell is the direction of travel: personnel move from the challenger's formation sites into the incumbent's, and almost never back.

The second reply is grander and slower. Passive revolution names a transformation of the social order carried out from above, in molecular doses, precisely in order to prevent a transformation carried out from below by a hegemonic subject. The ruling bloc concedes the substance of selected demands while withholding the form: the reform is granted, the movement that demanded it is denied authorship, organisation, and momentum, and the concession arrives administered by the state rather than won by the ally. Bismarck's insurance settlement, audited earlier as a purchase that failed, can be re-read in this register as the strategy's classic attempt, and its failure is the category's most instructive datum, since the recipients' own organisations survived to interpret the gift against the giver, which is what the strategy exists to preclude. Where it succeeds, the analyst watches a society change considerably while its relations of leadership change barely at all: industries modernised, franchises extended, services built, and the bloc's composition quietly recomposed around the same leading group. The second question, which prices concessions, needs the category to avoid a systematic error, since a ledger full of concessions looks like consent being purchased when it may be consent being retired, the demands bought off one by one before they can be assembled into an alternative programme. The distinguishing test is authorship. A concession that strengthens the recipients' own institutions belongs to a settlement; a concession designed to bypass and dissolve them belongs to a passive revolution, and the two entries sit in the same column of the ledger with opposite signs.

Both replies teach the same lesson about the framework's temper, and it is worth stating before the cases resume. The method does not divide the world into a creative challenger and a merely defensive incumbent. It credits the incumbent with strategy, often with the more patient strategy, and it grades the Risorgimento's Moderates, the Bismarckian state, and the postwar settlement's managers as practitioners whose work the challenger must understand to answer. This even handedness is not neutrality of allegiance; Gramsci's allegiance is on every page of the notebooks and in the biography that produced them. It is neutrality of audit, the refusal to grade one side by its intentions and the other by its results, and it is the quality that made the framework available, in the end, to incumbents and challengers of every later allegiance. The survey was drawn by a man surveying the fortress that held him. Its accuracy about fortresses did not depend on his side of the wall, and neither, the five questions insist, does its use.

## How the analyst knows consent is real

The audit's credibility rests on a question too often skipped: consent is an interior condition attributed to millions, and the analyst who claims to detect it owes the reader the instruments. The notebooks and the applications together suggest four, each defeasible, and together strong. The first instrument is participation. Consent, being active, leaves behavioural traces: subordinate groups staff the settlement's institutions, volunteer for its organisations, litigate in its courts, send their children through its schools with expectations rather than under compulsion alone, and defend the arrangement, in the pubs and the parish halls where common sense is audited nightly, against its critics in arguments they have made their own. The analyst counts what the led build and maintain for the order, since a settlement nobody maintains is being endured, not consented to. The second instrument is the phrasing of demands. Within a hegemonic order, subordinate groups phrase their grievances as claims on the settlement, in its vocabulary, addressed to its promises: the wage claim cites the national interest, the tenant cites the programme, the excluded group demands admission on the order's own stated terms. That phrasing is the settlement's deepest success, because it means even protest reproduces the bloc's common sense. When demands begin to be phrased against the order's vocabulary itself, in a rival idiom with rival institutions behind it, the analyst is watching consent being withdrawn in draft, and the third question's map will show where the rival idiom is being taught.

The third instrument is the defection schedule of the intellectuals. Strata performing the organising function are the settlement's seismographs, positioned at the joints between the leading group and the led, and their movements register strain before the led themselves move. An order whose teachers, priests, journalists, and local organisers begin, in ones and twos and then in cohorts, to elaborate a different account of the world is an order whose formation machinery has started to run in reverse, and the analyst who tracks careers and journals catches the organic crisis in its larval stage, years before it reaches the streets. The Italian case is the type specimen: the liberal settlement's intellectuals were defecting in print for a decade before the squads gave the defection an army. The fourth instrument is the negative one, and it disciplines the other three. Consent is also inferred from the coercive budget an order does not need. Where the armour is everywhere and always in use, where the police are the formation site that meets the population most often, the analyst records domination and closes the question, whatever the participation figures say, since participation organised at gunpoint is the armour's achievement and not consent's. The four instruments together give the second question its teeth: consent is real where participation is uncompelled, demands are phrased as claims, the intellectuals elaborate the settlement as their own work, and the armour is mostly in its barracks. An order meeting fewer tests is graded down the scale the Caesarism section completed, toward arbitration and toward rule by the ditch alone.

## A failure analysed entire: Italy after the Great War

The five questions earn their keep on a case where the answers are mostly negative, and Italy in the four years after the Great War is the case Gramsci himself never stopped reworking. The ruling formation was a liberal order in dissolution. Its historic bloc, assembled in the nineteenth century around Piedmontese administration, northern industry, and southern landownership, had been held together by trasformismo, the absorption of opposition leaders into ministerial majorities, and the war broke the mechanism: mass conscription had politicised the peasantry the bloc excluded, the industrialists had grown on war contracts into a power the liberal notables could not digest, and the returning soldiers carried a common sense the old formation sites had not made. On the first question, the analyst records a bloc whose settlement terms had expired and whose leading group, the liberal parliamentary stratum around Giolitti, retained the techniques of absorption after losing the material basis that made absorption possible. A bloc in that condition does not rule; it administers a vacancy, and the vacancy is the case's central fact.

The concessions audit, the second question, reads the vacancy's price list. The liberal state's answer to the postwar turbulence was a sequence of real concessions offered late and under pressure: the eight-hour day acknowledged, union recognition extended, and in the September of nineteen twenty, with the factories of the north occupied, a settlement that conceded wage gains and promised a law on workers' control that was never delivered in substance. Giolitti's handling of the occupation was tactically brilliant and strategically diagnostic. By refusing repression and letting the occupation exhaust itself against the problem of running factories without markets or credit, he demonstrated that the councils could take the terrain of production and still not hold the country, and the promised control law evaporated once the emergency passed. The second question's ledger therefore shows concessions functioning as a solvent rather than a settlement: granted to end a crisis, withdrawn when it ended, and priced by their recipients accordingly. Consent purchased on those terms is a wasting asset, and the workers who banked the wage gains while discounting the promises were performing the audit themselves.

The third and fourth questions map where the contest was actually decided. Common sense in postwar Italy was being formed in sites the old order did not control and the challengers did not build: the veterans' associations, where the grievances of the trenches were elaborated into a nationalism of the mutilated victory; the Catholic formations reorganising after the war, with a new popular party carrying the parish network into politics; the interventionist press, which had campaigned for the war and now prosecuted the peace; and, from nineteen twenty onward, the fascist squads, which functioned among other things as a travelling formation machine, teaching the rural and small town strata a new account of who had betrayed Italy and what order required. Against that map, the socialist movement's formation sites were its newspapers, its chambers of labour, and the councils of one industrial region, profound where they existed and absent across most of the peninsula. The terrain question then answers itself in the negative. The councils were a trench system dug in a single province of civil society, the factory, while the schools, the courts, the prefectures, the officer corps, and the countryside's institutions remained in other hands, and a change of government in Rome, had it come, would have inherited a garrisoned state. The movement's own strategists who dreamed of the frontal assault mistook the map; those who counseled waiting mistook it in the opposite direction, treating terrain they had not built as terrain that time would build for them.

The fifth question completes the score, and with it the analysis reaches its verdict without a single adjective of condemnation or excuse. On one side, the rising fascist movement was acquiring organic intellectuals at every level the method counts: rural schoolteachers, pharmacists, stationmasters, and small landowners elaborating a local leadership in the provinces; journalists and veterans' organisers connecting the squads to national industry and finance; and a leadership that understood, in practice, the function of the organiser as Gramsci was defining it in theory. On the other side, the socialist movement disposed of a famous parliamentary stratum, a maximalist rhetoric that performed the organising function in words, and the council commissars whose authority, as the fifth question's illustration recorded, stopped at the factory gate. The party neither trained nor connected them, and its intellectuals debated the revolution's timetable while the provinces were staffed against them. When the organic crisis resolved itself in the March on Rome and the appointment of a fascist government, the outcome had been legible in the five answers for two years: an expired bloc, concessions spent as solvent, formation sites lost by default, terrain unbuilt, and functions performed by the other side. That legibility, after the fact, is the method's credential. It does not prophesy; it audits, and an audit that can reconstruct a catastrophe from staffing tables and statute books is worth more to strategy than a vocabulary that can only deplore it.

## The prototype application: an essay on the Southern Question

The procedure was not invented for this article. Its prototype is an unfinished essay of nineteen twenty-six, written in the weeks before its author's arrest, on the Southern Question in Italy, and the essay deserves a section because it shows the five questions operating before the vocabulary existed to name them, in the service of the most practical problem his movement faced. The problem was an alliance that had never been built. The industrial workers of the northern cities and the peasants of the southern countryside were, in the analysis of the day, natural allies against the bloc that ruled them both; in practice they regarded each other across a wall of contempt that the ruling bloc maintained as carefully as a fortification. Northern workers absorbed from the dominant common sense a picture of the south as a biological ballast of idleness and crime; southern peasants experienced the northern state, and its socialist organisations by extension, as the tax collector and the recruiting sergeant. Gramsci's essay performs the audit on that failure. The bloc that ruled Italy rested on the division: southern landowners and the stratum of rural intellectuals who administered the peasantry's world were bound to northern industry in a settlement whose price was the south's arrested development, and the peasantry's consent to that settlement was organised through the very intellectuals, priests, lawyers, teachers, notables, who mediated every contact between the village and the wider world.

From the audit, the essay draws the counter-specification with a concreteness the later invocations of counter-hegemony rarely match. The northern workers' movement could lead only if it made the southern peasant's demands its own, land and the breaking of the rural bloc, and paid for the alliance in the coin the second question specifies: by surrendering the corporate comforts of the northern labour aristocracy, its protectionist reflexes and its contempt, which were the movement's own concessions to itself. The alliance had an organising condition as well, stated in the essay's most practical pages: the movement needed southern intellectuals of a new type, formed from the peasantry's own ranks and bound to it, to replace the mediating stratum that delivered the village to its rulers, and it needed them in numbers and over years. And the essay identifies the rival formation machines with the respect the third question teaches, the Church above all, whose southern clergy organised the peasant's cosmos and whose political weight any alliance would have to reckon as terrain rather than as superstition. Read as method, the essay is the five questions in their first employment: a bloc dissected by its settlements, an alliance priced, a formation map drawn, a terrain specified, a staffing requirement named. Read as history, it is also the record of a specification never executed, interrupted by the arrests of that autumn and overtaken by the catastrophe the Italian case above audits. The strategist's Gramsci begins in that essay because the essay already contains the article's whole argument in embryo: an analysis built as a procedure, portable because it is a procedure, and addressed to whoever will pay its costs in evidence and in time.

## The bloc's cultural condition: the national popular

Between the prototype essay and the procedure stands one more category the strategist must be able to operate, because the first question's answer is incomplete without it. A bloc holds materially, by settlements and prices; it holds durably only when the leading group's programme becomes the nation's common sense, when its particular interest is experienced by the allied groups, and beyond them, as the general interest of the people as a whole. Gramsci's name for the achievement is the national popular, and his standing example of its attainment is not from his own tradition. The Jacobins of the French Revolution, in his reading, made the bourgeoisie national by making its programme the peasantry's war: they bound the countryside to the revolution with land and with the levée, spoke in the idiom of the nation rather than of the counting house, and presented the class's interest as the people's destiny with a completeness that allowed them, in his judgement, to bury the old order rather than merely to succeed it. The Italian Moderates are the counter-case that gives the category its edge. They achieved unification without ever becoming national popular: no agrarian reform bound the southern peasant to the new state, no popular literature or school fused the nation's culture with the settlement, and the resulting state remained, in the phrase the notebooks return to, a thing of the caste rather than of the people, its blocs assembled in parliament because they could not be assembled in the country.

For the analyst, the national popular converts the first and third questions into a single test with a literary instrument, since the condition is legible in a society's culture before it is legible in its statutes. A formation approaching the condition produces the works in which the nation recognises itself: its school texts, its popular histories, its songs and serials carry the settlement's idiom as the obvious one, and the allied groups find their own experience dignified inside the leading group's story. A formation failing the condition exhibits the opposite symptom, a split between the official culture and the popular deposit, the intellectuals' Italy and the peasants' Italy glaring at each other across the notebooks' most famous divide. The strategist's use of the test is unsentimental in both directions. For the incumbent, the national popular is the audit of whether the settlement has become a civilisation or remains an arrangement. For the challenger, it prices the cultural work in the only currency that matters, the production of intellectuals and works that let the allied groups see themselves inside the alternative, without which the bloc in embryo remains a negotiating committee with a programme. Hall's analysis leaned on exactly this instrument: the project he audited was winning, in his account, because it was succeeding at the national popular operation, redefining what the nation was and who belonged to its story, while its opponents defended a settlement in the idiom of administration. The term's presence in an application is therefore one of the better quick grades of the borrowing's depth. A writer who uses hegemony alone may have imported a synonym. A writer who asks whether the bloc has become national popular has imported a research programme.

## The founding comparison: East and West

Behind the procedure stands a single comparison, made in the notebooks' most quoted strategic passage, from which the fourth question and the whole doctrine of the terrain descend. Gramsci contrasted the Russia of nineteen seventeen with the advanced West. In the East, as he put it, the state was everything and civil society was primordial and gelatinous: a formation in which the apparatus of rule stood nearly alone, without the dense undergrowth of institutions that in other societies absorbs shocks, so that a determined assault on the state could bring the whole structure down with it. In the West, the state was only an outer ditch, behind which stood a powerful system of fortresses and earthworks: churches, schools, associations, professions, press, the whole architecture of consent described in the third question, which meant that a crisis of the government, even a collapse of the government, left the order's defences substantially intact and its common sense still garrisoned. The strategic conclusion he drew reorganised his tradition's expectations. Where the ditch is the whole fortification, the war of manoeuvre, the frontal assault, is the rational form. Where the fortresses stand, the war of position is the only form that answers to the facts, and it must be waged with the patience the metaphor prescribes, position by position, in the institutions.

The comparison matters to the analyst for three reasons beyond its historical influence. First, it is the method's charter of comparative humility: the correct strategy is a function of the terrain's density, which varies by country and by epoch, so that no national experience, including the Russian one that dominated his movement's imagination, legislates for the others. The five questions are the comparison operationalised, since the fourth question's answer for any case is a measurement of exactly the density the passage describes, and the analyst who skips the measurement and imports a strategy has repeated, with better footnotes, the error the passage was written against. Second, the comparison is double edged in a way borrowers often flatten. The gelatinous East of the passage is not praised for its availability to revolution; the passage's author spent the same years analysing how a state that is everything becomes a state whose fall delivers society to whoever holds the armour, and the notebooks' accounts of Caesarism and of bureaucracy are the East's shadow in his thought. Third, the comparison is dated in its geography and exact in its logic, and the analyst must separate the two. The map of which societies have fortresses has been redrawn repeatedly since, not least by the transformations the terrain section of this article describes, but the instruction to count the fortresses before choosing the form of the contest has not aged at all. It is, in the end, the availability problem's mirror: the method travels everywhere precisely because its first command is to assume that everywhere is different, and to do the local audit before borrowing even from the method's own past successes.

## The English application: a realignment read as a hegemonic project

The most influential application of the framework in English was produced at the Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies in Birmingham, in the work of Stuart Hall and his collaborators, and it remains the model of how the procedure is run on a living conjuncture while keeping the analysis a method rather than a verdict. Its foundation was a collective study of a moral panic, Policing the Crisis, published in nineteen seventy-eight, which examined how the label of mugging, imported from American coverage, was assembled by police, courts, and press into a sign of general social breakdown. The book's Gramscian move was to refuse the panic as a topic and to treat it as evidence: a society in an organic crisis of its postwar settlement, the authors argued, was rehearsing consent for a more coercive order, learning to experience intensified policing as common sense before any government had campaigned on it. The formation sites were named, the sediment they worked on was the common sense of race and crime laid down over decades, and the analysis was published before the election it implicitly concerned, which is what an application of the method ought to risk.

The essay that carried the argument into strategy was The Great Moving Right Show, published in the British party's theoretical journal in January of nineteen seventy-nine, weeks before the government it analysed took office. Hall's claim was that the incoming British project, which he named Thatcherism after its leader, was not an electoral swing to be waited out but a hegemonic project under construction: an attempt to assemble a new bloc by connecting popular discontents, resentment of bureaucracy, hostility to organised labour's privileges, anxieties about crime and national decline, to a programme that would reprice the postwar settlement. The term he coined for the project's form of rule was authoritarian populism, defined by the joining of the two halves that liberal commentary kept apart: a strengthening of the coercive, disciplinary state, and a genuine popular consent actively organised from below, in which real grievances were answered in a particular direction rather than merely manipulated. The definition matters to this article because it is the concessions condition's mirror image worked in practice. Hall insisted that the project's appeal answered something real, in housing queues, in the experience of unaccountable bureaucracies, in the resentments of the respectable poor, and that an opposition treating the appeal as false consciousness would learn nothing and lose accordingly. Describing a strategy as Gramscian identifies a method rather than a lineage: Hall, a thinker of the left analysing a project of the right, demonstrates the availability problem in his own practice, running an imprisoned communist's questions over a formation that communist's party would have opposed at every point.

### What did Hall add to the framework he borrowed?

Three things. He made conjuncture the unit of analysis, the specific balance of forces in a moment rather than an epoch in general. He treated race and nation as constitutive of blocs, not decoration on class. And he insisted hegemony is never finished, only ever being won, contested, and lost.

Hall's borrowings were not costless, and the scholarly exchange that followed is part of the application's value, since it shows the method being graded by its own standards. Critics within the same broad tradition, most systematically a group of political economists writing in the following decade, argued that the account overstated the project's coherence and understated its improvisations, treating as a designed hegemonic strategy what was partly a sequence of expedients fortunate in their enemies. The objection is the first question turned against the analyst: a bloc must be evidenced by settlements and recruitments, and a project that exists mainly in its opponents' reconstruction has failed the audit its own method prescribes. Hall's later work on the theme conceded the improvisation while holding the central claim, that the settlement of the postwar years was being dismantled and a new common sense installed in its place, and the exchange as a whole illustrates the healthiest feature of the framework's migration: applied seriously, it supplies the terms in which its own applications are corrected. Readers following the adoption story should also register what Hall did not take. The economic nucleus remained in his work, the crisis of a particular model of accumulation, and the organising question was posed to his own side as a reproach, the absence of a counter-project with institutions of its own being the essay's practical burden. The borrowing, graded by the five questions, was substantive: the conditions travelled, including the condition that embarrassed the borrower's allies.

## Authoritarian populism in depth

The term Hall coined deserves a treatment of its own, because it is the most successful single export of the English application and the one whose career best displays both the method's yield and its transit losses. Authoritarian populism names a form of rule with a specific internal structure. A governing project constructs a popular constituency, the people, defined against an establishment held responsible for the nation's troubles, and it wins that constituency's active consent for a programme that includes, as its centrepiece, an expansion of the coercive apparatus: more policing, harsher sentencing, tighter discipline in schools and workplaces, a state that promises order in the idiom of common sense. The construction is populist because the consent is real and must be organised, articulated out of lived grievances about crime, taxation, bureaucracy, and decline, and it is authoritarian because the grievances are answered by strengthening the armour rather than by extending the settlement. The concept's discipline lies in the joining. Commentary of the period separated the halves, treating the law and order drive as manipulation imposed from above or the popular mood as a spontaneous weather from below; Hall's term insists that the phenomenon is the circuit between them, consent manufactured for coercion with the active collaboration of the coerced, and that an opposition which addresses only one half, defending civil liberties to an audience pricing its safety, or denouncing manipulation to an audience experiencing its grievances as real, will lose to the circuit every time.

The term travelled, as successful terms do, and its itinerary rehearses the transit losses in miniature. Analysts of other countries' law and order politics in the nineteen eighties borrowed it to name similar circuits, sometimes with the audit attached and sometimes with only the cadence. In looser usage it decayed into a synonym for any politics the writer found both popular and illiberal, at which point it stopped discriminating, since the concept's whole content is the mechanism joining consent to coercion, and a use that cannot specify the mechanism has kept the label and dropped the analysis. The grading rule from the artifact applies without alteration: an application of authoritarian populism that names the grievances being articulated, the settlement being repriced, and the formation sites carrying the construction has imported the method; one that applies the phrase as a verdict on a crowd has imported the letterhead. There is a further lesson in the term's origin that the availability argument should not waste. Hall coined it to analyse a project he opposed, and he built it from the notebooks of a thinker whose party that project opposed in turn, and the double crossing is not an irony to be savoured but the normal condition of strategic knowledge since the notebooks appeared. The survey serves every army. What distinguishes the surveyor's heirs from the slogan's repeaters is not allegiance. It is whether the conditions survived the journey into the new hands, and in Hall's coinage, unusually, they did.

## The culture war: a borrowed battlefield

Few phrases in the vicinity cause more confusion than culture war, and a strategy article owes its readers the genealogy, because the phrase and the war of position are constantly mistaken for each other. The words descend from the German Kulturkampf, the struggle of the eighteen seventies in which Bismarck's empire legislated against the Catholic Church's place in schools, pulpits, and civil life, and lost, in the sense that the Church's formation machine emerged with its constituency mobilised and its institutions deepened by persecution. The episode is a Gramscian parable before the fact: a state with the armour entirely in its hands assaulted a formation apparatus frontally, discovered the fortresses, and retired. The phrase migrated to the United States in a sociological study of nineteen ninety-one, which used it for the alignment of cultural orthodoxies across denominations, and from there into the general vocabulary of political combat, where it names the condition in which the symbolic questions, schooling, worship, family form, national memory, are experienced as the war itself rather than as one terrain of it.

The distinction from the war of position is the analytical point. In Gramsci's construction, the cultural terrain is decisive because consent is organised there, but the war is fought over a settlement whose centre of gravity remains the relations among classes and the concessions that bind them; culture is the medium, not the stakes entire. In the culture war as popularly prosecuted, the symbolic questions are the stakes entire, and the ledger is never opened, because the combatants' grievance is recognition rather than distribution. The two can coincide in a case, and the analyst's task is precisely to grade the coincidence: where a symbolic campaign is the formation work of a bloc repricing a settlement, the five questions run over it profitably; where it is a self-contained mobilisation that leaves every budget untouched, describing it in Gramsci's vocabulary flatters it with a depth it has declined to purchase. The forum version of the question, whether the culture war is Gramscian, therefore receives a split answer. The terrain is his discovery's home ground, and any politics that fights for the premises of an age is operating, knowingly or not, on the map the notebooks drew. The war becomes Gramscian in the strict sense only when its cultural offensives are audited as the consent work of a bloc with concessions priced and organisers staffed, and by that standard most of what flies the culture war flag is a skirmish advertising itself as a siege. The correction cuts in every direction, which is how the reader can tell it is a correction and not a side.

## The adoption from the right: metapolitics

On the other side of the spectrum, and in the same decades, a European intellectual current performed the adoption openly and theorised it as it went. The French Nouvelle Droite, organised from nineteen sixty-eight around the research association known as GRECE and the journals it sustained, announced a strategy it called metapolitics: the contest for the cultural and intellectual premises of an age, waged in journals, publishing houses, study circles, and the vocabulary of public debate, on the stated reasoning that political victory follows cultural preparation and that a movement which neglects the premises will lose with the machinery of the state in its hands. The current's theorists cited their source without coyness, speaking of a Gramscianism of the right, and the citation was accurate in the limited sense the availability problem predicts. They had read the war of position as a general account of how orders change: slowly, through the institutions of civil society, by the formation of a new common sense, under the direction of intellectuals performing an organising function. What they took was the terrain analysis and the patience. What they left behind is equally legible in the five questions, and the inventory of the leavings is the grading this article promised: the economic nucleus, gone, since the current's politics were cultural from the first premise; the concessions audit, gone, since no leading group in their account pays for consent with sacrifices of its corporate interest; the class anchorage of the bloc, replaced by civilisational identity; and the emancipatory end, inverted, since the new common sense they prepared was hierarchical where Gramsci's was to be egalitarian. The borrowing was therefore neither theft nor homage. It was a partial import, graded here as the terrain and the time horizon without the theory's material core, and describing it in those terms explains it better than either the celebration or the alarm with which it is usually reported.

### Did Gramsci invent the long march through the institutions?

No. The phrase belongs to the German student leader Rudi Dutschke, who proposed it in nineteen sixty-seven as a strategy of working within institutions to transform them, adapting the image of a famous military retreat. Gramsci never used it. The attribution survives because the war of position sounds like a summary of it.

### What did the metapolitical current take, and what did it leave behind?

It took the map and the clock: civil society as the decisive terrain and cultural preparation as the long precondition of political change. It left the ledger and the anchor: the concessions a leading group must pay, the economic nucleus of hegemony, and the class whose leadership the theory was built to analyse.

The adoption widened through the following decade along two channels worth distinguishing, because they show availability operating at different depths. Within France and Italy, intellectual currents on the right continued the metapolitical reading as a self-conscious strategy, debating in their journals how a cultural hegemony of their own might be prepared and treating Gramsci as a technician of power whose confession of faith could be discounted. In the English speaking world of the nineteen nineties, by contrast, conservative intellectual journals more often used Gramsci as an explanation than as an instructor: the transformation of universities, schools, and cultural institutions over the preceding generation was narrated as a Gramscian success story, the long cultural preparation of the other side, and the narration functioned as a diagnosis of defeat and a call to comparable patience. The two channels differ in the borrower's posture, student in one, patient in the other, and the difference matters for grading, since the explanatory use borrows the theory as a story about opponents while the strategic use borrows it as a discipline for oneself. Both confirm the namable claim from opposite ends. A description of how consent is organised can be lifted by the camp organising consent and by the camp that believes consent has been organised against it, and neither use is a misunderstanding of the description. Where the misunderstanding enters, in both channels, is the familiar one: the conditions fall away, the concessions ledger is never opened, and hegemony settles back into a synonym for the other side's cultural dominance, at which point the citation decorates a complaint. The reception history of such degradations is traced in [the companion account of how Gramsci was read and misread](/2015/05/01/gramsci-reception-and-misreadings/), which follows the term's career into the journalism where it does least work.

One question the adoption raises is often put as an accusation and deserves a straighter answer. If a theory can be used by a thinker's enemies, was the theory perhaps never really his, or is it perhaps empty? The accusation confuses a doctrine with a description, the distinction the availability problem rests on. A catechism used by heretics is a scandal; a survey used by an invading army is a compliment to the surveyor. Gramsci's strategic writings belong to the second class by design, since their author built them by surveying the terrain his own movement had to cross, including the parts held by the Church, the liberal state, and the fascist regime, and a survey does not ask who is reading it. The responsible conclusion is not that the borrowings are illegitimate but that they are incomplete in identifiable ways, and that the five questions make the incompleteness auditable. A right wing Gramscianism that never prices a concession and never finds the economic nucleus is not refuted by its politics; it is graded, coolly and on the evidence, as a partial application, exactly as a left wing application that skips the same rows is graded. Symmetry of grading is the method's answer to the scandal, and it is stronger than custodianship because it survives contact with the fact of availability instead of denying it.

## The academy as wholesaler: diplomatisation

Between the strategists stands the institution that made the vocabulary universally available, and its role deserves a section of its own, because the academy's borrowing has a characteristic shape. Universities do not need Gramsci to win anything. They need him to organise syllabuses, to name research programmes, and to supply a portable term for phenomena their disciplines had handled clumsily, and the borrowing is adjusted to those needs: the concept is clarified, systematised, and quietly relieved of the parts that embarrass a seminar, above all the parts that commit its user to finding concessions in budgets and organisers in staffing tables. The result, across several disciplines in the nineteen eighties and after, was a diplomatised Gramsci, welcome everywhere because he demanded little, his hegemony softened into a general name for cultural influence and his war of position into a metaphor for gradual change of any kind. Diplomatisation is not corruption; some of the academic work was exact and some of it, in textual scholarship, restored the notebooks' complexity against every simplification in this article's story. But the institutional gradient runs toward the portable version, and the gradient explains why the vocabulary reached the strategists in the thinned form it did.

International relations shows the process at its most instructive. The Canadian scholar Robert Cox, writing in nineteen eighty-one and nineteen eighty-three, rebuilt hegemony for the study of world order as a fit among material capabilities, ideas, and institutions, in which a dominant power's rule is stabilised when its order is accepted as natural by subordinate states and strata. Cox was explicit about his source and about his amendments: Gramsci's concept, formed for classes within a state, was lifted to the relations among states, and the lifting was defended as the concept's own logic, since the notebooks treat the international as a refraction of national formations. Graded by the five questions, the Cox borrowing is among the most substantive of the academic career: it keeps the insistence that durable order rests on consent rather than on preponderance, and it keeps institutions at the centre. What it necessarily thins is the concessions ledger, since the payments that bind a world order are harder to invoice than a statute book, and the formation question, since common sense among states is a thinner medium than common sense in a parish. A related borrowing, the Subaltern Studies collective of South Asian historians from nineteen eighty-two, ran the concept in the negative with great effect, describing colonial and postcolonial orders as dominance without hegemony, rule that never secured the consent it claimed, which is the second question returning a negative verdict across a whole historiography. The negative use is the academy's finest Gramscian product, because it proves the concept can still refuse a case. Where diplomatisation completed its work, by contrast, in the looser precincts of cultural commentary, hegemony became a compliment academics paid to any influence they disapproved of, and the word's discriminating life ended in exactly the synonymy the first section corrected.

## The mainstream strategist reading

A third family of borrowers is the least discussed and perhaps the most consequential: the professional strategists of ordinary politics, the party intellectuals, campaign theorists, and policy advisers who read Gramsci neither as scripture nor as scholarship but as a senior colleague's memorandum on how durable majorities are built. The reading has a long pedigree on the European left, where the postwar leadership of the Italian party presented its national road as the notebooks' practical continuation, and where the western European communist strategists of the nineteen seventies, grouped under the label Eurocommunism, rebuilt their alliance doctrine in explicitly hegemonic terms: leadership to be won across the middle strata before power, consent to be organised through the institutions of a plural society, the frontal model retired. Whatever verdict is passed on those projects, their strategists were running the first and fourth questions as working doctrine, drawing blocs and pricing terrain, and the vocabulary entered the mainstream of European political science from their practice as much as from any book.

The migration then crossed the aisle in the manner availability predicts. In the Britain of the late nineteen eighties, the modernising current within social democracy studied the opposing project's success through Hall's analysis and drew the strategist's moral: that elections are won in the years between them, in the formation of common sense about what a government is for, and that a party which treats the interval as an administrative pause will be governed by the common sense its opponents manufacture. Think tanks and strategy institutes of several allegiances generalised the lesson into a doctrine of the permanent campaign and the battle for the premises, phrases that are the war of position translated into the idiom of the consultancy. Graded by the procedure, the mainstream reading is a genuine but partial borrowing. It keeps the terrain and the clock, and it keeps, at its best, the bloc question, since coalition architecture is its daily bread. It habitually drops the concessions audit, because strategists experience concessions as tactics, priced in polls, rather than as the sacrifice of a leading group's corporate interest, and it drops the fifth question's discipline, counting celebrity endorsements where the method counts organisers. The result is a Gramsci of the seminar for practitioners: sharper than the journalistic synonym, weaker than the theory, and enormously influential precisely because it fits the needs of people whose business is the manufacture of consent and who recognised, in the notebooks, the only classic of their trade written from the losing side.

## Unmooring the concept: hegemony without a class anchor

The borrowing that changed the concept most, and widened its availability furthest, was performed inside the Marxist tradition itself. In Hegemony and Socialist Strategy, published in nineteen eighty-five, Ernesto Laclau and Chantal Mouffe rebuilt hegemony as a general logic of political construction: social identities, on their account, are not given by class position but articulated, linked into chains of equivalence among demands that share an opponent, and hegemony names the operation by which one such chain comes to stand for the whole. The reconstruction was argued from the notebooks with learning, and its authors presented it as the theory's own trajectory completed: Gramsci had loosened hegemony from a single class's strategy into the analysis of how any collective will is formed, and they proposed to finish the loosening by surrendering the guarantee that the forming agent is anchored in production. The gain was real. The rebuilt concept explains bloc formation among movements whose unity is political rather than economic, pensioners and patients and residents aligned against a closure, with a suppleness the class anchored version strains to match, and it gave the study of populism and of new movements a working instrument.

The cost is located by the second question. In the anchored theory, a bloc is held by concessions that can be priced, because the allied groups have material interests that the leading group must sacrifice to satisfy. In the unmoored theory, the chain is held by articulation, by the discourse that names the common opponent and the shared identity, and articulation can appear costless: a slogan aligns what a budget cannot. The appearance is partly an illusion, since successful articulations do distribute recognition, candidacies, and resources, but the theory's centre of gravity has shifted from the ledger to the lexicon, and with the shift the concept's availability widens again, because a discursive operation is even more portable than a descriptive one. Any project can attempt an articulation; not every project can pay for a bloc. The fuller treatment of this reconstruction, and of the post-Marxism it founded, belongs to [the companion account of post marxism](/2022/03/01/post-marxism-explained/), and the present article's concern is only its place in the availability story: after nineteen eighty-five, the word hegemony circulated in two strengths, the anchored original with its conditions and the unmoored descendant with its generality, and most subsequent borrowing does not say which strength it is using. The analyst's first act with any modern application is therefore a question of provenance. Which Gramsci is in the room, the analyst of concessions or the theorist of articulation? The five questions grade the first natively. Applied to the second, they must be asked in translation: what does this chain cost its leaders, in what institutions is its identity formed and renewed, and who performs the articulating work? A borrowing that survives translation has substance under either flag. One that dissolves under it was the word alone, travelling.

## How borrowed concepts lose their conditions

The losses catalogued in the adoptions above are not random, and their pattern is itself a piece of strategic knowledge. A concept's conditions are dropped in a predictable order, the most expensive first. The concessions condition goes earliest, because it is the costliest to honour: it requires budgets and statutes, it embarrasses borrowers whose subjects concede nothing, and it converts a flattering description into an audit the described may fail. The bloc goes next, replaced in transit by simpler dramatis personae, the elites, the establishment, the media, collectives vague enough to rule without the inconvenience of internal settlements that an analyst would have to reconstruct. The formation sites thin after that, from the full inventory of parish, school, profession, and press to a single site, usually the media, which is the site the borrower happens to work in or against. The organising function is the last to vanish, since even a slogan needs someone to repeat it, but it survives in degraded form, as the counting of famous names. What arrives at the end of the chain is cultural hegemony as the phrase is used in ordinary polemic: a name for the fact that the other side's assumptions fill the air. Nothing in that residue is false, exactly; some side's assumptions do fill the air. But the residue cannot discriminate, cannot price, and cannot be wrong, and a concept that cannot be wrong about a case has stopped being an instrument and become an epithet.

### How does a condition differ from a slogan?

A condition can fail. It specifies what must be present for the concept to apply, and it sends the analyst to evidence that might not be there. A slogan cannot fail, fits every case it is pointed at, and asks for no evidence beyond recognition. The difference is the whole method.

The intermediaries of transit do the stripping, and each has an innocent reason. The textbook needs the concept in a paragraph, and conditions take pages. The column needs it in a sentence, and a sentence carries a label better than a ledger. The speech needs it as a rallying name, and rallying names must not arrive with their defeaters attached. The strategist's memorandum needs it actionable by Monday, and the concessions audit is the work of a season. At no stage does anyone decide to empty the concept; the emptiness is the sum of reasonable compressions, which is why custodianship by complaint never reverses it. What reverses it, where it is reversed, is a user who needs the discrimination badly enough to pay for the conditions: Hall needing to know why his side was losing a country rather than an argument, the Subaltern historians needing a word for rule that never won consent, the analyst of the Italian catastrophe needing to say precisely what had been missing. The five questions are offered in that spirit, as the conditions in their most portable form. They are longer than a slogan and shorter than the notebooks, and their portability is the point: the transit losses happen at the joints between formats, and an instrument that fits on a card while keeping its defeaters has a chance of arriving with its edge on.

## The heir's reading: the notebooks in the party that inherited them

The first borrower was the author's own party, and its reading set the pattern of authorised inheritance against which the later, unauthorised borrowings define themselves. The Italian Communist Party that emerged from the resistance under Palmiro Togliatti, Gramsci's successor and the editor who supervised the notebooks' first publication, presented its strategy as the notebooks applied: a national and democratic road, alliances built across the middle strata and the Catholic world, reform pursued as the accumulation of positions within a plural society, the party itself conceived as the Modern Prince, the collective intellectual of the class it organised. The postwar publication of the notebooks in thematic volumes, arranged by subject rather than by the order of writing, served that reading, presenting a systematic strategist whose categories formed a doctrine of the Italian road. Scholars have debated ever since how much of the system was the editor's arrangement, and the critical edition of nineteen seventy-five, restoring the notebooks' chronological and fragmentary character, complicated every doctrinal use, the heir's included. For the availability argument, the episode carries a precise moral. Even the authorised inheritance was a borrowing, selective in the manner of all the others: the heir kept the war of position, the national popular, and the party as collective intellectual, and handled more cautiously the notebooks' analyses of party bureaucracy and of the failures of leadership, which sat awkwardly in a doctrine of continuity. Custodianship, exercised by the institution with the strongest claim, still graded its Gramsci.

The heir's reading also demonstrates the availability problem from inside the family, because the inheritance did not stay settled. Within the Italian debate of the nineteen fifties and sixties, rival readers, operaist theorists of the factory, Catholic interlocutors, liberal historians of the Risorgimento, each extracted a different Gramsci from the same notebooks, and the party's claim to administer the estate was contested in its own journals. Abroad, the inheritance fragmented further as translations spread: the English selection of nineteen seventy-one, organised for readers of a different crisis, made the strategist of hegemony central and the philosopher of praxis peripheral, an editorial grading as consequential as the first. The pattern generalises into a rule the analyst can use. There is no unmediated Gramsci in circulation, only successive editions, selections, and strategies, each keeping some conditions and dropping others under the pressure of a present problem. This is not a counsel of despair about the texts, since the critical edition allows every borrowing to be checked against the notebooks themselves, and the five questions are one form the checking takes. It is a counsel against innocence. When a strategist cites Gramsci, the citation descends through a chain of prior gradings, and the analyst who wants the concept rather than the pedigree asks the chain the only question that matters: which conditions survived the journey, and the answer is recoverable from the application itself, since an application displays, in what it bothers to evidence, exactly what it imported.

## A success analysed entire: the British settlement after the war

The Italian case returned negatives; the method's credibility requires a case it can pass, and the British settlement built after nineteen forty-five serves, because its five answers are unusually well documented and because the formation's later unmaking gave Hall's generation its problem. The bloc came first, and it was drawn wide. The government elected in nineteen forty-five governed as the manager of a cross-class alliance assembled by the war itself: organised labour, whose unions were inside the state's machinery; the industrial working class of the cities; much of the salaried middle class, whose wartime experience of planning and rationing had converted it to collective provision; and enough of capital, in the export industries, to accept the settlement as the price of social peace and American credit. The programme phrased each ally's demand as a national one: full employment for the workers, security for the middle class, modernisation for the industrialists. The settlement's durability across a change of government in nineteen fifty-one, when the incoming administration kept its pillars intact for over a decade, is the first question's external verification: governments changed, the bloc's terms held, which is what distinguishes a bloc from a ministry.

The concessions ledger is the settlement's most famous feature and the second question's easiest audit in this article. The National Health Service, founded in nineteen forty-eight, was a concession of the classic form: a sacrifice of the medical profession's market and of the Treasury's preference, made irreversible by universalism, since a service used by every class could not be withdrawn from any. Around it stood the rest of the priced settlement: the commitment to full employment maintained as a standing obligation, council housing built at scale, secondary education opened, national insurance generalised. The audit also records the settlement's exclusions, which the first question teaches the analyst to price: the empire's subjects, whose labour and materials underwrote the recovery, were outside the bloc, and the settlement's terms for them were set by domination rather than by consent, a boundary that later decades would invoice. Formation, the third question, was institutionalised with unusual self-consciousness: a national broadcaster carrying the settlement's idioms into every household, a reformed school system sorting and certifying the alliance's children, a press whose proprietors were inside the bloc's terms, and a monarchy refitted as the settlement's ceremonial guarantor. The terrain, fourth, was correspondingly deep: the incoming government of nineteen fifty-one inherited institutions that administered its predecessor's common sense, and governed inside them. The fifth question's staffing was the settlement's quiet triumph, a generation of organisers formed by the war: civil servants who had run planning, union officials who had run production committees, local councillors who had run evacuation and rationing, all performing the organising function for the new order as a matter of biographical routine.

The case's final service to the method is its ending, which the questions also grade. A settlement analysed as hegemonic in nineteen fifty is not thereby insured, and the analyst of its later decades watches the answers decay one by one: the bloc narrowing as its industrial pillar rusted, the concessions repriced under fiscal pressure, the formation sites fragmenting as broadcasting multiplied and the schools' settlement was contested, and a rival formation assembling, in the think tanks and journals of the nineteen seventies, the organic intellectuals of an alternative, whose project Hall then audited from the other side. Nothing in that decay refutes the earlier pass. It demonstrates the temporal clause written into every hegemonic verdict: the questions report a settlement's condition at a date, and a formation that once passed the audit becomes, under the same rows, a formation in organic crisis, its consent spent and its trenches held by administrators. The analyst who re-runs the questions at intervals owns a history of the settlement; the commentator who keeps the original label owns a souvenir.

## When the questions deadlock: Caesarism

The two worked cases show the audit returning clear verdicts, a vacancy and a settlement. A third outcome completes the method's range, and the notebooks supply its category: the deadlock, in which the contending blocs balance so evenly that neither can impose its settlement nor concede the field, and the crisis is resolved by an arbiter standing, or claiming to stand, above the contending forces. Gramsci named the resolution Caesarism, after the type of the great personality entrusted with the arbitration, and he graded its instances with characteristic refusal of a single verdict: Caesarism can be progressive, when the arbitration breaks a deadlock in a direction that reorganises society on a broader basis, or reactionary, when it freezes the balance to protect the stronger side's property from the weaker side's organisation, and the grading is performed, in his pages, by asking whose settlement the arbiter's peace turns out to be. The category extends a line of Marxist reflection that runs through the analysis of Bonapartism, the regime of Napoleon the Third, in which a state apparatus and its chief appear to rise above a society whose classes have fought themselves to a standstill, governing by plebiscite and police in the name of the whole. The analyst will recognise the five questions inside the phenomenon. A Caesarist resolution is what the audit predicts when the first question finds two blocs in equilibrium, the second finds both ledgers exhausted, the third finds a common sense split into two deposits with no arbiter between them, and the fifth finds the decisive organising function performed, in the event, by the army, the bureaucracy, or the plebiscitary machine of the arbiter himself.

The Italian case was resolved in this family of ways, and so, in the judgement of many historians, was the French crisis of nineteen fifty-eight, when the Fourth Republic's bloc dissolved over the Algerian war and the arbitration of a general recalled from retirement refounded the state on a new settlement whose terms, a strengthened executive plebiscited directly, bore the category's mark. The method's use of such cases is diagnostic rather than dramatic. Caesarism is the name for the exit a system takes when hegemonic construction fails on both sides, and its appearance in an analysis is a report about the blocs, not about the personality, however large the personality looms in the journalism. The analyst who reaches for the category has been sent back to the audit: an arbiter becomes necessary exactly where consent could not be organised by either contender, and the arbiter's own rule then faces the second question in its hardest form, since a settlement founded by arbitration must purchase, with concessions and formation, the consent it did not inherit, or govern in the armour permanently. The category thus closes the procedure's logical circle. Hegemony, domination, and Caesarism are the three verdicts the five questions can return about a contested order, leadership with consent organised, rule with consent absent, and arbitration with consent suspended, and a framework whose verdicts are three, and different, and evidenced, has answered in advance the charge that it calls everything by one name.

## The armour and the centaur: coercion in the method

No account of the framework as strategy is complete without its treatment of force, since the vocabulary's admirers and its borrowers alike are tempted to file Gramsci as the theorist who replaced violence with culture, a filing the notebooks contradict on the pages where the theory is stated most generally. His image for the whole matter is taken from Machiavelli: the centaur, half animal and half human, under whose sign he sets the dual perspective of political action, force and consent, authority and hegemony, violence and civilisation, as the two levels on which every durable power operates. The levels are not stages. Consent does not succeed force in time, as the softer receptions imply, and the war of position is not a promise that the armour has been retired; the armour is consent's enclosure, in his recurrent figure, the coercive apparatus that stands behind the settlement for those who refuse it, and its presence is one of the conditions that makes refusal expensive and consent, for the rest, the path of sense. The analyst therefore audits the armour as carefully as the settlement: its size relative to the order's claims, its tempo of use, its targets. An order whose hegemony is healthy uses the armour rarely and at the margins, against the refusal that tests the boundary; an order in decay uses it daily and at the centre, and the shift in the ratio between the two levels is among the most reliable dials the organic crisis possesses, readable in budgets and in court lists by any analyst who cares to look.

The dual perspective also disciplines the challenger, and this is the half of the doctrine the cultural receptions most consistently drop. A counter-hegemonic project that builds consent and neglects the question of force will meet, at the settlement's boundary, the armour its opponent kept in repair, and the Italian case, in which a movement with factories in its hands dissolved before prefects and squads, is the standing tuition in the asymmetry. Gramsci's own conclusions were measured and are quoted here in substance rather than in slogan: the frontal moment is not abolished by the positional war but subordinated to it, prepared by it, and ruinous without it, which is why the founding comparison grades terrains before prescribing forms. The strategist who emerges from the notebooks whole holds both halves of the centaur in view at once, and distrusts any version of the theory, friendly or hostile, that presents only one. A Gramsci of pure consent is a devotional image. A Gramsci of pure force is the old caricature. The method is the animal and the human in their proportion, and the proportion, changing by case and by decade, is one more thing the five questions exist to measure rather than to assume.

## The strongest objection: a vocabulary that restates everything

The objection must now be stated at the strength its best proponents would want, because the article's answer is worth only what the objection costs it. The objection runs as follows. The Gramscian framework, as actually used, is a machine for restating political outcomes in a more impressive vocabulary. Whatever happens can be redescribed in its terms after the fact: a durable order exhibits hegemony, a collapsing one enters organic crisis, a popular authoritarianism is authoritarian populism, a cultural shift is a war of position being won or lost. No conceivable result embarrasses the framework, since its categories are elastic enough to receive them all, and a framework that no result can embarrass generates no falsifiable prediction and therefore no knowledge. Worse, it generates no operational strategy either: the five questions, however elegantly printed in a table, terminate in a description of the situation richer than the one the analyst started with, but a description is not a decision, and the commander who asks what to do on Monday receives an essay on the terrain. Finally, the objection points to this article's own central exhibit as its best evidence. A theory adopted with equal enthusiasm by the left that inherited it, the right that opposes it, the academy that diagrams it, and the consultants who sell it is not a theory with unusual generality. It is a vocabulary with unusual prestige, and the cross-spectrum adoption is the market's verdict that the words commit their user to nothing. Emptiness, not availability, is the parsimonious explanation, and the availability problem is a courteous name for a vacancy.

Each element of that case deserves its answer, and the answers are of different kinds, which the objection's force requires be kept separate. To the charge of restatement, the reply is that redescription is the beginning of every analysis and the end of none, and the framework is distinguished from its journalistic shadow precisely by the point at which redescription is forced to stop. The five questions impose evidence demands at which a redescription can fail: a claimed bloc dissolves when the fiscal flows show extraction rather than settlement; a claimed consent evaporates when the concessions ledger is empty and the formation sites report sullen compliance; a claimed war of position turns out to be a sequence of publicity events when the terrain audit finds no institutions built or entered. The Italian case in this article is the demonstration. The socialist movement of nineteen twenty could be, and was, described by its own press in the full Gramscian vocabulary avant la lettre, as the rising hegemonic force of the new Italy; run through the questions, the description collapses at every row, and the collapse is a finding, not a relabelling. A framework that can convict the analyst's own side of vacancy has the property the objection denies it, the capacity to return verdicts its user did not want.

### What result would count against a hegemonic reading of a case?

The ledger and the map returning empty together. If the concessions audit finds no sacrifice priced and maintained, and the formation audit finds compliance resting on fear, habit, or purchase rather than on organised participation, the reading fails and the correct verdict is domination, however the arrangement describes itself.

To the charge that no prediction follows, the reply concedes the letter and disputes the spirit. The framework issues no dated prophecies, and any writer who extracts them from it is decorating. What it issues are conditional exposures, statements of where an order is brittle that events can confirm or disappoint: an order whose audit shows domination without hegemony is exposed to the withdrawal of fear; a settlement whose concessions are being silently withdrawn is consuming the consent it will need in a crisis; a challenger whose institutions face inward will not convert a crisis into a succession. These are the kinds of claim by which serious strategic traditions have always been judged, and they are falsifiable in the only tense strategy possesses, the retrospective audit of whether the brittleness was where the analysis said it was. To the charge that description is not decision, the reply is shorter: the framework never promised decisions, and the promise it keeps is prior and more valuable, that the decision maker confront the situation's actual structure rather than its dramatis personae. A general staff that knows which institutions renew the enemy's consent, what those institutions cost, and who staffs them has not been told what to do. It has been told what its doing will have to reckon with, and the history of strategy suggests that most catastrophes are failures of reckoning rather than of nerve.

The objection's strongest card remains the adoption itself, and it should be conceded what is true in it before the rest is answered. It is true that the vocabulary's prestige has outrun its discipline, that most published Gramscian commentary supplies none of the evidence the five questions require, and that for those texts the emptiness charge lands in full: they restate, they decorate, they predict nothing and audit less, and their authors' enthusiasm across the spectrum is indeed evidence about the texts. The concession is not fatal to the framework because the framework supplies the standard by which those texts are convicted, which is the service the objection cannot perform for itself. Restated as a grading rule, the objection becomes the method's quality control: an application that could not have been wrong about its case was not an analysis. What survives that rule is smaller than the literature and larger than the objection allows: a procedure, tested on closed cases in this article in both directions, one failure and one success, each legible in staffing tables and statute books, that tells the analyst what a durable order is made of and where a challenger must build. A grammar serves every army that speaks the language; it is not therefore blank, and the proof is that sentences in it can still be false.

## Errors in application: a field guide

The adoptions leave a residue of recurring mistakes, and collecting them is more useful than another statement of the method, because each error is a way of sounding Gramscian while skipping one of the questions. The first error is describing as explaining, the substitution of the label for the machinery. A column that calls an arrangement hegemonic has classified it; the analysis begins only when the bloc, the ledger, and the formation sites are produced, and the reader can detect the substitution by a simple test: remove the word hegemony from the piece and see whether any information is lost. In the substituting text, none is; the word was carrying prestige, not content. The second error is the vocabulary transplant, in which an older analysis is reissued in the new terms without new evidence: power becomes hegemony, supporters become a bloc, newspapers become formation, and the author reports the translation as a discovery. The third error assumes that an analysis issues a strategy automatically, as if the map drew the march. It does not, and the disappointment this causes is a recurring source of the emptiness charge: a borrower who expected an operational doctrine from a diagnostic method complains that the method is empty, when the correct complaint is against the expectation. Gramsci's own strategic writings are full of timings, judgements, and wagers that the categories frame but do not make, and the honesty of the framework consists partly in leaving the wager visible as a wager.

The fourth error is the genetic fallacy in both its directions, and it flourishes wherever the adoption story is told as scandal or as pedigree. In one direction, an idea is treated as contaminated because an opponent used it: the war of position is suspected because a right wing current studied it, as if a survey were poisoned by the army that captured it. In the other direction, an idea is treated as validated because a hero used it: the analysis is trusted because Hall ran it, the strategy honoured because a party of the resistance inscribed it in its programme. Both directions substitute lineage for grading, and the availability problem exists to block the substitution. An application stands on its evidence or falls by it, and the biography of its author is evidence about the author. The fifth error is the one-site analysis, the reduction of the formation question to the medium the analyst knows. Journalists find common sense manufactured in newsrooms, teachers in schools, clergy in pulpits, and each is right about a site and wrong about the system, since common sense is the deposit of all the sites in their weekly rhythm, and a change in one is routinely compensated by the others. The one-site error explains the perpetual surprise of media criticism, which announces a transformed public mind after every change in the press and is then puzzled by the electorate's continuity. The sixth error is the skipped audit, already named in the artifact's error column and repeated here because it is the most consequential: the refusal, usually excused by deadline or by sympathy, to price the concessions. An analysis of a favoured formation that never opens the ledger is a tribute, and an analysis of an opposed formation that never opens it is a prosecution brief, and the method recognises neither genre.

### Can the five questions be turned on a case the analyst admires?

They must be, or the method is a weapon of description rather than an instrument. The Italian party's own historians graded the resistance inheritance severely, and Hall graded his own tradition's failures without anaesthesia. Symmetry is the audit's only warranty, and an analyst exempting a favoured case has announced the exemption in the grades.

A seventh error belongs to the strategists rather than the commentators, and it is the confusion of the terrain with the whole war. A movement that has mastered the fourth question sometimes concludes that institutional patience is the entire strategy, that the trenches dug and held will one day simply be the country, and forgets that the war of position in the notebooks is a form of struggle ordered toward a transformation of the relations of force, not a retirement plan for activists. The German counter-society's fortress that became a waiting room is the standing warning, and its mirror image is the movement that scorns the trenches for the assault and discovers, as the councils did, that the state's fortresses were load-bearing. The method grades both errors with the same table, which is the advantage of a procedure over a temperament: it can tell the impatient that their formation sites are empty and the patient that their orientation is inward, in the same vocabulary, from the same evidence. An eighth and final error is the analyst's vanity, the treatment of the framework as a mark of sophistication rather than a standard of work. It appears in the prose before it appears in the conclusions, in the cadence that signals membership of the initiated, and its correction is the plainest in this guide: the questions are not improved by being asked in an impressive voice, and the notebooks themselves, written in a cell for readers who would have to build the world they described, are the least decorative strategic writing of their century.

## What the method cannot do

A procedure's limits are part of its teaching, and the five questions have three that a serious user keeps in view. The first is timing. The analysis grades the state of a settlement at a date and maps its brittleness, but it does not date the crisis, because organic crises ripen through accidents the framework does not pretend to schedule: a war, a harvest failure, a scandal, a death. Gramsci's own category of the conjuncture names the level at which such accidents operate, and his analyses move between the organic and the conjunctural with a discipline borrowers often lose, mistaking a structural audit for a calendar. The second limit is material shock. The method studies the organisation of consent, and there are stretches of history in which consent is not the variable that decides: invasion, famine, plague, and blockade settle questions that no formation site mediates, and an analyst who runs the five questions over a besieged city has mistaken the instrument's domain. Gramsci, who wrote under a regime that had settled the question of his own movement by police action, knew the limit in his person; the notebooks' attention to coercion, to the armour in which consent is enclosed, is the theory's internal acknowledgment that the armour sometimes does all the work. The third limit is the one the availability problem makes unavoidable, and it requires its own statement.

### Does the method oblige its user to share Gramsci's aims?

The descriptive core does not, which is the availability problem restated. The dispute is over the remainder: whether the concept's normative charge is part of its content or part of its author's biography. The method takes no side in that dispute, because grading an application needs only the conditions, not the borrower's soul.

The dispute deserves its two sides at full strength, since it decides what hostile use of the framework is. On the guardianship reading, hegemony is inseparable from the emancipatory project it was built to serve: the theory's categories are normed at birth, consent is distinguished from manipulation by the horizon of a society in which the distinction dissolves, and a use of the machinery to perfect a hierarchy is a misuse in the strict sense, a saw cutting against its teeth. On the availability reading, the concept's descriptive core is exactly what its history shows it to be, an account of a mechanism that operates wherever consent is durably organised, and the author's hopes travel with his biography rather than with the concept, as the hopes of a cartographer travel with his. This article has proceeded on the availability reading because the evidence of the adoptions compels it: the machinery demonstrably worked, as description, in Hall's hands and in the metapolitical reading and in the subaltern negative, across incompatible ends. But the procedural point is stronger than the verdict, and it is the one the analyst should carry away. Under either reading, applications can be graded, because even the guardianship position needs criteria to tell a use from a misuse, and the criteria it reaches for are the conditions themselves: concessions priced, the alliance drawn, the sites of formation named, the organisers counted. A hostile borrowing that honours the conditions is, on the guardianship view, a misuse performed with the genuine instrument; a friendly borrowing that skips them is a homage performed with a replica. The five questions convict the replica and describe the misuse, which is all that method, as distinct from loyalty, has ever been able to do.

## The counter-hegemonic question

Everything in the procedure so far has been phrased from the side of an existing order, because the analysis begins with what rules. The same five questions, reversed, state what a counter-hegemonic project would require, and stating it is a useful severity, since most invocations of counter-hegemony in the literature name a mood rather than a construction. Reversed, the first question demands a bloc in embryo: an alliance wider than the project's founding group, with terms its allies can read, since a sect that phrases every demand in its own idiom has not begun the work the Moderates finished and the councils never started. The second question, reversed, is the one challengers least enjoy: a counter-project must also pay for consent, in advance and from its own scarce resources, delivering real gains through the institutions it builds, the friendly society that actually pays the benefit, the school that actually teaches the child, the advice centre that actually wins the case. Consent is invoiced on both sides of the contest, and the challenger who promises payment after victory is asking for credit the incumbent, who pays weekly, does not need. The third and fourth questions reversed describe the construction familiar from the German case and the Turin warning together: formation sites that convene their audience as often as the incumbent's do, on terrain chosen for its load rather than its welcome. The fifth completes the specification: organisers formed inside the allied groups rather than imported to them, since a counter-project staffed by missionaries from its founding stratum reproduces, in miniature, the traditional intellectual's distance that the category of the organic was invented to correct. The organising dimension of such projects, and their record, is treated in [the companion account of marxism and social movements](/2022/04/01/marxism-and-social-movements/).

### Where does a counter-project find its intellectuals?

Inside the groups it organises, or it does not find them. The function is performed by people formed within the allied strata and rising with them, the union's own officials, the parish's own catechists, the trade's own instructors. Imported organisers can assist; they cannot substitute, because the function's substance is the trust of a shared formation.

The reversed questions also state the counter-project's one structural advantage and its standing temptation. The advantage is the crisis. Hegemony, being an achievement renewed weekly, can fail, and its failures are the challenger's opportunity in a way that a merely coercive order's failures are not: a settlement whose concessions are withdrawn, whose bloc narrows to its leading group, whose formation sites fall silent or cynical, enters the condition Gramsci called a crisis of authority, in which the old common sense loses its obviousness and the population becomes available to a new account of the world. The analyst detects the opening with the same audit, run over the incumbent: empty ledger, narrowed bloc, formation in decay. The temptation is to read the opening as the victory, to mistake the incumbent's crisis for the challenger's construction, when the five questions measure them separately and history is littered with crises inherited by the best organised mourner rather than by the most deserving heir. In the Italian case, the crisis of the liberal order was real and total, and it was harvested by the side that had staffed the provinces, not by the side that had diagnosed the crisis most brilliantly. Diagnosis is the analyst's virtue; it is not the organiser's. A counter-hegemonic project, graded strictly, is the rarest object in this article's landscape precisely because the reversed questions are a specification for decades of unglamorous construction, and movements that announce the title usually exhibit, on audit, a founding group, a vocabulary, and a media presence: one and a half answers out of five.

## The terrain changes: formation after the parish and the party press

The five questions are dated by their examples, drawn from a Europe of parishes, party newspapers, and mass unions, and a serious user will ask whether the procedure survives the transformation of civil society since. The formation sites of the middle twentieth century were transformed twice over: first by broadcasting, which concentrated the manufacture of common sense in a small number of studios and made the audience simultaneous and national; then by the multiplication of channels and the migration of attention to fragmented, commercially sorted streams, which dissolved the simultaneity while keeping the concentration of ownership in new forms. The method's answer is that the questions do not name technologies; they name functions, and the functions persist under every technical settlement. There is still a deposit of what goes without saying, still institutions that lay it down in weekly rhythms, still a bloc whose programme the deposit naturalises, still concessions priced somewhere, still organisers performing or neglecting the work. What changes is the audit's directory. The analyst of a broadcasting age inventories the schedules and the regulators; the analyst of a fragmented age inventories the platforms' sorting systems, the professional bodies that certify expertise, the schools, which survive every media revolution because childhood does, and the workplaces and municipalities where the deposit is still argued over face to face.

Two findings from the procedure survive the translation intact and are worth isolating. The first is that technical change does not abolish the concessions question, however thoroughly it transforms formation. A population whose common sense is manufactured in new ways still experiences its consent as a settlement, still prices the health service and the wage and the pension against the order that provides them, and still withdraws allegiance on a schedule the ledger predicts. Analysts dazzled by each new medium announce the end of material politics on a regular cycle, and the second question is the standing refutation: consent bought only with imagery is the Bismarckian sedative in modern dress, and it decays at the speed of the first unpaid bill. The second finding is that fragmentation raises the value of the fifth question. When formation was concentrated, a challenger needed to enter or build a few great institutions; when it is dispersed, the organising function is dispersed with it, and the side that trains and connects organisers across a thousand small sites holds an advantage that no single channel purchase replicates. The war of position, translated, is a war of staffing. That translation is the method's claim to durability: it was never a theory of newspapers. It was a theory of how the obvious gets made and by whom, and the obvious is still being made, on schedules, by identifiable people, in places an analyst can visit.

## Small scales: the method inside a profession

The five questions are usually run over nations, and a reader may doubt whether the instrument survives reduction in scale. It does, and the reduction is a useful test of the analyst's grip, because small formations display the machinery with the casing off. Take a self-governing profession of the classic kind, medicine in its twentieth century settlement, as the constructed case. The bloc is legible at once: the profession's leading stratum, its consultants and royal colleges, in alliance with the state's health bureaucracy and the research universities, holding the allegiance of the rank and file practitioner by a settlement whose terms the second question prices exactly. The concession at the settlement's centre was clinical autonomy: the state funded, the profession judged, and the practitioner's freedom from lay direction in the consulting room was the sacrifice the paying side made and renewed daily, defended as fiercely as any statute in the national cases. The formation sites meet the third question's standard for depth: the medical school, where the deposit is laid down in the years of maximum impressionability; the journals, where the profession talks to itself in its own idiom; the examination and the licensing body, where the common sense is certified. The terrain, fourth, is the hospital and the surgery, institutions a challenger, whether a health minister or a rival healing occupation, must enter or build against at the cost the German fortress taught. The fifth question finds the function performed by the profession's own: the examiners, the editors, the professors, the senior nurses and administrators elaborating the order's account of itself, rising from within the allied strata in the classic organic pattern.

The small case returns the same three verdicts as the national ones, which is the point of the exercise. Where the settlement's concessions are repriced from outside, a funding system that converts autonomy into audit, the analyst watches the profession's consent to the wider order cool in exactly the sequence the ledger predicts, and its intellectuals begin their defection in the journals years before any confrontation. Where a rival formation builds its own schools and its own certification, an alternative therapy with colleges and registers of its own, the war of position proceeds at consulting room scale, indifferent to its miniature. And where the profession's own common sense splits, between an older deposit of vocation and a newer one of management, the contradictory consciousness of the practitioner, who speaks both idioms in the same week, becomes the friction from which change starts. Scale changes nothing in the questions because the questions were never about size. They are about the anatomy of durable rule wherever it occurs, and an analyst who can run them over a profession, a university, or a firm owns the instrument in the only sense that matters for strategy: the sense in which it works when no one is watching it be impressive. The notebooks' author, who built the theory from a cell by studying a church, a party, a factory system, and a peninsula, would have recognised the exercise. The scale was always the reader's to choose. The audit is the same.

## The analyst's two clocks: epoch and conjuncture

One discipline remains to be stated explicitly, because it governs the use of every answer the procedure returns, and its neglect is responsible for a large share of the framework's false alarms. The notebooks distinguish two tempos of political life. The organic tempo belongs to the structure: the composition of blocs, the depth of civil society, the sedimentation of common sense, all moving at the speed of institutions and generations. The conjunctural tempo belongs to the occasion: ministries rising and falling, scandals, strikes, elections, the weather of a season. Gramsci's warning, written against his own movement's excitements, is that the two tempos must never be invoiced to each other. A conjunctural victory, an election won, a ministry formed, is not an organic shift, and an organic shift, a settlement quietly rotting, may produce no conjunctural sign until the moment it produces nothing else. The five questions run on the organic clock. They grade settlements, not seasons, and the analyst who re-runs them after every headline will manufacture crises and triumphs at the frequency of the news, which is the operational form of the restatement fallacy the objection warns against.

Hall's work is the standing demonstration of the two clocks used together, which is why the English application occupies its place in this article. The conjuncture he analysed, the crisis of the nineteen seventies, was read as the surfacing of an organic exhaustion, the postwar settlement's bloc narrowing, its concessions repriced by stagflation, its common sense losing the consent of the very strata it had formed. The authoritarian populist project mattered, in that reading, because it addressed the organic level from a conjunctural opening: it proposed a new settlement, not merely a new ministry. An analyst equipped with the two clocks asks of every political event the sorting question the method supplies: does this change who governs, or does it change what governing rests on? Most events do the first. The rare events that do the second are the ones the five questions exist to catch, and catching them early, while the ledger and the formation map still show the work in progress, is the closest the framework comes to the predictive virtue the objection demands. The discipline also protects against despair's version of the same error. A conjunctural defeat, however total it feels, leaves the organic audit untouched, and a movement that re-runs the questions in a bad season usually finds its trenches where it dug them, its ledger partly intact, and its task reduced from refoundation to repair. The notebooks were written by a man whose conjuncture was a prison cell and whose organic analysis never confused the cell with the century. The transferable part includes the composure.

The two clocks also govern the analyst's working calendar, and the point is practical enough to state as a rule of the craft. The five files described in the practice section are reviewed on the settlement's rhythm, which for most formations means annually, or on the rhythm of the institutions themselves, a school generation, a parliament, a collective agreement's term, and not on the rhythm of the feed. Between reviews, the analyst logs conjunctural events in a separate column, dated and ungraded, and promotes an event into the audit only when it survives to the second review or when it visibly alters a row's evidence, a concession withdrawn, an ally absorbed, an organiser defected. The rule sounds like bookkeeping and functions as epistemology: it is the mechanism by which the method refuses to chase the weather while never missing a change in the climate, and it is the precise habit whose absence produces the commentary cycle of weekly hegemonies, in which an order is declared transformed on Monday and restored by Friday. A framework built in a cell, by an author whose news arrived in censored parcels at long intervals, has the slow clock in its bones. The borrower who keeps the vocabulary and loses the calendar has dropped the condition that costs the most patience, which is perhaps why it is the condition most often dropped.

## The fragment as invitation: the notebooks' form

One cause of the migration remains unexamined, and it is bibliographical. The Prison Notebooks are not a treatise. They are thirty-three notebooks of fragments, translations, summaries, and arguments resumed across years and abandoned mid-thought, written in a vocabulary bent by censorship, left unordered by an author who died with the arrangement unmade. Every edition since has had to decide what the book is, and the decisions, as the heir's reading showed, are themselves gradings. For the availability argument, the form has a consequence beyond the editions: fragments invite completion. A finished system resists partial borrowing, because its parts lean on each other and removing one announces the theft; a fragmentary corpus licences the borrower to lift the piece that answers to a present problem and to complete it with local materials, honestly or otherwise. Gramsci's concepts travel in separable units, the bloc, the intellectuals, the war of position, each small enough to carry and dense enough to reward the carrying, and the corpus does not pursue the borrower who leaves the rest behind. Even the famous formulations arrive as aperçus rather than as theorems, quotable in a breath and applicable in a career, and the quotability is a property of the prison writing itself, which compressed a system's worth of thought into sentences built to survive confiscation of everything around them.

The analyst should resist drawing the sentimental conclusion, that the theory is what its readers make of it and all completions are equal. The fragments lean on each other more than their separability suggests, and the critical edition's restoration of their order and stages has repeatedly convicted confident borrowings of resting on a sentence its author revised, a translation he was testing, or a polemic whose target the borrowing had forgotten. That is the scholarly form of the grading this article performs strategically: provenance first, then the questions. But the form does explain the migration's scale in a way the conceptual explanation alone cannot. Doctrines with finished architectures, and there were several in the same decades, mostly stayed home. The unfinished notebook went everywhere, because an unfinished work recruits its readers as collaborators, and collaborators feel, sometimes correctly, that the fragment was waiting for their situation. The method's response to its own source's hospitality is the discipline that runs through this article: borrow the fragment by all means, since the corpus invites it, and then submit the borrowing to the audit the fragment's neighbours would have imposed, the ledger, the bloc, the formation sites, the staffing. A completion that survives the neighbours has earned its place in the corpus's afterlife. One that cannot is a postcard from the prison, treasured and unread.

## Who performs the function after the mass party

The fifth question has a history of its own, and the analyst applying the method across the twentieth century watches the organising function's address change. The mass party was the century's great organising machine, and Gramsci's Modern Prince was its theory written in advance: an organism that recruited from the allied strata, trained its own intellectuals in its schools and its press, and kept the formation sites of its counter-society staffed across generations. The machine's European zenith, in the decades after the war, is also the background of the British settlement case, whose staffing miracle was partly the party form's maturity: organisers existed in depth because institutions had spent fifty years manufacturing them. The century's closing decades thinned the machine almost everywhere, through the professionalisation of politics, the migration of formation to the broadcasters and then to the platforms, and the conversion of parties into electoral cartels staffed by a circulating stratum of advisers, researchers, and presenters. The analyst running the fifth question over such a landscape must not conclude that the function has vanished. It has moved, and the audit's task is to find its new addresses: the institutes and foundations that train the stratum, the professional associations that certify it, the media careers that sort it, the consultancy and the campaign staff through which it circulates among employers.

The migration changes the audit's findings in two ways worth stating. First, a circulating stratum is loyal to the function rather than to a formation, and its availability to hire is the availability problem embodied in a labour market: the same organiser, pollster, or speechwriter may serve successive and opposed projects, carrying the method's techniques across the spectrum in a briefcase. The strategist's Gramsci is, in that stratum, not a reading preference but a trade skill, and the analyst grades its applications knowing that the personnel of consent manufacture are now substantially mercenary. Second, the thinning of the mass party weakens the challenger's fifth answer more than the incumbent's, since the incumbent inherits the state's staffing and the professions' reproduction, while the challenger must manufacture organisers without the machine that once manufactured them as a byproduct of its ordinary life. Much of the strategic literature's nostalgia, and much of its despair, is an unfiled fifth question: the recognition that the counter-society's staffing table no longer fills itself. The method draws the practical conclusion without sentimentality. A project that cannot say where its next thousand organisers are being formed has answered the fifth question in the negative, whatever its other answers, and the building of the forming institution, the school, the journal with a training function, the association with a ladder, is not a cultural garnish on the strategy. It is the strategy, arriving, as the notebooks said it would, one position at a time.

## The method at work: an analyst's practice

It remains to describe, briefly and practically, what using the procedure looks like as a working habit rather than as a doctrine, since the article's claim is that the durable contribution is a method, and methods live in practices. The analyst keeps five files, one per question, and treats each as an account to be kept current rather than a verdict to be reached once. The bloc file holds the settlement's terms: the programme read as a price list, the personnel movements between allied groups, the exclusions and their costs, revised when an ally defects or is absorbed, which the trasformismo watch exists to catch early. The ledger file holds the concessions with dates and irreversibility assessments, and its most valuable entries are withdrawals, since an incumbent quietly repricing its settlement is the organic crisis in its first, deniable form. The formation file is a directory before it is an argument: the sites, their schedules, their staffing, their reach into the uncommitted, kept with the jealousy of a general staff counting bridges. The terrain file prices entry: what it would cost, in years and organisers, to build or enter each load bearing institution, and it marks the decorative sites where the analyst's own side has been spending cheaply and calling the expenditure strategy. The functions file is a staffing table for both sides, ruthless about the celebrity substitution, and it is the file whose deterioration the analyst learns to fear first, because organisers are the one input that cannot be purchased quickly when the conjuncture turns.

Three habits govern the files' use. The first is cadence: the audit is re-run on the organic clock, at intervals set by the settlement's rhythm rather than by the news, and conjunctural excitements are logged as weather until they survive into a second audit. The second is symmetry, already stated and repeated here because practice erodes it: the files are kept on the analyst's own side with the same severity, and the annual grading of one's own formation, ledger, and staffing is the exercise that keeps the instrument honest, as Hall's reproach to his own tradition and the notebooks' severity toward their own movement attest. The third is provenance: every borrowed term in the analyst's prose is marked, at least in the files, with what it cost the borrower to import, which conditions came with it, and which were left at the border. A practice so kept will not produce the thrill of the vocabulary, and it will sometimes produce the unwelcome verdict, the favourite formation graded as a clique with a printing press, the despised incumbent graded as a settlement still paying its bills. Those verdicts are the method's signature. An instrument that can disappoint its owner in both directions is the only kind worth the decades the war of position demands, and it is, in the end, the whole of what the imprisoned author left that could not be confiscated: not a map of one country's trenches, but the habit of counting trenches before speaking of victory.

## Written from defeat: the method's temper

A last fact about the source explains the method's temper better than any doctrine, and the strategist should have it plainly. The theory of hegemony was written by a man whose movement had been annihilated, in the prison of the regime that annihilated it, across years in which the news reaching the cell reported the defeat's consolidation. Strategic literatures are usually written from victory or in expectation of it, and they bear the marks: the enemy is a foil, one's own side's virtues are the argument, and the analysis stops where the celebration starts. The notebooks invert every habit. The enemy's strength is the text's obsession, studied in its most accomplished forms, the Church's centuries, the liberal state's absorptions, the adversary regime's own consent work, with a respect that is never admiration and never underestimate. The author's own tradition is audited with a severity no opponent improved on: its missed alliances, its rhetorical maximalism, its intellectuals debating timetables while the provinces were staffed against them are recorded in the same ledger as the enemy's concessions. And the future the theory plans for is not imminent rescue but a long war of position whose timescale is generational, costed in institutions and in organisers formed one by one. Defeat, in the notebooks, is not a mood. It is a method of inquiry, the decision to treat the catastrophe as data about how orders actually hold and break, and to refuse every consolation that would corrupt the data.

This is why the procedure survives translation into other hands better than the victory literatures of the same century, and the point completes the availability argument from the side of the reader. A theory built to explain why the strongest fortresses hold, written by the prisoner of one, contains nothing that flatters a borrower into exempting a favoured case, and its habits, the audit, the ledger, the staffing table, the enemy studied at full strength, transfer to any strategist whose situation includes an opposing order that is winning. The borrowers who took the method whole, Hall auditing his own tradition's absence of a project, the subaltern historians returning the negative verdict on whole empires, were borrowing the temper as much as the terms. The borrowers who took the terms alone usually wanted the opposite of the temper: a vocabulary that would let a favoured case be described as history's protagonist without the audit. The five questions are offered, finally, as the temper made portable. They assume the reader's side may be the one failing them, they price the enemy's settlement before envying it, and they treat a catastrophe, the reader's own included, as the most expensive education available and therefore the one whose lessons are not to be wasted on adjectives.

## An exercise in grading: two applications examined

The article closes its argument with the exercise it has been training for, the grading of applications, performed on two constructed cases. Constructed cases are used deliberately: they are no one's property, they carry no contemporary names, and their evidence can be stipulated in full, which lets the grading show its workings. The first case is a textbook paragraph of a kind that circulates widely in survey courses. A declining industrial town, the paragraph says, remains loyal to the party of its grandfathers although the party's programme no longer serves the town's interests, and this is explained by the cultural hegemony of the party's worldview, absorbed through the local newspaper, the working men's club, and the chapel. Grade it. The first question is unasked: no bloc is identified, only a party and an electorate, so the analyst cannot say whose settlement the loyalty sustains. The second question is answered in the negative by the paragraph's own stipulation, since the programme no longer serves the town, yet no concession is sought that might explain the loyalty's price; habit, identity, and the absence of a rival are all likelier mechanisms, and the framework has names for them that are not hegemony. The third question gestures at real formation sites, the paper, the club, the chapel, and then wastes the gesture, since nothing is said about what those sites teach, how often, or against what friction from the town's daily experience of decline, which is contradictory consciousness waiting to be interviewed. The fourth and fifth questions are absent entire: no rival's terrain, no organisers on either side, not even the party's own. Verdict: the paragraph uses the vocabulary to dignify an observation about habit, and the dignity is the whole of the borrowing. One condition out of five is gestured at; the grade is slogan, and the exercise's sting is that the paragraph is, in its small way, the median product of the framework's fame.

The second case is a strategist's memorandum, constructed on the same terms, from a movement that has lost three elections in a country stipulatively described. The memorandum declines to discuss the defeats as verdicts on its programme and instead audits its position. It records that the governing bloc has narrowed to its leading group and two allies whose concessions are visibly being repriced, that its own side's formation sites reach the already convinced weekly and the uncommitted never, that the rival's organisers are recruited from the allied strata while its own are imported from a single profession, and that the terrain of the schools and the municipalities is held by administrators of the old settlement whose neutrality it has mistaken for its own opportunity. It recommends no message. It recommends a decade of staffing, priced concessions offered to a named ally whose defection would narrow the governing bloc further, and the building of two institutions before the next contest. Grade it. All five questions are run, four return negative verdicts against the memorandum's own side, the concessions are treated as costs rather than as announcements, and the strategy that follows is visibly the audit's child rather than its decoration. The borrowing is substantive at the level the availability problem grades, and the memorandum's author may be of any allegiance the reader cares to imagine, which is the point of constructing the case without one. The method does not ask who wrote it. It asks whether the questions were run and whether the evidence was allowed to convict the writer's own side, and on those two tests the distance between the textbook paragraph and the memorandum is the distance between the vocabulary and the theory. Everything in this article has been an attempt to make that distance visible, gradable, and, for the reader who intends to use Gramsci rather than to quote him, crossable. The reader who performs the exercise on the next borrowed concept encountered, in a speech, a syllabus, or a strategy paper, owns the article's argument in the only form its subject would have recognised: as a practice, kept up.

## For students and exam candidates

Applied Gramsci questions are marked on method, and they are lost by candidates who display the vocabulary and never run an analysis. The structure that scores is the one this article models: state the five questions as your procedure, apply them to a closed historical case where the evidence is settled, and grade the case honestly, including the questions it fails. Choose the case for its documentation rather than its drama; the Italian catastrophe and the postwar British settlement both work because their ledgers and staffing tables are in the textbooks. Handle the availability problem explicitly in any answer about the concept's influence, since it converts an embarrassed observation, that opponents cite him too, into the analytical centre of the answer, and close with the emptiness objection and its reply, which is where evaluation marks concentrate on this topic. The worked example structures, with model openings and the commonest marking deductions, are set out in [the cluster's exam guide](/2015/05/15/gramsci-exam-answers/); the portable discipline to carry into the hall is smaller. Never let the word hegemonic appear in your answer without its evidence in the same paragraph, and never grade a formation you admire more gently than one you do not. Examiners can tell, and the habit is the whole subject in miniature.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

This article converts into a two session exercise with very little adaptation. Give the first session to the grading: issue the five-question analysis as a worksheet, the companion study template at [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) is designed for exactly this transfer, and set two texts side by side, a textbook paragraph that uses the vocabulary and a strategist's memorandum that runs the audit, asking the seminar to mark each against the rows and defend the marks from the evidence column. The discussion reliably discovers the availability problem on its own, since students notice that the memorandum could belong to any camp and the paragraph to none, and the second session can then take up the adoptions, Hall and the metapolitical current as the paired case, with the symmetry rule enforced throughout: every application examined must be graded by the same rows, and every participant must run the audit over a formation they sympathise with before grading one they oppose. Guard the exercise's neutrality by keeping the cases closed and historical, as this article does, and by treating the normative dispute in the limits section as an open question with two strong sides rather than as a settled preliminary. Students leave with a transferable skill, the grading of borrowed concepts, which survives their forgetting every date in the cluster, and with the rarer experience of watching a method convict its friends in public.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The availability problem is, for your trades, a sourcing discipline. When a subject describes a strategy as Gramscian, or a source attributes a movement's patience to the long cultural preparation, the citation establishes a method claimed, not a lineage proved, and the reportable facts are the borrower's cargo: which conditions travelled, what the application evidences, and where its audit stops. The five questions make a serviceable interview instrument, since a strategist who has imported the method can answer them and one who has imported the letterhead cannot, and the difference is quotable. Historians will find the framework most valuable in its negative verdicts, dominance without hegemony and concessions repriced, which convert the familiar narrative of an order's decline into an auditable sequence of ledger entries and defections; the reception history of the vocabulary itself, including its degradation into a synonym for dominance in the journalism of several decades, is documented in [the companion account of Gramsci's readers and misreaders](/2015/05/01/gramsci-reception-and-misreadings/). One caution from the notebooks' own scholarship applies to quotation in your work: the formulations travelled through editions and translations that graded them silently, so check the passage's standing in the critical edition before building on a sentence, and prefer the construction to the slogan wherever the two diverge.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Why do people across the political spectrum cite Gramsci?

Because what travels is an account of a mechanism, not a programme with its flag sewn in. Every strategist, whatever the allegiance, faces one recurring problem: how support for an order is assembled, maintained, and eventually lost. The notebooks address that problem directly, anatomising alliances, the price of consent, the sites where opinion is formed, and the organisers who do the work, and an anatomy can be consulted by readers who share nothing else. A party intellectual preparing an alliance, a scholar modelling a world order, a movement theorist planning a decade of institution building, and a consultant selling patience to a campaign are all, in that limited sense, reading the same book. Their citations therefore reveal little about their politics. They reveal, at best, a common recognition that consent has a machinery and that the machinery can be examined. The quarrels that matter start after the reading, over what the machinery should be made to do, and those quarrels are as wide as the spectrum the citations cross.

### Q: What is metapolitics?

Metapolitics is the label a French intellectual current, the Nouvelle Droite that organised from the late nineteen sixties around the GRECE association and its journals, gave to a strategy it acknowledged taking from Gramsci. The doctrine holds that the premises of an age, its vocabulary, its scholarly authorities, its sense of the obvious, are settled before its elections are, in journals, publishing houses, and study circles, so that a movement serious about power works first on the culture that will later ratify the politics. Political defeat, on this reading, is a symptom; the disease is a hostile common sense, and the cure is measured in decades of intellectual preparation. The current's debt to the war of position was stated openly by its own theorists. Its departures from the source are equally definite: the economic foundation of hegemony disappears, the price a leading group pays for consent is never audited, no class anchors the alliance, and the end served is hierarchical where the notebooks' end was emancipation. A partial borrowing, then, of map and patience, with the ledger left behind.

### Q: How did Stuart Hall use Gramsci?

Stuart Hall, directing the Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies in Birmingham, turned the notebooks into an instrument for reading a crisis while it was still unfolding, and his work remains the reference case for the method in English. With his collaborators in Policing the Crisis, published in nineteen seventy-eight, he treated a panic about street robbery as a symptom: a postwar order entering organic crisis, its population being schooled to welcome a more coercive state. His essay of January nineteen seventy-nine, in the British party's theoretical journal, then read the incoming government not as a swing of the electoral pendulum but as a bid to rebuild the ruling alliance, stitching popular grievances into a programme that would reprice the postwar settlement, and he named its governing form authoritarian populism. Three moves were his: the conjuncture as the unit of study; race and nation treated as load bearing within any bloc; and hegemony understood as a process never completed. He aimed the same audit at his own camp, whose missing counter-project was his essays' practical burden.

### Q: What is authoritarian populism?

Authoritarian populism is the term Stuart Hall coined for a specific form of rule, in the essay of January nineteen seventy-nine that analysed the incoming British project as a hegemonic construction. It names the joining of two halves that commentary kept apart. From one side, a governing project strengthens the coercive apparatus: policing, sentencing, discipline in schools and workplaces, the state promising order. From the other, that strengthening wins genuine popular consent, because the project articulates real grievances, about crime, bureaucracy, taxation, and decline, into its programme and answers them in its own direction. The consent is not a trick played on the audience; it is organised, and the grievances are not invented, which is exactly why the construction is formidable and why denouncing it as manipulation fails. The concept's discipline is the circuit it describes: coercion legitimated by consent, consent manufactured for coercion. Applications that cannot specify the grievances, the settlement being repriced, and the sites carrying the construction have kept the label and dropped the analysis.

### Q: How do you actually do a Gramscian analysis?

By working through five questions in sequence and refusing to let any of them be answered with adjectives. Begin with the alliance: reconstruct who is bound to whom, on what terms, from budgets, careers, and programmes rather than from rally speeches. Move to the price of the settlement, listing what the leading group gives up, in statute and in money, to keep its allies loyal, and watch the dates on which items are withdrawn. Then map where the population learns what goes without saying, naming the schools, pulpits, journals, and professional bodies involved and how often they convene their audiences. With those three answers in hand, draw the ground a rival would need to enter or build, separating the institutions that carry the order from the ornamental ones. Finish with the personnel: the men and women who organise, connect, and elaborate for each camp, identified by their work. An analysis that can conclude against its own hopes, finding domination where it wanted hegemony, has performed the procedure; anything less is recitation.

### Q: Is hegemony just a word for dominance now?

In a great deal of journalism, and in the usage of international relations, where the word hegemon denotes no more than the preponderant state among states, the term has indeed thinned into a synonym for dominance, and something real is lost in the thinning. In Gramsci's strict usage the two terms are opposites at the decisive point. Dominance describes rule exercised over groups whose consent has not been secured, resting on coercion, habit, or purchase. Hegemony describes leadership: consent actively organised through the institutions of civil society, paid for with concessions that cost the leading group something real, and renewed against rivals who might win that consent away. The distinction is not pedantry, because the diagnoses prescribe differently. A dominant order is brittle where its fear is withdrawn; a hegemonic order survives defeats that would shatter a merely dominant one, since its settlement is reproduced daily by the led themselves. Analysts who use the words interchangeably cannot state that difference, and stating it, with evidence, is most of what the concept is for.

### Q: Does Gramsci offer any practical strategy?

He offers a strategic orientation with prohibitions, not a manual, and the distinction decides how the notebooks should be read by practitioners. There is no timetable in the prison writings, no organisational chart, no instruction for a campaign's next quarter, and readers who open them for one close them disappointed. What the writings supply is prior and more durable: an account of what a political order is made of, alliances, concessions, formation, terrain, organisers, from which strategies for unforeseen situations can be built, together with refusals that mark the method's edges. Build leadership before power. Price every alliance. Study the adversary's formation machinery without contempt. Treat no winning season as a change in the geology beneath politics. Expect change in the West to be positional and generational. The wagers themselves, the timings and the choices, remain the strategist's own, visibly and uncomfortably, which the notebooks treat not as a gap in the theory but as the honest boundary between analysis and action.

### Q: Why does the radical right cite Gramsci?

Because a current within it, the French Nouvelle Droite from the late nineteen sixties, read the notebooks carefully and said so. Its theorists concluded from their own defeats that political change follows cultural preparation, found that conclusion stated with unmatched precision in the war of position, and adopted the strategy under the name of metapolitics, speaking openly of a Gramscianism of the right. The adoption is explained by the availability problem rather than by any secret affinity. Hegemony describes how consent is organised, and a description of a mechanism can be lifted by any project that wants to organise consent, including projects whose ends the author would have fought. What the borrowing left behind is as documented as what it took: the economic nucleus, the concessions ledger, the class anchorage, the emancipatory end. Describing the adoption accurately, as a partial import graded by what travelled, explains the phenomenon better than the two usual responses, celebration of the theory's reach or alarm at its contamination.

### Q: Is cultural struggle a substitute for economic struggle?

No, and the notebooks are emphatic on the point wherever industry is discussed. In his construction the two are joined at the root: a group leads in the culture of an order on the foundation of its weight in production, and an account that omits the economic core has described the weather while missing the climate. His notes on Fordism pursue the point into the factory, treating the high wage as the price of a new kind of worker and the corporation's campaigns over drink, sex, and domestic life as a pedagogy fitted to the tempo of the line. The cultural terrain matters in the theory because consent is organised there, in schools, churches, press, and professions, not because production stopped mattering and the factory emptied into the studio. The substitution reading survives because the cultural vocabulary is vivid and quotable while the economic analysis is slower, and because it flatters borrowers whose work is cultural. Graded by the source, it is a misreading: culture is where hegemony is felt, and the economy is where it is paid for.

### Q: What would a counter-hegemonic project require?

The five questions reversed, read as a construction specification. A bloc in embryo, wider than the founding group, with alliance terms the allies can read and a programme that phrases their demands as its own. Concessions paid in advance from scarce resources, real gains delivered through the project's institutions, since consent is invoiced on the challenger's side too and promises after victory are credit the incumbent does not need. Institutions of formation with a rhythm of their own, a school that teaches weekly, a journal that argues continuously, an association that meets whether or not an election impends, planted where the order is renewed rather than where admission is cheapest. Organisers formed inside the allied groups and rising with them, not imported missionaries from the founding stratum. And time, measured in the institutions' generations rather than in electoral cycles, since the war of position is the form the contest takes where civil society is dense. A project that can exhibit only its founders, its phrases, and its press coverage has answered, at most, one and a half of the five.

### Q: How does the availability problem grade a concept borrowed from Gramsci?

By cargo, not by citation. A borrowed concept arrives carrying up to three things: the word, the picture, and the conditions. The word alone, hegemony as a prestige synonym for influence, grades as slogan, however eminent the borrower. The picture, trenches and terrain and slow cultural siege, grades as metaphor, suggestive and unaudited. The conditions grade as method when they survive the journey: the concessions a leading group must price, the bloc drawn from fiscal and recruitment evidence, the formation sites named with their schedules, the organisers counted by function, and the possibility, preserved throughout, that the case fails the test. The availability problem explains why grading is necessary at all: since the theory describes a mechanism, anyone may borrow it, friend, opponent, scholar, or consultant, and the borrowing's allegiance says nothing about its quality. Grading restores the discrimination the citation cannot supply, and it applies symmetrically, which is why the exercise in this article grades a failing textbook paragraph and a serious memorandum without asking either author's politics.

### Q: What should an analyst check before applying hegemony to a new case?

Four preliminaries, before the five questions are run. Bound the case by domain and period, since hegemony is always of a specified field at a specified date, and unbounded claims, a party's hegemony over an era or a class's hegemony over a culture, are where the vocabulary goes to become unfalsifiable. Inventory the evidence the audit will need and confirm it exists: budgets and statutes for the concessions, institutional histories for the formation sites, career records for the organisers; a case whose archive cannot answer the questions should be described in poorer words. Name the defeaters in advance, the verdicts that would displace hegemony if the evidence returned them, domination, patronage, inertia, arbitration, so that the analysis is committed before its sympathies engage. And run the symmetry check: state, in writing, what the same audit would ask of the formation the analyst supports, since the exemption of a favoured case is the first and commonest corruption of the method, visible in inflated grades exactly where sympathy begins.

### Q: Why do borrowed concepts lose their conditions in transit?

Because every hand the concept passes through is rewarded for lightening it. A condition is heavy luggage: it demands budgets, archives, and the risk of a negative verdict. A textbook condensing a theory into a paragraph drops the qualifications to fit. A columnist with a sentence to fill keeps the label and sheds the ledger, since labels are what sentences carry best. A speaker needs a name the crowd can hold, and names that arrive trailing their own defeaters do not rally anyone. A practitioner with a deadline trades the audit for the allusion. Nobody in the chain resolves to hollow the concept out; the hollowing is the arithmetic of many reasonable shortenings, and it proceeds in a fixed order, the most expensive condition discarded first, until the residue is a word for cultural influence that its user happens to dislike. What reverses the process is never complaint about decline. It is a user, an analyst facing a case that matters, who needs the discrimination badly enough to pay the conditions' price again.

### Q: How does the five-question analysis order its questions?

The sequence is a chain of dependencies, not a presentational habit. Until the alliance is drawn, nothing else can be priced, since a concession is a payment between identified parties and consent is always some group's consent to someone's leadership. The settlement's cost comes next because it tells the analyst what the institutions are for: they exist, strategically, as the machinery that renews that settlement or lets it decay. Where the renewal happens is the third matter, the sites at which the population's picture of the obvious is maintained, and only with those sites named can the fourth question, the map of ground a challenger must enter or construct, be drawn from evidence rather than from a constitution's table of contents. Personnel closes the sequence because an organiser is defined by a task, and the tasks are set by the four answers already reached: running the alliance, servicing the settlement, staffing the sites, contesting the ground. Begin at the end, with famous names, and every earlier answer degenerates into biography.

### Q: What separates a strategic concept from a slogan in this method?

A concept forbids, and a slogan accommodates. A strategic concept in working order specifies what must be present for it to apply, dispatches its user to look for things that may turn out not to exist, and returns negative verdicts that embarrass its user: no bloc, no concessions, no hegemony, however often the word is repeated. It can therefore be wrong about a case, and its capacity to be wrong is the whole of its value, since a description that fits every arrangement distinguishes none. A slogan fits every case it is pointed at, asks for no evidence beyond recognition, and costs its user nothing to repeat, which is why slogans travel further and faster than concepts and why the transit of a famous vocabulary so often ends in one. The five questions are the method's device for keeping its own terms on the concept side of the line: each question is a condition with a defeater attached, and an application that answers all five with evidence has earned the vocabulary it uses.
