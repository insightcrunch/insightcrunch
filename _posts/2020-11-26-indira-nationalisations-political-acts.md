On Saturday, 19 July 1969, the President of India, Varahagiri Venkata Giri, signed an ordinance that transferred the undertakings of the fourteen largest commercial banks in the country to fourteen new corporations owned by the Indian state. Three days earlier, on 16 July 1969, Morarji Desai had left the Union Cabinet, resigning as Deputy Prime Minister and Finance Minister after Indira Gandhi took the finance portfolio from him in a reshuffle he refused to accept. The ordinance was promulgated on a Saturday, two days before the monsoon session of Parliament opened on 21 July 1969, and Indira Gandhi announced the decision to the country in a broadcast on All India Radio that evening. The fourteen banks held deposits of fifty crore rupees or more each, and together they accounted for the great bulk of scheduled commercial bank deposits in India. Any reading of that Saturday has to hold two facts in one frame. The first is a policy fact: a state that had debated the ownership of banks since the 1950s acquired, in a single evening, the commanding heights of commercial credit. The second is a political fact: a Prime Minister who was losing control of her own party acquired, in the same evening, the most popular single measure of her career to that date, a weapon against the senior colleagues who were preparing to remove her, and a programme around which a new Congress could be built. This article defends the second fact as the deeper one. Its claim, stated plainly, is that the nationalisations carried out by Indira Gandhi's governments between 1969 and 1980 operate as political acts, distinct in kind from the socialist economic policy decisions they are usually filed as, and that the timing, the selection of targets and the legal form of each takeover were fixed by the political situation of the government that ordered it.

The claim needs its terms defined before the evidence is walked through. A political act, as the phrase is used here, is a state action whose principal effects are produced in the field of power: it rearranges who can command a party, a parliament, a bureaucracy or an electorate, and its economic consequences, however large, are subordinate to that rearrangement at the moment of decision. The reading developed in this series for the reforms of 1991, examined in [the 1991 reform as politics reanalysed](/2020/11/15/1991-reform-as-politics-reanalysed/), treats economic policy as a species of political action rather than as technical administration, and this article applies the same discipline backwards to the decade when the Indian state was expanding rather than retreating. Four takeovers carry the argument. The first is the nationalisation of fourteen banks on 19 July 1969. The second is the takeover of general insurance, begun by emergency ordinance on 13 May 1971 and completed by the General Insurance Business (Nationalisation) Act of 1972. The third is the nationalisation of the coal industry, completed by the Coal Mines (Nationalisation) Act of 1973 after the coking coal mines had been taken in 1972. The fourth is the nationalisation of six more banks by ordinance on 15 April 1980, three months after Indira Gandhi returned to office in the general election of January 1980. Around those four acts sits a penumbra of related measures: the takeover of foreign oil companies between 1974 and 1977, the nationalisation of 103 sick textile mills in 1974, the abolition of privy purses in 1971, and the regulatory statutes on foreign exchange and monopolies that narrowed the space in which private capital could operate. The article examines the four principal acts in sequence, adjudicates the political-acts reading against the two framings it displaces, and closes by tracing how the architecture built between 1969 and 1980 set the terms on which the reforms of 1991 later had to be argued.

### Which sectors did Indira Gandhi's governments nationalise between 1969 and 1980?

Between 1969 and 1980 the Union government nationalised fourteen banks in 1969 and six more in 1980, general insurance in 1971 to 1973, coking coal in 1972 and all coal mines in 1973, three foreign oil companies between 1974 and 1977, and 103 sick textile undertakings in 1974.

The list itself makes the first analytical point. Banks, insurance and coal were not chosen at random from the commanding heights, and they were not chosen together in a single plan. Each sector was taken at a different political moment, by a different legal route, and against a different configuration of opponents. The banks fell in the middle of a leadership struggle inside the Congress. Insurance fell while a victorious government was consolidating the mandate of the 1971 election. Coal fell in stages across the months when that same government was absorbing the economic shock of war and refugee relief. The second bank round fell in the first hundred days of a restored government that needed to prove that the interregnum of 1977 to 1979 had changed nothing about who governed. A policy-history framing, which treats the decade as the unfolding of a socialist programme adopted in principle and implemented sector by sector, cannot explain why the programme advanced in bursts synchronised with crises of political survival. A socialist-policy framing, which treats each act as the application of an ideology to an industry, cannot explain why the same ideology produced an ordinance on a Saturday in 1969, a two year administrative absorption in insurance, and a quiet restatement of an eleven year old measure in 1980. The political-acts reading explains both, because it starts from the situation of the decision maker rather than from the doctrine in whose name she acted.

## July 1969: A Leadership Struggle Inside the Congress

The road to 19 July 1969 ran through the wreckage of the Congress position after the general election of February 1967. The Congress lost power in eight states, its Lok Sabha majority shrank to a margin that made every by-election a crisis, and the party's organisational bosses, the regional leaders whom Rajni Kothari's contemporaries called the Syndicate, concluded that the defeat was the price of weak leadership. K. Kamaraj, S. K. Patil, Atulya Ghosh, Sanjiva Reddy and their allies had installed Indira Gandhi as Prime Minister in January 1966 in the expectation that she would govern under their guidance. By the winter of 1968 that arrangement had collapsed into open friction. The Prime Minister's secretariat under P. N. Haksar was building an alternative centre of policy advice. The left of the party, organised around figures such as Chandra Shekhar and the informal group of younger MPs hostile to the old guard, was pressing for a radical economic programme that would embarrass the Syndicate's business allies. Morarji Desai, brought into the Cabinet in March 1967 as Deputy Prime Minister and Finance Minister to balance the Prime Minister, embodied the opposite pole: fiscal conservatism, suspicion of controls, and a public record of opposition to bank nationalisation stretching back to the debates of the 1950s.

The presidential election of 1969 converted that friction into a succession contest. Zakir Husain, the third President of India, died in office on 3 May 1969, and Vice President Giri became acting President. The Congress Parliamentary Board met on 12 July 1969 and, by a majority in which the Syndicate's weight was decisive, chose Neelam Sanjiva Reddy as the party's presidential candidate over Indira Gandhi's preference for Jagjivan Ram. Four days later, on 16 July 1969, Indira Gandhi divested Morarji Desai of the finance portfolio, offering him continued office as Deputy Prime Minister without finance; Desai resigned from the government the same day. The sequence matters more than any single date in it. The Prime Minister struck at the Finance Minister after losing the presidential nomination battle and before the Congress Working Committee could consolidate the Reddy candidacy. Bank nationalisation was the ground she chose for the counterattack, and she chose it because the ground had been prepared. At the Bangalore session of the All India Congress Committee in July 1969, days before the portfolio change, she had circulated a note on economic policy, the stray thoughts memorandum associated with her secretariat, which listed bank nationalisation among the measures a revitalised Congress should adopt. The Syndicate's leaders read the note as electioneering. Within a fortnight it was the law of the land.

The evidentiary value of the Bangalore note lies in what it reveals about method. Indira Gandhi did not commission a study of the banking system in the spring of 1969, receive its findings, and act on them. She reached for a measure that the Congress left, the communist parties and a long line of party resolutions had kept in circulation for two decades, at the moment when that measure could split her opponents, outflank her Finance Minister and rename the terms of the leadership contest. The standard narrative of [the Indira Gandhi personality and institution question](/2019/03/21/indira-gandhi-personality-institution/) treats July 1969 as the moment she escaped tutelage. This article adds the institutional point: the escape was executed through an economic instrument, and the instrument was selected for the escape. That is what it means to call the act political before it is economic. The banking system that the ordinance seized was real, the credit grievances it answered were real, and the consequences for Indian finance were enormous and long lasting. None of that is in dispute. The argument is about the order of causation at the point of decision, and on that point the calendar is the strongest witness. A government that nationalises its largest banks on the Saturday between a Finance Minister's resignation and the opening of Parliament is conducting politics by other means, whatever doctrine it cites in the preamble.

The parliamentary passage of the bill in the first fortnight of August 1969 showed the same alignment of forces in the open. Morarji Desai, speaking from the opposition benches of his own party's Lok Sabha contingent, warned that the acquisition would not by itself move a single rupee of credit to the farmer, and that the government was buying applause with the depositors' money. The warning was the considered position of the man who had run the finance ministry until three weeks earlier, and it deserves recording at full strength: social control, he argued, had been strangled before it could work, by the very colleagues who pronounced it dead. The Congress left answered through the debate that ownership was the precondition of direction, and the government's managers counted the division lobbies as a rehearsal for the party contest to come. Outside Parliament the organised peak bodies divided on predictable lines, the All India Bank Employees Association, which had demanded nationalisation for two decades, hailing the ordinance as the settlement of its oldest claim, and the bank officers' associations reserving their position until service conditions in the new corporations were known. The employees' support mattered to the execution: an ordinance that the staff of the seized banks welcomed could be implemented over a weekend, and it was. By Monday, 21 July 1969, the fourteen banks opened under their custodians with their staff in place, a continuity that converted a Saturday seizure into an administrative fact before the opposition could organise a single protest meeting. Speed, in this sequence, was not a by-product of the method. Speed was the method, and it is the feature that every subsequent act in this article copied, in insurance by the emergency ordinance device and in 1980 by the identical Saturday logic of a rested government striking inside its first hundred days.

The Syndicate understood the move instantly, which is itself evidence for this reading. The presidential contest became a referendum on the Prime Minister. Giri resigned as acting President on 20 July 1969 to contest as an independent, Indira Gandhi called on Congress MPs to vote according to conscience rather than the party whip, and on 16 August 1969 Giri defeated Reddy by a narrow margin in the second preference count. The Congress Working Committee's disciplinary proceedings then produced the open rupture: on 12 November 1969 the Working Committee expelled Indira Gandhi from the party, and the organisation divided into the Congress (Organisation) of the Syndicate and the Congress (Requisitionists), later Congress (R), that followed her. The full mechanics of that division are treated in [the 1969 Congress split as decisive moment](/2019/03/26/1969-congress-split-decisive-moment/) and in [the 1969 split as organisational pivot](/2019/11/23/1969-split-organisational-pivot/). What belongs here is the place of the ordinance inside the sequence. Nationalisation preceded the expulsion by nearly four months and made the expulsion survivable: when the party bosses removed her from the organisation, she held the state, the programme and the public argument. The banks were, among other things, the fortress from which she fought the split, and their seizure cannot be separated from that function without falsifying the record.

## Social Control: The Compromise That Failed on Schedule

To see why nationalisation, rather than regulation, was the available weapon in July 1969, the article turns to the experiment it replaced. The idea of social control over banks had been Congress policy since the mid 1960s as the moderate alternative to public ownership: the state would steer private banks through law and moral pressure, reserving nationalisation as a threat. The National Credit Council was constituted in December 1967, with Morarji Desai as its chairman, to coordinate credit policy between the government, the Reserve Bank and the banking system. The Banking Laws (Amendment) Act of 1968, effective from 1 February 1969, wrote the scheme into statute. Boards of the larger banks were reconstituted to include representatives of agriculture, small industry, cooperatives and the professions. Loans to directors and to firms in which directors held interests were restricted. The Reserve Bank acquired strengthened powers over appointments and policy directions. Desai defended the arrangement in Parliament and in his public statements as the rational course: ownership was a blunt instrument, credit direction was the real objective, and direction could be had without the fiscal cost and administrative burden of acquisition.

The defence was coherent, and its failure was a failure of politics before it was a failure of design. Social control had been in force for less than six months when the ordinance displaced it, far too short an interval for its credit outcomes to be measured, and the government that displaced it never published an evaluation of its workings. What had failed, by July 1969, was not the scheme but its sponsor. Desai's name on the policy made the policy a liability for a Prime Minister constructing a left platform against the Syndicate, just as the scheme's moderation made it the perfect foil: retaining it would have meant governing on the Finance Minister's terms. The left critique of social control, pressed by the Communist Party of India in Parliament and by the Congress left in party forums, supplied the public argument, that a banking system owned by the industrial houses that borrowed from it could not be steered by councils and conventions. That critique had documentary support in the pattern of bank ownership, in which the great industrial families of Bombay and Calcutta held the controlling blocks of the largest banks and sat on their boards. The Hazari inquiries of the 1960s into industrial licensing and credit concentration had given the pattern an official description. Indira Gandhi's broadcast of 19 July 1969 used the vocabulary of that critique: banking, she told the country, had to serve the purposes of national policy rather than the interests of a few large houses, and public ownership was presented as the only instrument equal to the task.

The four takeovers that this article examines can be set out as a single map before the sequence is walked through in detail. The map is the article's findable artifact, and it is reproduced here as the Four Acts Map of Indira Gandhi's nationalisations.

Read as a map rather than as a list, the four acts share a grammar. Each was executed or initiated by ordinance, the instrument of executive urgency, rather than by a bill matured through the ordinary legislative timetable. Each was threshold based, using a deposit figure, a mine count or an amalgamation scheme to draw a bright line between the taken and the spared. Each was announced as the fulfilment of a standing national commitment, the Directive Principles of State Policy and the Congress resolutions of the 1950s supplying the standing text. And each landed within weeks of a political event that raised the value of a dramatic assertion of state power: an expulsion crisis, a war mandate, an energy emergency, a restoration. The grammar is the argument in miniature. Programmes do not fire on such cues; politicians do.

## Act One: Fourteen Banks in a Single Evening

The Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Ordinance of 19 July 1969 nationalised every scheduled commercial bank with deposits of fifty crore rupees or more, measured on the last Friday of June 1969. Fourteen banks crossed the line: the Central Bank of India, the Bank of India, the Punjab National Bank, the Bank of Baroda, the United Commercial Bank, the Canara Bank, the United Bank of India, the Dena Bank, the Syndicate Bank, the Union Bank of India, the Allahabad Bank, the Indian Bank, the Indian Overseas Bank and the Bank of Maharashtra. Their undertakings vested at midnight in fourteen corresponding new banks owned by the central government, and custodians appointed by the government took charge of each institution while its old board stood dissolved. The State Bank of India, created in 1955 from the Imperial Bank, and its seven associate banks, nationalised in 1959, were already public; the 1969 act folded the commanding heights of the private system into the same ownership. The scale can be taken from the Reserve Bank's statistics. In June 1969 the commercial banking system had 8,262 offices for a population that yielded one office for roughly every 64,000 people, and only 1,833 of those offices stood in rural areas. Scheduled bank deposits stood near Rs 4,646 crore. The fourteen banks held the overwhelming share of those deposits, which is why the threshold, though stated in money, functioned as a political classification: above the line stood the banks of the great houses, below it a long tail of smaller institutions that the state left alone.

### Why was 19 July 1969 chosen for bank nationalisation?

19 July 1969 was chosen because Morarji Desai had resigned on 16 July, the Congress presidential contest was open, and an ordinance signed by acting President V. V. Giri on the Saturday before Parliament met let Indira Gandhi seize the initiative from the Syndicate before it could consolidate against her.

The drafting history confirms the haste that the calendar suggests. The ordinance was prepared in the Prime Minister's secretariat and the finance ministry under close secrecy, with P. N. Haksar among the officials managing the file, and the Cabinet approved it at short notice on the day of promulgation. Giri's constitutional position sharpened the timing: he was an acting President who intended to resign and contest the presidency, and his signature on 19 July, the day before he laid down the office on 20 July, gave the measure a promulgator whose own candidacy the measure's popularity would assist. Parliament converted the ordinance into the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act within weeks; the bill received presidential assent on 9 August 1969 as Act 22 of 1969. Indira Gandhi held the finance portfolio herself through the passage, presenting the bill as the economic charter of her wing of the party. The detailed event history of the takeover, its ordinance mechanics and its reception is set out in [the bank nationalisation 1969 politics account](/2019/04/01/bank-nationalisation-1969-politics/), which this article deliberately does not retell: the concern here is what the act's design discloses about the class of action it belongs to. Three design features carry the disclosure. The threshold spared the small banks, so the measure could be sold as an attack on concentration rather than on banking. The compensation was set by principles stated in the Act rather than by negotiated purchase, so no bargaining with the houses could delay the seizure. And the instrument was an ordinance effective at midnight, so the fait accompli preceded the debate. Each feature optimises for political effect under time pressure. None of them is what a ministry designing a permanent banking policy at leisure would have chosen first.

The opposition's response in Parliament and outside it fixed the terms on which the act would be judged for a generation. The Swatantra Party, founded by C. Rajagopalachari on a platform of economic liberty, attacked the measure as expropriation dressed as economics; Minoo Masani, its leader in the Lok Sabha, argued that the state had seized the people's savings to finance its own power. The Bharatiya Jana Sangh opposed the takeover on the ground that public ownership would politicise credit, while welcoming the rhetoric against concentration in carefully separated terms. The Communist Party of India supported the act and demanded its extension to foreign banks and to the commanding heights beyond banking; the Communist Party of India (Marxist) supported the seizure while doubting, in its party documents, that ownership without a change in the class character of the state would alter credit outcomes for the poor. Business opinion organised through the Federation of Indian Chambers of Commerce and Industry recorded its alarm at the precedent. The government's stated case, in the Act's own preamble, ran in terms of the Directive Principles: acquisition was justified in order to control the heights of the economy and to serve the needs of development in conformity with national policy and objectives. Both registers, the preamble's and the critics', are part of the record this article adjudicates, and the adjudication section below returns to them. For the narrative, the point is simpler: on 19 July 1969 every political formation in India was compelled to define itself against a single act of the Prime Minister. That compulsion, not the deposit total, is the signature of the political act.

### What did the R.C. Cooper judgment hold on 10 February 1970?

The eleven judge bench in R.C. Cooper v Union of India held by ten to one on 10 February 1970 that the 1969 Act violated the fundamental rights to property and to carry on business, because its compensation principles were illusory and it discriminated against the fourteen banks it named.

The petitioner, Rustom Cavasjee Cooper, held shares in the Central Bank of India and the Bank of Baroda and sat as a director of the Central Bank; his petition became the vehicle for the shareholders' and the banks' combined challenge. Justice J. C. Shah wrote the majority opinion for ten judges, with Justice A. N. Ray alone in dissent. The Court held, first, that the Act impaired the guarantee under Article 19(1)(f) to acquire, hold and dispose of property and the guarantee under Article 31(2) that property be acquired only for a public purpose and under a law fixing compensation or specifying principles for its determination that were relevant to the value of what was taken. The principles in the 1969 Act, the majority found, did not yield a fair equivalent: they froze valuation in ways that excluded assets and goodwill, and so the compensation was not compensation in the constitutional sense. Second, the Court held that the Act discriminated, because it barred the fourteen named banks from carrying on banking business while leaving other banks, Indian and foreign, free to conduct the identical business, and it treated that bar as an unreasonable restriction on the right to practise a trade under Article 19(1)(g). Third, and with the longest reach, the majority rejected the doctrine that a law's object could shield its direct effect on fundamental rights, restoring the interconnected reading of Part III that the Bank Nationalisation case, as lawyers call it, bequeathed to Indian constitutional law. The undertakings of the fourteen banks, which the state had held for nearly seven months, reverted in law to the companies that had owned them.

The government's answer was drafted before the judgment was delivered, a detail preserved in the Reserve Bank's official history, whose account records that officials studied the arguments during the hearing precisely so that a corrected instrument could issue at once if the Act fell. On 14 February 1970, four days after the judgment, the President promulgated a fresh ordinance, Ordinance 3 of 1970, which re-acquired the undertakings with effect from 19 July 1969, removed the bar on the old companies carrying on non-banking business, and fixed compensation as a stated sum for each bank, aggregating Rs 87.40 crore, payable in cash or in securities at the holder's option. Parliament enacted the replacement as the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, Act 5 of 1970, which received presidential assent on 31 March 1970. The political reading of this sequence is not that the government defied the Court; it is that the government had priced the Court into the act from the beginning. A measure whose constitutional defects were repaired from a contingency draft within four days was a measure whose authors understood it as a move in a contest, with the judiciary as one of the contest's arenas. The contest then migrated to the Constitution itself. The Twenty Fourth Amendment of 1971 restored Parliament's power to amend fundamental rights, and the Twenty Fifth Amendment of 1971 replaced the word compensation in Article 31 with the word amount, and inserted Article 31C to shield laws giving effect to the Directive Principles in Articles 39(b) and 39(c) from challenge under Articles 14, 19 and 31. When the thirteen judge bench decided Kesavananda Bharati in April 1973, the basic structure doctrine it announced was forged substantially in the fire that the bank case had lit, a lineage traced in [the Kesavananda Bharati judgment reanalysed](/2020/08/02/kesavananda-bharati-1973-reanalysed/). Nationalisation, in short, generated constitutional doctrine because it was politics conducted at the level of the state itself.

## The Machinery: Compensation, Custodians and Credit Direction

A political act that seizes a banking system must still run one on Monday morning, and the machinery improvised in 1969 became the template the later acts inherited. The first element was the custodian. Each corresponding new bank was placed under a custodian appointed by the central government, who exercised the powers of a board until boards could be constituted under the scheme of administration the Act authorised. The custodians of 1969 were senior bankers and Reserve Bank officials, men whose professional standing reassured depositors that the vaults would open. The second element was compensation. The 1969 Act had stated principles for valuing the acquired undertakings, and it was the inadequacy of those principles, as the Supreme Court read them in R.C. Cooper, that voided the statute. The replacement ordinance of 14 February 1970 abandoned principles for figures: a schedule named a sum for each bank, aggregating Rs 87.40 crore, payable in cash or in government securities at the holder's option within the period the Act prescribed. Readers who open the text of that settlement in a browser based [office file viewer for the acquisition Acts](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) can watch the constitutional argument change form on the page: where the 1969 statute had argued about value, the 1970 statute simply paid, and payment, unlike principle, could not be pronounced illusory by a court applying Article 31 as it then stood. The third element was the dissolution of the old companies' banking business combined with permission to pursue non banking business, which removed the discrimination ground of the Cooper judgment while leaving the companies as shells whose only asset was the compensation claim.

The fourth element, credit direction, was built more slowly and outlasted the rest. The Lead Bank Scheme of December 1969 made a named public sector bank responsible for each district's credit survey and branch plan. The Differential Rate of Interest scheme of 1972 lent to the weakest borrowers, families below an income line drawn in rupees, at four percent. The priority sector, defined by the Reserve Bank's informal group in 1972 and given its target of a third of net credit by 1979 in 1974, converted the preamble's rhetoric into an inspectable number that every bank chairman's annual review could be judged against. The Regional Rural Banks, opened from 2 October 1975 under an ordinance of 26 September 1975 and a statute of 1976, added a hybrid tier: locally staffed banks owned jointly by the centre, the state and a sponsoring nationalised bank, designed to carry the system's credit into districts the commercial branches had not reached. Each of these instruments was a genuine administrative artefact with its own file history, and each is grist to the policy-history framing. Their common origin, though, sits in the settlement of 1969 and 1970. Direction of this granularity is possible only for an owner. A regulator armed with social control had been able to exhort; the ministry that owned the banks could instruct, appoint and audit, and the instructions flowed from the political priorities of the government of the day. The machinery is therefore evidence for both readings at once, and the adjudication below has to weigh, not merely collect, it: the instruments served development purposes their designers sincerely held, and they existed because a political act had put the banks at the ministry's disposal.

The deposit figures measure what the machinery accumulated. Scheduled commercial bank deposits, which stood near Rs 4,646 crore in 1969, crossed Rs 2,01,199 crore by March 1991 on the Reserve Bank's statistics, and the number of bank offices rose in the same span from 8,262 to 60,220, with the average population served by an office falling from roughly 64,000 to roughly 14,000. Priority sector advances approached 37.7 percent of net bank credit by 1991, short of the forty percent target but within sight of it. These are the strongest numbers the socialist-policy framing owns, and they were produced by the system the political acts created. What the numbers cannot settle is the question this article asks, which concerns the decisions of 1969 to 1980 rather than the performance of 1970 to 1991. A machine can be built for one reason and run for another; the history of the Indian public sector banks is the history of exactly that divergence, and it is why the article keeps the two questions, why the banks were taken and what the taken banks did, in separate compartments of the argument.

## The Doctrine Behind the Act: Property Against Plan

Every political act of the scale of 1969 needs a legitimating text, and the nationalisations found theirs in Part IV of the Constitution. The Directive Principles of State Policy, and above all Articles 39(b) and 39(c), direct the state to secure that ownership and control of material resources are distributed to subserve the common good and that the operation of the economic system does not result in the concentration of wealth and means of production. The founding debate over the justiciability of those principles, and over whether they were a manifesto or a mandate, is examined in [the Directive Principles against fundamental rights account](/2018/12/23/directive-principles-vs-fundamental-rights/). The Nehruvian settlement had held the two halves of the Constitution in a working tension: property under Part III was protected, planning under Part IV was persuasive, and the state's economic expansion proceeded by building new public enterprises rather than by seizing old private ones. The Avadi resolution of January 1955 committed the Congress to a socialist pattern of society, and the Industrial Policy Resolution of 1956 reserved the commanding heights, in Schedule A, to the state; but the method of the 1950s was creation, not acquisition, as [the Avadi resolution and socialist pattern account](/2019/02/05/avadi-resolution-socialist-pattern-1955/) records. Steel plants rose at Bhilai, Rourkela and Durgapur on green fields. The one great exception, the creation of the Life Insurance Corporation in 1956 out of 245 insurers, was defended as a rescue of a scandal marked industry rather than as a seizure of a commanding height.

Indira Gandhi's innovation was to convert the Directive Principles from a horizon into a warrant for acquisition, and to do so in the name of the same texts her father's generation had written. The preamble language of the banking Acts, and the parliamentary speeches of 1969 and 1970, returned insistently to Articles 39(b) and 39(c), and the Twenty Fifth Amendment then wrote the priority of those clauses into the constitutional text itself through Article 31C. The political function of the doctrine deserves emphasis. A Prime Minister accused by her own party's elders of personal ambition answered in the vocabulary of the founding document; a Finance Minister's resignation over policy became, in her telling, a defence of concentrated wealth against the Constitution. The doctrinal contest between Part III and Part IV, which constitutional lawyers narrate as an argument about interpretation, was simultaneously a contest for the Congress's identity, fought with the founders' words as ammunition. That double character, legal doctrine as party weapon, is exactly what the political-acts reading predicts and what a pure policy-history framing leaves out. It also explains why the property clause, and not any economic ministry's plan document, became the battlefield: the argument was never only about banks. It was about which text, and therefore which leader, defined what the republic's economic constitution permitted.

The property war that Cooper continued had been running since the republic's first year, and the genealogy disciplines any account that begins it in 1969. When the Bihar zamindari abolition statute was challenged, Parliament answered with the First Amendment of 1951, inserting Articles 31A and 31B and the Ninth Schedule to place land reform laws beyond the property clause's reach. The Seventeenth Amendment of 1964 widened the shield. The Supreme Court's counterstroke came in I.C. Golaknath v State of Punjab, decided on 27 February 1967 by eleven judges by six to five, which held that Parliament could not amend the fundamental rights at all and applied the bar prospectively. Golaknath is the hinge between the two eras of the property question. Before it, acquisition proceeded inside a constitution whose amendment power was contested but usable; after it, every major act of economic acquisition carried the risk of a constitutional confrontation that only a counter amendment could settle. The bank ordinance of 1969 was drafted in Golaknath's shadow, and the sequence that followed, Cooper in 1970, the Twenty Fourth and Twenty Fifth Amendments in 1971, Kesavananda in 1973, reads as a single campaign fought in alternate institutions. The political-acts reading absorbs the genealogy naturally: a leadership that knows its signature measure will be fought through the courts and the Constitution, and prepares its replacement ordinance and its amendments in advance, is conducting a campaign, not administering a policy. The Nehru government had amended the Constitution for land reform too, but it had amended to protect a settlement already legislated by the provinces; Indira Gandhi amended to protect an acquisition she had personally proclaimed, and the personalisation of the constitutional stakes is one more marker of the distance between the two regimes of the Congress.

The baseline against which all of this broke was the Nehruvian era settlement itself, whose three tensions, between planning and property, between the centre and the states, and between party and government, are set out in [the Nehruvian era settlement of three tensions](/2019/01/25/nehruvian-era-settlement-three-tensions/). Nehru had nationalised by construction and had declined, when pressed by his own left in the 1950s, to nationalise the banks, judging the administrative cost greater than the political gain. His daughter reversed the judgement because her political arithmetic was inverted: for her the administrative cost was bearable and the political gain was the point. The contrast is the cleanest single proof of the article's thesis. The doctrine available to both leaders was identical. The institutions to be seized were identical. What differed was the situation of the leadership, and the outcome differed accordingly.

## Act Two: General Insurance and the Consolidation of 1971

The second act opened in the afterglow of a landslide. The mid term Lok Sabha election of March 1971, fought on the slogan Garibi Hatao, abolish poverty, returned the Congress (R) with 352 seats, a majority that buried the Syndicate's Congress (O) and gave Indira Gandhi the parliamentary instrument her 1969 minority had lacked. The political content of that mandate, and its place in her consolidation, is traced in [the 1971 election and Bangladesh war verdict](/2019/04/12/1971-election-bangladesh-war-india/). A government in that position does not need a dramatic seizure to survive. It needs a programme that converts votes into an administrative fact, and insurance supplied it. On 13 May 1971 the President promulgated the General Insurance (Emergency Provisions) Ordinance, which took over the management of the general insurance companies operating in India and vested control in custodians answerable to the central government. The ownership statute followed at legislative leisure, by the standards of 1969: the General Insurance Business (Nationalisation) Act, Act 57 of 1972, passed in September 1972, acquired the shares of the Indian insurers and the undertakings of the foreign insurers with effect from 1 January 1973. One hundred and seven entities, fifty five Indian insurance companies and the Indian undertakings of fifty two foreign insurers, were amalgamated into four companies: the National Insurance Company at Calcutta, the New India Assurance Company at Bombay, the Oriental Fire and General Insurance Company at New Delhi, and the United India Fire and General Insurance Company at Madras. The General Insurance Corporation, incorporated on 22 November 1972 under the Act, held the shares of the four as their holding company and took the reinsurance functions of the whole system.

### How many insurers did the general insurance nationalisation absorb?

The general insurance nationalisation absorbed 107 entities in all, fifty five Indian insurance companies whose shares the state acquired and the Indian undertakings of fifty two foreign insurers, amalgamated into four subsidiaries held by the General Insurance Corporation from 1 January 1973.

The contrast with the banks is instructive in both directions. Where 1969 had been an ambush, 1971 to 1972 was an occupation conducted in daylight: management first by emergency ordinance, ownership later by statute, amalgamation by administrative scheme. The two stage method was itself a lesson drawn from R.C. Cooper: management takeover under emergency provisions was harder to strike down than instant acquisition, and the interval let the state prepare compensation schedules that would survive the courts. But the political function had also changed. The insurance act did not split a party or unseat a rival. It demonstrated reach. A government that could absorb an entire industry of 107 firms, pension their boards off into custodianship and redraw the map into four regional subsidiaries without a parliamentary crisis was advertising administrative omnipotence to every other sector watching. The State Trading Corporation's expanding remit, the takeover of the wholesale trade in wheat in 1973 and its retreat the following year, and the tightening of industrial licensing under the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act of 1969, effective from June 1970, belonged to the same demonstration. Insurance also carried a quieter fiscal logic that the political reading does not need to deny: premium income and insurance funds were a pool of investible resources the state wished to direct, as the Life Insurance Corporation's funds had been directed since 1956 into government securities and approved investments. The point of adjudication is not which motive existed, since both are documented, but which motive fixed the timing and the form. The timing was the first spring of the Garibi Hatao Parliament. The form was a holding corporation modelled on the state's own administrative convenience. Both facts sit naturally in the political reading and awkwardly in a framing that treats the act as the next item on a socialist checklist compiled in 1955.

The design of the insurance settlement also carried a federal signature that the policy framing rarely notices. The four amalgamated companies were headquartered in four different cities, National Insurance at Calcutta, New India Assurance at Bombay, Oriental at New Delhi and United India at Madras, and the General Insurance Corporation above them was incorporated at Bombay. A ministry consolidating an industry for efficiency alone might have built one corporation in the capital. A political leadership distributed the headquarters as it distributed other spoils of the state, giving each great region a flagship institution, a board to appoint to, and a head office to point to. The funds the system gathered were likewise an instrument: the investment rules of the Insurance Act framework channelled the insurers' accumulations toward government securities and approved investments, so that the premium income of every insured warehouse and mill in the country entered the pool from which the state's development expenditure was financed. None of this required bad faith, and the four companies ran their books as insurers for three decades. It required only the recognition that an amalgamation map, like a threshold, is a political drawing before it is an organisational chart.

Why insurance, and why then, also has an answer in the sociology of the industry. General insurance in 1970 was a business of fire, marine and miscellaneous covers sold to commerce and industry; its policyholders were firms, not households, and its nationalisation injured no voting constituency while pleasing the rhetoric of anti-monopoly. The industry's Indian ownership was interlocked with the same managing agency houses whose bank directorships had been the scandal of 1969, and foreign insurers held a large share, which let the government present the takeover as economic nationalism as well as socialism. No mass protest defended the insurers. The contrast with the political cost of, say, land acquisition or food trade takeover, which the same government attempted in 1973 and abandoned in 1974 after procurement failures and price rises, maps the selectivity of the programme precisely. The state took what was cheap to take and symbolically rich. Selectivity of that kind is a politician's calculus, not a planner's, and it recurs in every act this article examines.

## Act Three: Coal, Taken in Two Movements

Coal was the largest physical acquisition of the decade and the one whose stated grounds were most nearly industrial. The private coal industry of the Raniganj and Jharia fields had a documented record, in official committee reports reaching back to the 1950s, of unscientific working, slaughter mining of the best seams, falsified production figures, and labour conditions that the state had repeatedly condemned and repeatedly failed to police through the Coal Mines (Conservation and Safety) Act and the inspectorate. The industry's owners were fragmented, politically weak outside their districts, and dependent on the railways for transport, which made them the softest of targets. The takeover proceeded in two movements that tracked the war economy of 1971 to 1973. On 16 October 1971, with the Bangladesh crisis building toward the December war, the President promulgated the Coking Coal Mines (Emergency Provisions) Ordinance, taking over the management of the coking coal mines and coke oven plants that fed the steel plants. The Coking Coal Mines (Nationalisation) Act of 1972 then acquired 226 coking coal mines and the coke oven plants, the captive mines of the Tata Iron and Steel Company and the Indian Iron and Steel Company excepted, and vested them from 1 May 1972 in a new public company, Bharat Coking Coal Limited. The second movement generalised the seizure: the management of all coal mines was taken over on 31 January 1973, and the Coal Mines (Nationalisation) Act of 1973 transferred ownership of 711 mines, coking and non coking, across seven states to the state from 1 May 1973, under a Coal Mines Authority that gave way in November 1975 to Coal India Limited as holding company.

The two movement structure is the coal case's contribution to the article's argument. Emergency management first, ownership after an interval, a state corporation built to receive the assets, captive exceptions carved out for the steel industry whose cooperation the state required: this is the mature form of the instrument that 1969 had improvised and 1970 had repaired. By 1973 nationalisation had become a repeatable administrative technology with a standard sequence, and its deployment no longer needed a leadership crisis to justify haste. What it still needed was a political warrant, and the warrant was supplied by the post war atmosphere: the government that had won the December 1971 war and the March 1972 state elections governed India in 1973 as a state in command, extending its ownership over the fuel of the industrial economy as a matter of course. The energy argument was genuine; coal output had stagnated under private ownership and the Fourth Plan's targets assumed a public sector expansion. But the choice to acquire the entire industry, including hundreds of small non coking mines whose economics were wretched, rather than to regulate, consolidate or acquire selectively, expressed the era's premise that ownership itself was the proof of seriousness. Francine Frankel, in India's Political Economy 1947 to 2004, her study first published in 1978 and revised in 2005, reads the post 1969 expansion of the public sector as the economic arm of a political strategy she calls the politics of gradualism abandoned: a leadership that, lacking an organisational machine, bought mass support with state action. The coal case fits her description with unusual neatness, because the industry's workers, concentrated in the Bihar and Bengal fields, were an organised constituency whose unions the left parties and the Congress each courted, and public ownership converted their employer into the ruling party's government.

The minister who drove the coal takeover embodies the era's fusion of doctrine and office. Mohan Kumaramangalam, a leading member of the Communist Party of India who had joined the Congress and entered the Cabinet in 1971 as Minister of Steel and Mines, was the acquisition's administrative author: the coking coal ordinance of October 1971 and the general takeover of January 1973 were his department's work, defended by him in Parliament as the recovery of a looted national asset. Kumaramangalam died in the air crash of 30 May 1973, weeks after the Coal Mines (Nationalisation) Act completed his design, and the timing froze his reputation into the act itself. His presence in the Cabinet is a fact each framing claims. For the socialist reading he is proof of ideological seriousness at the centre of government. For the political reading he is proof of coalition engineering, a communist talent installed to give the programme its left credential and its administrative drive in one appointment. The industry's own reckoning came on 27 December 1975, when an explosion and flooding at the Chasnala colliery of the Indian Iron and Steel Company killed 372 miners, the worst disaster in Indian mining history, in a mine the state itself owned and a sector the state had taken over in the name of the safety record of private owners. Chasnala did not reverse the settlement; no voice of consequence proposed returning the mines. It stands in the record instead as the moment the costs of the coal act acquired a casualty list, and the adjudication of the act has had to carry both the committee reports that indicted the private owners and the disaster that indicted their successors.

Coal also exposes the costs that the political reading must carry honestly if it is to adjudicate rather than merely prosecute. Output per man shift in the nationalised industry stagnated for years even as total production climbed from roughly 79 million tonnes in the year of the Act toward 114 million tonnes by 1980 and 1981 on the official series; Coal India inherited the private owners' underinvestment along with their pits, and the gap between demand and supply widened into the shortages of the later 1970s. The tonnage rose because the state poured capital and labour into the fields, which is precisely the distinction the critics drew: the acquisition could mobilise inputs on a scale the fragmented owners never had, and it could not, by the same token, make the inputs answer to a commercial test, because the corporation's instructions ran from the ministry's plan targets and the politics of the coal constituencies rather than from the price of coal. The efficiency critique of nationalisation, pressed at the time by the Swatantra tradition and later by economists such as Jagdish Bhagwati in his writings on the Indian controlled economy, rested on evidence of exactly this kind, and the adjudication section weighs it. What the record does not support is the inference that the costs were the purpose or that the decision was taken in ignorance of them. The committee reports on the coal industry had forecast the investment burden for a decade. A government that reads the forecasts and acquires anyway has told the observer its order of preferences. In 1973 the order of preferences put command first.

## The Wider Field: Oil, Textiles, Privy Purses and the Regulatory Ring

The four principal acts did not stand alone, and the measures around them disclose the same grammar at smaller scale. The abolition of the privy purses, the payments guaranteed to the former princely rulers at accession, began as a constitutional bill that the Rajya Sabha rejected on 5 September 1970, when the motion fell one vote short of the required two thirds majority, 149 votes to 75. The government answered within a day with a presidential order derecognising the rulers wholesale, and when the Supreme Court struck that order down in December 1970 in the case brought by Madhavrao Scindia, the government waited for its 1971 majority and abolished the purses, the titles and the privileges by the Twenty Sixth Amendment of December 1971. The sequence, traced in full in [the privy purse abolition account](/2019/04/06/privy-purse-abolition-1971-india/), is the purest specimen of the era's method: an institution is attacked, a court or a chamber blocks the attack, and the blockage is converted into the argument for a larger majority and a constitutional amendment. The sums involved were trivial against the Union budget. The symbol, a republic abolishing its last hereditary payments in the name of equality, was the substance, and the symbol was aimed at the same audience as the bank ordinance.

Oil followed the grammar in its most diplomatic register. The three foreign oil companies that dominated refining and marketing, Esso, Burmah Shell and Caltex, were acquired in sequence as their concession arrangements came up for negotiation: the Esso (Acquisition of Undertakings in India) Act of 1974 transferred Esso's Indian undertaking to what became the Hindustan Petroleum Corporation; the Burmah Shell acquisition of January 1976 created Bharat Refineries, renamed Bharat Petroleum; and the Caltex undertaking passed to the state in 1977, its assets merged into Hindustan Petroleum, while the Indian Oil Corporation, public since 1964, absorbed the marketing network. Each acquisition was negotiated rather than seized, compensation was settled rather than imposed, and the diplomatic costs with the United States and Britain were managed as statecraft, a reminder that the political-acts reading also governs [the foreign policy as domestic politics framework](/2020/09/21/non-alignment-indian-domestic-politics/) in reverse: here a domestic assertion was calibrated to what foreign capitals would absorb. The timing still obeyed the domestic calendar. The oil takeovers of 1974 to 1977 rode the politics of the first oil shock, when the quadrupling of crude prices after October 1973 made foreign ownership of the fuel supply a standing grievance that any opposition could exploit, and they were complete before the Emergency ended.

Textiles supplied the defensive form of the instrument. The National Textile Corporation had been created in 1968 to manage sick mills whose managements the state had taken over under the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act, and the Sick Textile Undertakings (Nationalisation) Act, Act 57 of 1974, preceded by an ordinance of 21 September 1974 and effective from the appointed day of 1 April 1974, nationalised 103 such mills outright. Here the state acquired not a commanding height but a hospital ward: mills that private owners could not run and could not be allowed to close, in Bombay, Ahmedabad, Kanpur and the other mill towns where a closure meant thousands of workers on the street. The political content of the act was employment protection wearing the clothes of socialism, and its fiscal content was a permanent subsidy charged to the exchequer. A planner's programme would have triaged the mills. A politician's programme nationalised the category, because the category, not the mill, was what the unions and the mill town MPs could see. The regulatory ring completed the field: the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act of 1973, in force from 1 January 1974, capped foreign equity in most sectors at forty percent and required dilution of the great multinational subsidiaries, a requirement whose enforcement in 1977, under the Janata government rather than Indira Gandhi's, drove IBM and Coca Cola out of India rather than dilute. That the instrument was wielded with equal enthusiasm by her successors and opponents is the strongest evidence that by the middle 1970s the grammar had ceased to be one leader's property and had become the common language of Indian economic statecraft.

The one great failure of the repertoire is as instructive as its successes. In April 1973 the government took over the wholesale trade in wheat, announcing that the Food Corporation of India and the state agencies would buy the marketable surplus and end the trader's margin between farmer and consumer. The takeover had every formal feature of the nationalisation grammar: a commanding commodity, a monopoly intermediary class cast as the villain, a state corporation as the instrument, and a Garibi Hatao warrant. It collapsed within a year. Procurement fell far short of target because the official price was set below the market the state had abolished on paper and could not abolish in the mandis, the rabi crop of 1974 was lifted under a restored private trade, and the episode entered the record as the decade's clearest demonstration that the grammar worked only where the state could actually operate the seized machinery. Banks, insurance and coal could be run by custodians and corporations because their operations were ledger based, salaried and concentrated. A grain trade dispersed across millions of transactions could not. The selectivity of the four acts, in other words, was not an accident of drafting. The state took what its administrative machine could hold, and the failure at wheat marks the machine's true boundary, which the successful acts had silently respected. A socialist programme in the abstract has no such boundary; a political instrument, calibrated to what a government can seize over a weekend and run on Monday, has exactly this one.

## The Opposition's India: The Critics and Their Long Defeat

No adjudication of the nationalisations is complete without the opposition that lost to them, because the opposition's defeat, repeated across the decade, is part of the evidence for what the acts were doing. The organised defence of private property in Indian public life had an institutional home older than the acts themselves: the Forum of Free Enterprise, founded in Bombay in 1956 by the industrialist and banker A. D. Shroff, which published pamphlets, convened lectures and briefed parliamentarians against the expanding public sector through the 1960s. The Forum's campaign against bank nationalisation in 1969 restated the argument Rajagopalachari had made since he founded the Swatantra Party in 1959: that the Indian state's appetite for ownership was a function of its politicians' appetite for patronage, that public ownership would not democratise credit but bureaucratise it, and that the true beneficiary of every acquisition was the ruling party's machine. C. Rajagopalachari, writing in his journal Swarajya through the 1969 crisis, treated the ordinance as a constitutional impropriety as much as an economic error, an executive seizure timed to a party quarrel, which is, from the opposite pole of Indian politics, the same observation this article has made from the record. The critics, in other words, saw the political act plainly. What they could not do was defeat it, because the constituency for the defence of the fourteen banks' shareholders could never be assembled in a country where the depositors, the employees and the borrowers all gained standing from the seizure.

The Jana Sangh's trajectory shows the constraint from the other side. A party of traders and the urban middle classes, committed in its programme to swadeshi and to limits on concentration, it could not defend the banking houses without branding itself as their instrument, and its parliamentary spokesmen accordingly attacked the manner of the acts, the ordinance route, the compensation, the politicisation to come, while conceding the end of breaking concentrated control. The concession disarmed the critique: a voter persuaded that concentration had to be broken had no reason to prefer the party that would break it by regulation to the leader who had broken it by midnight. Within a decade the argument from property had lost its national vehicle altogether. Swatantra dissolved in 1974, its remnants absorbed into Charan Singh's formations, and the Janata coalition that won in 1977 contained socialists and former Congressmen who retained every acquisition of the decade. The political-acts reading draws the conclusion the sequence invites. The nationalisations were not merely popular in the diffuse sense of opinion polls; they were structured so that every organised opponent had to concede their premise, split their criticism, or defend an indefensible client. That is craftsmanship of a specifically political kind, and it is invisible to any account that begins with the economics of banking and ends with the ideology of the Congress.

## Garibi Hatao: What the Mandate Bought

If the nationalisations were political acts, their returns should be visible in political currency, and the returns were collected in March 1971. The Congress (R) fought that election on Garibi Hatao against the opposition's counter slogan Indira Hatao, remove Indira, and the campaign's economic content was substantially a recital of the acts this article has walked through: the banks taken, the purses abolished, the ceilings promised, the monopolies named as enemies. Inder Malhotra, in Indira Gandhi: A Personal and Political Biography, published in 1989, treats the bank nationalisation as the decisive turn in her public image, the act that converted a Prime Minister derided in 1966 as a gungi gudiya, a dumb doll, in Ram Manohar Lohia's phrase, into the leader the electorate rewarded in 1971. Pranay Gupte's Mother India: A Political Biography of Indira Gandhi, published in 2009, reaches a compatible judgement from the other end of her career, reading the nationalisations as instruments of a centralising populism that substituted spectacular state action for party organisation. The electoral arithmetic supports both biographers. The Congress (R) won 352 of 518 seats with 43.7 percent of the vote, the Congress (O) collapsed to 16 seats, and the Swatantra Party, the parliamentary home of the property critique, fell from 44 seats in 1967 to 8. The state elections of March 1972 extended the sweep. Whatever else the acts had done to credit, coal or insurance, they had rebuilt a ruling majority out of a party that had expelled its own Prime Minister sixteen months earlier.

The programme also bought administrative facts that outlasted the slogans. The Lead Bank Scheme of December 1969, prepared by the Reserve Bank on the recommendations of the Gadgil study group and the Nariman committee, assigned every district to a public sector bank charged with surveying credit needs and leading branch expansion; the branch network grew from 8,262 offices in June 1969 to more than thirty thousand by the end of the following decade, and the rural share of offices rose from 1,833, roughly a fifth, toward two fifths of the total. Priority sector lending, formalised as a category in 1972 and given a target of a third of net bank credit by 1979 at the finance minister's conference of 1974, redirected the flow of the nationalised system toward agriculture, small industry and exports. Defenders of the acts, from the government's own white papers to sympathetic economists, rest their case on these numbers, and the numbers are real. The political-acts reading does not dispute them; it disputes their place in the explanation. Branch expansion on that scale was achievable because ownership had removed the commercial objection to unprofitable rural branches, and ownership had been acquired for reasons of party survival in a particular July. The effects were economic. The act was political. Holding both propositions at once is the whole of the argument, and the failure to hold them at once is what makes the rival framings, socialist celebration and liberal lament alike, read the decade wrong.

The Twenty Point Programme, announced on 1 July 1975 in the first week of the Emergency, showed the programme in its administered form: land ceiling enforcement, abolition of bonded labour, which followed by statute in 1976, liquidation of rural debt, house sites for the landless, and a schedule of production and price commitments. Political scientists who studied the Emergency's economics noted that the programme's content was largely a compilation of the promises of 1969 to 1971, henceforth to be delivered by executive order rather than by legislation. That continuity is the point. The nationalisations had established a repertoire in which the state's ownership and command were the standing proof of the government's intentions toward the poor, and the repertoire could be replayed, in 1975 as emergency discipline and in 1980 as restoration, whenever the leadership required it.

## The Janata Interregnum: A Ratchet That Did Not Reverse

The severest test of any reading of the nationalisations is the behaviour of the government elected to repudiate their author. The Janata Party won the general election of March 1977 on a platform of restoring democracy after the Emergency, and its economic thinking contained a genuine anti-statist strand, Gandhian in origin, suspicious of both private monopoly and public gigantism. Morarji Desai, the Finance Minister expelled from Indira Gandhi's Cabinet on the road to the 1969 ordinance, became Prime Minister. If nationalisation had been merely one leader's policy preference, 1977 was the moment of reversal. No reversal came. The banks, the insurance corporations and Coal India remained in public ownership throughout the Janata years of 1977 to 1979 and the Charan Singh caretaker months that followed. The Janata government criticised the management of the public sector, appointed the Arjun Sengupta era committees of a later decade in embryo through its own industrial policy statement of December 1977, which reserved fresh ground for small scale industry, and wielded FERA against IBM and Coca Cola with an enthusiasm Indira Gandhi's own government had not mustered. It did not denationalise a single undertaking acquired between 1969 and 1974.

The Janata government's own economic signature confirms the reading from the positive side. Its Industrial Policy Statement of 23 December 1977, presented by the Industries Minister, George Fernandes, did not promise a private sector restoration; it promised a different distribution of state favour, shifting emphasis to small scale and cottage industry, reserving an expanded list of products, eventually more than eight hundred items, for the small sector alone, and creating District Industries Centres from 1978 to carry official credit and clearances to the taluka town. The grammar of command was retained in full; only the designated beneficiaries changed, from the commanding heights to the dispersed small producer. A government that had campaigned against the Emergency's authoritarianism found, on taking office, that the instruments of economic direction built between 1969 and 1974 were the instruments nearest its hand, and it used them. The settlement was not Indira Gandhi's possession by 1977. It was the Indian state's.

The non reversal is data. It shows that by 1977 the nationalisations had passed out of the category of contestable policy into the category of settlement, part of the institutional baseline that [the Nehruvian era settlement](/2019/01/25/nehruvian-era-settlement-three-tensions/) had once supplied and that every subsequent government inherited. The reasons were structural as much as ideological: hundreds of thousands of employees, a branch network woven into every district administration, and an electorate that associated the public banks with its own savings made restitution to the former owners unthinkable for any party that wished to govern. The political-acts reading predicts exactly this ratchet. An act performed to rearrange power, once performed, rearranges the interests that later politicians must consult; the banks acquired to defeat the Syndicate became, within eight years, a constituency no Prime Minister could afford to offend. Policy framings that treat nationalisation as a reversible instrument setting, like a tax rate, cannot explain why the setting never moved. The ratchet also disciplines the celebratory version of the socialist framing: a programme whose irreversibility rests on the constituencies it creates is being sustained by politics, not by demonstrated economic superiority, and its defenders and critics alike argued from 1977 onward inside a settlement that politics had made.

## Act Four: Six Banks and the Meaning of Restoration

Indira Gandhi returned to office in January 1980 at the head of a Congress (I) majority of 353 seats, in the election whose dynamics are traced in [the 1980 Indira return to power account](/2019/05/04/1980-indira-return-power/). On 15 April 1980, within the first hundred days of the restored government, the President promulgated the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Ordinance of 1980, nationalising every bank whose deposits, measured on 14 March 1980, stood at two hundred crore rupees or more. Six banks crossed the line: the Andhra Bank, the Corporation Bank, the New Bank of India, the Oriental Bank of Commerce, the Punjab and Sind Bank, and the Vijaya Bank. Parliament replaced the ordinance with the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, Act 40 of 1980, which received presidential assent on 11 July 1980. The Act's statement of objects and reasons reached back across the decade for its vocabulary, declaring the purpose to be further control over the heights of the economy, and it tied the acquisition to the government's commitment to the Twenty Point Programme and to raising priority sector credit toward forty percent of total advances. With the six added to the fourteen of 1969 and the State Bank group, the public sector's share of scheduled commercial bank deposits rose to roughly ninety one percent. No Indian bank of consequence remained outside state ownership except the foreign banks and a residue of small private institutions.

The campaign that preceded the act had promised order rather than transformation: the Congress (I) fought the 1980 election on the appeal to elect a government that works, and its manifesto treated the economic programme of the 1970s as a settled inheritance to be administered competently rather than a frontier to be extended. That makes the April ordinance the more telling. A government elected on administrative competence, with no socialist crusade in its manifesto and no rival inside its party left to outflank, still reached within a hundred days for the signature instrument of 1969. The explanation that remains is the one this article has built: the act was addressed to memory. It told the electorate of the mill towns, the bank counters and the coalfields that the order they had known, in which the state owned the heights and answered for the economy in person, had resumed after an interregnum that the ordinance retrospectively reclassified as an interruption. Restoration governments deal in such signals because their legitimacy is comparative. The six banks were the comparison, stated in the only language the previous decade had made unmistakable.

### Why did the second bank nationalisation come in April 1980?

The second nationalisation came in April 1980 because a government restored after three years out of power needed an unmistakable signature act, and repeating the most popular measure of 1969, against banks that had grown past a new threshold, announced that the settlement of the 1970s remained in force.

Nothing in the banking situation of the spring of 1980 compelled the date. The six banks were solvent, growing and already subject to the full apparatus of Reserve Bank direction, priority sector targets and social control obligations that bound every scheduled bank. No committee had reported in favour of a second round that winter; no crisis in the six institutions required rescue, as sickness had required it in textiles. What the spring of 1980 contained was a political requirement. The restored Prime Minister governed a party rebuilt in her own name, faced state assembly elections due within weeks, and confronted a single question that her opponents in the Janata remnant and the Lok Dal pressed daily: whether the verdict of 1977 had permanently discredited her method of rule. The ordinance answered the question in the vocabulary of 1969. Its threshold arithmetic was a quotation: where 1969 had drawn the line at fifty crore, 1980 drew it at two hundred crore, the same instrument recalibrated to the same logic, that any private bank which grew to commanding size would be taken. The message to the banking system was that the ceiling on private banking was permanent policy, and the message to the electorate was that the author of Garibi Hatao had resumed her authorship. An act whose entire content is a restatement is the limiting case of the political act, and it is the strongest evidence in this article, because a policy framing has nothing to explain it with. There was no new policy in April 1980. There was only the signature.

The legal ground, meanwhile, had been cleared by the government's enemies. The Forty Fourth Amendment of 1978, enacted by the Janata Parliament in its project of constitutional repair after the Emergency, deleted the fundamental right to property from Part III and left property as the constitutional right of Article 300A, defeasible by law. The amendment's authors intended to close the era of property litigation that had run from the zamindari cases through Cooper and Kesavananda. Its effect in 1980 was that no R.C. Cooper could arise against the second nationalisation: the shareholders' remedy lay in the statute's own terms, and the statute fixed the amounts. The six banks passed into public ownership without a constitutional convulsion, their custodians appointed, their boards reconstituted, their names retained, in a transaction the newspapers treated as routine. The contrast with 1969 and 1970 could not be sharper, and it completes the arc this article has traced: an instrument invented in a crisis, tested in the courts, entrenched by constitutional amendment, protected by its own beneficiaries' successors, and finally reapplied as a formality. By 1980 nationalisation no longer needed Indira Gandhi's desperation to justify it. It needed only her return.

## A World of Nationalisations

India's decade of acquisition unfolded inside a world season of nationalisation, and the comparison disciplines any claim of Indian exceptionalism. Zambia took majority control of its copper mines in 1969 and 1970. Chile nationalised its great copper companies by constitutional amendment in July 1971 under Salvador Allende. The oil exporters of the Middle East and North Africa converted concessions into state ownership across the same years, and Britain's Labour governments of the 1970s brought shipbuilding and aerospace into public ownership by statute. The repertoire was international, its techniques standardised: threshold, compensation in bonds, a holding corporation, a rhetoric of resource sovereignty. Indian administrators studied the same precedents foreign administrators studied, and the Reserve Bank's historians recorded the parallels. What distinguishes the Indian sequence inside the world season is not the instrument but the use. In Zambia and Chile nationalisation was the programme of a governing ideology carried into office on the question itself. In India the doctrine had been available since 1955 and had slept for fourteen years; it woke on the weekend of a Deputy Prime Minister's resignation. The world supplied Indira Gandhi's governments with a respectable repertoire and a ready vocabulary, and the vocabulary's respectability was part of its utility: a seizure that finance ministries on three continents were performing could be presented to the Indian voter not as a party manoeuvre but as the spirit of the age. The political-acts reading accommodates the comparison without strain. It claims not that the acts were invented in New Delhi, but that their Indian timing was made there, in the arithmetic of the Congress Parliamentary Party, the calendar of a presidential election and the first hundred days of a restoration. The tide was global. The Saturdays were local.

## Adjudicating the Three Framings

Three readings of the decade compete in the literature, and the stance of this article requires each to be given at its strongest before the verdict. The socialist-policy framing, held by the Congress tradition's own historians and by sympathetic scholars of the period, reads the acts as the delayed execution of a founding commitment. Its evidence is formidable: the Karachi resolution of 1931 had demanded state ownership of key industries before independence; the Avadi resolution of 1955 named the socialist pattern as the party's objective; Articles 39(b) and 39(c) supplied the constitutional warrant; and the credit record after 1969, the branch network in the villages, the priority sector targets, the deposit mobilisation from Rs 4,646 crore in 1969 toward a mass banking system, shows a state using ownership for the purposes it proclaimed. On this reading the timing is opportunism in the service of doctrine, and opportunism does not falsify the doctrine. The policy-history framing, held by the administrative and banking histories, including the Reserve Bank's own official history of the period, reads the acts as the Indian state's learning curve: social control tried and found unenforceable, acquisition substituted, defects litigated and repaired, the method then standardised across insurance, coal and oil as each sector's file matured. Its evidence is the documentary trail of committees, from the Banking Laws Committee to the coal industry inquiries, and it explains the two stage forms and the compensation repairs better than any rival.

The efficiency critique, the third voice, belongs properly to neither framing and must be heard separately, because it judges outcomes rather than intentions. Jagdish Bhagwati, writing on the Indian economy's control regime in the 1990s with the nationalised decades in view, argued that public ownership fused with directed credit converted the banking system into an arm of political discretion, with consequences in loan quality that the following decades exposed. The coal record gives the critique its hardest facts: a nationalised industry that missed its production targets through the later 1970s while demand compounded. Insurance under monopoly drew the service critique that the Malhotra Committee of 1993 to 1994 later documented in detail when it recommended reopening the sector. A political-acts reading that waved these costs away would be apologetics. The costs were real, they were foreseeable, and committee reports had forecast several of them before the acquisitions were made. The question the adjudication must answer is not whether the costs existed but what explains a sequence of decisions that repeatedly accepted them.

The political-acts reading answers that question where the other two cannot, on the four facts that any adequate account must digest. First, the timing: every principal act fell within weeks of a political event that raised the return on dramatic state action, the Desai resignation and presidential contest in 1969, the Garibi Hatao landslide and the refugee war in 1971, the post war assertion of command in 1973, the restoration of 1980, and no act fell in the politically quiet years of 1966 to 1968 or 1978 to 1979 when the same doctrines and the same committees' reports were available. Second, the instruments: ordinances at midnight, thresholds as classifications, compensation fixed to preclude negotiation, each optimised for speed and fait accompli rather than for deliberative policy design. Third, the escalation: when the courts blocked the programme, the response was not redesign but constitutional amendment aimed at the blocking clauses, the Twenty Fourth and Twenty Fifth Amendments, a response intelligible only if the acts carried a value beyond their economic content. Fourth, the ratchet: the opposition that inherited the state in 1977, led by the very man whose expulsion from the finance ministry had triggered the first act, reversed nothing. Doctrines can be repealed by successors who disbelieve them; settlements created by political acts defend themselves through the interests they organise.

The strongest counter to this reading is the one the brief for this article itself insists on facing: that the socialist content was not a costume. The governments of 1969 to 1980 believed, at the level of their own stated reasons and of much of their cadres' conviction, that they were building a socialist order, and the Garibi Hatao programme's redistributive content, land ceilings, debt relief, the abolition of bonded labour, was pursued with an energy that pure theatre cannot explain. The counter is accepted in its facts and answered in its logic. The political-acts reading has never required the actors to be cynics. It requires only that the form, timing and selection of the measures be explained by the political situations in which they were taken, and those situations explain why this doctrine, among the many the Congress tradition contained, was the one cashed, and why it was cashed on those Saturdays and in those springs. A belief sincerely held can still be a weapon; indeed only sincerely held beliefs make durable weapons, since the electorate of 1971 could not have been mobilised by a programme its own author visibly disowned. Francine Frankel's account in India's Political Economy 1947 to 2004 remains the most serious scholarly version of the counter, because she concedes the politics and contests the depth: for Frankel the nationalisations were the economic arm of a strategy of accommodation continued by other means, radical in gesture and conservative in structural effect, leaving land relations and the industrial houses' diversified empires substantially intact. The concession is the verdict. A reading that foregrounds gesture, timing and coalition management, and treats structural transformation as the road not taken, is the political-acts reading under a sociologist's vocabulary. Where this article parts from Frankel is only in emphasis: the gesture was not a substitute for the act's content but its mechanism, since the banks really were taken, the credit really did move, and the settlement really did bind her successors. Politics was not the wrapping of these nationalisations. It was their operating system.

## The Architecture the Reforms Inherited

The decade built an architecture that the rest of the century had to reckon with. By 1980 the Indian state owned the banking system's commanding heights, the whole of general and life insurance, the coal industry, the greater part of refining and petroleum marketing, the largest steel capacity, and a textile corporation that warehoused the industry's failures. The reforms of 1991, whose politics are analysed in [the 1991 Rao government silent reforms account](/2019/05/26/1991-rao-government-silent-reforms/), dismantled the licensing system that had governed private industry and opened trade and investment; they did not, in their first decade, denationalise the commanding heights. The banks acquired in 1969 and 1980 were recapitalised, partially diluted in the markets after 1994, and set to compete with new private banks licensed in 1993 and 1994; they were not returned. Insurance was reopened by the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority Act of 1999, a generation after the General Insurance Corporation's incorporation, and the four subsidiaries were delinked from it in 2000 to 2002 rather than sold. Coal allocation was liberalised by amendment in stages across the same decades. The pattern is the final proof of the ratchet described above: what had been acquired by political acts could be modified only by political acts of comparable deliberation, and the architects of 1991, governing with a minority and a crisis, chose to build beside the nationalised architecture rather than demolish it.

The architecture's later pathologies belong to the reckoning, because they were the price the settlement eventually presented. In Indira Gandhi's second term the discretionary possibilities of public ownership were exploited in the open: the loan melas of 1982 onward, mass lending ceremonies conducted by the Minister of State for Finance, Janardhan Poojary, at which public sector bank officers sanctioned loans to assembled applicants under political direction, became the emblem of credit as patronage in the critics' literature. Through the 1980s the system's directed lending, its tolerance of arrears from public enterprises and its administered interest rates compressed the banks' profitability until, by the end of the decade, several of the nationalised banks carried balance sheets that the prudential norms of the coming era would expose. The Narasimham Committee of 1991, appointed by the reform government and reporting that November, recommended the reduction of statutory pre-emptions, the introduction of income recognition and provisioning norms, and the phasing down of directed credit, a verdict on the settlement's operating costs delivered in the driest committee prose. The committee did not recommend denationalisation. Even its reformers treated the ownership settlement of 1969 to 1980 as a fixed fact and confined their surgery to its management. The pathology and the forbearance together close the article's evidentiary circle: the acts had created interests and expectations so deep that the most reform minded committee of the century audited the machine and left the title deeds untouched.

That is the sense in which the nationalisations of 1969 to 1980 remain the foundational economic acts of the republic's second generation. The Nehruvian state had built public enterprise on green fields and left the inherited private commanding heights standing, a duality recorded in [the Nehruvian planned economy reanalysed](/2020/11/20/nehruvian-planned-economy-reanalysed/). Indira Gandhi's governments abolished the duality by acquisition, and they did it four times, in four sectors, across eleven years, under four different political skies, with a consistency of method that no doctrine alone can explain and no administrative history alone can motivate. The method was the message: the state, acting through ordinance, threshold and holding corporation, at the moment of its leader's maximum need, in the vocabulary of the founding Constitution. Read the acts as socialist policy and the Saturdays are a mystery. Read them as policy history and the expulsions, amendments and restorations that surround them fall out of the frame. Read them as political acts and the whole record, the doctrine and the deposits, the judgments and the landslides, the ratchet and the reforms that later worked around it, resolves into one intelligible sequence. That resolution is the claim with which this article began, and the record, walked through in order, is its evidence.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: When were 14 banks nationalised by Indira?

The fourteen banks were nationalised on 19 July 1969, when the President promulgated the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Ordinance on the advice of Indira Gandhi's government. The ordinance took effect at midnight and covered every scheduled commercial bank with deposits of fifty crore rupees or more, including the Central Bank of India, the Bank of India, the Punjab National Bank, the Bank of Baroda and the Canara Bank. Parliament converted the ordinance into an Act that received presidential assent on 9 August 1969. The date fell three days after Morarji Desai resigned as Finance Minister and two days before the monsoon session of Parliament opened.

### Q: What was the 1980 second bank nationalisation?

The 1980 second bank nationalisation was the acquisition of six more commercial banks by ordinance on 15 April 1980, three months after Indira Gandhi returned to office in the January 1980 general election. The ordinance covered banks with deposits of two hundred crore rupees or more measured on 14 March 1980: the Andhra Bank, the Corporation Bank, the New Bank of India, the Oriental Bank of Commerce, the Punjab and Sind Bank and the Vijaya Bank. Parliament enacted the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, Act 40 of 1980, in July 1980. With this round, the public sector's share of scheduled bank deposits rose to roughly ninety one percent.

### Q: What was the 1971 General Insurance nationalisation?

The general insurance nationalisation began on 13 May 1971, when an emergency provisions ordinance took over the management of every general insurance company operating in India and placed the business under government custodians. Ownership followed through the General Insurance Business (Nationalisation) Act of 1972, which acquired the shares of fifty five Indian insurers and the undertakings of fifty two foreign insurers with effect from 1 January 1973. The 107 entities were amalgamated into four companies, the National Insurance Company, the New India Assurance Company, the Oriental Insurance Company and the United India Insurance Company, held as subsidiaries of the General Insurance Corporation, incorporated on 22 November 1972.

### Q: What was the 1973 coal mines nationalisation?

The 1973 coal mines nationalisation was the transfer of ownership of India's coal mines to the state under the Coal Mines (Nationalisation) Act of 1973, effective from 1 May 1973. It completed a sequence that began with the management takeover of coking coal mines by ordinance on 16 October 1971 and the acquisition of 226 coking mines under Bharat Coking Coal Limited from 1 May 1972. The management of all mines was taken over on 31 January 1973, and the 1973 Act then vested 711 coking and non coking mines across seven states in public ownership, administered first by the Coal Mines Authority and from November 1975 by Coal India Limited.

### Q: How did bank nationalisation operate as political act?

Bank nationalisation operated as a political act because its timing, instrument and targets were fixed by the leadership struggle inside the Congress rather than by a banking timetable. Indira Gandhi struck three days after Morarji Desai's resignation, by ordinance on a Saturday, while the Congress presidential contest was open and the Syndicate was preparing her removal. The act split her opponents, supplied the programme on which her wing fought the party split of November 1969, and built the popular platform that won the 1971 election. Its economic consequences were vast, but the decision's order of causation ran from party survival to the banks, which is what the political-acts reading claims.

### Q: What was the post-1969 Garibi Hatao political content?

The post-1969 Garibi Hatao political content was the programme that converted the bank nationalisation into an electoral mandate. Garibi Hatao, abolish poverty, was the Congress (R) slogan for the March 1971 mid term election, and its content joined the nationalisations to land ceilings, the abolition of privy purses, credit for small farmers and artisans through the nationalised banks, and action against monopoly houses. The slogan answered the opposition's Indira Hatao campaign by making the Prime Minister's economic acts the substance of her appeal. The Congress (R) won 352 seats, and the programme was later administered in extended form as the Twenty Point Programme announced in July 1975.

### Q: What is the four-architectural-act framework for Indira nationalisations?

The four-architectural-act framework is the analytical map this article defends for reading Indira Gandhi's nationalisations as one class of action. Act One is the nationalisation of fourteen banks on 19 July 1969, with its court defeat in R.C. Cooper and its repair in 1970. Act Two is the takeover of general insurance, begun in May 1971 and completed by the Act of 1972. Act Three is the coal nationalisation of 1972 to 1973. Act Four is the second bank nationalisation of 15 April 1980. The framework holds that the four acts share a grammar of ordinance, threshold and holding corporation, each timed to a political conjuncture, and that the shared grammar, rather than socialist doctrine alone, explains the sequence.

### Q: How did the Twenty Fifth Amendment change the compensation rule after the Cooper judgment?

The Twenty Fifth Amendment of 1971 changed the compensation rule by replacing the word compensation in Article 31 of the Constitution with the word amount, so that a law acquiring property had only to fix an amount or state principles for determining it, and the adequacy of that amount could not be questioned in court on the ground that it was not a fair equivalent. The amendment answered the R.C. Cooper judgment of 10 February 1970, which had struck down the 1969 banking Act partly because its compensation principles were illusory. The same amendment inserted Article 31C, shielding laws that gave effect to Articles 39(b) and 39(c) from challenge under Articles 14, 19 and 31.

### Q: Which foreign oil companies were taken over in the 1970s, and in what order?

Three foreign oil companies were taken over in sequence. Esso came first, under the Esso (Acquisition of Undertakings in India) Act of 1974, its refining and marketing business passing to the corporation that became Hindustan Petroleum. Burmah Shell followed in January 1976, its undertaking acquired and renamed Bharat Refineries, later Bharat Petroleum. Caltex came last in 1977, its Indian undertaking merged into Hindustan Petroleum. Unlike the banks, the oil companies were acquired by negotiated settlement as their concession arrangements matured, with compensation agreed rather than imposed, and the Indian Oil Corporation, in public ownership since 1964, absorbed much of the combined marketing network.

### Q: Why were sick textile mills nationalised in 1974 rather than closed?

The sick textile mills were nationalised in 1974 because closure would have thrown thousands of mill workers out of employment in politically sensitive mill towns, while continued private ownership had already failed. The National Textile Corporation, created in 1968, had been managing mills taken over under the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act after their owners could no longer run them. The Sick Textile Undertakings (Nationalisation) Act, Act 57 of 1974, preceded by an ordinance of 21 September 1974 and effective from 1 April 1974, acquired 103 such mills outright. Nationalisation converted an employment guarantee the state was already honouring in practice into a permanent public liability, which a government of the Garibi Hatao period preferred to the politics of mass closure.

### Q: How did the 1973 coal nationalisation treat the captive mines of the steel companies?

The coal nationalisation exempted the captive mines of the integrated steel producers. When the Coking Coal Mines (Nationalisation) Act of 1972 acquired 226 coking coal mines and coke oven plants for Bharat Coking Coal Limited, the mines held by the Tata Iron and Steel Company and the Indian Iron and Steel Company for their own steelworks were left outside the acquisition, and the Coal Mines (Nationalisation) Act of 1973 preserved the exemption. The carve out was pragmatic statecraft: the steel plants depended on their captive coking coal for supply security, and the government, which owned the Indian Iron and Steel Company itself after its takeover in 1972, had no wish to disrupt steel production in the act of securing its fuel.

### Q: What share of banking deposits did the state control after the 1980 nationalisation?

After the 1980 nationalisation the public sector controlled roughly ninety one percent of scheduled commercial bank deposits in India. The State Bank of India and its associates, public since 1955 and 1959, the fourteen banks of 1969 and the six banks of 1980 together left only the foreign banks and a residue of small private banks outside state ownership. The figure is the measure of the settlement the four acts created: banking had become a public utility in ownership terms, and it remained so through the reforms of 1991, which recapitalised and partially diluted the public banks and licensed new private competitors rather than returning the nationalised institutions to their former owners.

### Q: How did the Swatantra Party and the Jana Sangh attack the nationalisations in Parliament?

The Swatantra Party attacked the nationalisations as expropriation that would subordinate the citizen's savings to the ruling party's power; Minoo Masani, its leader in the Lok Sabha, argued against the 1969 banking bill that public ownership of credit was the road to political control of economic life, in the tradition of C. Rajagopalachari's founding critique of the permit licence system. The Bharatiya Jana Sangh attacked from a different flank, conceding the case against concentration of wealth while arguing that state ownership would politicise lending and corrupt the banks' commercial judgement. Both parties voted against the measures; both critiques were later quoted by defenders of the 1991 reforms, which gave the parliamentary record of 1969 a second life.

### Q: Why did the government choose a deposit threshold to select banks in both 1969 and 1980?

The deposit threshold served three purposes at once. It gave the acquisition an objective, administrable test, deposits as returned to the Reserve Bank on a stated Friday, which made the seizure executable at midnight without valuation disputes. It confined the takeover to banks of commanding size, so the government could present the act as a measure against concentration while sparing hundreds of small banks whose owners and depositors were locally influential. And it created a standing rule for the future: the line moved from fifty crore rupees in 1969 to two hundred crore in 1980, announcing that any private bank growing to commanding size would in time be taken, a ceiling on private banking that lasted a generation.

### Q: How did life insurance in 1956 differ from general insurance in 1971 to 1973 as nationalisation exercises?

Life insurance in 1956 differed in motive, method and politics. The Life Insurance Corporation was created in 1956 by the amalgamation of 245 insurers and provident societies after a series of failures and mismanagements had made the industry a byword for insecurity, and the Nehru government presented the measure as a rescue of policyholders rather than a seizure of a commanding height. General insurance in 1971 to 1973 had no comparable scandal to answer: the industry was solvent, its policyholders were firms rather than households, and the takeover was avowedly a matter of controlling investible funds and extending the post 1969 programme. The 1956 act built a new institution by construction; the 1971 to 1973 acts extended an existing political architecture.

### Q: Why did nationalisation stop short of the foreign banks operating in India?

Nationalisation stopped short of the foreign banks for reasons of diplomacy, law and utility. The foreign banks, such as the Chartered Bank, Grindlays and the Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corporation, were incorporated abroad, and acquiring their Indian branches would have entangled the government in compensation claims under foreign law and in friction with the governments whose trade and aid India courted. Their share of deposits was modest beside the fourteen banks, and their business was concentrated in the port cities and in foreign trade finance, where their correspondent networks served Indian exporters. The communist parties pressed for their inclusion in 1969 and after; the government held the line, judging that the political yield of the domestic commanding heights had been collected without the foreign bill.

### Q: What happened to the boards and managers of the fourteen banks on the night of the takeover?

On the night of 19 July 1969 the boards of the fourteen banks stood dissolved by the ordinance, and custodians appointed by the central government, in consultation with the Reserve Bank, took charge of each bank as its chief executive authority. The custodians were drawn largely from the senior ranks of the banking system and the Reserve Bank itself, and the branch networks opened for business as usual on the next working day under the same staff, the same account books and new letterheads. The former owners were reduced to shareholders of companies that no longer conducted banking, entitled only to the compensation the statute fixed, while the managing directors who survived did so as public sector executives answerable to the finance ministry.

### Q: How did nationalisation change the relationship between the Finance Ministry and the Reserve Bank?

Nationalisation made the Finance Ministry the owner of the banking system the Reserve Bank was charged to regulate, and the dual role shaped every later conflict between the two institutions. The ministry acquired the power of appointment over the boards and custodians of the nationalised banks and a direct channel, through ownership, for the credit directions it wished to issue, from priority sector targets to branch licensing priorities. The Reserve Bank retained its statutory powers of inspection, licensing and monetary control under the Reserve Bank of India Act and the Banking Regulation Act. Critics of the arrangement, including former governors in their memoirs, argued that ownership by the ministry compromised the central bank's regulatory distance; defenders answered that ownership finally gave credit policy a democratic address.

### Q: Did the 1980 nationalisation face a court challenge like the one in 1969?

The 1980 nationalisation faced no equivalent challenge, because the constitutional ground had shifted beneath the shareholders. The Forty Fourth Amendment of 1978, enacted by the Janata Parliament, had deleted the fundamental right to property from Articles 19(1)(f) and 31 and left property protected only by Article 300A, which requires authority of law but no justiciable standard of compensation. A Cooper style petition, arguing that compensation principles were illusory, therefore had no fundamental right to attach to. The six banks of 1980 were acquired under amounts fixed by the statute, their shareholders' remedy confined to the statute's own terms, and the transfer passed without the constitutional litigation that had consumed the first nationalisation for seven months.

### Q: Why does the political-acts reading treat timing as evidence?

The political-acts reading treats timing as evidence because the timing is the fact the rival framings cannot digest. The doctrines invoked in 1969 had been Congress doctrine since the 1950s, the committee reports on banking and coal had been available for years, and the same measures were not taken in the quiet years before or between the crises. Each act instead fell within days or weeks of a political event that raised the value of dramatic state action: a Finance Minister's resignation, a landslide and a war, a post war assertion of command, a restoration after three years in opposition. When a policy fires only on political cues, across four sectors and eleven years, the pattern of the cues is the most economical explanation of the sequence.
