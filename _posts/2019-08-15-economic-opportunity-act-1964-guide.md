The Economic Opportunity Act opened with a puzzle of institutional design, and the puzzle explains the statute better than any list of programs. Why would Congress place an antipoverty command outside every existing department? The Department of Labor ran manpower training. The Department of Health, Education, and Welfare ran education, health, and public assistance programs. The Department of Agriculture ran rural credit and farm programs. Each department held one fragment of poverty, and each fragment came with a constituency, a bureaucracy, and a settled routine. A statute that divided new money among those departments would have reproduced the fragmentation that the Council of Economic Advisers had identified as a central defect, because a poor family met low schooling, poor health, racial exclusion, weak local labor demand, and inadequate income as one combined condition, not as separate departmental files. Congress instead created a new office, placed that office beside the President, and gave the office both programs of its own and a duty to pull the fragments into one campaign.

The statute was the Economic Opportunity Act of 1964, Public Law 88-452, 78 Stat. 508, signed August 20, 1964, and codified at 42 U.S.C. 2701 et seq. before repeal. Its declaration of policy committed the nation to eliminate the paradox of poverty in the midst of plenty, and its titles built machinery for that commitment: employment and training for youth, community action in urban and rural areas, loans for farmers and small businesses, work experience for adults, and a domestic volunteer corps. Three words and a backlash supply the shortest accurate summary of the career that followed. The three words were maximum feasible participation, the phrase in section 202(a) requiring that a community action program be developed, conducted, and administered with the maximum feasible participation of residents of the areas and members of the groups served. The backlash came from mayors, governors, and members of Congress who learned that a federally funded local agency could organize poor residents, challenge municipal decisions, and answer to Washington rather than to city hall.

The design choice carried a second consequence that the sections below trace title by title. Because the new office owned programs rather than merely advising departments, each program acquired its own constituency, its own local institutions, and its own claim on appropriations. Community action agencies formed in cities and rural counties across the country. Head Start began in the summer of 1965 under community action authority, although the enacted text never named the program. Job Corps centers, legal services projects, community health centers, and volunteer placements each developed records and defenders separate from the office that had launched them. The contrast with the education legislation of the same period is instructive, because [the Elementary and Secondary Education Act of 1965](/2014/08/01/elementary-secondary-education-act-1965/) sent federal money through state and local education agencies, while community action deliberately built a channel that could run around established local government when local government was judged part of the problem.

The office itself lasted a decade under its original name. Transfers in 1969 sent the Job Corps to the Department of Labor and Head Start to the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare. An attempt to dismantle the agency by executive action in 1973 failed in federal court. The Head Start, Economic Opportunity, and Community Partnership Act of 1974 converted the office into the Community Services Administration from 1975, and the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981 abolished that successor and replaced direct federal community action funding with the Community Services Block Grant. The programs followed a different arc. The official poverty rate fell from 19.0 percent in 1964 to 12.1 percent in 1969 and 11.1 percent in 1973, and economists and historians disputed how much of that fall belonged to economic growth, how much to expanded transfers, and how much to the programs described here. What no account disputed was the survival pattern: the agency disappeared, the clause that caused the backlash was rewritten within three years, and a large share of the program portfolio operated under other statutes for decades afterward.

## Origins, Conditions, Drafting, and Passage

The Economic Opportunity Act entered Congress as a statute about poverty, but the drafting history turned on a prior argument about visibility. By the early 1960s, aggregate prosperity had become the standard measure of national success. Against that record, a substantial minority of Americans lived on incomes that left rent, food, medical care, heat, and schooling in direct competition with one another month after month. The problem for the drafters was not a lack of federal activity. Federal and state governments operated public assistance, vocational programs, housing programs, manpower training, and aid tied to dependent children, old age, disability, and unemployment. The problem, as the Council of Economic Advisers framed the problem in 1963 and 1964, was that prosperity had reduced poverty without eliminating the conditions that reproduced poverty across generations, and that the existing program structure treated those conditions in fragments. A family could encounter a welfare office, a school system, an employment service, a housing authority, and a county health clinic without any single institution holding responsibility for the combined effect of low schooling, poor health, racial exclusion, weak local labor demand, and inadequate income. The statute that emerged from that diagnosis joined two decisions. The first defined poverty in measurable income terms and treated education, training, health, and community organization as connected remedies. The second placed coordination in a new Office of Economic Opportunity inside the Executive Office of the President, rather than distributing the entire effort among established departments.

### The Poverty the Drafters Confronted

Harrington, a socialist writer and editor, published The Other America: Poverty in the United States in March 1962. The book argued that poverty had become socially segmented and politically easy to overlook, concentrated among older Americans on inadequate fixed incomes, migrant and agricultural workers, residents of depressed rural districts, Black Americans subject to segregation and labor market exclusion, families headed by women with limited earnings, and residents of urban neighborhoods marked by substandard housing. Harrington estimated that between 40 million and 50 million Americans lived in poverty, a figure higher than the estimate the Council of Economic Advisers later adopted, because Harrington applied a broader judgment about the income required for a minimally decent standard of life. Harrington combined federal statistics with reporting from places that aggregate growth figures concealed, including Appalachian coal country, migrant labor camps, and southern agricultural counties. Poverty, in that account, persisted not only from lack of work but also from low wage work, irregular work, ill health, limited schooling, old age, and discrimination.

The influence attributed to Harrington requires precise statement. Maurice Isserman, Harrington biographer, and James Sundquist, a political scientist who participated in drafting antipoverty legislation, credited The Other America with ending piecemeal thinking about problems that earlier measures had treated separately, including slum housing, juvenile delinquency, and unemployment. Dwight Macdonald extended that reach through a long review essay, Our Invisible Poor, published in The New Yorker in January 1963. Accounts of the Kennedy administration, repeated by historians of the period, reported that the Macdonald review prompted President John F. Kennedy to read the Harrington book and to direct attention toward antipoverty legislation later in 1963. The causal chain was indirect rather than singular. Harrington did not draft a bill, set the federal poverty line, or design the Office of Economic Opportunity. The Harrington contribution, as Isserman described the contribution, was to define poverty as a social and moral issue that demanded a coordinated federal response.

The Council of Economic Advisers under Chairman Walter Heller supplied the analytical core. Robert Lampman, a senior staff economist on the Council, held principal responsibility for the poverty analysis that became Chapter 2 of the Economic Report of the President transmitted in January 1964, titled The Problem of Poverty in America. The Council set a poverty threshold of $3,000 in annual cash income for a family and $1,500 for an unrelated individual. Applying that threshold to 1962, the year with complete data available during drafting, the Council counted about 35 million persons below the line. The Council rate corresponded to roughly one fifth of the population, and the official Census measure later placed the poverty rate at 19.5 percent in 1963 and 19.0 percent in 1964. The Council acknowledged that a line drawn at $3,000 involved judgment, and that a higher or lower line would change the measured size of the problem. The value of a fixed line, in the Lampman argument for an absolute standard adjusted for price changes rather than repeatedly redefined, was clarity.

The analysis behind the threshold rejected a simple expectation that general economic growth alone would finish the task. Rapid growth during and after the Second World War had lowered poverty substantially, but the rate of reduction had slowed between 1957 and 1962, as the remaining poor population contained a larger share of persons facing barriers that a tighter labor market alone did not remove. The Council analysis associated poverty with limited education, female headed households with restricted employment options, old age, disability and chronic illness, residence in depressed areas, nonwhite status under discriminatory labor and housing practices, and employment in low wage agriculture and service work. The drafting consequence followed directly. Cash assistance could prevent destitution for persons unable to work, and macroeconomic policy could expand total employment, but a statute aimed at opportunity had to intervene earlier and more locally, through preschool preparation, basic education, job training, work experience, health related services, legal assistance, and community planning. The Economic Report distinction between attacking causes and relieving symptoms became the organizing distinction of the bill, repeated by President Lyndon B. Johnson across administration testimony during the legislative contest of 1964.

Kennedy era antecedents extended beyond a book and a report chapter. During 1963, the Heller Council solicited ideas from departments and agencies for a program to alleviate poverty, and Council and Bureau of the Budget staff examined a coordinated community approach. A Council memorandum dated December 20, 1963, addressed to presidential adviser Theodore Sorensen, outlined a Coordinated Community Action Program as the central element of a realistic attack on poverty. The memorandum favored proposals aimed at specific localities marked by poverty, reliance on organized local initiative and self help under federally approved plans and federal support, and local action programs able to evaluate and coordinate federal, state, local, and private efforts while testing new approaches. The formulation contained, in embryo, the community action design that the Sargent Shriver task force later placed at the center of the bill. Federal precedents existed as well, including the Area Redevelopment Act, manpower training legislation, and pilot community projects supported through Ford Foundation gray areas work. None of those precedents amounted to a general antipoverty statute. The Kennedy era work therefore left Johnson an unfinished design problem rather than a finished bill, namely whether to proceed with limited demonstrations in selected cities or to seek a national program with a new coordinating office.

### From Kennedy Era Inquiry to a Presidential Declaration

The transfer of power after the assassination of President Kennedy on November 22, 1963, converted an exploratory effort into a presidential commitment within one day. Johnson memoir, The Vantage Point, recorded that Heller came to Johnson on the evening of November 23, 1963, the first full day of the Johnson presidency, and reported that Kennedy had discussed poverty with Heller three days before the assassination and had approved development of an alleviation program by the Council and the Bureau of the Budget. Heller and Budget Director Kermit Gordon initially contemplated a pilot venture in a limited number of demonstration cities. Johnson rejected the limited scale. In the Johnson account, a small start would fail to propel a program through Congress and would invite a charge of tokenism, particularly from Black leaders pressing the federal government for substantive action on civil rights and economic exclusion. Johnson directed Heller to proceed at the highest priority and to broaden the scope toward a national impact.

Johnson announced the commitment in the State of the Union address delivered on January 8, 1964. The address declared an unconditional war on poverty in America and urged Congress and the public to join the effort. War language signaled total commitment, centralized direction, and a standard of success higher than incremental improvement, and administration officials understood that the same language would later invite judgment against an absolute promise. The metaphor elevated poverty to the defining domestic initiative of the new presidency and placed the effort before Congress at the opening of the session. The aim, Johnson told Congress, extended beyond relieving symptoms toward curing poverty and preventing poverty, through opportunity, training, and work rather than permanent dependence on public generosity. The distinction allowed the administration to answer two opposing objections at once. Against a charge that the bill expanded relief rolls, Johnson emphasized education and employment. Against a charge that general prosperity made a federal program unnecessary, Johnson emphasized Americans who lived on the outskirts of hope because of poverty or race. The pairing reflected the political reality of 1964, when the Civil Rights Act occupied Congress during the same months and when any federal program distributing funds in the segregated South would confront questions about access, control, and exclusion.

The declaration preceded a finished legislative design by design. Johnson committed the presidency publicly before the Shriver task force had settled program titles, funding levels, or agency assignments, creating pressure that forced departments to negotiate against a deadline rather than against an abstract proposal. Six weeks separated the formation of the Shriver task force in February from Johnson transmission of the proposal to Congress on March 16, 1964. Speed served legislative strategy, because delay would allow each affected department, governor, mayor, and congressional committee to mobilize around protection of existing jurisdiction. Faced with a measured population of about 35 million poor persons under the Council line, research arguing that growth alone had slowed as a remedy, and a declaration that promised a war rather than a study, the drafters had to decide which institution could plausibly conduct that war.

### Why did the drafters create a new office instead of using existing departments?

The drafters created a new office because the Council of Economic Advisers and the Bureau of the Budget judged that existing departments would absorb antipoverty funds into established programs and constituencies. A separate Office of Economic Opportunity in the Executive Office of the President promised coordination, speed, experimentation, and direct presidential control.

Distrust of departmental absorption rested on institutional observation rather than personal accusation. The Department of Health, Education, and Welfare administered education and welfare programs through state agencies and school systems with established eligibility rules. The Department of Labor administered employment and training programs in close relation to state employment services, organized labor, and employers accustomed to manpower categories. The Department of Agriculture administered rural programs through land grant institutions and county structures, at a moment when southern committee leadership exercised substantial influence over rural policy. Each department also possessed routines that sorted clients into preexisting categories and answered to authorizing committees protective of jurisdiction. Administration planners, including Heller at the Council and Gordon at the Bureau of the Budget, argued that a poverty program divided at birth among those departments would become a set of modest increments to existing appropriations, reported as antipoverty spending without a unified strategy for a particular place. A neighborhood or rural county might receive a training grant from Labor, a welfare service through a state agency supervised by Health, Education, and Welfare, and a rural loan or food program through Agriculture, with no authority empowered to require a common local plan.

Budget officials under Gordon favored coordination that did not depend on interagency committees, because committees of cabinet departments typically operated by consensus, protected member programs, and lacked authority to compel reallocation. The Council made a complementary economic argument. Poverty in a particular community reflected combined deficits, so the unit of intervention should be the community plan rather than the departmental appropriation line. Federal funds should support a local public or private nonprofit agency able to assemble education, training, health, legal, employment, and social service components in proportions fitted to local conditions, subject to federal approval and evaluation. That reasoning produced community action as more than a delivery device. Community action became the mechanism that justified a new office, because approving varied local combinations and coordinating the contributions of older departments required a director with presidential backing and discretion to fund approaches that no single department would have generated from an established mission statement.

Opposition to departmental placement also reflected a judgment about state and local establishments in the segregated South and in machine governed cities. Administration planners understood that routing funds exclusively through governors, state welfare departments, or city halls could exclude Black residents and poor residents from planning in jurisdictions where those residents lacked voting power or faced discriminatory administration. The design therefore contemplated direct federal grants to local community action agencies, public or private nonprofit, with federal standards and federal review. The same design predictably alarmed governors and mayors who expected federal domestic funds to pass through elected general governments. The administration accepted a gubernatorial veto over certain projects within a state during congressional negotiation, while preserving authority for the director to override a veto under statutory conditions. The compromise revealed the underlying theory. Local initiative was essential, existing general governments could not hold an unconditional gatekeeping monopoly, and a presidential office would arbitrate the boundary.

### The Shriver Task Force and the Deliberate Placement Decision

Johnson gave the drafting assignment to Sargent Shriver, who took charge of the poverty program on February 1, 1964, according to the Johnson memoir. Shriver directed the Peace Corps. The Task Force on the War Against Poverty assembled in February 1964 drew staff from the Council of Economic Advisers and the Bureau of the Budget together with officials from the Departments of Justice, Agriculture, Labor, and Health, Education, and Welfare. Departmental representation supplied technical knowledge about training, education, rural programs, and legal constraints, and gave department heads a stake in a design that nevertheless denied any single department control of the whole effort. The Johnson account stated that the program stood ready six weeks after the task force first assembled, and Johnson transmitted the proposal on March 16, 1964. To reduce departmental resistance, the administration assigned certain established functions and funds to Labor and to Health, Education, and Welfare, while the remainder of the war on poverty, and in particular community action, ran through the new office.

Placement inside the Executive Office of the President converted that drafting judgment into law. In the March 16 message, Johnson rejected a series of uncoordinated and unrelated efforts and announced creation of the Office of Economic Opportunity in the Executive Office, with a director serving as the presidential chief of staff for the war against poverty and Shriver as the intended appointee. Executive Office placement made the effort nimble for specific reasons. The director reported to the President rather than through a cabinet secretary with competing programs. The office held no inherited field structure, eligibility bureaucracy, or client constituency at creation, so staff could approve varied local plans, move funds among experimental components, and evaluate results against a single antipoverty mission. Presidential proximity also supplied leverage in disputes with departments, governors, and mayors, because a grant decision carried the authority of a program Johnson had personally declared.

The same placement made the effort politically exposed for equally specific reasons. A departmental program could survive presidential transitions inside a larger mission, defended by a cabinet secretary, career staff, and congressional committees with jurisdiction over that department. The Office of Economic Opportunity possessed a conspicuous name, a single identifiable director, and a mission stated as war, so failure or local conflict attached directly to the President and to the office rather than dispersing across the federal establishment. Direct grants that bypassed governors and city halls generated disputes that Executive Office status intensified, because each dispute tested presidential authority over local priorities. Representative Robert Taft warned that the bill allowed the director wide freedom to act, with limited obligation to consult beyond finding or creating a local agency willing to proceed. Administration supporters accepted broad discretion as the price of coordination and experimentation. Opponents treated that discretion as evidence that a new bureaucracy would answer mainly to the White House.

### Passage Through Congress

The bill moved as S. 2642, sponsored by Senator Patrick McNamara of Michigan, through a Congress absorbed by civil rights. The Senate passed the bill on July 23, 1964, by 61 votes to 34. The House passed the bill on August 8, 1964, by 226 votes to 185. Democrats supported the bill by 204 votes to 40, while Republicans opposed the bill by 145 votes to 22. Johnson signed the Economic Opportunity Act of 1964, Public Law 88-452, on August 20, 1964. The authorization for the first year stood at $947.5 million, with Congress later appropriating $800 million for fiscal year 1965. Republican opponents presented the same amount, joined to broad director discretion, as an open ended expansion of federal power undertaken before pilot results justified national operation.

Southern opposition requires attribution rather than a single motive assigned to a region. A substantial bloc of southern Democrats joined most Republicans in opposition, while other southern Democrats supported the bill, and the final House Democratic margin depended heavily on northern and western Democrats. Senators Richard Russell and Herman Talmadge of Georgia pressed Shriver during negotiation for gubernatorial authority over certain programs in a state, according to Shriver recollection recorded by historians of the office, and the administration accepted a governor veto subject to override by the director as a condition of passage. The concession addressed federalism concerns stated publicly by southern officeholders, who objected to federal grants made directly to local organizations without approval from a governor accountable under state law. Racial politics operated within that federalism argument in documented ways. In 1964, federal funds entering segregated jurisdictions under local control could be administered through institutions that excluded Black citizens from decision making and services, a pattern civil rights organizations documented across southern public programs during the period. A federal capacity to fund community agencies directly, and to require nondiscriminatory administration, therefore carried racial consequences that participants understood, even when debate language emphasized home rule, bureaucracy, or fiscal restraint. Neutral description requires both facts together. Opposition included stated objections to cost, bureaucracy, and federal displacement of state authority, and the structure being opposed would have weakened segregated local monopolies over federal money. Support included stated commitments to opportunity and training, and the structure being supported expanded presidential and federal authority to an extent that later Congresses repeatedly revisited.

Passage therefore ratified a theory of government as much as a list of programs. Congress accepted the Council finding that poverty persisted amid prosperity in forms that general growth and fragmented categorical programs had not resolved. Congress accepted a local planning mechanism drawn from the December 1963 Council memorandum and developed by the Shriver task force into community action. Congress also accepted, by a narrow House margin and after a gubernatorial veto compromise, an office placed beside the President so that coordination could override departmental routine. Placement also ensured that every later dispute over community action, race, local control, and federal discretion would arrive at the White House as a dispute about the office itself. The statute began with that bargain already embedded in the design.

## The Architecture of the Statute, Title by Title

Section 2, headed Findings and Declaration of Purpose, supplied the reasoning that every later title claimed to serve. Congress found that the economic well being and prosperity of the United States had progressed to a level surpassing any achieved in world history and that the benefits were widely shared throughout the nation, yet poverty remained the lot of a substantial number of people. Congress reasoned that the United States could achieve its full economic and social potential only if every individual had the opportunity to contribute to the full extent of individual capabilities and to participate in the workings of society. Congress therefore declared a policy of eliminating the paradox of poverty in the midst of plenty by opening to everyone the opportunity for education and training, the opportunity to work, and the opportunity to live in decency and dignity. The stated purpose was narrower than War on Poverty rhetoric suggested, an effort to strengthen, supplement, and coordinate efforts in furtherance of that policy. The statute instead authorized categorical programs, each with a defined population, a defined administering channel, and a defined way of moving federal money, with coordination at the center of the design.

### What did each title of the statute actually create?

The statute created a single coordinating office and eight titles covering youth employment, community action, adult literacy, rural credit, migrant assistance, small business loans, work experience for adults unable to support families, coordination rules, and domestic volunteers, financed mainly through federal grants, direct and guaranteed loans, wages, allowances, and project payments.

Read in order, the titles moved from the young to the adult and from direct federal action to local action financed with federal funds. Title I addressed youth who had left school, youth who faced leaving school, and students who needed earnings to remain in college. Title II addressed the community as a unit and, in a separate part, adults whose limited literacy blocked employment. Title III addressed rural families and migrant agricultural workers through credit and services. Title IV addressed very small businesses as a source of employment. Title V addressed adults, including recipients of public assistance, who lacked work experience. Title VI created the administering office. Title VII handled a technical interaction between program earnings and public assistance eligibility. Title VIII authorized the domestic volunteer service.

### Title I and the three doors open to youth

Part A of Title I created the Job Corps. Section 101 stated the purpose as preparation for the responsibilities of citizenship and an increase in the employability of young men and young women aged sixteen through twenty one, delivered in rural and urban residential centers through education, vocational training, and useful work experience that included work directed toward the conservation of natural resources. Section 102 established the Job Corps within the Office of Economic Opportunity, and section 103 authorized the Director to enter into agreements with federal, state, and local agencies and with private organizations for the establishment and operation of conservation camps and training centers. Eligibility rested on age, on low income background, and on a determination that educational deficits and environment made the residential model appropriate, and enrollment was voluntary. The federal government financed the centers through agreements and contracts with operating agencies and organizations, paid subsistence, instruction, clothing, medical care, and transportation for enrollees, and paid enrollees a modest allowance during participation, with provision for allotments to dependents and a readjustment amount tied to length of service. Removal from home was the defining feature.

Part B authorized work training programs, the part under which the Neighborhood Youth Corps operated. Section 111 stated the purpose as the provision of useful work experience for unemployed young men and young women, aged sixteen through twenty one and from low income backgrounds, through state and community programs, so that employability might increase or education might resume or continue, and so that public agencies and private nonprofit organizations could carry out public interest projects that would not otherwise have been provided, including conservation and recreation projects. A Neighborhood Youth Corps enrollee remained at home and worked part time during the school year or full time during the summer for a local public or nonprofit sponsor, such as a school, a hospital, a library, or a park system. Administration ran through grants. The Director made financial assistance available to states, local agencies, and nonprofit sponsors that proposed projects and supervised enrollees, the federal contribution covered the larger share of project costs, and sponsors contributed a smaller share in cash or in kind. The money reached the young worker as wages for hours worked, a mechanism that made the program legible as employment rather than as relief. The contrast with the Job Corps was deliberate, residential rebuilding at high cost per enrollee against a subsidized job in school and community at lower cost per enrollee.

Part C created work study programs. The purpose was to stimulate and promote the part time employment of students in institutions of higher education who came from low income families and who needed the earnings to pursue courses of study at such institutions. Eligibility turned on enrollment or acceptance at an eligible institution, on financial need, and on pursuit of a course of study. The Director entered into agreements with colleges and universities, and federal grants to the institution financed the larger share of compensation paid to student workers, with the institution or another employing organization supplying the remaining share. Jobs could sit inside the institution or with public and nonprofit organizations outside the institution under prescribed conditions. The mechanism was a wage subsidy delivered through an educational institution, joining access to college with retention in college. Title I thus used three mechanisms for three situations, direct financing of centers and allowances, project grants that funded wages, and institutional grants that subsidized student wages.

### Title II and community action as the organizing idea

Part A of Title II addressed general community action programs. Section 201 stated the purpose as the provision of stimulation and incentive for urban and rural communities to mobilize resources to combat poverty through community action programs. Section 202 defined a community action program as a program that mobilized community resources in an attack on poverty, that provided services and activities of sufficient scope and size to give promise of progress toward elimination of poverty or a cause of poverty through developing employment opportunities, improving human performance, motivation, and productivity, or bettering the conditions under which people lived, learned, and worked, that was developed, conducted, and administered with the maximum feasible participation of residents of the areas and members of the groups served, and that was conducted, administered, or coordinated by a public or private nonprofit agency other than a political party. The participation element required involvement of area residents and members of the groups served, a requirement that later amendments narrowed and restructured. The agency element created the community action agency, a designated public or private nonprofit organization that received funds, planned across service categories, and coordinated providers in a city, a county, or a rural multi county area.

The money mechanism in Part A was the federal grant, supplemented by technical assistance for planning, staff, facilities, services, and coordination of fragmented local efforts. Because a community action program could combine employment services, education, housing assistance, health services, day care, and neighborhood centers inside one local plan, Title II functioned as the flexible title in a statute otherwise built from narrow categories. Eligibility for services was local, anchored in low income residence in the area served, while eligibility for funding turned on agency designation, a program satisfying the section 202 definition, and federal assurances that included nondiscrimination and fiscal accountability. Administration was indirect at the point of service and direct at the point of funding, because the Office of Economic Opportunity could fund a local nonprofit agency without routing the grant through a state government, a choice that generated conflict with governors and mayors.

Part B of Title II addressed adult basic education, a program of instruction for persons aged eighteen and older whose inability to read and write English operated as an impediment to employment. Eligibility rested on age and educational need, and the population included adults who had left school years earlier and adults for whom limited literacy blocked entry into training or promotion at work. Federal grants went to state educational agencies, which supported programs operated through local school systems and other approved providers, so the program moved through the established state education structure rather than through the new community action network. The mechanism was a grant in aid to a state, with federal and state shares prescribed in the statutory scheme, and the service was instruction in reading, writing, arithmetic, and English usage. Part C, which included section 219, created a voluntary assistance program for needy children. Section 219 stated the purpose as an opportunity for individual Americans to participate in a personal way by voluntarily assisting in the support of one or more needy children, in a program coordinated with city or county social welfare agencies. The part established an information and coordination function rather than a benefit, matching private voluntary support to children identified through local welfare structures.

### Title III and the special problems of rural poverty

Section 301 stated the purpose of Title III as an effort to meet some of the special problems of rural poverty and thereby to raise and maintain the income and living standards of low income rural families and migrant agricultural employees and their families. Part A authorized loans to low income rural families. Eligibility centered on rural residence, on low income, on inability to obtain credit from other sources on reasonable terms, and on a plan under which borrowed funds would permanently increase family income, through purchase of livestock, equipment, or other productive assets or through improvement of a small farm or rural enterprise. Administration placed the program with the Secretary of Agriculture acting through established rural credit channels, under arrangements with the Director, so that the statute borrowed an existing field structure rather than building a parallel rural bureaucracy. The mechanism was the direct loan, capped in program descriptions of the enacted statute at loans not exceeding two thousand five hundred dollars to a family, repayable from the increased income the loan was intended to generate. Congress authorized no grants under Part A, because the intervention aimed at a small increment of productive capacity for a family that commercial lenders had classified as uncreditworthy.

Part B addressed assistance for migrant agricultural employees and their families. A family that followed harvests crossed county and state lines during a single season, lost continuity of schooling and medical care, and often lived in housing supplied by an employer or a labor camp operator, a jurisdictional problem that ordinary programs handled poorly. The part authorized assistance to state and local governments and to public and private nonprofit agencies for projects meeting basic needs of migratory workers and their families, including housing, sanitation, education, health services, and day care. Administration ran through grants from the Director to sponsors operating in areas of significant migrant activity, and eligibility followed migrant status and family need rather than fixed residence. The mechanism combined grants for facilities and services with assistance for operating projects, a contrast with Part A that reflected the economics of the population, because a landless migrant family had no asset base against which a small loan could reliably raise permanent income. A rural county could therefore host a community action agency funded under Title II, a loan program operating under Title III through agricultural offices, and a migrant project funded under Title III through a nonprofit sponsor, with coordination assigned to the office created in Title VI.

### Title IV, Title V, and help aimed at adult workers

Title IV, Employment and Investment Incentives, turned from training people for jobs to financing the small enterprises that might employ people. Section 401 stated the purpose as assistance in the establishment, preservation, and strengthening of small business concerns, improvement of managerial skills in such enterprises, and mobilization of private as well as public managerial skills and resources. Eligibility centered on very small businesses and on borrowers who lacked access to conventional credit, with a showing that credit would support employment and attention in administration to areas and populations with high unemployment and low income. Administration ran through the Small Business Administration, the Director acting through the Administrator under delegation and agreement, placing antipoverty lending inside an agency with lending staff, field offices, and procedures for evaluating business credit. The mechanism was credit, direct loans and guarantees of loans made by other lenders, initially limited to loans not in excess of twenty five thousand dollars to a single borrower, a ceiling confirmed in Congressional Research Service histories of the Economic Opportunity Loan program that grew from Title IV. Credit was paired with management and technical assistance because a borrower denied by banks often lacked bookkeeping and marketing systems as well as collateral.

Title V, Work Experience Programs, addressed adults whom the youth titles could not serve. Section 501 stated the purpose as an expansion of opportunities for constructive work experience and other needed training for persons unable to support or care for themselves or their families, and directed the Director to make maximum use of programs under the Manpower Development and Training Act of 1962 and the Vocational Education Act of 1963, tying the new authority to training statutes already in force. Eligibility reached unemployed adults with family responsibilities, persons receiving public assistance who were judged able to participate, and persons whose lack of recent work experience blocked hiring. Administration operated through payments for experimental, pilot, and demonstration projects. The Director could make payments to public and nonprofit sponsors and to state agencies, in cooperation with federal departments administering related manpower and welfare programs, for projects combining paid work on useful public undertakings with training, counseling, and basic education where needed. The mechanism was the project payment rather than an individual entitlement, wages or training related payments reaching a participant through a defined project, so that approaches that worked could be documented and approaches that failed could be ended without terminating a permanent benefit.

### Title VI and the office that held the parts together

Title VI, Administration and Coordination, supplied the institutional core. Part A created the Office of Economic Opportunity in the Executive Office of the President. Section 601 established the office and provided that the office would be headed by a Director appointed by the President by and with the advice and consent of the Senate, with one Deputy Director and three Assistant Directors appointed in the same manner and performing functions prescribed by the Director. Placement inside the Executive Office of the President, rather than inside a cabinet department, allowed a coordinating office close to the President to press departments to align programs with a single strategy and to experiment with direct grants to local agencies, while the same placement exposed the office to direct political attack.

The Director administered programs assigned to the office, delegated functions where a title so provided, as Title III did for rural loans through the Secretary of Agriculture and Title IV did for business loans through the Small Business Administration, approved or disapproved grants under Title II, entered into the agreements that created Job Corps centers under Title I, coordinated federal antipoverty efforts, and collected and published information. Title VI also authorized an Economic Opportunity Council, an interagency body that brought departments and agencies with antipoverty responsibilities into a formal coordination structure chaired through the new office. Part B contained general provisions governing administration across titles, including conditions for financial assistance and fiscal and reporting requirements. Congress authorized appropriations for the titles, and actual funding depended on annual appropriations acts, so authorization created legal capacity to operate a program while appropriations determined scale in any given year, a distinction that explained why a title could exist in full legal form and operate in only a limited number of communities where grant funds reached.

### The remaining titles and the program that the text did not name

Title VII, Treatment of Income for Certain Public Assistance Purposes, addressed how payments to participants were treated in determining need and eligibility under state public assistance plans approved under the Social Security Act, so that earnings or allowances connected with work training and work experience did not produce an automatic and complete loss of assistance for a family whose total resources remained low. Congress understood that a wage from a Neighborhood Youth Corps job or a payment inside a Title V project could disqualify a household under rules written for ordinary employment, and Congress adjusted the interface rather than rewriting public assistance law. Administration sat at the boundary between the Director, the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare, and state assistance agencies, and the mechanism was not a payment at all but a rule about counting payments made under other titles.

Title VIII, Domestic Volunteer Service Programs, authorized Volunteers in Service to America, known by the acronym VISTA, the domestic volunteer service administered through the Office of Economic Opportunity under the Title VI machinery. The title empowered the Director to recruit, select, train, and assign volunteers to serve in programs sponsored by state and local agencies and by private nonprofit organizations, in projects directed toward the alleviation of poverty, including literacy, health, housing, community organization, and assistance to migrant workers. A volunteer needed to meet age and citizenship or residency conditions prescribed in the title and to accept a term of service, commonly framed in the early program as a year of full time service, and the statute did not confine service to the young. The mechanism separated VISTA from every employment title. A volunteer received support for living expenses, transportation, and service costs, together with a modest stipend connected with completion of service, rather than a market wage or a salary, and a sponsor received the labor and organizing capacity of a full time worker whose subsistence the federal program financed. The model drew openly on the Peace Corps, created in 1961 for service abroad, and translated the model to domestic poverty.

One absence in the enacted text requires precision, because later public memory attached the statute to a program that the statute never named. The famous program absent from the text was Head Start, whose creation under community action authority in the following summer belongs to a later section of the profile. The enacted titles addressed children indirectly through family income, migrant services, and voluntary assistance for needy children under Title II Part C, while preschool education entered through administrative action under Title II rather than through a dedicated title in the act signed on August 20, 1964. Taken title by title, the architecture revealed a legislature working by category and by mechanism. Youth received residence, wages, or subsidized campus employment depending on circumstance. Communities received flexible grants conditioned on participation by the poor. Adults received literacy instruction through state education agencies and work experience through demonstration projects. Rural families received small repayable loans, migrant families received services, and very small businesses received credit and managerial help through an existing lending agency. A new office received authority to coordinate the parts, volunteers received subsistence to serve where sponsors needed capacity, and a technical title prevented program earnings from colliding mechanically with public assistance rules. The coherence of the statute lay in that division of labor, and the tensions lay in the same place, because each title created a constituency, a channel, and a claim on appropriations that the coordinating office had to hold together after enactment.

## Maximum Feasible Participation: The Clause, Its Origins, and What It Did on the Ground

Few phrases in federal statute produced consequences as far beyond their drafting room as the participation clause of the Economic Opportunity Act. The clause occupied a single subordinate position inside a definition. It named no board, set no quota, created no office, and appropriated no money by itself. Within three years, arguments over the War on Poverty turned on the clause. Residents of poor neighborhoods treated the phrase as a promise that programs addressed to poverty would not be designed entirely by other people. The statute left the central question open. Participation in what, the text did not say. Representation on governing boards, consultation during planning, employment in program jobs, and control over budgets were all plausible answers, and different communities pressed each answer in turn. The history of the clause is a history of an undefined term filled in by earlier demonstration projects, then by administrative guidance, and finally by funded agencies and the residents who organized around them.

### The Words in the Statute

The clause lived in section 202(a) of the act, the provision that defined a community action program for purposes of Title II. The definition required that the program be "developed, conducted, and administered with the maximum feasible participation of residents of the areas and members of the groups served." The three verbs spread the requirement across the life of a program, from development through conduct to administration, so an agency that consulted residents during planning and then excluded residents from later decisions had a weak claim to compliance on a plain reading. The text spoke of residents of the areas served and members of the groups served, language that reached the people a program targeted rather than civic leadership in general.

Section 202(a) prescribed no proportion of seats, no method of selecting resident representatives, no election procedure and no sanction in the definition itself. The word feasible did much of the work. In an oral history recorded for the Lyndon B. Johnson Library, C. Robert Perrin, who had followed the bill through the legislative process, recalled that the phrase never became a subject of much discussion during that process and that feasible was, in his recollection, the key word, because the term allowed administrators to go as far as they judged practicable or to stop short of that point. Maximum pointed toward the greatest participation attainable. Feasible pointed toward the limits of the attainable, as judged by someone. The statute did not identify the judge. Between 1964 and 1967, the contest over the clause was a contest over who would supply the missing standard, in Washington, in city halls, or in the neighborhoods where funded programs operated.

### Where Did Maximum Feasible Participation Come From?

The phrase came from demonstration projects financed before the War on Poverty, principally Ford Foundation Gray Areas programs and the President's Committee on Juvenile Delinquency projects such as Mobilization for Youth, where planners argued that residents had to help design services if institutions were to change and if blocked opportunities were to open.

The intellectual path to the clause ran through juvenile delinquency research rather than through welfare administration. In Delinquency and Opportunity, published in 1960, Richard A. Cloward and Lloyd E. Ohlin of Columbia University argued that delinquency was not primarily a product of individual pathology. Cloward and Ohlin contended that delinquency arose in neighborhoods where young people absorbed ambitions for success while finding legitimate routes to success closed to them, so that illegitimate routes acquired a rational appeal. The argument, called opportunity theory, redirected attention from the character of young people to the structure of surrounding institutions. A program built on the theory therefore had to change how local institutions behaved, coordinate services that had operated in isolation, and bring residents into the process, because residents experienced the blockages directly.

Personnel carried the theory into government. After the election of 1960, President John F. Kennedy asked David Hackett to develop a federal program against delinquency, and Hackett worked with Ohlin and, later, Richard Boone in shaping the work of the President's Committee on Juvenile Delinquency and Youth Crime. Sanford Kravitz and Ferne Kolodner, in a 1969 account of community action origins, described the Gray Areas program and the committee demonstrations as the initial testing ground for the major themes later embodied in the Community Action Program. The committee projects shared a recognizable design. A single local agency planned across institutional lines, services were coordinated rather than scattered, and residents were involved in shaping the effort, on the theory that programs designed without the participation of the people subjected to them reproduced the failures of earlier agencies. When the Johnson administration assembled a poverty program during the winter of 1963 to 1964, planners from the delinquency demonstrations carried the design into the drafting of the Economic Opportunity Act, and the participation clause condensed that experience into a statutory phrase.

### Gray Areas and the Search for a Local Institution

Under Paul Ylvisaker, the Ford Foundation developed the Gray Areas program at the start of the 1960s to address the deterioration of older urban neighborhoods. Edward Banfield summarized the ideas shared by the foundation and delinquency efforts as the prevention of poverty, especially among the young, through concentration of effort by a wide range of public and private agencies, directed at changing the character of an entire district under a carefully made plan. Ford financed pilots in a small set of cities, among them New Haven, Oakland, Boston, and Philadelphia, and each rested on a new kind of local institution, an agency charged with coordinating existing organizations and drawing residents into planning.

The Gray Areas experience taught two lessons. Concentrated funding, a coordinating agency, and a comprehensive plan for a defined area appeared to offer an alternative to fragmented categorical programs that had left poor neighborhoods served by many agencies and changed by none. The cautionary lesson concerned participation itself. Histories of the foundation projects record that Gray Areas agencies met resistance from established agencies and drew anger from African American residents because the projects offered residents no significant role in executing the programs. Participation in the foundation design had meant consultation more often than authority. The statutory phrase can be read as an attempt to strengthen the element the demonstrations had handled weakly, but the phrase inherited the central vagueness. Nobody had settled whether participation meant advice, employment, representation, or control.

### Mobilization for Youth on the Lower East Side

The most influential single demonstration was Mobilization for Youth, organized on the Lower East Side of New York City. Mobilization for Youth applied the opportunity theory of Cloward and Ohlin directly, with Cloward involved in shaping the project, and the design treated the neighborhood as the unit of intervention. The project combined work and education programs for young people with efforts aimed at neighborhood institutions, and the organization moved over time from services toward community organization, helping tenants and welfare recipients press claims against landlords and public agencies. Mobilization for Youth demonstrated, in a form federal planners observed closely, that a program founded on opportunity theory did not remain a neutral service mechanism. Once an organization employed local residents, gathered grievances systematically, and treated institutional change as a goal, the organization acquired a capacity for conflict with municipal agencies and political structures.

Daniel Patrick Moynihan chronicled the Mobilization for Youth affair at length in Maximum Feasible Misunderstanding, published in 1969, reflecting the notoriety the demonstration had achieved by the middle of the decade. Press attacks in New York in 1964 charged the organization with harboring radicals, and investigations followed. The sequence mattered because it unfolded as the Economic Opportunity Act was moving through Congress and the Office of Economic Opportunity was preparing to replicate the community action design at national scale. The demonstration that had done the most to prove the concept had also revealed the political exposure the concept carried. An agency that organized residents to demand changes from a housing authority, a welfare department, or a school board created opponents with access to mayors, governors, and members of Congress, and the choice between service and conflict would be made locally, program by program.

### A Phrase That Passed With Little Debate

The legislative record of 1964 gave administrators almost no guidance, because the clause attracted remarkably little attention during passage. Committee hearings ran to many pages of testimony on Title II, the Job Corps, cost, and the role of state governments, yet administration witnesses did not explain the participation item, and the phrase drew no sustained interrogation from lawmakers. Several circumstances help account for the silence. Opposition to the participation of residents in programs serving residents was difficult to articulate in public, and the word feasible reassured members who worried about extreme readings, because any administrator could describe a limited arrangement as the maximum that feasibility allowed. Attention during 1964 also concentrated on the civil rights bill, on the cost and novelty of the Office of Economic Opportunity, and on provisions, such as the gubernatorial veto over certain grants, that affected the states directly.

Because Congress supplied no definition, the meaning of the clause was determined after enactment, in administrative practice and political conflict. Moynihan argued in 1969 that the government had enacted a consequential requirement without a settled understanding of the conduct demanded, and the review symposium that greeted the Moynihan book in Urban Affairs Quarterly in 1970 disputed parts of that interpretation while agreeing on the underlying fact of improvisation. Local officials who applied for funds tended at first to treat participation as a formality. Residents and civil rights organizations, reading the same statute, treated participation as an entitlement that the first grants had failed to honor, and complaints reached Washington within months.

### What the Office of Economic Opportunity Made of the Words

The Office of Economic Opportunity, under its first director, Sargent Shriver, gave the clause an administrative content that the statute had withheld. During the first round of grants in the second half of 1964, reviewers asked whether representatives of the poor had participated in developing a proposed program to the maximum extent feasible, and the agency initially accepted answers that later reviewers would have rejected as inadequate. Complaints altered the practice. The NAACP and the Urban League pressed to be consulted, and demands soon arrived from new local groups with no prior standing in municipal politics. By the spring of 1965, the agency had moved toward a numerical expectation. Paul Peterson and J. David Greenstone, in a study published in a 1977 assessment of federal antipoverty programs, recorded that the agency began to suggest that community action programs include approximately one third representation of low income groups, chosen wherever feasible in accordance with democratic techniques. The formulation was guidance rather than statute, but applicants understood the practical force of a suggestion from the office that approved their grants.

The one third expectation changed the politics of local boards. A proportion converted an abstract commitment into a countable fact, and residents could challenge a board by arithmetic rather than by argument about intentions. The agency pressed for democratic selection methods, including elections in target areas, because appointment by the officials whose agencies residents might challenge emptied representation of content. Congress wrote a board structure into law in the Economic Opportunity Amendments of 1967, where the Quie Amendment established tripartite boards of one third public officials, one third representatives of the poor, and one third representatives of the private sector, ratifying the proportion that practice had developed in 1965. The Green Amendment of the same year allowed state or local elected officials to designate the community action agency.

### Money That Did Not Pass Through City Hall

The participation clause cannot be separated from the funding structure of Title II, because the structure determined whose participation mattered. The act authorized community action grants to public and private nonprofit agencies, and funds did not have to flow through state governments or city governments to reach a neighborhood. The Office of Economic Opportunity dealt directly with the local agency that held the grant. For a mayor, the arrangement meant that a federally financed organization could operate inside the city and command resources comparable to a municipal department, without answering to the municipal government. For residents, the arrangement meant that an organization claiming their participation could acquire money, staff, and legal standing without the consent of officials whom residents often regarded as the source of their grievances.

Agencies used the money, and the independence that accompanied the money, in ways that ranged from conventional services to open advocacy. Agencies operated Head Start centers, health programs, manpower training, and neighborhood service centers, and agencies employed residents as aides, organizers, and paraprofessionals, turning participation into wages. Beyond services, a substantial number of agencies supported organizing. Staff helped tenants document housing code violations and press landlords and housing authorities for repairs. Agency supported legal services programs represented poor clients in disputes with welfare departments, landlords, and local governments, and legal services attorneys carried challenges to welfare eligibility rules into federal court. A history of community action prepared for practitioners recorded that legal services lawyers won cases at the Supreme Court that dismantled the distinction between the deserving poor and other eligible persons and established that a person eligible for a benefit held an entitlement to the benefit. In some communities, agency staff and neighborhood organizations assisted residents with voter registration, drawing on networks of churches and civil rights volunteers. Each activity followed from a reading of the clause under which residents were actors with claims, rather than clients with needs.

### Syracuse and the Politics of Direct Action

Syracuse, New York, supplied an early example of that friction. The community action effort in Syracuse, conducted through the local Community Development Association, moved beyond service planning into mobilization of poor residents for direct action on neighborhood grievances. Residents organized through the program pressed demands that included protection against unjust eviction and better garbage collection, and marches directed at Mayor Walsh confronted city hall in public form. The United States Conference of Mayors adopted a resolution in 1965 charging that maximum feasible participation, as administered, was instigating tensions in American cities, and mayoral pressure formed part of the background to the 1967 amendments.

### Mississippi and the Child Development Group

The Child Development Group of Mississippi carried the logic of the clause into the setting where the stakes of participation were highest. The organization formed in the spring of 1965 to operate Head Start centers, drawing on the networks built by the Mississippi civil rights movement. The Mississippi Encyclopedia records that the group began during the summer of 1965 with a federal grant of 1.5 million dollars, the largest inaugural Head Start program in the nation, and that during the first summer the group employed eleven hundred working class people in eighty four centers across twenty four counties, serving six thousand children with school readiness instruction, two meals each day, and basic medical services. The founders included Tom Levin, a psychoanalyst who had worked with the Medical Committee for Human Rights during the Mississippi Freedom Summer of 1964, and the board included Victoria Gray Adams of the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party and Marian Wright Edelman. The Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee Digital Gateway records that local movement leaders, contacted through Council of Federated Organizations and Freedom Democratic Party networks, set up school boards and hiring committees and canvassed for pupils, so that the program rested on the infrastructure of the freedom movement.

The Mississippi Encyclopedia notes that the organization was overwhelmingly Black in composition and closely allied with civil rights organizations, that employees included civil rights veterans such as Unita Blackwell, and that for some employees the program offered the only available employment because white reprisals had closed other work to people identified with civil rights activity. A Head Start center governed by a local board of Black parents, staffed by Black teachers and aides paid with federal funds, and independent of the white controlled school boards of the state, embodied maximum feasible participation in concrete institutional form. Opposition followed the same logic in reverse. Mississippi political leaders and the Mississippi State Sovereignty Commission alleged that federal dollars were subsidizing civil rights activity, and Senator John Stennis of Mississippi charged that the program promoted integration and Black militancy. Under political pressure, the Office of Economic Opportunity withdrew funding after the first summer. Funding returned in altered form after a congressional investigation, and the agency withdrew support again in October 1967.

### Why Bypassing State and Local Government Was a Design Choice

The direct federal to local funding channel was not an administrative accident, and the consequences in the segregated South were not incidental to the design. Earlier federal social programs had run through state and local governments, and state administration in the South had meant administration by white officials within a segregated order, with power to distribute benefits unequally or to exclude Black citizens from benefits and program employment altogether. A poverty program routed through the same machinery in 1964 would have delivered federal money to the institutions that many Black residents identified as the administrators of their exclusion. Historian Jill Quadagno, in a 1994 analysis, argued that the Johnson administration circumvented local welfare authorities in order to bypass southern obstruction of racial equality, through institutions outside the established state channels.

Stated neutrally, the choice presented a trade that no drafting could dissolve. Routing funds through state and local governments would have enlisted experienced machinery and the cooperation of elected officials, at the price of subjecting a program addressed to the poor, a disproportionate share of whom were Black citizens in the South, to officials who in many jurisdictions owed office to a restricted electorate and administered segregated institutions. Routing funds around those governments preserved the capacity to employ, serve, and be governed by people excluded from the established machinery, at the price of creating federally financed institutions that competed with elected governments for authority. The participation clause sat at the center of the trade, because participation by residents legitimated the bypass. Attacks on the clause therefore challenged the justification for the funding channel itself.

### Moynihan and the Retrospective Argument

Daniel Patrick Moynihan gave the clause its most famous retrospective treatment in Maximum Feasible Misunderstanding, published in February 1969. Moynihan reconstructed the path from the delinquency demonstrations through the drafting of the act, with extended attention to Mobilization for Youth, and argued that community action rested on ideas that the social scientists and foundation and government officials who promoted them had advanced without adequate acknowledgment of how tentative the underlying knowledge was. In Moynihan's account, professional reformers had displaced an older pattern in which reform impulses arose from the groups most affected by existing conditions, substituting reform designed by persons whose profession was the design of reform. The participation requirement promised residents a role that the professional sponsors had never clearly specified, and the gap between promise and specification produced conflict.

Reviewers disputed the Moynihan allocation of responsibility. A review symposium in Urban Affairs Quarterly in 1970 observed that Moynihan simplified the intentions of the drafting task force, noting that task force members drawn from the President's Committee on Juvenile Delinquency had wanted the involvement of the poor as a strategy for changing institutions, not solely as consultation. The clause survived in altered form, in the tripartite board structure and in the expectation, carried through later community services legislation, that agencies serving the poor would reserve a defined share of governing seats for representatives of the poor.

## The Mayoral Backlash and the Amendments That Followed

The resistance that reshaped community action came from inside the coalition that had enacted the statute. The mayors who objected most forcefully to the Community Action Program included Democrats who governed large cities and whose support the Johnson administration needed on other matters. The structural reason sat in Title II. Federal money moved from the Office of Economic Opportunity to local community action agencies, and section 202(a) required that a community action program be developed, conducted, and administered with the maximum feasible participation of residents of the areas and members of the groups served. Where an agency board gave poor residents a genuine share of authority, and agency staff organized tenants, welfare recipients, and neighborhood groups, the program created a federally financed institution that the mayor had not appointed, did not control, and could not dissolve. Mayors who had expected antipoverty money to arrive through city hall, in the pattern of earlier federal urban programs, confronted a parallel structure with its own board, its own staff, and a mandate that invited its constituency to press demands on municipal government.

The mayoral complaint developed along three lines. The first line was political: mayors charged that federal funds were paying for organization directed against city hall itself, in protest marches, picketing of municipal offices, and challenges to urban renewal plans and to the officials elected to run the city. The second line was accountability: an agency answered to the Office of Economic Opportunity and to its own board, and residents who disliked its decisions had no electoral remedy against decision makers whom no election had placed in office. The third line was administrative: mayors argued that a separate antipoverty agency duplicated work that municipal departments already performed in welfare, health, recreation, and employment services, producing competing bureaucracies with no single official responsible for the combined result.

### Why did mayors object to community action agencies?

Mayors objected because federal grants went directly to local agencies that elected officials did not control, agencies whose boards included poor residents, whose staff could organize protests against city hall, and whose programs overlapped city services without mayoral approval or accountability to voters at election time.

The objection rested on two competing understandings of local self government. In the understanding that the Office of Economic Opportunity carried into many cities during 1965 and 1966, participation was itself a remedy for a defect in municipal government, because the poor had been present in city politics mainly as clients of welfare departments and as objects of renewal and clearance decisions, and a board with resident members corrected that absence. In the understanding that mayors presented to Congress, democratic legitimacy ran through elections, and a board that no electorate had chosen possessed no comparable legitimacy, however representative its members appeared to be. A mayor who had won a citywide election could present himself as the only official who answered to all of the voters, including the poor, and could treat an appointed neighborhood board as a narrower body claiming public authority it had never received at the polls. That framing turned a fight over grants into a fight over the meaning of democratic representation.

Money and patronage sharpened the dispute. An agency hired staff, let contracts, operated centers, and distributed jobs in neighborhoods where a mayoral organization also distributed jobs and favors, and in a machine city an agency that hired residents outside the machine competed with the machine on its own ground. Historian Jill Quadagno, in The Color of Welfare, published in 1994, concluded that community action as the Office of Economic Opportunity defined the term was absent in Chicago because Mayor Richard J. Daley kept tight control of the city antipoverty program.

The United States Conference of Mayors gave the complaint an institutional channel. Individual mayors could bargain over a single city program, but the conference allowed city officials to formulate a common position and present that position to the administration and to Congress as the view of municipal government in the United States. In 1965 the conference adopted a resolution that treated maximum feasible participation as a source of tension in the cities, converting separate local grievances into an organized national claim. Sargent Shriver, the director of the Office of Economic Opportunity, treated the conference as a forum that had to be answered in person. In an oral history interview recorded for the Lyndon B. Johnson Library, Shriver recalled going several times to the conference and to meetings of the mayors of the largest cities to ask for cooperation, and he recalled being attacked by a number of mayors in those settings.

### Flashpoints in Chicago, Los Angeles, and Syracuse

Chicago supplied the clearest case of a mayor defeating the federal design by absorbing it. Daley stated his position during the legislative process in 1964, arguing, in testimony summarized by Adam Cohen and Elizabeth Taylor in American Pharaoh, published in 2000, that a program of this kind had to be administered by the duly constituted elected officials of the area in order to succeed. After enactment, Daley organized the local program as the Chicago Committee on Urban Opportunity, a body created by city ordinance in 1965, and placed himself at its head as chairman in an ex officio capacity. Cohen and Taylor recorded the composition that resulted: the board Daley named had ninety members, of whom seven were residents of poor neighborhoods, and the remaining membership drew heavily on machine loyalists, city officials, and business figures aligned with the mayor. The committee was administered by Deton Brooks, a Daley appointee, and Quadagno found that the committee delegated little authority downward and that its neighborhood centers operated as visible extensions of city government. The Office of Economic Opportunity recognized the arrangement as a dilution of the participation requirement, but Cohen and Taylor recorded that the Johnson administration overruled objections inside the agency rather than provoke a rupture with Daley, whose support the president valued. Chicago demonstrated that a determined mayor with presidential protection could hold the program inside city hall from the start.

Los Angeles supplied a prolonged fight over which organization would be recognized as the community action agency at all. The Youth Opportunities Board, a joint powers body created in 1962 by city, county, school, and probation authorities to address youth unemployment and delinquency, was an organization of officials and professionals. When the federal program arrived, the board reorganized under the name Economic and Youth Opportunities Agency and sought recognition as the local antipoverty agency. Mayor Sam Yorty supported that course, and opponents who regarded the board as controlled by the mayor organized an alternative body, the Economic Opportunity Federation, to contest the designation. Scholarship on the Los Angeles program, including the case study published by Dale Rogers Marshall in 1971 under the title The Politics of Participation in Poverty, treated board composition as the central political question in the city program, because the board decided whose participation counted as the participation of the poor. The dispute delayed the construction of an effective program, and the delay acquired a grave significance in August 1965, when unrest in the Watts district, beginning on August 11, 1965 after a traffic stop involving a young Black motorist, Marquette Frye, continued for six days. The documented record does not show that community action caused the Watts unrest, and the California commission that investigated the unrest, chaired by John A. McCone, directed its findings toward employment, housing, education, transportation, and relations between residents and the police. The significance of Watts for the antipoverty fight was narrower and political: a city in which the federal program had spent its first year in a designation struggle had little operating machinery in the affected district when the unrest came, and that gap became part of the national argument over whether the program raised expectations it had no capacity to meet.

Syracuse supplied the flashpoint in which an agency used participation for direct action against a mayor. The local organization, identified in accounts of the period as the Syracuse Community Development Association, mobilized poor residents around concrete municipal grievances, among them protection against unjust eviction and the quality of garbage collection, and residents marched against Mayor William F. Walsh. Each element of the Syracuse program that the Office of Economic Opportunity could describe as participation in action, a mayor could describe as federally financed agitation against an elected government, and the demands concerned services that city hall provided or failed to provide, so the mayor was placed in the position of answering, at demonstrations organized with the help of a federally funded staff, for the performance of his own departments. Other cities added episodes to the record that Congress weighed in 1967, among them Newark, where local officials blamed the antipoverty program for contributing to disorder in connection with the unrest of July 1967, a charge that program defenders disputed by pointing to agency efforts to calm neighborhoods during disturbances. The pattern across the episodes mattered more than any single episode: mayoral control produced friction between the federal agency and city hall, independence produced friction between organized residents and city hall, and a contested designation produced delay and a public contest over legitimacy.

### The Defense Offered by the Administration

Shriver defended the program on the ground that conflict was evidence the design was working as intended. Addressing the National Committee for Community Development in Washington in March 1965, Shriver argued in substance that the program could not succeed unless the poor participated, and that participation retained its value even when participation took the form of criticism of the institutions conducting the program. Where mayors treated friction as proof that federal money had escaped democratic control, Shriver treated friction as the predictable consequence of admitting into public decision making a population that established institutions had excluded.

Shriver carried a second line of defense into the congressional hearings of 1967, an empirical claim about the conduct of agencies in disturbed cities. Testifying in support of the program, Shriver argued that agencies in the communities where community action existed had calmed fears and frustrations, bridged the gap in communication between the poor and the rest of the community, and put people to work with training and education. On that account, the agencies deserved credit as institutions that stood between grievance and disorder, staffed by residents with standing in the neighborhoods where disorder was most likely. Shriver urged legislators to look past what he characterized as baseless criticism and smear tactics, and he asked Congress to distinguish between agencies that organized protest through lawful channels and the violence of the urban disorders of 1965, 1966, and 1967.

The defense did not prevail, and part of the reason lay in the administration conduct in the cases that mattered most to the mayors. The Johnson administration had protected Daley in Chicago, accepting a committee structure that the Office of Economic Opportunity regarded as inconsistent with the participation requirement, because the political cost of a confrontation with the mayor of Chicago exceeded the value the White House placed on enforcing the requirement in that city. Defenders of independent agencies could observe that the administration enforced participation against mayors with less national weight while accommodating the strongest machine in the country, and mayors could observe that the administration itself treated mayoral control as acceptable where a mayor was powerful enough to insist on it. By 1966 the administration had also accepted congressional changes that narrowed the program from within: the amendments of 1966 earmarked antipoverty funds for specified programs, and Shriver stated publicly that the combined effect of earmarking and reduced appropriations left the agency with less community action money than the minimum figure in the administration budget request and less than communities had spent in 1966.

### The Congressional Turn

The elections of November 8, 1966 changed the arithmetic under which the defense had to be made. The Democratic Party lost forty seven seats in the House of Representatives to the Republican Party, and although the Democratic Party retained majorities in both chambers, the losses fell heavily on the liberal members elected in 1964 who had supplied the votes for the Great Society program, and the conservative coalition of Republicans and southern Democrats emerged with greater practical weight in the House. A mayoral complaint that the administration might have contained in the Eighty ninth Congress faced a different reception in the Ninetieth Congress, where members of both parties had reason to distance themselves from a program associated with urban disorder, rising federal spending, and the war in Vietnam.

Representative Edith Green, a Democrat from Oregon serving on the Committee on Education and Labor, became the central House figure in the rewriting, a position of consequence because Green had built a congressional career on education and labor measures and had helped carry antipoverty legislation in earlier sessions. During hearings in August 1967, Green stated a rationale that joined the mayoral complaint to a theory of democratic accountability, declaring disapproval of the expenditure of federal funds to finance people outside government who worked for the express purpose of changing the political structure and overturning decisions made by mayors, council members, and other officials chosen by a majority of the people through the democratic process. Green did not argue that the poor should receive no services, or that residents should have no voice in agency affairs. Green argued that federal money could not legitimately finance an effort to displace the authority of elected officials, an argument that allowed members who supported antipoverty spending in principle to vote for structural limits on the agencies that delivered the spending. Representative Albert H. Quie, a Republican from Minnesota and a senior Republican voice on education and labor matters, supplied the complementary proposal on board composition: a governing board divided into three equal parts, one third public officials, one third representatives of the poor, and one third members drawn from the private sector, including business, labor, and civic organizations. The formula preserved a guaranteed place for representatives of the poor while ending any possibility that representatives of the poor might command a board majority on their own. A one third share guaranteed a voice and denied control.

### The Amendments Enacted Into Law

Congress enacted the settlement in the Economic Opportunity Amendments of 1967, Public Law 90-222, signed on December 23, 1967. The provision known as the Green Amendment provided that a community action agency had to be designated by the state or by local elected officials, so that recognition of an agency became an act of the elected government of the area served rather than a determination made in Washington between competing local claimants. The provision known as the Quie Amendment wrote the tripartite board into the statute, requiring that the board of a community action agency consist of one third public officials, at least one third representatives of the poor, and the remainder representatives of the private sector. Read together, the two amendments redistributed authority at both points where authority had been contested since 1964, at the door of the program, where designation decided which organization received federal recognition, and at the table, where board composition decided how the recognized organization would be governed.

The amendments also addressed the conduct that had supplied the mayors with their most vivid examples. The statute, in the amended section 603, barred programs assisted under the act from being carried on in a manner that identified the programs with partisan or nonpartisan political activity associated with a candidate or a contending faction in an election, with providing voters with transportation to the polls, or with voter registration activity, and directed the director, after consultation with the Civil Service Commission, to issue enforcement rules. Related provisions required agencies to adopt rules defining the duties of advocacy on behalf of the poor in a manner that precluded employees, in connection with the performance of their duties, from participating in picketing, protest, or other direct action in violation of law, together with agency standards on salaries, conflicts of interest, and employee competence and integrity. Advocacy on behalf of the poor remained a recognized function of an agency employee, while the use of a federally financed position for electoral activity, voter registration drives, and unlawful direct action fell outside the protected function. Congress did not repeal the participation clause of section 202(a). Congress instead surrounded the clause with a designation rule and a board rule, so that participation after December 1967 meant, at board level, the one third share the statute guaranteed, exercised inside an agency that elected officials had designated and on a board where public officials and private sector members together held two thirds of the seats.

### How Much Changed on the Ground

Scholarly judgment on the effect of the amendments divided, and the division turned on what counted as the life of the program. Daniel Patrick Moynihan, in Maximum Feasible Misunderstanding, published in 1969, argued that community action as practiced had departed from what the officials who drafted and enacted the program had intended, and that a provision adopted with limited purposes had been converted into an instrument of political mobilization that the government itself had not understood. A review symposium on the book in Urban Affairs Quarterly disputed the premise, arguing that Moynihan simplified the intentions of the Shriver task force and that sources associated with Peter Marris and Martin Rein, whose Dilemmas of Social Reform appeared in 1967, showed that task force members drawn from the President's Committee on Juvenile Delinquency had wanted the poor involved as a strategy for changing institutions, rather than as a means of coordination alone. On the first account, the amendments of 1967 corrected a departure. On the second account, the amendments cut back a purpose that had been present from the start.

A second division concerned institutional survival. Jim Masters, in a history of community action prepared for the community action network, recorded that after months of negotiation more than ninety five percent of the existing agencies were designated and recognized under the new procedure, that most designations of a different agency occurred in large cities where mayors perceived a shift in political power and designated a public agency instead, and that the Green and Quie Amendments ultimately had a positive effect on most agencies by connecting agency boards formally to the political, economic, and community power structures of the areas served. Other accounts of the period reach a different emphasis, treating the amendments as the point at which agencies turned from organizing and advocacy toward the administration of services under boards on which the poor held a permanent minority. The Chicago evidence assembled by Quadagno supports a further qualification: in a city where a mayor had held control from 1965, the amendments changed little, because the settlement Congress imposed on the country in 1967 resembled the arrangement Daley had imposed on Chicago in 1965. The record therefore supports neither a simple story of termination nor a simple story of continuity. The amendments left a national network of designated agencies in place, financed by federal grants and governed by boards on which representatives of the poor sat by statutory right, and the amendments ended the period in which an agency might claim, under the participation clause, a governing authority independent of the elected government of the place where the agency operated. Community action survived the mayoral backlash in a moderated and regularized form, as a program of services administered through locally designated institutions. On the question the statute of 1964 had left open, whether maximum feasible participation would mean a share in governing the program or a power capable of challenging the governments around the program, Congress in 1967 answered that participation would mean a share.

## The Dismantling Sequence

The agency created in August 1964 did not end in a single act of repeal. Between 1969 and 1981 the Office of Economic Opportunity lost its largest programs to the permanent departments of the executive branch, survived an attempt by the Nixon administration to abolish it by executive action alone, operated for six years under a new name and a replacement statute, and then lost its independent existence when Congress folded its remaining functions into a block grant administered through the states. Each stage followed a different legal route. The transfers of 1969 removed programs but left the agency and its community action authority intact. The confrontation of 1973 tested whether a president could terminate a statutory agency and a congressionally funded program without new legislation, and a federal court answered that a president could not. The settlement enacted at the end of 1974 accepted a smaller and less prominent agency in place of the office that had sat inside the Executive Office of the President. The abolition of 1981 succeeded where the attempt of 1973 had failed for one central reason: in 1981 Congress itself enacted the termination, as one title of a budget reconciliation law, and provided a successor funding mechanism in the same statute.

### Transferring Programs to the Permanent Departments

President Richard M. Nixon took office in January 1969 with a stated preference for reducing the Office of Economic Opportunity to a narrower role. The administration described the office as an incubator for programs during an initial experimental phase, after which successful programs were to move to the permanent departments that administered related functions on a continuing basis. Under that rationale, delegation was presented not as abandonment but as mainstreaming: a preschool program belonged with the department responsible for health and education, and a residential training program for young workers belonged with the department responsible for labor and manpower policy. The first director to carry out that design was Donald Rumsfeld, a Republican member of the House of Representatives from Illinois whom Nixon nominated in April 1969 and who was sworn in on May 26, 1969, at the age of thirty six, with the additional titles of Assistant to the President and membership in the Cabinet by presidential designation. The institutional irony was a matter of public record. As a congressman, Rumsfeld had voted against the creation of the Office of Economic Opportunity in 1964. In his 2011 memoir Rumsfeld recorded that he initially declined the appointment because of a belief that the agency did more harm than good, and that he accepted after Nixon added the assistant and Cabinet level status to the position. A director who had opposed the agency at its creation therefore presided over the first systematic redistribution of its programs, and under his directorship the office was reorganized to operate in the laboratory role the administration had described.

The largest transfer involved Head Start. The program had begun in the summer of 1965 as Project Head Start, operated by the Office of Economic Opportunity under its community action authority, and the 1964 statute had not named the program at all. In 1969 the Nixon administration transferred Head Start from the Office of Economic Opportunity to the Office of Child Development in the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare. Edward Zigler, a Yale psychologist who had served on the committee that planned Project Head Start, became director of the Office of Child Development. The transfer placed the preschool program inside the department that administered federal education and social welfare programs, and Head Start remained in that departmental lineage after the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare was divided in 1979 and 1980. The second major transfer involved the Job Corps. President Nixon moved the Job Corps from the Office of Economic Opportunity to the Department of Labor effective July 1, 1969. On April 11, 1969, Secretary of Labor George P. Shultz announced the closing of fifty nine Job Corps centers by July 1969, alongside a plan for smaller urban skill centers, so the transfer to the Labor Department arrived together with a contraction of the residential center network. Departmental histories of the Job Corps identified the administrative logic as linkage: placement inside the Labor Department tied the Job Corps to the department manpower training activities and to the state employment services that matched trained workers with employers. Other functions followed the same pattern. The administration sought transfer of the comprehensive health centers and the Foster Grandparents program to the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare. Community action, the function most closely identified with the 1964 act, remained within the shrinking office because no permanent department administered a comparable program of locally governed antipoverty agencies.

The transfers changed the character of the office that remained. An agency created to run a broad set of new programs directly from the Executive Office of the President became, under the Nixon design, a smaller organization whose principal operating responsibility was the Community Action Program and the network of local agencies that received its grants. Rumsfeld served as director until December 1970, when Nixon named him a counselor to the President, and the office passed to successors with a reduced program base and a reduced claim on presidential attention. The delegation strategy also created a political fact that shaped every later fight over the agency. Programs with identifiable departmental homes and identifiable beneficiary constituencies, including preschool children in Head Start and young trainees in the Job Corps, acquired defenders inside the permanent departments and inside the congressional committees that oversaw those departments. Community action had no equivalent departmental shelter. When the administration moved from delegation to outright abolition in 1973, community action funding was the target placed at the center of the proposal, and the local agencies that depended on that funding became the plaintiffs who blocked the attempt in court.

### Why did the attempt to abolish the agency by executive action fail?

The attempt failed because abolition required an act of Congress. A budget proposal that Congress had not enacted supplied no legal authority, a federal district court in the District of Columbia held in April 1973, and the court enjoined the acting director from terminating the agency and its community action funding.

The confrontation began with the budget the Nixon administration submitted in early 1973. The budget recommended termination of federal funding for community action activities, on the stated ground that new funding for such activities was to be a decision for local communities rather than for the federal government. A budget submission, however, is a proposal to Congress, and Congress had enacted no law ending the Office of Economic Opportunity or the Community Action Program. In January 1973 Philip Sanchez, who had become director of the office in 1971, announced his resignation. On January 31, 1973, the President named Howard J. Phillips as acting director. Phillips was an outspoken opponent of the agency he was appointed to run, a founder in 1960 of the conservative youth organization Young Americans for Freedom, and an administrator who described the community action approach in published interviews as conceptually flawed. Phillips treated the budget proposal as an instruction to wind the agency down. He issued directions for community action agencies to prepare for phase out, set in motion the termination of grants and the separation of employees, and proceeded on the premise that the agency would cease operating at the end of the fiscal year. Congressional committees held hearings in the first session of the Ninety third Congress on the proposed elimination of the office and on bills that would have preserved it, and Congress declined to enact the abolition the administration had requested.

Litigation decided the immediate question. Employees, their labor organizations, and community action agencies filed suit in the United States District Court for the District of Columbia, and Judge William B. Jones consolidated the actions, among them the suit brought by the headquarters employees union, Local 2677 of the American Federation of Government Employees, and the suit brought by four community action agencies led by the West Central Missouri Rural Development Corporation on behalf of the more than nine hundred community action agencies that received funds under section 221 of the act. The consolidated litigation, decided as Local 2677, American Federation of Government Employees v. Phillips, 358 F. Supp. 60, produced an order on April 11, 1973, enjoining Phillips from terminating, dissolving, or abolishing the Office of Economic Opportunity and from terminating the funding or functioning of the community action agencies. Judge Jones held that the dismantling was unauthorized by law and in excess of statutory authority. The reasoning rested on the sequence of enactments. The President had proposed a budget that eliminated the agency, but Congress had not acted on the proposal, so the proposal itself supplied no authority to abolish a program Congress had created and funded. The court also found that Phillips had failed to follow the procedure of the Reorganization Act for abolishing a federal agency, which required submission of a reorganization plan to Congress, and that the termination of employees had proceeded without the notice the Economic Opportunity Act required.

A second judgment removed the official who had directed the phase out. In Williams v. Phillips, 360 F. Supp. 1363, decided June 11, 1973, four United States senators, led by Senator Harrison A. Williams Jr., chairman of the committee with legislative jurisdiction over the agency, challenged the lawfulness of the appointment itself. The statute governing the office, codified at 42 U.S.C. 2941(a), provided for a director appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate. Phillips had been installed as acting director without nomination and confirmation, and the district court held that he was not serving validly in the office and enjoined him from taking action as acting director. Phillips resigned shortly after the June 1973 judgment. The two decisions together established the practical boundary that governed the remainder of the decade. The executive branch could transfer programs where Congress permitted transfer, could propose abolition, and could seek reduced appropriations, but the executive branch could not on its own authority extinguish an agency and a grant program that Congress had established by statute and had continued to fund. Abolition, if abolition was to occur, had to arrive in the form of legislation.

### A Renamed Agency Under a Replacement Statute

Congress supplied that legislation, in a form short of abolition, at the end of 1974. The Head Start, Economic Opportunity, and Community Partnership Act of 1974, H.R. 14449, was approved on January 4, 1975, as Public Law 93-644, 88 Stat. 2291, and the two dates attached to the law require precise statement. The act carries the year 1974 in its title because the Ninety third Congress enacted it during the 1974 session and completed passage before that Congress ended, and it belongs to the series of public laws numbered for the Ninety third Congress. The law took legal effect upon signature by President Gerald R. Ford on January 4, 1975, in the opening days of the Ninety fourth Congress. References to a 1974 act and references to a successor agency dating from 1975 therefore describe the same event from different sides: the authorizing statute was a product of 1974, and the renamed agency existed under that authority from 1975 forward.

The substance of the act was repeal and replacement rather than amendment at the margins. The 1974 act repealed the framework of the Economic Opportunity Act of 1964 as the governing charter for the federal antipoverty agency and substituted a new statutory structure under which the agency continued with a different name, a different position in the executive branch, and a narrowed set of functions. The Office of Economic Opportunity became the Community Services Administration. The change of position was as consequential as the change of name. The 1964 act had placed the Office of Economic Opportunity in the Executive Office of the President, a placement that signaled that the war on poverty was a presidential undertaking directed from beside the President. The Community Services Administration stood outside the Executive Office of the President as an independent agency in the executive branch, comparable in formal standing to other independent establishments rather than to a unit of the presidential staff. The director of the renamed agency no longer operated from within the institution that housed the President closest advisers, and the agency lost the access and the visibility that the original placement had conferred. In his statement on signing the act on January 4, 1975, President Ford praised the Congress for ending a stalemate over the legislation and stated an intention to avoid seeking funds for duplicate program authorities the enrolled bill contained, a reservation that reflected the compromise character of the settlement. The community action agencies survived the replacement of their authorizing statute, the tripartite board structure enacted in the amendments of 1967 continued as the governance form for local agencies, and the federal agency survived with them, reduced in rank within the government and separated from the presidency that had created the original office a decade earlier.

### The Community Services Administration Years

The six years of the Community Services Administration, from 1975 to 1981, were years of service delivery conducted under financial constraint. The mobilizing ambition associated with the early community action program, under which local agencies were expected to organize poor residents and press local institutions to change, receded as the operative description of the work. The local agencies funded through the Community Services Administration concentrated on the administration of identifiable services for low income households, among them weatherization of homes, emergency food and nutrition assistance, employment related assistance, and the community food programs that local agencies operated under federal grants. Federal oversight emphasized grant administration, eligibility rules, and the orderly distribution of funds to established local organizations. The network the agency administered was broad. At the time of its abolition in 1981, according to the Congressional Research Service history of the block grant that succeeded it, the Community Services Administration was administering grants involving nearly nine hundred community action agencies and about forty local community development corporations, together with several small categorical programs that local agencies typically operated.

Funding did not expand with the workload or with prices. Training and reference materials prepared for the community action network during the following decade summarized the pattern of the Community Services Administration period as funding for community action agencies that was basically flat, or below the rate of inflation, across the period. The dated anchors behind that summary are visible in the economic record of the late 1970s, when consumer prices rose at rates that reached double digits in 1979 and 1980, so that an appropriation held level in nominal dollars delivered a materially smaller quantity of services in each successive year. The agency also operated within the energy politics of the period, as rising fuel costs increased demand for the emergency energy and weatherization assistance that local agencies provided, while the flat funding pattern limited the capacity of those agencies to meet the added demand. Politically, the renamed agency occupied a weaker defensive position than the office of the 1960s had held, with no seat inside the Executive Office of the President and no presidential campaign attached to its mission. Survival through the Ford and Carter administrations rested on the congressional committees that authorized its programs and on the local agencies in nearly every state whose operations depended on its grants. That combination held for six years. It did not hold after the election of 1980.

### Abolition Through a Budget Law

President Ronald Reagan took office in January 1981 proposing a broad consolidation of federal grant programs. For the Community Services Administration, the administration proposal was to consolidate the agency functions with eleven other social service programs into a single block grant to the states, a design that would have ended the separate federal antipoverty agency and merged its activities into a general stream of state administered social service funding. Congress rejected that consolidation proposal in the form the administration submitted it. The enacted settlement, in the assessment recorded by the Congressional Research Service, created two block grants instead of one merged grant: the Social Services Block Grant under Title XX of the Social Security Act, and a separate Community Services Block Grant composed of the activities the Community Services Administration had administered. The separate block grant preserved a distinct federal funding stream for community action, routed through a new administrative path.

The vehicle for abolition was the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981, Public Law 97-35, enacted in August 1981. Title VI of that act, at section 671, enacted the Community Services Block Grant Act, codified at 42 U.S.C. 9901 et seq., and repealed the statutory basis of the Community Services Administration. The agency was abolished effective September 30, 1981, the final day of fiscal year 1981. Administration of the successor program passed to a newly created Office of Community Services inside the Department of Health and Human Services, the department whose predecessor, the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare, had received Head Start from the original office twelve years earlier. The 1981 termination succeeded by the route the courts had specified in 1973. Congress enacted the abolition in a statute, set an effective date at the close of a fiscal year, and provided in the same statute for the disposition of the functions being terminated, so no question of executive action in excess of statutory authority arose.

The design of the successor grant determined what survived. Under the Community Services Block Grant, the federal government made grants to states, territories, and tribal governments, and the states in turn distributed the funds to local eligible entities. The statute directed states to pass through ninety percent of their allotments to the local level. The eligible entities at the local level were in substance the same organizations that the Office of Economic Opportunity and the Community Services Administration had funded: the community action agencies, whose eligibility under the earlier determinations carried into the block grant structure, together with other designated local organizations in areas without a community action agency. The funding relationship changed even where the recipient organizations did not. Before October 1981 a community action agency received its principal federal grant through a direct relationship with a federal antipoverty agency whose sole mission was the antipoverty program. Under the block grant the same agency received federal funds through a state government intermediary, under a state plan, with the state responsible for monitoring and for the distribution formula within the state. Federal direction narrowed to the requirements the block grant statute imposed, and state governments acquired the discretion that the federal agency had exercised over allocations among local organizations.

The dismantling sequence that began with the transfers of 1969 therefore ended in 1981 with a division between institution and network. The institution, the independent federal antipoverty agency created as the Office of Economic Opportunity in 1964, renamed in 1975, and abolished effective September 30, 1981, ceased to exist after a life of seventeen years. The network, the locally governed community action agencies with tripartite boards that the 1964 act and its 1967 amendments had called into being, passed into the block grant structure as the designated local recipients of the successor program. Programs that had been transferred to permanent departments in 1969 and afterward, including Head Start in the Department of Health and Human Services and the Job Corps in the Department of Labor, continued under their departmental authorities, unaffected by the repeal that ended the agency from which each program had originated.

## The Survivors, the Poverty Record, and the Honest Limits

The Economic Opportunity Act of 1964 was repealed in stages, and the Office of Economic Opportunity disappeared through renaming and abolition between 1975 and 1981. The programs the statute had launched followed a different path. The best known creations of 1964 and 1965 left the Office of Economic Opportunity one by one, acquired separate statutory authority in education, labor, health, and service law, and operated under that authority at dated points through 2019. The act functioned less as a permanent code than as an incubator, a short lived administrative home in which programs were invented and tested at small scale.

Survival was not the same as victory. The official poverty rate fell sharply in the decade after 1964 in the Census Bureau series that defined the public argument. Yet the cause of the fall was contested from the start, producing two durable and incompatible accounts, one crediting economic growth and expanded transfers and one blaming transfers for eroding work and family formation. A third position, developed by Martha Bailey and Sheldon Danziger in scholarship published in 2013, held that the official measure itself distorted the verdict. Behind the dispute stood a plainer fact. The statute never guaranteed an income, never created employment at the scale of joblessness among the poor, and never received appropriations close to the sums that eliminating poverty required.

### Which Programs Survived the End of the Office of Economic Opportunity?

Five creations outlived the Office of Economic Opportunity under separate authority by 2019: Head Start, Job Corps, Volunteers in Service to America, federally funded legal services, and community health centers, joined by the community action agency network funded through the Community Services Block Grant after 1981.

Each survivor escaped by a distinct legal route. Head Start and Job Corps survived by transfer, moving in 1969 to cabinet departments with established grant making machinery. Volunteers in Service to America survived by separation, receiving an independent statutory home in the Domestic Volunteer Service Act of 1973. Legal services survived by incorporation, passing in 1974 to a federally chartered nonprofit corporation. Community health centers survived by absorption into the Public Health Service Act, where section 330 gave the model a permanent place in health law. The community action agencies survived by conversion, gaining a state administered block grant in 1981 that kept the local network and its board structure in statute.

### Head Start and the Move to a Permanent Early Childhood Statute

Project Head Start began in the summer of 1965 as a summer program for preschool children from low income families, financed under the community action authority of the Economic Opportunity Act rather than under any provision that named the program in the 1964 text. The design joined early education to health screening, nutrition, and parent participation. In 1969 the Nixon administration transferred Head Start to the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare. The transfer removed the program from an agency whose abolition was already under discussion.

The decisive legal change came in 1981. The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981, Public Law 97-35, carried the Head Start Act as a freestanding title, codified at 42 U.S.C. 9801 and following sections, and grants flowed directly from the Department of Health and Human Services to local grantees under that authority after 1981. Congress reauthorized the act through the Coats Human Services Reauthorization Act of 1998, Public Law 105-285, and again through the Improving Head Start for School Readiness Act of 2007, Public Law 110-134, which revised standards, monitoring, and teacher qualifications. By 2019 Head Start operated under the 2007 reauthorization as amended, administered by the Office of Head Start within the Administration for Children and Families.

### Job Corps and the Migration Into Workforce Law

Job Corps was the one major program named and constructed in the Economic Opportunity Act itself, authorized in Title I of the 1964 statute as a residential education and training program for unemployed young people. The Office of Economic Opportunity operated the centers directly and through contractors during the first five years. In 1969 administration transferred to the Department of Labor, and Job Corps spent the following decades inside the sequence of general workforce statutes rather than inside antipoverty law. Authority appeared in the Comprehensive Employment and Training Act, in the Job Training Partnership Act of 1982, and in the Workforce Investment Act of 1998, each preserving the residential center model and federal contracting for center operations.

The Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act of 2014, Public Law 113-128, carried Job Corps in Subtitle C of Title I and restated the program purpose in the language of connection to the labor force, recognized credentials, and economic self sufficiency. By 2019 Job Corps operated under the 2014 act, administered by the Employment and Training Administration of the Department of Labor. Survival carried a cost in identity. Within workforce law, Job Corps was evaluated as a training intervention for disadvantaged youth, measured against employment and earnings outcomes, rather than as one arm of a coordinated attack on the causes of poverty.

### Volunteers in Service to America and the National Service Framework

Volunteers in Service to America, known by the acronym VISTA, began operations in 1965 under Title VIII of the Economic Opportunity Act, placing full time volunteers with local projects that addressed illiteracy, health, housing, and community organization in poor areas. Administration moved away from the Office of Economic Opportunity in the early 1970s, and the Domestic Volunteer Service Act of 1973, Public Law 93-113, gave VISTA a separate statutory basis within the ACTION agency. The 1973 act supplied the authority under which assignments, volunteer terms, and stipends operated for the following two decades.

The National and Community Service Trust Act of 1993, Public Law 103-82, reorganized the field again. The 1993 act created the Corporation for National and Community Service, folded VISTA into the AmeriCorps family as AmeriCorps VISTA, and tied service to an education award financed through the National Service Trust. The Edward M. Kennedy Serve America Act of 2009, Public Law 111-13, reauthorized both the national service laws and the Domestic Volunteer Service Act. By 2019 AmeriCorps VISTA operated under the Domestic Volunteer Service Act as amended, administered by the Corporation for National and Community Service, with the antipoverty assignment of volunteers retained in the statutory purpose.

### Legal Services and the Independent Corporation

Federal legal services for the poor began in 1965 as a program of the Office of Economic Opportunity, making grants to local legal aid organizations and funding litigation over welfare administration, housing, consumer debt, and access to benefits. The program generated sustained opposition because federally funded lawyers sued state and local governments. President Richard Nixon proposed an independent corporation in 1971, presenting separation as a method of insulating legal assistance from political pressure. Congress enacted the design in the Legal Services Corporation Act of 1974, Public Law 93-355, signed on July 25, 1974, which amended the Economic Opportunity Act to transfer the program to a private nonprofit corporation governed by a board appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate.

The corporation began operations in 1975 and distributed federal funds to local legal aid programs rather than providing representation directly. The authorization of appropriations expired after fiscal year 1980, and Congress enacted no further reauthorization through 2019, but annual appropriations acts funded the corporation in every year of that interval and carried restrictions on grantee activities, with major restrictions added in 1996. By 2019 the Legal Services Corporation operated under the 1974 act as modified by annual appropriations law, funding civil legal assistance in noncriminal matters for clients who met income eligibility standards.

### Community Health Centers and Section 330 of the Public Health Service Act

The health centers began in 1965 as neighborhood health centers financed by the Office of Economic Opportunity, following proposals by physicians Jack Geiger and Count Gibson for comprehensive primary care joined to community participation. The first centers were funded in Boston and in rural Mississippi in 1965. Administration migrated to the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare, and the centers operated for a period under section 314(e) of the Public Health Service Act. The Special Health Revenue Sharing Act of 1975, Public Law 94-63, formalized the program in a new section 330 of that act, renamed the centers as community health centers, required service to medically underserved areas, and required consumer majorities on governing boards.

Later amendments consolidated programs for migrant health, health care for homeless individuals, and residents of public housing within the section 330 structure, and the Health Centers Consolidation Act of 1996, Public Law 104-299, restated section 330 in the form that governed the Health Center Program in the following decades. By 2019 the Health Center Program operated under section 330, codified at 42 U.S.C. 254b, administered by the Health Resources and Services Administration, with grants to community based organizations meeting statutory requirements for services, sliding fee scales, and consumer majority governance.

### The Community Action Agency Network and the Community Services Block Grant

The community action agencies were the delivery system of the original statute, designated under Title II to plan and operate local antipoverty programs with the maximum feasible participation of residents of the areas served. The network passed to the Community Services Administration when the Head Start, Economic Opportunity, and Community Partnership Act of 1974 replaced the Office of Economic Opportunity, effective in 1975. The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981 abolished the Community Services Administration effective September 30, 1981, and created the Community Services Block Grant, codified at 42 U.S.C. 9901 and following sections, administered through the Department of Health and Human Services with allotments to states. States were required to pass at least 90 percent of the allotment to eligible entities, primarily agencies designated before 1981.

Congressional Research Service reporting and federal program materials through 2019 described a network of roughly one thousand agencies serving the large majority of American counties. The governance settlement of the late 1960s survived the conversion. The tripartite board structure set by the Quie Amendment of 1967, dividing membership in thirds among public officials, representatives of the low income community, and representatives of private organizations, was carried into the block grant statute at 42 U.S.C. 9910. The Community Services Block Grant Act of 1998, Public Law 105-285, reauthorized the program and added performance measurement requirements. By 2019 the agencies administered portfolios combining the block grant with Head Start, weatherization, and energy assistance.

### The Education Programs That Moved Elsewhere

Two smaller components survived by migration into education law. Adult basic education began under Title II of the Economic Opportunity Act as a state grant program for adults whose inability to read, write, or speak English impaired employment. The Adult Education Act of 1966, Title III of the Elementary and Secondary Education Amendments of 1966, Public Law 89-750, moved the program into the education statutes, and authority later passed through the Adult Education and Family Literacy Act, Title II of the Workforce Investment Act of 1998 and of the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act of 2014. The College Work Study Program began under Title I of the 1964 statute, and the Higher Education Act of 1965 absorbed the program into the federal student aid structure as Title IV work study, under which the Federal Work Study Program operated through 2019.

### The Poverty Record in the Official Measure

The statistical record came from the official poverty measure of the Census Bureau, built on economy food plan thresholds developed by Mollie Orshansky in 1963 and 1964 and adopted as the federal statistical standard in 1969. The measure compared pretax cash income against thresholds that varied by family size and composition and were adjusted for inflation, and the series excluded in kind benefits such as food stamps, Medicaid, and housing assistance, as well as taxes and tax credits. Within that series, the poverty rate stood at 19.0 percent in 1964. The rate fell to 12.1 percent in 1969 and reached 11.1 percent in 1973, the lowest value in the official series through 2019, a decline of 7.9 percentage points concentrated between 1964 and 1969.

The same series supplied the evidence for disappointment. After 1973 the official rate moved within a band bounded by recession driven increases and expansion driven decreases, and the rate in no year through 2019 fell below the 11.1 percent recorded in 1973. Elderly poverty behaved differently, falling from 28.5 percent in 1966 to far lower levels by the middle 1970s and remaining far below the 1966 level through 2019, a pattern analysts across the dispute connected to Social Security benefit increases and cost of living adjustments enacted between 1968 and 1972. Child poverty fell sharply in the 1960s and reversed part of the decline in later decades.

### Growth, Transfers, and the Fight Over Credit

One family of explanations assigned the 1964 to 1973 decline principally to the economy and to cash transfers that the official measure counted. The economy expanded without recession between 1961 and 1969, labor markets tightened, and earnings among low wage workers rose, so that market income alone carried many families across the thresholds. Social Security increases raised beneficiary incomes in a form the official measure recorded directly, and expansions of Aid to Families with Dependent Children and of the Food Stamp Program between 1964 and 1974 enlarged the transfer system. Analysts who emphasized growth and transfers treated the Economic Opportunity Act programs as too small in budgetary terms to account for a movement of nearly eight points in the national rate.

A measurement problem complicated both credit and blame. Because the official measure excluded in kind benefits, the measure understated the effect of fast growing instruments including food stamps, Medicaid, and subsidized housing. Research associated with the Supplemental Poverty Measure, which the Census Bureau began publishing in 2011 and which researchers extended backward in anchored form, found larger declines than the official series showed, because the supplemental framework counted food assistance, housing subsidies, and refundable tax credits as resources and subtracted necessary expenses. Christopher Jencks, reviewing the Bailey and Danziger volume in 2015, accepted that the official rate misled readers about program effects while insisting that President Johnson had promised an unconditional elimination of poverty, so that judgment by the headline rate retained a political logic.

### The Case Associated With Charles Murray

The strongest contrary account appeared in Losing Ground, published by Charles Murray in 1984, with the subtitle American Social Policy, 1950 to 1980. Murray argued that the policy changes of the middle 1960s altered the incentives facing low income Americans in ways that reduced work effort, delayed marriage, and increased births outside marriage, and that expanded transfers made those outcomes more likely by raising the value of benefits relative to low wage employment and by weakening the stigma and eligibility rules that had limited receipt. In the Murray account, the slowdown in measured progress after the middle 1960s and the rise in nonmarital births formed a single pattern explained by rational responses to changed rules. The argument implied that transfer expansion had, over a longer horizon, entrenched the conditions that produced poverty, and Murray proposed eliminating most federal transfers for working age people.

The response from poverty researchers was immediate and largely negative on method. A special report issued by the Institute for Research on Poverty at the University of Wisconsin in Madison in 1985 collected assessments from affiliated researchers who rejected the broad condemnation of Great Society policy in Losing Ground, while agreeing that program design required rethinking. Christopher Jencks, in a 1985 review, disputed Murray readings of several statistical series and argued that changes in family structure reflected longer running trends that transfer rules alone could not explain. Later evaluations of specific incentive claims, including studies of whether benefit levels explained differences in nonmarital childbearing across states, generally found effects small relative to the magnitudes the Murray argument required. The influence of the book outlasted the scholarly verdict, supplying a framework for the emphasis on work requirements, time limits, and family formation in the welfare legislation of 1996.

### What Bailey and Danziger Found in the Evidence

Martha Bailey and Sheldon Danziger addressed the dispute most systematically in Legacies of the War on Poverty, the 2013 Russell Sage Foundation volume that Bailey and Danziger edited, assembling program by program evaluations covering education, health, nutrition, housing, and income support. The assessment rejected two summary verdicts at once. Against the verdict of failure, the studies documented substantial effects in particular domains, including long term gains for Head Start participants in analyses by Chloe Gibbs, Jens Ludwig, and Douglas Miller, reductions in infant mortality associated with Medicaid and community health centers in the analysis by Barbara Wolfe, and a child poverty rate in 2010 that Jane Waldfogel estimated would have stood three percentage points higher without the nutrition and income support programs descended from the period. Against the verdict of success, Bailey and Danziger emphasized that the aggregate rate depended on forces outside the programs, including wage stagnation after 1973 and the growth of single parent families, so that program effects could be real while the headline rate stagnated.

The evidentiary position in the synthesis was conditional. Programs were to be judged against counterfactual estimates of outcomes without the programs, domain by domain, and not against a single cash income rate that excluded many delivered benefits. On that standard, the studies collected in 2013 supported conclusions that food assistance improved nutrition and child outcomes, that health programs improved access and reduced infant mortality, that early childhood programs produced gains persisting in schooling and earnings even where test score gains faded, and that adult training produced the weakest results in the portfolio.

### What the Statute Did Not Do

The limits of the statute were structural. The Economic Opportunity Act created no entitlement to income. No provision guaranteed a minimum income to families below the thresholds, and the cash assistance programs that existed in 1964, principally Aid to Families with Dependent Children and Old Age Assistance, rested on the Social Security Act and remained outside the statute. The Family Assistance Plan proposed by President Nixon in 1969 failed in Congress, and the 1964 design supplied services, training, and community organization rather than income.

The statute likewise created no employment program at the scale of joblessness among the poor. Job Corps, the Neighborhood Youth Corps, and the work experience programs of 1964 and 1965 enrolled participants in the hundreds of thousands in peak years, while the number of poor Americans stood above 25 million in 1964 and program capacity fell short of need by an order of magnitude. Public service employment at a scale of millions of jobs arrived only with the Comprehensive Employment and Training Act of 1973, and that experiment ended with the repeal of the 1973 act in 1982. The 1964 theory of employment ran through preparation, credentials, and removal of barriers, on the assumption that a growing economy would supply the jobs.

Appropriations imposed the third limit. The sums authorized and appropriated for the act in the first years amounted to roughly one billion dollars annually across all titles, divided among community action, youth programs, rural loans, work study, adult education, and administration, while the aggregate income deficit of the poor stood at approximately 10.1 billion dollars for 1969 alone in Census Bureau calculations, after five years of decline. The arithmetic fixed the character of the enterprise. A service strategy financed at that level could change institutions, create programs that later statutes enlarged, and improve outcomes for identifiable participants, and the record assembled by Bailey and Danziger documented gains of that kind. The same arithmetic meant the statute could not, by its own resources, eliminate the condition that the declaration of 1964 had promised to end.

## Closing Assessment

The fate of the agency and the fate of the programs diverged so completely that each requires a separate verdict. The agency failed as an institution. The Office of Economic Opportunity lost its best known programs to the permanent departments in 1969, lost its original name and its place in the Executive Office of the President in the settlement enacted at the end of 1974, and lost its existence when Congress abolished the Community Services Administration effective September 30, 1981. No independent federal antipoverty agency of comparable design replaced the office after 1981. A coordinating command placed beside the President, intended to stand above departmental routine, depended on presidential protection, and presidential protection ended with the administration that had created the office. The institution also carried a structural defect from the beginning: the participation clause generated political conflict faster than the office generated defenders with power equal to the mayors and members of Congress arrayed against the clause.

The programs told the opposite story. Job Corps operated by 2019 under the workforce statute of 2014. Head Start operated under a dedicated Head Start Act. The volunteer program operated as AmeriCorps VISTA under the Domestic Volunteer Service Act. Legal services operated through an independent corporation created in 1974. Community health centers operated under section 330 of the Public Health Service Act. Community action agencies operated through a block grant to the states, governed by boards that preserved the tripartite settlement of 1967. Each survivor had migrated from an omnibus antipoverty statute into a permanent corner of federal law, with a department, a constituency, and an appropriations history of its own. The migration explains the paradox of the record: the statute lost every fight about the agency and won, program by program, most of the fights about the services.

Precision about limits belongs beside that survival record. The statute plainly did not end poverty, and the official rate, which stood at 11.1 percent in 1973 after a decade of growth and expanded transfers, recorded a reduction rather than an elimination. The statute did not guarantee income to any family, did not create public employment at a scale matching unemployment among the poor, and did not appropriate sums proportioned to the declaration of 1964; roughly one billion dollars a year across all titles in the first years divided quickly among a national portfolio. The statute did not name Head Start, the program most associated with the act in public memory, and did not address housing discrimination, which Congress took up separately in [the Fair Housing Act of 1968](/2015/11/01/fair-housing-act-1968-complete-guide/). The statute also did not settle the meaning of its own central phrase. Congress rewrote maximum feasible participation in 1967, three years after enactment, and the argument over participation continued inside the block grant structure afterward.

Readers following the longer arc of federal antipoverty law can trace the next redesign in [the welfare reform act of 1996](/2019/08/01/welfare-reform-act-1996-guide/), weigh the measured record in [the outcomes of welfare reform](/2019/10/15/welfare-reform-1996-outcomes/), and place both statutes in sequence through [the United States legislation study guide](/2022/06/15/us-legislation-study-guide/). A statute whose programs scattered across half a dozen successor authorities rewards careful record keeping, and readers tracing each migration can [keep your statute notes, citations, and case chronologies together free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html). The Economic Opportunity Act rewards that care because the central lesson sits in the divergence itself. Congress in 1964 built an agency that later Congresses dismantled piece by piece, and built programs that later Congresses, including Congresses hostile to the agency, chose repeatedly to preserve. The declaration promised to eliminate a paradox. The durable achievement was narrower and stranger: a set of services, a network of local agencies, and a contested idea about who governs programs for the poor, each carried forward under law that bore other names.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the Economic Opportunity Act create?

The act created the Office of Economic Opportunity in the Executive Office of the President and a portfolio of programs distributed across eight titles. Title I created the Job Corps, the Neighborhood Youth Corps, and a college work study program. Title II created the Community Action Program and the local community action agencies that delivered most services. Title III authorized loans and assistance aimed at rural poverty, Title IV authorized Economic Opportunity Loans for small businesses, and Title V authorized a work experience program for adults. Title VIII authorized Volunteers in Service to America, the domestic volunteer corps known as VISTA. The office held the portfolio together, set program rules, funded local agencies directly, and assigned Sargent Shriver as the first director charged with turning a declaration of policy into operating institutions across the country within a single fiscal year and under congressional scrutiny.

### Q: What is maximum feasible participation in the Economic Opportunity Act?

Maximum feasible participation was the standard that section 202(a) of the act attached to the definition of a community action program. The statutory sentence required that such a program be developed, conducted, and administered with the maximum feasible participation of residents of the areas and members of the groups served. The phrase governed who sat at the table when a local agency planned spending, hired staff, and judged results. In practice the Office of Economic Opportunity read the clause to require representation of poor residents on agency boards and in program design, not mere consultation after decisions had been made. The wording left the degree of participation undefined, and that gap between a mandatory verb and an unspecified measure allowed local agencies, mayors, and federal officials to construct incompatible accounts of compliance, each grounded in the same sentence of enacted text.

### Q: Why did mayors turn against the Economic Opportunity Act?

Mayors turned against the act because community action money and authority flowed to local agencies that city hall did not control. Federal grants reached community action agencies directly, and those agencies hired organizers, funded legal services and neighborhood programs, and gave poor residents board seats from which municipal decisions on housing, renewal, and welfare administration could be challenged in public. Mayors such as Richard J. Daley of Chicago argued that elected officials answered to the whole electorate, including the poor, while appointed neighborhood boards answered to no electorate at all. The United States Conference of Mayors adopted a resolution in 1965 treating maximum feasible participation as a source of tension in the cities, and mayoral complaints reached Congress as an organized national case that the federal government was financing a rival political structure inside municipal boundaries.

### Q: Did the Economic Opportunity Act create Head Start?

The enacted text did not create Head Start by name, and no title of the statute mentioned the program. Head Start began in the summer of 1965 as Project Head Start, launched by the Office of Economic Opportunity under the broad community action authority of Title II, which funded comprehensive local programs against poverty. The office used that authority to finance a child development program combining preschool education, health screening, nutrition, and parent involvement, delivered through local agencies and school systems. In 1969 the program moved to the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare, where a permanent administrative home replaced the improvisation of the launch period. Congress later supplied a dedicated statutory basis in the Head Start Act, under which the program operated by 2019, decades after the community action authority that had launched the program had been repealed.

### Q: Where was the Economic Opportunity Act office placed?

Congress placed the Office of Economic Opportunity in the Executive Office of the President, alongside the instruments of presidential management rather than inside a cabinet department. The placement was deliberate. A director seated beside the President could summon departments that owned pieces of the poverty problem, press for coordination across Labor, Agriculture, and Health, Education, and Welfare, and claim presidential priority when budgets were assembled. Placement outside the departments also protected new programs from absorption into older routines during the launch period, and gave Sargent Shriver direct access to President Lyndon B. Johnson. The same placement carried a cost that later events exposed. An office without a departmental base had no large permanent bureaucracy or cabinet secretary to defend the agency when presidential sponsorship ended and Congress reconsidered the design during the late 1960s and the 1970s.

### Q: What happened to the agency from the Economic Opportunity Act?

The agency passed through transfer, judicial rescue, renaming, and abolition. In 1969 the Nixon administration transferred the Job Corps to the Department of Labor and Head Start to the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare. In 1973 an attempt to dismantle the Office of Economic Opportunity by executive action under acting director Howard Phillips failed in federal court. The Head Start, Economic Opportunity, and Community Partnership Act of 1974 renamed the office as the Community Services Administration, effective in 1975, with a reduced portfolio centered on community action. The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981 then abolished the Community Services Administration effective September 30, 1981, and replaced direct federal funding of community action with the Community Services Block Grant administered through the Department of Health and Human Services. The sequence ended the independent antipoverty agency seventeen years after creation.

### Q: Which Economic Opportunity Act programs still exist?

By 2019 several programs from the 1964 portfolio operated under successor statutes. Job Corps operated under the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act of 2014. The college work study program operated as Federal Work-Study under the Higher Education Act of 1965. Head Start operated under the Head Start Act, and Volunteers in Service to America operated as AmeriCorps VISTA under the Domestic Volunteer Service Act. The Legal Services Program operated through the Legal Services Corporation created in 1974. Community health centers that began with office funding operated under section 330 of the Public Health Service Act. Community action agencies operated through the Community Services Block Grant, and adult basic education operated under the Adult Education and Family Literacy Act. Other titles ended, including the Neighborhood Youth Corps and the work experience program.

### Q: Who ran the Economic Opportunity Act programs?

Sargent Shriver ran the federal office as the first director of the Office of Economic Opportunity, appointed by President Lyndon B. Johnson after leading the task force that drafted the legislation. Shriver set program rules, recruited staff from the Peace Corps and the departments, and defended community action before mayors and congressional committees. Below the federal office, designated community action agencies ran most local services. Those agencies were public or private nonprofit bodies governed by boards that, after the amendments of 1967, divided seats among public officials, representatives of the poor, and private organizations. Federal program offices supervised the Job Corps, the volunteer corps, and other national programs, and after the transfers of 1969 the permanent departments administered the programs that had migrated, each under the departmental leadership and appropriations structure of the receiving agency.

### Q: What role did Sargent Shriver play in shaping the statute?

Shriver chaired the presidential task force that converted the antipoverty decision of early 1964 into a draft bill, and Shriver argued successfully for a new office rather than distribution of the funds among existing departments. The task force worked at speed, drawing on the Council of Economic Advisers, officials from the President's Committee on Juvenile Delinquency, and program designs circulating in the departments, and produced the architecture of titles that Congress enacted with limited structural change. Shriver brought administrative credibility from direction of the Peace Corps, and President Johnson treated that record as evidence that a new agency could be staffed and launched quickly. After enactment Shriver served as the first director, approved community action grants, interpreted the participation clause generously toward resident involvement, and absorbed the mayoral backlash that the interpretation produced during the first years of operation.

### Q: How close were the votes that passed the statute in Congress?

The margins were comfortable in the Senate and narrower in the House. The Senate passed the bill, S. 2642, on July 23, 1964, by 61 votes to 34. The House passed the measure on August 8, 1964, by 226 votes to 185, a margin of 41 votes in a chamber where defections during the amendment process had threatened the community action title. Support crossed party lines, since a bloc of Republicans joined the Democratic majority, and opposition also crossed party lines, joining conservative Democrats, many from southern states, with Republicans who objected to federal direction of local programs and to the participation clause. President Johnson signed the act on August 20, 1964. The recorded margins mattered afterward because defenders of the office could claim an enacted majority mandate, while opponents treated the House margin as evidence that the design had never commanded settled consent.

### Q: What did the Green Amendment change about community action?

The Green Amendment, carried by Representative Edith Green of Oregon in the Economic Opportunity Amendments of 1967, Public Law 90-222, changed who controlled designation of a community action agency. Before the amendment, the Office of Economic Opportunity could recognize a local agency, often a private nonprofit body, without the consent of elected local government. The amendment gave state and local elected officials the power to designate the community action agency for an area, which allowed a city or county government to claim the designation for a public body or to shift recognition toward an agency acceptable to elected officials. The change answered the central mayoral complaint without ending federal funding, and the amendment converted community action from a federally sponsored alternative structure into a program that local elected government could capture, supervise, or join through the designation decision.

### Q: What did the Quie Amendment require of community action boards?

The Quie Amendment, carried by Representative Albert Quie of Minnesota in the amendments of 1967, fixed the composition of community action agency boards by thirds. One third of a board consisted of public officials or designated representatives of public officials. At least one third consisted of representatives of the poor, chosen through a democratic selection process in the areas served. One third consisted of members drawn from business, labor, religious, welfare, education, and other private organizations in the community. The formula replaced open ended argument about maximum feasible participation with an arithmetic settlement that guaranteed poor residents a defined share of governing seats while guaranteeing elected officials an equal share. Congress later carried the tripartite structure into the Community Services Block Grant statute at 42 U.S.C. 9910, where board composition remained a condition of funding by 2019.

### Q: Why did the attempt to dismantle the agency by executive action fail in court?

The dismantling attempt of 1973 failed because the executive branch acted without statutory authority. Acting director Howard Phillips, installed in January 1973, directed community action agencies to prepare for phase out and set terminations in motion on the premise that a presidential budget proposal had ended the office. In Local 2677, American Federation of Government Employees v. Phillips, 358 F. Supp. 60, decided April 11, 1973, Judge Jones enjoined Phillips from terminating, dissolving, or abolishing the office and from terminating the funding of the community action agencies, holding the dismantling unauthorized by law and in excess of statutory authority, since Congress had created and funded the programs and had not enacted the proposed abolition. A second judgment, Williams v. Phillips, decided June 11, 1973, held that Phillips served invalidly because the directorship required presidential nomination and Senate confirmation, and Phillips resigned shortly afterward.

### Q: What was the Community Services Administration and how did the succession work?

The Community Services Administration was the renamed successor to the Office of Economic Opportunity under the Head Start, Economic Opportunity, and Community Partnership Act of 1974, Public Law 93-644, signed January 4, 1975. The succession preserved a federal antipoverty agency while reducing the office in rank and reach. The successor agency sat outside the Executive Office of the President, administered community action and related community services programs, and operated without the Job Corps and Head Start, which had moved to the permanent departments in 1969. The settlement of 1974 accepted the argument that abolition by executive action had been unlawful while conceding the political demand for a smaller institution. The Community Services Administration continued until Congress abolished the agency effective September 30, 1981, ending the line of succession that had begun in the Executive Office in 1964.

### Q: What changed when Congress converted community action funding into a block grant?

The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981, Public Law 97-35, abolished the Community Services Administration and created the Community Services Block Grant, codified at 42 U.S.C. 9901 et seq. and administered through the Department of Health and Human Services. The conversion changed the federal relationship at the center of the original design. Funds flowed as allotments to states rather than as direct federal grants to local agencies, and states were required to pass at least 90 percent of the allotment to eligible entities, primarily community action agencies designated before 1981. Federal officials lost the power to recognize a new local agency over the objection of state and local government. The tripartite board requirement survived the conversion, and the Community Services Block Grant Act of 1998 added performance measurement to the conditions attached to the money.

### Q: Where did Job Corps come from and how was the program designed?

Job Corps came from Part A of Title I of the act, which Congress designed for young men and young women aged sixteen through twenty one whose schooling and employment prospects had failed in their home communities. Section 101 stated the purpose as preparation for the responsibilities of citizenship and an increase in employability. The design was residential. Enrollees lived at rural and urban centers, received education and vocational training, and performed useful work that included conservation of natural resources, a feature that recalled the Civilian Conservation Corps of the 1930s. Residential removal was the point of the design: the program separated enrollees from the labor markets and household conditions that had produced the failure, and substituted a controlled setting with instruction, work discipline, health care, and placement at completion. The Department of Labor administered Job Corps after the transfer of 1969.

### Q: Where did Volunteers in Service to America come from?

Volunteers in Service to America, known as VISTA, came from Title VIII of the act, the title on domestic volunteer service programs. The title empowered the director of the Office of Economic Opportunity to recruit, select, train, and assign volunteers to projects sponsored by state and local agencies and by private nonprofit organizations, in work directed toward the alleviation of poverty. Volunteers served where the need was located, in urban neighborhoods and rural communities, and received a living allowance rather than a salary, a structure modeled on the Peace Corps service that Sargent Shriver had directed overseas. The program survived the abolition of the founding agency. Under the Domestic Volunteer Service Act, the volunteer corps operated by 2019 as AmeriCorps VISTA, retaining the assignment of individual volunteers to local antipoverty projects as the defining method of the program.

### Q: What does the official poverty rate show about the decade after enactment?

The official Census Bureau measure recorded a substantial fall in poverty during the decade after enactment. The rate stood at 19.5 percent in 1963 and 19.0 percent in 1964, fell to 12.1 percent in 1969, and reached 11.1 percent in 1973. The figures establish the direction and scale of the change and the limits of the achievement at the same time, since roughly one American in nine remained below the official line in 1973. Economists and historians disputed the allocation of credit among three causes: a strong labor market that raised earnings, expanded federal transfer programs that raised incomes directly, and the service and training programs of the Economic Opportunity Act itself. The dispute persisted because the rate moved with all three forces at once, and no research design separated the contribution of community action from the contribution of growth with precision.

### Q: What was the Moynihan critique of community action?

Daniel Patrick Moynihan published the critique in Maximum Feasible Misunderstanding in 1969. Moynihan argued that community action as practiced had departed from the limited purposes of the officials who drafted the program, and that a clause adopted with modest expectations had been converted into an instrument of political mobilization that the federal government itself did not understand. On that account, officials promised a transformation in the political position of the poor that the statute never authorized, and the backlash from mayors and Congress followed predictably. Reviewers disputed the allocation of responsibility. A symposium in Urban Affairs Quarterly in 1970 observed that Moynihan simplified the intentions of the drafting task force, and pointed to Peter Marris and Martin Rein, whose Dilemmas of Social Reform appeared in 1967, as evidence that planners drawn from the juvenile delinquency committee had intended participation as a strategy for changing institutions.

### Q: How was the statute codified, and what replaced the original codification?

The act was codified at 42 U.S.C. 2701 et seq., in the chapter of the United States Code devoted to the Economic Opportunity Program, and program provisions appeared across that chapter, including the community action definition and the provisions governing the office itself. The codification lost operating force in stages as Congress transferred programs, renamed the agency, and finally abolished the successor agency in 1981. Each surviving program acquired a codification of its own. The Community Services Block Grant stood at 42 U.S.C. 9901 et seq., Head Start under the Head Start Act, legal services under the Legal Services Corporation Act of 1974, and Job Corps under the workforce statutes that culminated in the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act of 2014. By 2019 a reader citing a surviving program cited a successor statute, and the 1964 codification served as the historical root of the authorities in force.
