Feminist criticism of Romeo and Juliet begins from a refusal: the refusal to let the most famous love story in English literature be read as a story about two people and their feelings alone. Behind the lovers stands a city. In that city a father disposes of a daughter in marriage as an instrument of policy, a mother delivers the sales pitch for a husband the daughter has never spoken to, a nurse carries messages until the messages become dangerous, and a feud conducted entirely by men prices everything, including grief. Feminist criticism is the body of scholarship that made that city visible. Before its arrival, the play's criticism ran on two tracks that the feminist readers of the 1970s inherited and rejected: the romance track, which celebrated the lovers as the standard of pure feeling, and the moral track, inherited from Arthur Brooke's poem of 1562, which blamed them for disobedience. Both tracks treat Verona as scenery. The feminist reading treats Verona as the subject.

![Juliet and the women of Verona read through feminist criticism - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The verdict this survey reaches, and defends section by section, can be stated in advance. Feminist criticism has established three things about the play beyond serious dispute. First, the tragedy has a social engine: the patriarchal household, which owns its daughters' marriages and trains its sons for violence, and not the stars, drives the plot from the first brawl to the tomb. Second, the love that appears to escape that engine was manufactured inside it: the lovers' desire arrives in borrowed poetic forms, speaks in the metaphors of property and exchange, and aims at marriage, the very institution the fathers administer. Third, the play exposes the order that kills its heroine rather than endorsing it, because the text assigns its causal weight, its pricing vocabulary, and its final obscene transaction to the fathers' system, while giving Juliet the most searching intelligence in Verona with which to judge that system. The first finding is Coppelia Kahn's, the second is Dympna Callaghan's correction of Kahn, and the third is the verdict the two findings produce when they are held together, with Catherine Belsey's account of desire and language explaining how the manufacturing feels from inside. The celebratory reading that began the field, Juliet Dusinberre's claim that Shakespeare was an ally of women ahead of his age, survives only in fragments. What survives whole is the materialist core: a criticism that reads the household before the heart.

One piece of arithmetic frames everything the survey reports. The play contains four women who speak: Juliet, Lady Capulet, the Nurse, and Lady Montague, against a male cast that fills the square, the feast, the friar's cell, and the tomb. Between them the four women hold a small fraction of the play's lines, and the fraction is distributed with an exactness that looks designed: the daughter speaks torrents, in soliloquy and in darkness; the mother speaks fluently and only on the market's business; the nurse speaks volumes and is discarded; the other mother barely speaks at all and dies unheard. Feminist criticism did not invent that distribution. It was the first criticism to treat the distribution as evidence, to ask what a play means by who it lets talk, and the question reorganised the play's study more thoroughly than any single reading that followed from it.

That verdict has to be earned against the evidence, and the evidence is the play itself, scene by scene. The survey therefore proceeds in the order the field proceeded: the questions feminist criticism asks, the Victorian foremothers who asked early versions of them without the name, the celebratory wave of the 1970s and the historical correction that followed, then the three landmark positions in the depth a survey allows, the map that gathers them, the silences the map cannot show, the hardest question of endorsement, and the verdict.

## What Does Feminist Criticism Ask of Romeo and Juliet?

Feminist criticism of the play asks five questions, and the five questions distinguish it from every earlier way of reading the tragedy. The first concerns agency: what does Juliet do, as distinct from what is done to her? The question sounds simple and overturns centuries of reception, because the Juliet of the romance tradition is a girl things happen to, while the Juliet of the text proposes the marriage (2.2), sets its terms, dismisses her confessor when the counsel turns pragmatic, plans her own apparent death, and performs it alone. The second question concerns the market: what is a daughter worth in Verona, to whom, and in what currency? The third concerns masculine violence: why does the feud take the form it takes, and what is the relation between the swords in the square and the arrangements in the household? The fourth concerns silence: which women in the play are permitted speech, in whose presence, and at what price? The fifth question, the hardest, concerns the play's own position: does the tragedy endorse the order it depicts, exposing the lovers as the order's deserving victims, or does it expose the order, exhibiting its costs so exactly that the depiction becomes the indictment?

The agency question repays a ledger, and the ledger is worth setting out because the romance tradition's passive Juliet survives largely by never having to face it. Juliet proposes the marriage and fixes its terms and timetable (2.2). She conducts the negotiation with the Nurse as her envoy and audits the envoy's report like a principal. She extracts the wedding night from a household in mourning by managing appearances her parents read as obedience. When the Nurse's counsel turns to compliance, she breaks with her oldest intimate in a single scene and does not reopen the account (3.5). She takes her case to the Friar, threatens suicide in his cell rather than accept the Paris marriage (4.1), accepts the potion, dismisses every attendant, talks herself through the vault's terrors aloud, and drinks (4.3). She wakes in the tomb, reads the situation faster than the Friar who flees it, and chooses her death with the play's most economical line of decision. Set end to end, the record is not of a girl to whom things happen but of the plot's most continuous practical intelligence, and the feminist point is not the celebration of that intelligence. It is the observation that the intelligence is given no lawful instrument: every item on the ledger is a workaround, an act of management performed inside a structure that owns her, so that agency in the play is real, strenuous, and priced in advance at death. That paradox, the heroine's competence and the competence's futility, is the datum every school in the survey must explain.

Each question has a method folded inside it. The agency question requires counting decisions rather than admiring speeches, which is why feminist readings keep returning to the distribution of initiative in the orchard scene, where Juliet interrupts, corrects, and contracts while Romeo swears and rhapsodizes. The market question requires reading marriage negotiations as negotiations, attending to the vocabulary of tender, jointure, and consent that the play's fathers use when they talk about their daughters. The violence question requires treating the feud's bawdy and its swordplay as one curriculum rather than two registers, the joke and the duel as stages in a single training. The silence question requires noticing who is absent: Rosaline, who never speaks or appears; Lady Montague, who dies offstage of a grief the play reports in two lines; the mothers of the two houses, who never meet. The endorsement question requires a judgment about where the play's sympathies are engineered to fall, which is a judgment about structure and emphasis rather than about any single speech.

Underneath the five questions sits one procedural rule that the whole field shares, whatever its internal quarrels: begin from the household, not from the feelings. The feelings are the play's most famous product, and the feminist suspicion, stated in its mildest form by Kahn and in its strongest by Callaghan, is that famous feelings are exactly where a culture hides its arrangements. A reading that starts with the lovers' hearts will find what the romance tradition found, a universal story of young love and bad luck. A reading that starts with Capulet's house, its servants joking about maidenheads in the first scene (1.1), its master weighing a suitor's suit in the second (1.2), its mistress marketing that suitor to a thirteen-year-old in the third (1.3), will find a system with the lovers inside it. The difference between the two readings is not a difference of taste. It is a difference about what kind of object a play is: a mirror of eternal emotion, or a machine built in a particular city at a particular moment, whose workings can be inspected.

The five questions also impose a discipline of quotation that the survey observes throughout. Feminist claims about this play stand or fall on small verbal facts: that consent enters the text as "but a part" of a father's will (1.2), that the peace is settled in the vocabulary of a marriage settlement (5.3), that the rebuke which polices the hero's manhood reaches for the word "womanish" (3.3). A criticism built on structures can drift into abstraction, and the field's best work is distinguished by its refusal to drift, its habit of returning the largest claim to the smallest line and testing the fit. The habit matters for the endorsement question most of all, since a verdict about a play's politics that cannot survive contact with its lines is worth nothing. Where a school's claim exceeds its evidence, the survey says so and records the discount; where the evidence carries the claim farther than the school itself pressed it, the survey says that too.

The fifth question has a career of its own that previews the survey's whole itinerary, since each school is recognisable by its answer to it. Dusinberre's celebration implied that the play stands with its heroines against the doctrine of its age, exposure by sympathy. Jardine refused the implication as a category error, arguing that a transvestite stage's sympathies are evidence about the stagers, not about the order staged. Kahn's answer is exposure with a scapegoat's ambiguity: the play exhibits the order destroying its children and then exhibits the order purchasing peace with the corpses, so that the indictment and the absorption are staged in the same final scene. Callaghan radicalised the absorption into the answer itself: the play functions as endorsement whatever it depicts, because its depiction installs the doctrine it appears to mourn. Belsey dissolved the question's terms, replacing intention with structure: an interrogative text neither endorses nor exposes in the manner of a pamphlet, it leaves the contradictions where a reader must trip over them. The survey's verdict, defended in its own section below, is that the construction supports exposure and the function supports Callaghan's warning, and that a reading is only mature when it can hold the two in that order.

The survey's own procedure follows the field's rule. Each school is given its strongest form, its best evidence, and its standing after the objections it has absorbed. Where the series has already given a critic a full article, the survey compresses to the position and the verdict, and leaves the depth to the dedicated study.

## The Victorian Juliet: Women's Criticism Before the Name

Feminist criticism of the play did not begin in 1975. It began, in embryo, with the women who wrote about Shakespeare's heroines in the nineteenth century and treated Juliet, for the first time in the play's reception, as a mind to be defended rather than a caution or an ornament. Anna Jameson's Characteristics of Women, published in 1832 and later read as Shakespeare's Heroines, placed Juliet among the characters of feeling and defended her with a seriousness the male criticism of her century rarely matched. Jameson's Juliet is, in her famous compression, love itself: not a girl who falls in love but the passion embodied, frank in the orchard because the feeling is honest, and ruined by a world organised against the honesty. The defense mattered because the charges it answered were real. The wedding-night speech of 3.2, in which Juliet summons the night and her husband in language of open desire, had drawn the period's charge of indelicacy; Jameson answered that a wife's longing for her husband was the speech's whole subject and that the delicacy being protected belonged to the critics, not to the character.

Mary Cowden Clarke extended the enterprise in The Girlhood of Shakespeare's Heroines, issued in parts in 1850 to 1852, which invented childhoods for the heroines the plays present as finished persons. Her Juliet tale is instructive beyond its sentiment. Clarke staffs the Capulet nursery with the Nurse as its emotional sovereign and keeps the parents at the distance the play itself keeps them, the father a voice of command from outside the women's rooms, the mother a visitor in state. The invention, made by a critic working from the text's own allocations of attention, discovers in the play a structure the twentieth century would name: the heroine is raised inside a female economy of care that operates within, and entirely at the mercy of, a male economy of command. The women of the house form the girl; the men of the house will dispose of her; and the tragedy, when it comes, is in part the collision of the two economies, the care that taught her to want a self and the command that budgeted no self for her to have. Clarke could not have written that sentence. Her tale is its evidence arranged as fiction, waiting ninety years for the criticism that could read it. Clarke's Juliet tale imagines the Capulet nursery, the Nurse's ascendancy, the household's atmosphere of indulgence and command, and the imagining is a critical act despite its fictional dress: it asserts that a heroine has a formation, that the girl of 1.3 was produced by a house, a nurse, and a city before she was ever produced by a lover. That assertion, that character is made by conditions, is the seed from which the whole later field grows, though Clarke herself drew from it only pathos, never analysis of the conditions as a system.

One further fact about the Victorian station matters to the survey, because it anticipates the field's institutional history. Jameson's book was a commercial and critical success written by a woman working outside every institution that certified criticism: no university, no journal, no profession, in a decade when a woman publishing literary argument at all did so against the grain of the trade. The readership that bought the Characteristics, and the girl readers to whom Clarke's tales were given as presents a generation later, constitute a female public for Shakespeare criticism a full century before the universities admitted that public's descendants to the faculty. The academic field of the 1970s was therefore not a beginning ex nihilo but a professionalisation: the questions women readers had carried privately for generations acquired journals, anthologies, and tenure. That genealogy explains both the field's confidence and its polemical edge. Its practitioners understood themselves as recovering a tradition of attention that the certified criticism had ignored, and the recovery gave the school's founding documents their characteristic tone, part scholarship and part repossession.

The limits of the Victorian station are as instructive as its achievements. Jameson and Clarke defend Juliet by enlarging her: more feeling, more girlhood, more interiority. Neither asks what a daughter was worth in the marriage arrangements of the play's world, because neither possesses the concept of a structure that produces both the feeling and the ruin. Their Juliet is a heroine vindicated, not a system exposed, and the vindication leaves the system offstage where the romance tradition kept it. The Victorian station also established, without theorising it, the fact that the later field would make central: the household is a subject. Clarke's tale lingers in the Capulet nursery because she understood that the girl who receives Paris's suit in 1.3 with a formula of managed compliance had been manufactured for that compliance by thirteen years of a particular house, its indulgent nurse, its absent father, its mother married as a child. The tale sentimentalises the manufacture; it cannot name it as manufacture. But the instinct that sent a critic backward into the heroine's childhood, hunting for the conditions that produced the woman the play presents, is the instinct of every materialist who followed, equipped at last with the vocabulary Clarke lacked. Jameson's instinct was the complementary one: she trusted the speeches over the conditions, treating Juliet's frankness as the eruption of a nature that no arrangement could have scripted. The field's whole later quarrel, between the desire as nature and the desire as manufacture, is present in embryo in the difference between the two foremothers, which is why the survey stations them together.

When the twentieth-century field looked back, it honoured the foremothers for the attention and discarded the method. Attention was not nothing: the Victorian women established that Juliet could bear the weight of a book, that her frankness needed defense rather than apology, and that the household which formed her was a legitimate object of critical curiosity. The academic field of the 1970s kept all three convictions and added the missing instrument, an account of power.

## Dusinberre's Wager: The Celebratory First Wave and Its Correction

The academic field opens with a wager. Juliet Dusinberre's Shakespeare and the Nature of Women, published in 1975, argued that the drama of the period between 1590 and 1625 registered a genuine rise in the estimation of women, driven in part by the Protestant teaching that made the wife a companion and the marriage a fellowship, and that Shakespeare's heroines embody the rise. On this account the playwright stands ahead of his culture's official doctrine: where the period's sermons hedged women about with silence and subjection, the plays give their women wit, initiative, and the best arguments. Juliet is a prize exhibit for the wager. She out-talks every man in her play, conducts her courtship as a negotiation between equals, corrects Romeo's oath-swearing with a theologian's precision (2.2), and faces the potion with a rigour of imagination no adult in Verona attempts (4.3). Dusinberre's Shakespeare is, in the word the decade reached for, a feminist before the term was available, and the claim was exhilarating to a generation of women entering the profession and finding the canon's central author apparently on their side.

The exhilaration was the problem. Lisa Jardine's Still Harping on Daughters, published in 1983, subjected the celebratory reading to the period's records and found the records uncooperative. Daughters in the England the plays were written for were educated for disposal; the traffic in women between households was the ordinary machinery of alliance and property; and the stage that presented such spirited heroines presented them through boy actors, in a theatre from which women were excluded as performers, before audiences whose women sat in a social order the heroines' speeches did nothing to disturb. Jardine's correction did not deny the speeches. It denied the inference from speeches to emancipation. A heroine's eloquence on a transvestite stage is evidence about what male playwrights and male audiences found pleasurable or useful to imagine, and only indirectly, if at all, evidence about the standing of women. The distinction between a representation and a condition is the distinction the celebratory wave had skipped, and once Jardine stated it, the field could not unstate it.

Jardine's evidence base is worth a paragraph of its own, because it set the evidentiary standard the field still uses. Her Verona and her England are built from conduct literature, educational treatises, and the records of actual marriages among the propertied: daughters taught accomplishment as a market finish, marriages negotiated by kin as instruments of estate policy, wives' legal persons absorbed into their husbands' by the doctrine of coverture. Against that archive, a heroine's brilliant speech in a playhouse is a datum of a different order, and the confusion of the orders is the celebratory wave's characteristic error. The error is worth naming generously, since the archive has its own limit, which Jardine's successors noted: records of arrangements describe what a society prescribed, and a play is under no obligation to be a prescription's photograph. The mature field works between the two limits, reading the text against the archive and the archive against the text, and the Kahn and Callaghan landmarks are best understood as the two directions of that double reading performed at genius level.

Between the wager and the correction stands the anthology that gave the field its institution: The Woman's Part: Feminist Criticism of Shakespeare, edited by Carolyn Ruth Swift Lenz, Gayle Greene, and Carol Thomas Neely, which gathered the new work between covers and announced, in its title's quiet pun, that women were claiming their portion of the author and of the profession. The volume carried Kahn's Verona essay to the audience that made it a landmark, and its existence taught the field a habit the survey depends on: Shakespearean feminism was from its first decade a collective, quarrelsome enterprise, in which celebration and suspicion were printed side by side and set to argue. Marianne Novy's Love's Argument, published in 1984, kept the celebratory impulse alive in a disciplined form: Novy reads the lovers' language as an education in mutuality, two speakers revising the inherited scripts of dominance into a shared idiom, and finds in the mutuality a genuine resistance to the patriarchal patterns around it. Novy's version survives Jardine's correction better than Dusinberre's because it claims less: not that Shakespeare freed women, but that the play imagines, inside the lovers' dialogue, a relation its world nowhere else permits. The imagination is real and its confinement is the point. Dusinberre's mechanism deserves its strongest statement before the correction is allowed to stand, because the mechanism was archival rather than sentimental. The Protestant reform of marriage in the century before the play had attacked compulsory celibacy, exalted the married estate, and described the wife in the language of companionship and counsel; the household manuals and marriage sermons that carried the doctrine gave wives a recognised spiritual standing that the older dispensation had not. Dusinberre's claim was that a dramatist writing for that culture absorbed its revaluation, and that the heroines' authority on stage is the revaluation's deposit. The claim's weakness is the step from doctrine to drama, which her book asserts more often than it demonstrates, and its deeper weakness is the one Jardine named: doctrine about wives is itself an instrument of the order that arranges wives, and a companionate ideology can govern a daughter as efficiently as a tyrannical one, a point Callaghan's generation would build an entire reading upon. Irene Dash's Wooing, Wedding, and Power, published in 1981, worked the same decade's seam from the records side, tracking what the plays' marriage plots assume about the actual distribution of power between spouses, and her findings, less exhilarated than Dusinberre's and less austere than Jardine's, mark the wave's centre of gravity as it settled: the plays know more about women's constrained negotiations than the romance tradition admitted, and less about women's freedom than the celebration wished.

The field's mature shape emerges from the exchange: celebration survives as a claim about what the lovers' language models, suspicion survives as the account of everything that contains it.

Novy's position rewards a closer statement, since it is the survey's one standing bridge between the celebration and the suspicion, and its evidence is the verse itself at its most granular. Where Romeo begins in borrowed Petrarchan postures, addressing an absent mistress in oxymorons she cannot hear, the lovers together build a different practice: the shared sonnet distributes its lines between them as an exact equality of utterance; the orchard dialogue proceeds by correction accepted, each speaker revising the other's figure and being revised in turn; the aubade turns disagreement about a bird into collaboration about a fact. Novy calls the pattern mutuality and argues that Shakespeare returns to it across the canon as his imagination of what a man and a woman might be to each other outside the scripts of mastery. The claim's discipline is its boundary. Novy does not argue that Verona permits the relation, or that the relation survives it; the mutuality exists in dialogue, for three days, under sentence of the world that hosts the dialogue, and its destruction is the tragedy restated in the celebration's own terms. A survey must record that the strongest form of the field's first impulse is also a form of its second: the mutuality is real, and it is confined, and the confinement is the indictment the suspicion was built to file.

## Kahn's Verona: The Feud as a Machine for Making Men

Coppelia Kahn's essay "Coming of Age in Verona," published in Modern Language Studies in the winter of 1977 to 1978 and expanded in Man's Estate in 1981, is the founding landmark of the play's modern gender criticism, and the survey's first full position. Kahn's opening move refuses the romance in a sentence: the play is about a pair of adolescents trying to grow up. Growing up, in the Verona she reconstructs, is a task set by a patriarchal order, a social organisation centred on the father: his name, his honour, his house, his power to dispose of children in marriage and to conscript sons into his quarrels. The feud, on this reading, is neither backdrop nor bad luck. It is the order's enforcement arm and its training school, the mechanism by which boys are certified as men through violence offered to the fathers' honour, and girls are transferred between houses as the instruments of alliance and inheritance.

The evidence Kahn marshals begins where the play begins, with the servants' opening exchange about thrusting maids to the wall (1.1), in which sexual conquest and street violence appear as a single idiom, and continues through the Friar's rebuke to the weeping Romeo, "Thy tears are womanish" (3.3), in which the play's kindest adult polices the boundary of manhood at the moment of its collapse. Romeo's tragedy, for Kahn, is a failed coming of age: the son who attempts to exit the fathers' system through marriage, the one sanctioned door, discovers that the door opens back into the system, since marriage itself is the fathers' instrument. Juliet's tragedy is the obverse. Verona's daughters are given no adolescence at all: a girl passes from her father's house to her husband's without interval, an object of transfer at exactly the age when her brother in the other house is being blooded in the square. The lovers die, in Kahn's term, as the tragic scapegoats of the feud, their deaths purchasing the reconciliation that the order then conducts, obscenely, as a further transaction between the fathers, complete with a handshake named as a daughter's jointure and a bidding war in golden statues (5.3).

The Prince, in Kahn's account, completes the system's portrait from above. His authority is real and theatrical: he can banish, threaten, and fine, and he cannot govern, because the order he polices is not a conspiracy he might arrest but a curriculum taught in every household he rules, including the two whose servants open the play in a street he owns. His three interventions mark the state's rhythm, proclamation, sentence, summary, each delivered after the event it was meant to prevent, and his final accounting, which fines the heavens and the houses in one breath, is the official version of the fate the feminist reading refuses: when the system's referee blames the sky, the system has found its perfect spokesman. The scapegoat mechanism and the failed referee are the same fact seen from below and above. The children absorb the violence the fathers' code generates, the state absorbs the blame into weather, and the houses survive, diminished and decorated, to teach the curriculum again.

### What changes when the feud is read as a system for manufacturing men?

Everything downstream changes. The brawls stop being local colour and become a curriculum: the servants' maidenhead jokes of 1.1 are its first lesson, Tybalt its prize graduate, and the duel of 3.1 an examination Romeo refuses and then sits under compulsion. The Prince's failed edicts become evidence that no magistrate can regulate a system reproducing itself inside every household.

The Man's Estate expansion states the mechanism as a war between two definitions of manhood: manhood as violence performed on behalf of the fathers, and manhood as separation from the fathers through union with a woman. Romeo is torn between the definitions because the play allows no third. His refusal to fight Tybalt in 3.1 is the second definition speaking, the new kinship of marriage; his capitulation minutes later, after Mercutio falls, is the first definition collecting its debt, and his own verdict on the capitulation is delivered in the system's vocabulary, the cry that his bride's beauty has made him effeminate. The daughter's side of the mechanism is stated in a single phrase of her father's that Kahn made famous: "the hopeful lady of my earth" (1.2), in which the sole heir is addressed as a landed estate's future, hope and property in one word. Where the son's ordeal is public, examined in the square with the law taking notes, the daughter's is private and solo: the potion soliloquy of 4.3, in which a girl of thirteen inventories the terrors of the ancestral vault and sits, alone, an examination in mortality that no son in the play is ever required to sit. Kahn's framing of that scene, as the adolescence Verona denied its daughters conducted in one night and one room, is the essay's most durable single perception.

The reading drew fire in its own decade that the survey must register, since the fire refined it. Charles Conaway disputes the claim that the lovers' marriage separates the son from the father's house, arguing that marriage in the period ordinarily extended paternal networks rather than escaping them; the objection is absorbed, on the adjudication the series' Kahn study reaches, by the distinction between marriage as an institution and this marriage, contracted in secret, without consent, settlement, or knowledge, which the household experiences and punishes as theft. The psychoanalytic vocabulary drew a second line of objection, against rites and scapegoats presented as human constants, and the objection stands as the toll discussed below. Neither objection touches the mechanism's textual core: the play does stage its catastrophe as the feud's operations, scene by scene, and no rival account has relocated the cause.

The survey compresses Kahn where the series has already expanded her: the full hearing of the essay, its psychoanalytic machinery, its scapegoat vocabulary, its contested spots and its final adjudication, stands in the series' study of [Coppelia Kahn: Coming of Age in Verona](/2026/12/18/coppelia-kahn-coming-of-age-verona/), which finds the reading right about the play's mechanism and standing in need of one correction. The correction is the theoretical caution that Callaghan and, from another direction, Jonathan Goldberg pressed: Kahn writes, at her argument's most exposed moments, as though the lovers' desire were a natural force that Verona merely represses, an authentic feeling crushed by an artificial order. The play's own machinery refuses that simplicity, since the desire arrives in the most artificial forms the culture stocked, sonnets and Petrarchan oxymorons, and aims at the order's central sacrament. The mature position the ensuing debate produced keeps Kahn's mechanism and pays the correction's toll on it: the feud is real, the scapegoats are real, and the love that opposes them is also an artifact of the city that kills it. Kahn survives the toll. No subsequent account of masculinity, patriarchy, or adolescence in the play has been able to begin anywhere except in her Verona, including the accounts written to correct her, and a founding reading is precisely the one its correctors cannot stop citing. Peter Erickson's Patriarchal Structures in Shakespeare's Drama, published in 1985, built a general theory of Shakespearean patriarchy on the ground she cleared, and the masculinity studies that followed work in the same clearing.

## The Marriage Market in Romeo and Juliet: A Daughter Priced

The market question has its own dedicated study in the series, [Patriarchy and the Marriage Market in Verona](/2024/02/23/patriarchy-marriage-market-verona/), and the survey's task is to place the market where the feminist schools place it: at the centre of the play's arrangements, visible in three scenes that the romance tradition read as preliminaries. In the first, Capulet receives Paris's suit with a courtesy that contains the whole system in one line: "My will to her consent is but a part" (1.2). The sentence sounds like deference and functions as a title deed. Consent is acknowledged as a component of the transaction, a part, whose other parts, the father's will and the suitor's standing, belong to men; and the same speech prices the asset, "the hopeful lady of my earth," an only child and sole heir whose marriage will settle the house's future. In the second scene, the marketing is delegated to the women: Lady Capulet presents Paris to her daughter as a book to be read by its cover, "This precious book of love, this unbound lover, / To beautify him only lacks a cover" (1.3), a metaphor in which the girl figures as the binding a valuable volume requires. The mother performs the sale because the system prefers its transactions upholstered, and her performance is itself evidence, since she was married at her daughter's age and speaks for the arrangement that made her.

### What does Lady Capulet's book metaphor admit about her own marriage?

It admits that she was bound in the transaction she is arranging. Married before fourteen to a man decades older, she was acquired as a cover for a husband's volume, and her fluency is the mark of the market's subject: a woman selling the system to the next daughter, because it is the only language she was given for desire.

The market's legal shadow deserves notice, because the play's plot turns on it. A marriage contracted by present consent before witnesses, as the Friar's ceremony contracts the lovers', was binding in the church's law whatever the fathers said, which is why the Thursday wedding to Paris would be bigamy rather than embarrassment, and why Juliet's whole strategy consists in enforcing a sacrament against a settlement. Feminist criticism draws the moral the romance tradition missed: the daughter's one lawful weapon is a rival institution's paperwork, and her access to it runs through a male office-holder's cell. The church that licenses her escape is as much a patriarchal jurisdiction as the household she escapes, merely a competing one, and the competition between jurisdictions, father against friar, market against sacrament, is the narrow corridor down which her agency is permitted to run. No school in the survey disputes the corridor's narrowness. They dispute only whether walking it counts as freedom, and the dispute is the Kahn and Callaghan quarrel in legal dress.

The third scene is the market with the upholstery torn off. After Tybalt's death has repriced the household's position, Capulet fixes the Paris wedding within days and meets his daughter's refusal with the vocabulary of ownership at its rawest: "Speak not, reply not, do not answer me" (3.5), followed by the threat to drag her to church and the alternative of begging and starving in the streets. Feminist criticism's contribution here is not the observation that Capulet is cruel, which any century could make, but the observation that the cruelty is structural and continuous with the courtesy: the consent that was a part in 1.2 is a part that can be dispensed with when the market tightens, and the daughter learns in one scene what her formal standing always was. The Nurse's counsel in the same scene, to marry the County and let the banished husband die, completes the survey of the daughter's world: the one intimate ally she possesses advises compliance, because compliance is the only survival strategy the system teaches its women to teach. Juliet's response, the play's quietest revolution, is to begin lying to everyone, and to carry her case to the one institution that operates a rival jurisdiction, the Friar's cell, whose sacrament can remove her from the market by marrying her out of it in secret. Two further features of the market complete the survey's account. The first is the acceleration. In 3.4, with Tybalt a day dead and the household's credit damaged, Capulet offers Paris the match on the spot in language that drops the courtesy of 1.2 entirely, calling the offer a desperate tender of his child's love: the word "tender" is contract language, an offer formally submitted, and the desperation is the market's, a house repairing its position with its only asset. The wedding is fixed for Thursday, then pulled forward to Wednesday, and Act 4's preparations, the cooks, the music, the bridegroom's early arrival, convert the house into a machine for delivering the daughter on schedule. The second feature is the appraisal's direction. In 1.3 it is the women who appraise the groom as merchandise, reading Paris as a volume in want of a cover, and the reversal is not a refutation of the market reading but its completion: the system's idiom is spoken most fluently by its subjects, and a daughter raised inside it learns to price as she has been priced. The market's triumph is that even its merchandise talks like a merchant.

The feminist schools divide over what that manoeuvre means, Kahn reading a daughter seizing the fathers' own instrument, Callaghan reading a desire so thoroughly formatted that its rebellion takes the form of a wedding. The division is the field's central quarrel, and the next two sections stage its principals.

## Belsey: Desire as the Residue of Language

Catherine Belsey's reading, built from Critical Practice in 1980 through her essay "The Name of the Rose in Romeo and Juliet," published in The Yearbook of English Studies in 1993, to the book-length Romeo and Juliet: Language and Writing in 2014, supplies the survey's third position and its most unsettling one. Belsey begins by refusing the compliment the play is usually paid, that its love is nature itself, immediate and self-evident, crushed from outside by an artificial order. What audiences take for spontaneous passion arrives in the play already written: in a shared sonnet at the feast (1.5), in Petrarchan oxymoron, in an orchard dialogue that is explicitly a seminar on naming. "What's in a name?" (2.2) is, for Belsey, the play theorizing itself. Juliet's argument that the name is no part of the man founders on the play's own demonstration that the name is the part that kills: Montague is not Romeo's hand or foot, as she reasons, but it is the name that makes him un-marriageable in the open, un-greetable in the square, and finally dead in the tomb. Language does not decorate the lovers' desire. Language constitutes it, and desire, in Belsey's formulation, is the unspeakable residue that the constituting leaves behind, the longing for an unmediated presence that every instrument the lovers possess, being mediation, defeats in the act of expressing.

Belsey's machinery, which the dedicated study expounds, can be compressed to one doctrine the survey needs: interpellation, the process by which an identity is conferred by being hailed. A name called across a square, a father's "my daughter," a prince's proclamation of banishment, a church's form of words over two kneeling speakers: Verona is a city of hailings, and to be hailed is to be assigned the part the call describes. Romeo is hailed as Montague by every voice in the play, by Tybalt's challenge, by the Friar's counsel, and in the end by his own mouth, and the orchard scene becomes, in this vocabulary, a hailing declined in words and obeyed in everything else, since the name refused at midnight is the name that governs the duel at noon. The doctrine gives the feminist reading its account of why Juliet's great argument fails on its own terms. She is right that the name is no part of the man, and the rightness is useless, because the name was never a part of the man: it is the whole of his social existence, the address at which the feud, the law, and the market can reach him.

The play announces the doctrine before the lovers meet. The Act 2 Chorus describes the transfer of Romeo's desire in the vocabulary of inheritance law: "Now old desire doth in his deathbed lie, / And young affection gapes to be his heir" (2 Chorus). Desire is an estate; its objects are successive tenants; the feeling that will present itself, within the hour, as a unique and world-defying singularity is introduced by the play's own narrator as a routine succession, one tenancy ending and another gaping to begin. Belsey does not need to labour the point, since the Chorus labours it for her: the structure of wanting persists while the names change, which is the definition of a discourse doing its work. Rosaline's function clarifies in the same light. The first beloved is desire's draft, an object composed entirely of other people's sentences, Mercutio's blazon, Benvolio's diagnosis, Romeo's oxymorons, never interfered with by a person, and the purity of the case is the proof: a woman made of discourse alone behaves in the economy exactly as a woman should, which suggests how much of the economy's heroines is discourse too.

The feminist weight of the reading falls on the subject it dissolves. The autonomous self that liberal criticism celebrates in Juliet, the intrepid girl who knows her mind, is for Belsey an effect assembled out of names, conventions, and other people's sentences, at a moment early enough in the history of that self for the assembly to remain visible. The play is what she calls an interrogative text: it exposes the contradictions of the ideologies it inhabits rather than smoothing them into a seamless story. Hence the ending, in which two bodies are converted into golden statues and a tale told by the surviving authorities, representation swallowing the represented under official management. The confiscation of the story is, for Belsey, the play's final linguistic act, and it begins before the statues are promised: the Friar's long narration in 5.3 is the first official edition of the lovers' lives, a summary delivered to the Prince by the one man who could have prevented the ending, in which the couple's own account of themselves is replaced by a case history. Juliet, who spoke her desire in the first person for four acts, is spoken for, in the third person, forever after. The feminist schools rarely agree on a sentence of the play's criticism, but they converge on the significance of that transfer: whoever tells the story owns the lovers, and the play ends by showing the ownership changing hands, from the girl in the garden to the magistrate, the friar, and the fathers with their gold. The survey's adjudication of Belsey follows the series' dedicated study in giving her the naming, the borrowed rhetoric, and the ending, and in registering the two corrections that stand. Edward Snow hears, in the orchard dialogue, two speakers building a shared idiom by mutual adjustment rather than reciting scripts, and the hearing is textually earned; Susan Snyder restores the legal fact that marriage in the period really did rename a wife, so the name speech carries a practical charge Belsey's semiotics underplays. The aubade of 3.5 is the reading's test case at the level of the single scene, and worth a paragraph because the corrections meet there. The lovers' parting is conducted as a quarrel over names: whether the bird is the lark, herald of the day that separates them, or the nightingale, emblem of the night that joins them. Belsey reads the exchange as desire negotiating with the signifier itself, the couple attempting to rename the dawn out of existence and failing, since the names obey the world's order and not the bedroom's. Snow's counter-hearing, registered in the dedicated study, finds in the same lines two speakers improvising a shared instrument, correcting each other's figures until the figures fit the feeling, collaboration rather than script. The survey's adjudication keeps both: the scene shows a shared idiom being built, and shows it being built out of inherited parts under a deadline set by the fathers' city, whose watch is already climbing to the chamber. Manufacture and improvisation are not opposites. They are the two ends of the lovers' practice, and the tragedy is that the practice had three days.

Neither correction reaches the central claim. The play stands where Belsey locates it, early enough to show desire being manufactured by the discourses that claim only to describe it, and a feminist criticism that ignores the manufacture will keep mistaking the product for a heroine's soul.

## Callaghan's Reversal: Romantic Love Gets a Date

Dympna Callaghan's essay "The Ideology of Romantic Love: The Case of Romeo and Juliet," first published in The Weyward Sisters, the 1994 volume she wrote with Lorraine Helms and Jyotsna Singh, executes the field's great reversal, and the survey stages it as the second full position. Kahn had asked what Verona does to the lovers. Callaghan asks what the lovers' love does for Verona, and for the culture that has consumed the play for four centuries as the certificate of romantic love itself. Her answer is that the love is an ideology in the strict sense: a historically specific formation of desire, installed in the decades around the play through Protestant marriage teaching, conduct literature, and the stage itself, which teaches its consumers to experience a social arrangement, companionate marriage founded on the couple's chosen affection, as the most private and least historical fact about a person. The play is not the ideology's victim. The play is one of its founding documents, and its vast popularity is the mechanism of the installation still running.

The archive beneath the claim is the period's own argument about marriage, conducted in sermons, household manuals, and church courts in the decades around the play. The household manuals of the 1590s, Cleaver and Dod's Godlie Forme of Householde Government among them, instruct the married couple in a government at once hierarchical and companionate: the wife subjected, and in the same breath addressed as a partner in the household's spiritual economy. The church courts handled a constant traffic in spousals, annulments, and defamation over sexual honour, the paperwork of a culture negotiating, case by case, what a promise between a man and a woman was worth. Callaghan's point is that the play belongs to that negotiation and takes the rising side in it: the side of the couple's chosen, sacramental, exclusive bond against the lineage transaction. A doctrine does not need to be new to be installed. It needs advocates, occasions, and a story that makes it feel like the truth of the heart, and the play supplied the story at a scale no sermon could match.

The evidence is the desire's own shape. Juliet's wanting does not aim at escape from Verona's institutions; it aims at the central one. "If that thy bent of love be honourable, / Thy purpose marriage, send me word tomorrow" (2.2): the orchard scene's most radical speech is a marriage proposal conducted under the forms, and the bounty speech that follows speaks the market's own idiom at the moment of supposed transcendence, "My bounty is as boundless as the sea, / My love as deep" (2.2), an economics of inexhaustible supply offered as the opposite of economics. For Callaghan, the lovers' flight from the fathers' market into the Friar's cell is the ideology's masterstroke rather than its refutation, since what the flight secures is a wedding, sacramental, exclusive, and fertile in intention: the new doctrine of marriage, chosen love ratified by the church, triumphing over the old doctrine of marriage as lineage transaction precisely by feeling like a rebellion against it. Even the deaths serve the installation, mourned by a machinery that converts the couple into monuments and sells the story of their feeling back to the city as its own reconciliation.

Even the Friar's motives submit to the reading, and the play states them without embarrassment: he agrees to the marriage as policy, hoping "this alliance may so happy prove, / To turn your households' rancour to pure love" (2.3). The lovers believe they are escaping into nature; the officiant believes he is conducting diplomacy; the culture, on Callaghan's account, is conducting an installation, using the couple's sense of election to ratify the doctrine that election is what marriage is for. The scene is the ideology essay in miniature, three incompatible descriptions of one ceremony, and the ceremony's own description, the feeling of free and fated choice, is the one that four centuries of readers carried away. A criticism that cannot explain why the least reliable description won is not finished, and Callaghan's is the first feminist account of the play that even poses the question in a form that admits an answer: the description won because winning was its function.

### Why does Juliet's desire run toward marriage rather than away from it?

Because the desire was formed by the culture that marriage governed. The sermons, manuals, and conventions taught a daughter to want a husband chosen by affection and ratified by sacrament, so her rebellious act imaginable was a wedding of her choosing. The direction of the wanting is the fingerprint of its manufacture: escape and installation arrive as the same event.

The full adjudication of the essay, including the historians' quarrel over the timetable it inherits from Lawrence Stone's The Family, Sex and Marriage in England, stands in the series' study of [Dympna Callaghan and Feminist Materialism](/2027/01/29/dympna-callaghan-feminist-reading/), and the survey adopts its verdict: the reading stands, discounted at two points. The discount on chronology replaces invention with installation: Alan Macfarlane's Marriage and Love in England and Ralph Houlbrooke's gradualism show affectionate, chosen marriage running far deeper in the record than a sudden birth around 1600, so the essay's strong claim becomes the weaker and sufficient one, that the decades around the play elevated chosen love into public doctrine, which is an event whatever the practice's age. The discount on functionalism concedes to Paul A. Kottman and to Kahn's side of the quarrel that an account of manufactured consent underprices the play's grief: formations can turn on their formers, and the tragedy's causal weight falls on the fathers' machinery, not on the lovers' feeling. What is not discounted is the reversal itself. After Callaghan, no feminist reading can treat the lovers' desire as an innocent outside to the system, and no survey can place the field's two landmarks side by side without seeing that they complete each other: Kahn explains how the order kills, Callaghan explains why the weapon it kills with was loaded by the victims' own most cherished feeling, and the completion is the field's central achievement. Callaghan's later work extends the reversal to the institution that staged it: Shakespeare Without Women, published in 2000, argues that the original Juliet was a boy apprentice in a theatre containing no women at all, so that the founding heroine of romantic love is a representation of a woman manufactured entirely by men, and her Bedford edition of the play in 2003 built the argument into an apparatus, surrounding the text with the period's documents on marriage, desire, and the traffic in daughters, and letting the archive make the essay's point in the classroom.

The boy-actor argument deserves its own weight in the survey, because it is the point at which Callaghan's feminism turns on the field's own heroine. Shakespeare Without Women argues that the Renaissance stage contained no women as performers, that its female figures were presented by boy apprentices within a theatrical institution owned, written, and staffed by men, and that criticism which celebrates Juliet as a woman's voice, or even as a representation of women in any direct sense, has mistaken an artifact of that institution for a person of the other sex. The argument's target is Dusinberre's generation as much as the Victorians': the heroine whose eloquence was offered as evidence for women's rising estimation was a male construction performed by a male body, and the estimation it evidences is of what that institution found profitable in imagining femininity. Callaghan does not conclude that the figure is therefore worthless to feminist reading. She concludes that the reading must change its object, from the woman represented to the representing machine, its economics, its erotics, and its uses for the culture that consumed it. The survey records the turn as the field's coming of age in the strict sense: the moment feminist criticism of this play stopped asking for a heroine it could keep.

Read as a single document, the map shows the field's centre of gravity moving in one direction across five decades, from the woman to the system that priced her speech. It also shows what did not move. Every row, from Jameson's vindication to Levenson's edition, is an argument about the same dozen scenes, and the map's deepest pattern is the constancy of the evidence under the rotation of the instruments: the pitch in 1.3, the garden in 2.2, the tirade in 3.5, the vault in 4.3, the handshake in 5.3. A reader who masters those scenes in the map's order possesses the feminist criticism of the play in working form, and a teacher who assigns the rows in sequence assigns an education in how criticism itself changes: the same lines, priced as feeling by one generation, as mechanism by the next, as language by the next, and as an editor's decision by the last.

The map's omissions are principled rather than accidental, and one belongs on the record. Phyllis Rackin's Shakespeare and Women, published in 2005, is the field's own stocktaking rather than a reading of this play, and the survey stations it here, at the map's edge, because its judgment governs how the map should be used. Rackin surveys three decades of feminist Shakespeare criticism and finds its gains real and its habits in need of the discipline it once imposed on others: a suspicion, hardened into reflex, of repeating the archive's exclusions in mirror image, writing women out of the period's actual, documented presence in favour of a cleaner story about their erasure. The caution lands on this play with particular force, since Verona's women are few, silenced, and priced, and the temptation to make the silencing total is the temptation to stop reading. The map's rows survive Rackin's audit because each was built from the text's allocations rather than from a theory of them, but the audit stands at the survey's shoulder throughout: the order priced its daughters, and the daughters, in the play and in the period, were also there, speaking, in the spaces the pricing could not seal.

## Silence and Speech: The Women Verona Will Not Hear

A map of readings cannot show the survey's fourth question, since silence leaves no landmark. Yet the feminist account of the play's women is incomplete without an inventory of the unsaid, and the inventory is damning in a pattern no single school owns. Rosaline, the play's first beloved, never appears and never speaks; she exists as a vow reported by men, an oath of chastity that functions in the plot as a locked door and in the feminist reading as the control case, the woman who refuses the market altogether and is rewarded with total erasure from the stage. Lady Montague speaks a single line in the first scene, restraining her husband's sword arm with more sense than any man in the square, and then vanishes from the play until her death is reported in the final scene: "Grief of my son's exile hath stopped her breath" (5.3). Her husband speaks the sentence, inside his son's story, and moves on. A woman of the ruling generation dies of the feud's consequences in silence, offstage, unlamented by name, and the play's economy of attention, which lavishes a long set speech on the apothecary's poverty, gives her two. Lady Capulet, the play's most fluent woman after Juliet, spends her fluency as the market's agent, and her one moment of unscripted feeling, the wish that her daughter were married to her grave (3.5), is a curse. Lady Capulet's own arithmetic belongs in the inventory, since she states it herself while delivering the pitch: she was a mother at the age her daughter has now reached, "much upon these years" (1.3). The line is usually read as encouragement. The feminist reading hears it as the system's cycle stating its period: a girl married before fourteen, a mother at fourteen, a wife to a husband decades older, and at twenty-eight or so already the market's recruiting officer, preparing the next girl for the same rotation with a book metaphor and a nurse's bawdy chorus. Nothing in her portrait suggests cruelty of temperament. Everything suggests completion: she is what the machine makes of its daughters given twenty years, and her presence in 1.3 is the play's quietest prophecy of the future Juliet is being sold, the future the tomb interrupts. The two mothers of the feuding houses, finally, never meet and never speak, though their children marry, kill, and die in a quarrel conducted in their houses' names. A drama interested in its women as persons might have staged the interview. This drama's women are kept in separate compartments of the machine, and the compartmentalisation is itself the portrait.

The pattern the schools converge on is that Verona permits its women speech in proportion to their service to its arrangements: the Nurse, the great exception in volume, talks for two acts as the lovers' go-between and is struck from Juliet's confidence the moment her counsel serves the arrangement instead.

### Why is Rosaline's silence a piece of evidence rather than an absence?

Because the play sets its heroine's eloquence against the beloved's erasure and means the contrast to register. Rosaline is the woman who refused the economy: she vowed chastity, kept herself off the market, and her reward is to be spoken of and forgotten without a line of her own. Her silence prices the only exit a daughter could choose unaided.

The inventory has a subtler item that the schools claim jointly: Juliet's one extended speech in her father's hearing after the crisis begins is a performance of submission so accomplished that it disarms him. "I have learnt me to repent the sin / Of disobedient opposition" (4.2) is spoken by a girl already married, already resolved, already lying, and its fluency is the system's own training returned as camouflage. Capulet, delighted, accelerates the wedding, and the acceleration kills her: the better she performs the daughter the order demands, the faster the order moves to dispose of her. Feminist readings treat the scene as the silencing argument's completion, since it shows the price of the alternative to silence. A daughter in Verona may speak the truth in darkness, in soliloquy, or in a cell, or she may speak falsely in the light and be praised for it; the play offers no third register, and the distribution is the indictment. Even the Nurse's name belongs to the inventory: the woman who raised Juliet is called Angelica once in the whole play, by Capulet, in a barked instruction about baked meats (4.4), as though the text itself could only name her in the idiom of household service.

The wedding-night speech of 3.2 concentrates the whole inventory into one exhibit, and the survey pauses on it for that reason. "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds" opens the most celebrated utterance of female desire in the period's drama, a wife summoning the night and her husband in imperatives, impatient, exact, unashamed. It is also a soliloquy. The speech is addressed to Phoebus's horses, to the night, to an absent bridegroom, to anyone except a person in Verona who might answer it, and it is delivered by a girl alone in a chamber in her father's house on the one night the house's arrangements have been outmanoeuvred. The Victorian defense treated the speech's frankness as its scandal. The feminist reading treats its solitude as the scandal: the play permits its heroine desire at full volume on condition that the desire be overheard by no one it concerns, and the condition is the order's own rule, stated by the staging more plainly than any character states it. When Juliet does address her desire to a present hearer, in the garden, the hearer is a boy in the dark below a window, in a space the household does not patrol. Verona's acoustics are its politics. Even Lady Montague's handful of lines obeys the distribution: her one intervention in the play is to hold her husband back from the brawl, restraining male violence with her body and a sentence, the single act of peace-making any adult in the first scene attempts. The system gives its most sensible speaker ten words and a husband's arm to hold, then reports her death as a side effect of her son's story. The feminist inventory counts such allocations the way an auditor counts a ledger, and the ledger balances to the same figure at every line: speech follows service, silence follows refusal or exhaustion, and the one voice that escapes the accounting is a dead girl's, preserved only in the tale the men agree to tell about her.

Juliet's own speech is the inventory's final item, and the schools divide over its meaning with a consistency the survey can use. For the celebratory line, the speeches are the emancipation itself, the mind outrunning the cage. For Kahn, the eloquence is real and confined: Juliet speaks supremely in soliloquy, in the orchard's darkness, and in a cell, which is to say where the system cannot hear her, and her one public utterance under pressure is a feigned submission so accomplished that her father praises the performance. For Belsey, the speeches are the assembly made audible. For Callaghan, the finest of them, the bounty speech, is the market dreaming of its own abolition in the market's vocabulary. The survey's adjudication is that the distribution settles the argument: a play that wished to endorse its order would not have concentrated its intelligence where the order cannot hear it, nor made the concentration itself so conspicuous. The silencing of the women is not the play's oversight. It is among the play's most carefully engineered effects, and feminist criticism's recovery of it, from the servants' jokes that open the action to the mother who dies unreported, is the finding least likely to be reversed by any future school.

## Does the Play Endorse or Expose Its Patriarchy?

The brief for this survey carries the complication plainly: hold the endorse-or-expose debate open. The survey holds it open by giving each side its strongest counsel before the verdict, because the question is genuine and the evidence runs in both directions farther than partisans admit. Whether the verdict licenses calling the play itself a feminist work is a separate question, with a separate hearing in the series under the title [Is Romeo and Juliet a Feminist Play?](/2031/04/18/is-romeo-and-juliet-feminist/); the question here is narrower and prior: in which direction does the play's own construction lean?

The debate has a prehistory that sharpens it. The play's principal source, Brooke's poem of 1562, arrives with its verdict printed in the address to the reader: the lovers are held up as a warning, their disobedience and their recourse to friars and potions catalogued as sins the audience is to shun. The endorsement case is therefore not a modern suspicion but the reading the story carried into the theatre on its title page, and the feminist question can be put with precision: did the play keep its source's frame, or break it? The performance history supplies a second datum. Garrick's adaptation of 1748, which governed the stage for a century, cut Rosaline, softened the feud, and rewrote the tomb so that Juliet wakes before Romeo dies and the lovers expire in each other's arms exchanging farewells. The alteration is the endorsement reading enacted by sentiment: the catastrophe becomes a misfortune to be wept over rather than a mechanism to be judged, the order dissolves into pathos, and the audience leaves reconciled to everything. That the stage's most successful version of the play is the one that strips its causality is itself a finding about what cultures prefer to feel, and Callaghan's side of the debate claims it.

The case for endorsement, stated at full strength, runs as follows. The play punishes disobedience with death, exactly as Brooke's moralizing preface promised the story would, and it never once imagines its order reformed: no character proposes that daughters choose, that the feud be outlawed by anything stronger than exhaustion, or that the Prince govern his city. The survivors are the order's own officers, and the settlement they reach is a transaction between fathers, priced in gold statues, in which the daughter's corpse functions as the consideration that finally makes the contract binding. Callaghan's darker point belongs to this side of the ledger: the audience's tears are not a protest against the installation but a stage of it, since the play teaches its consumers to feel the system's own preferred emotion, reverence for chosen love, at the system's own preferred site, a wedding that death has made safe for admiration. A text can expose nothing while meaning only to celebrate, and its exposure can be reabsorbed as prestige: the statues in the square are the order's way of framing the evidence against itself as decoration.

The Prologue belongs in this section as the exposure case's first exhibit, because the feminist reading has taught the profession to hear it as an official document. The Chorus speaks the city's authorised version: two households, an ancient grudge, a pair of star-crossed lovers, a fate. Every term in the summary is the order's own bookkeeping, in which causes are assigned to the stars and the grudge is ancient, agentless, and nobody's responsibility. The play that follows is the audit of that summary, scene by scene, and the audit finds men and mechanisms where the summary put heaven. A Chorus is not an author; the frame is not the verdict; and the distance between the Prologue's accounting and the plot's accounting is the space in which the feminist reading lives. Readings that treat the frame as the play's own voice are, on this account, making the error the play exists to correct, mistaking Verona's press release for its history.

The case for exposure answers at the level of construction. The play assigns its causes with a consistency that moral bookkeeping cannot explain. Every catastrophe in the plot is machined by the order's own operations: the brawl its masculinity trains, the duel its code compels, the forced marriage its market requires, the clandestine counter-marriage its consent fiction makes necessary, the tomb its monument provides. Against that machinery the play sets a standard of judgment in the lovers' own idiom, the shared sonnet and the orchard dialogue, where speech is mutual, exact, and free of price, and it lets the audience measure the order against the standard for three acts before the machinery closes. The tomb scene then audits the order in its own coin. Paris, the market's approved buyer, dies at the vault's threshold in a duel over the merchandise, killed by the rival purchaser the market never approved; minutes earlier, in Mantua, the play staged its starkest transaction, Romeo buying poison from a starving apothecary with gold he calls a worse poison to men's souls than the draught he purchases (5.1), while the law that forbids the sale is priced and found affordable. Every approach to Juliet's body in the final act is a male negotiation conducted in money, steel, or office. The girl herself, waking, contributes the act's only unpriced act, and its instrument is her husband's dagger, borrowed like everything else she was ever allowed to touch. The final transaction then reads not as the order's vindication but as its confession performed by itself: two fathers, over two corpses, conducting the feud's competition by other means, bidding in statues, while the Prince, the order's highest officer, distributes the blame upward to the heavens and homeward to the houses in the same breath, "All are punished" (5.3).

### What does Capulet mean by calling a handshake his daughter's jointure?

He means it in the market's vocabulary, and convicts himself. A jointure is the settlement a bride's family secures for her widowhood, a marriage negotiation's property instrument. Capulet reaches for the word to name what his daughter has purchased for him, peace with Montague: a father so fluent in pricing daughters that he prices her corpse in settlement language, unnoticing.

The metaphor is the play's smallest complete feminist exhibit. The editor's evidence belongs to this section, because editions decide which of the two plays, the endorsing one or the exposing one, a generation is able to read. Jill Levenson's Oxford edition of 2000, treated at survey length here as the map's final row records, printed the 1597 first quarto entire as a second text beside the familiar second quarto, and the editorial act has a feminist consequence independent of its editor's intentions: the first quarto's Juliet is swifter, less monumental, a figure of theatrical action rather than of lyric enshrinement, and her presence in the same volume dissolves the single iconic heroine into a range of performed possibilities. An editor who multiplies the text multiplies the woman, and a criticism concerned with how representations harden into doctrine can ask for no better ally than an edition that refuses to let the representation settle. The survey's adjudication of the whole section follows. The exposure case carries the construction, and the endorsement case survives as the standing warning that exposure is not emancipation: the play exhibits its order's workings with a completeness no apology could survive, and the exhibition changed nothing in Verona, whose square got statues instead of reform. Feminist criticism's mature position is to hold both truths in that order, indictment first, warning second, because the order of holding them is the difference between reading the play and being read by it.

## The Verdict: What Feminist Criticism Has Established

The survey can now collect its findings in the order of their certainty. Established beyond serious dispute: the play's tragic engine is social and gendered. The feud is a masculine institution that trains sons for violence and prices daughters for transfer, and the catastrophe follows from its operations with the regularity of a proof; fate, in the feminist account, is the name the order's beneficiaries give to the order's consequences, which is why the criticism dissolves the stars into the street wherever the text permits, and the text permits it nearly everywhere. Established with one discount: the love is manufactured. Its forms are borrowed, its idiom is the market's, its aim is the altar, and its prestige in later culture is an installation the play helped to perform; the discount is that manufacture does not exhaust meaning, since formations turn on their formers, and the play's grief at the turning is a real remainder no functional accounting invoices. Established as method: representations are not conditions, characters are not souls, and editions are arguments; the field that began by defending a heroine's frankness ends by inspecting the machinery that produced the heroine, the defence, and the inspectors. Retired: the claim that Shakespeare was a feminist in any sense his century could have recognised, and with it every argument that proceeds from the author's supposed sympathies rather than from the text's demonstrable constructions.

Two corollaries of the findings deserve the verdict's emphasis, because they govern how the play is now read outside the field as well as inside it. The first concerns the hero. A criticism that began with daughters ended by rereading sons: Romeo in the feminist account is not the romance's ardent boy but a conscript of the manhood machine, fluent in its bawdy before he is fluent in anything else, shamed for his tears by the play's kindest adult, and permitted exactly one act of self-definition, a marriage, which the machine repossesses. Masculinity studies as a field is the corollary institutionalised, and its existence is the proof that the feminist reading of this play was never only about its women: it was about the order that assigns both sexes their parts and bills both for the performance. The second corollary concerns reading itself. The survey's schools disagree about nearly everything except procedure: distrust the famous feeling, inspect the arrangement, price the words, watch who speaks and who is spoken for. That procedure, exported from this play to the whole canon, is the field's largest bequest, and it is why a survey of one play's feminist criticism reads, in the end, like a history of modern Shakespeare studies in miniature.

What remains genuinely open, the survey leaves open with the questions sharpened rather than settled. The intention question, what the playwright meant by the exposure his construction performs, is unanswerable from the evidence and harmless once quarantined from the questions evidence can settle. The residue question, whether the lovers' feeling contains an element no formation explains, divides Belsey's heirs from Kottman's, and divides them productively, since each side's evidence is the other side's blind spot. The stage question, what the boy actor's Juliet does to every claim in the survey, stands as the field's permanent reminder that its founding heroine was a representation before she was anything else. A criticism is healthiest when its open questions are this well mapped, and the mapping is itself among the findings.

For the reader who would test the verdict rather than inherit it, the survey prescribes an order of reading, and the order is the field's logic made practical. Begin with Kahn, because mechanism comes before correction and her Verona is the ground every later position builds on or bills. Read Jardine beside her, as the standing instruction to check every celebration against the archive. Then Callaghan, whose reversal only lands at full force on a reader who has first felt the pull of the account it reverses; then Belsey, whose machinery explains the feeling the first three argue about; then Novy, last and deliberately, because her mutuality reads most honestly after its confinement has been established, as a measurement of what the machine destroyed rather than as a rumour that it failed to. The Victorian foremothers belong at the front of the syllabus as its conscience: Jameson and Clarke demonstrate that attention to Juliet long predates the instruments, and that instruments without attention, the field's besetting temptation in its theoretical decades, produce readings of great sophistication that cannot hear a girl thinking in the dark. The map in this survey is arranged to enforce the order. Its rows are the syllabus; its standings column is the examination.

The cliché says the play is a simple romance. Feminist criticism's restoration of the structures around the women, the fathers who price, the mothers who pitch, the nurse who complies, the sons who bleed for a name, the heroine who thinks faster than her world and is buried in its monument, is the single largest change in the play's understanding since the romance tradition formed. The change holds because it was built the way this survey has rebuilt it: question by question, scene by scene, school by school, with the disagreements adjudicated rather than smoothed, and the verdict left standing where the evidence put it. Verona's order killed its daughter and then raised her statue. The criticism that learned to see the transaction whole, and to name the handshake a jointure, is the criticism the play now cannot be read without.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch feminist map, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch feminist map is the table in this survey that sets the main feminist readings of the play side by side: maker and date, central claim, strongest evidence, and standing after adjudication. The pattern it exposes is a migration of attention. The earliest readings defend Juliet herself, her frankness, her girlhood, her passion. The landmark readings defend nothing and instead inspect the arrangements around her: the feud that trains the men, the market that prices her, the language that manufactures her desire. Read down the columns, the heroine shrinks as the system grows, until the map's final row restores her in the only form the mature field trusts, as a text that editions construct and readers can learn to reconstruct.

### Q: When did feminist criticism of Romeo and Juliet become a field, and what marks its beginning?

Feminist criticism of the play became a field in the second half of the 1970s, and three publications mark its beginning. Juliet Dusinberre's Shakespeare and the Nature of Women appeared in 1975 and made the celebratory claim for Shakespeare's heroines. Coppelia Kahn's essay "Coming of Age in Verona" appeared in Modern Language Studies in the winter of 1977 to 1978 and supplied the field's founding mechanism, the feud as patriarchy's machine. The Woman's Part, the anthology edited by Carolyn Ruth Swift Lenz, Gayle Greene, and Carol Thomas Neely, then gave the enterprise an institution, printing the new readings side by side. Women had written seriously on Juliet since Anna Jameson in 1832, but a field begins when its practitioners argue with each other in print, and that argument starts with these books.

### Q: What does the word patriarchy mean as feminist critics of this play use it?

Patriarchy, in the usage feminist critics of the play inherit from Kahn, names a social order organised around the father: his name, his honour, his house, and his power to dispose of children. The term is precise rather than atmospheric. It identifies who may command a marriage, whose insult requires a son's sword, whose consent is the operative part of a daughter's consent, and whose handshake settles the peace over the children's bodies. Verona qualifies because every one of those powers is vested in fathers and exercised through households, and because the order reproduces itself by training the next generation to want what it requires: sons who reach for swords, daughters who receive suitors, mothers who deliver the sales pitch for arrangements they themselves were sold under.

### Q: What divides the celebratory feminist readings of the 1970s from the materialist readings that followed?

The divide is a single question: whether a representation can be taken as evidence of a condition. The celebratory readings, led by Dusinberre's 1975 book, argued from the heroines' eloquence and initiative to a rising estimation of women in the period, with Shakespeare ahead of his culture. The materialist readings that followed, Jardine's historical correction and then Callaghan's ideology argument, denied the inference. A spirited heroine performed by a boy actor in a theatre closed to women proves what male playwrights and audiences enjoyed imagining; it does not prove what women were permitted to do. The materialists redirected attention from the character's soul to the structures producing her: household, market, church, stage. The celebratory impulse survived only in disciplined forms, such as Novy's claim that the lovers' dialogue imagines a mutuality the world around it nowhere permits.

### Q: Why do feminist critics distrust the phrase "star-crossed lovers"?

Feminist critics distrust the phrase because it functions as an alibi. "Star-crossed" assigns the catastrophe to the sky, to a fate written before the action starts, and a fate has no address: no father to indict, no market to inspect, no code of manhood to reform. The feminist relocation of the tragedy from the stars to the street depends on refusing that assignment. Scene by scene, the deaths follow from identifiable operations, a brawl the masculine code requires, a duel its honour compels, a forced marriage its market demands, a secrecy its consent fiction makes necessary. To call the lovers star-crossed is to accept the Prologue's framing as the play's verdict, when the feminist reading treats the framing as the first exhibit: the story a city tells itself to avoid naming its own machinery.

### Q: Do feminist critics read Romeo as a victim of the order that privileges him?

Yes: the strongest feminist readings treat Romeo as the order's second casualty class, privileged and damaged by the same system. Kahn's account sets the pattern. Verona's patriarchy gives its sons the sword, the square, and the succession, and in exchange it conscripts them: manhood must be proved by violence offered to the fathers' honour, grief must be swallowed as womanish, and the one exit, marriage, is the fathers' own instrument, so the son who takes it remains inside the machine he fled. Romeo's tragedy on this reading is a failed emancipation from an order that armed him, shamed his tears through the Friar's rebuke, and accepted his corpse as a bargaining counter in the fathers' settlement. Privilege and victimhood are not opposites in the account. They are the order's two hands.

### Q: What separates feminist criticism of the play from gender criticism of it?

The separation is one of aim. Feminist criticism is an argument with a defendant: it identifies patriarchy as a structure of unequal power, exhibits its operations in the play, and reaches verdicts about what the play does with them, whether the tragedy endorses the order or exposes it. Gender criticism is the wider and more neutral study of how masculinity and femininity are constructed and performed in the text, which may proceed without any verdict on power at all. Kahn's essay is feminist because it indicts; a study of the play's fashions of manhood that declined to indict would be gender criticism only. The two fields share evidence and often share practitioners, and the later gender work, including masculinity studies, grew from ground the feminist argument cleared. The survey keeps the narrower term because the narrower term keeps the verdict.

### Q: Why did feminist criticism of the play gather force in universities in the 1970s and 1980s?

The timing follows the profession's own transformation. Women entered literary faculties and graduate programmes in large numbers during the 1970s, women's studies courses created syllabi that needed texts reread, and the political feminism of the period supplied the questions: about marriage as an institution, about daughters and property, about whose violence counts as order. Shakespeare was the unavoidable test case because Shakespeare was the curriculum's centre; to reread the canon from the position of its daughters was to change what the centre meant. The institutional markers are the books themselves: a university-press monograph in 1975, a landmark essay in a scholarly journal in 1977, a founding anthology from a university press soon after, and by the middle 1980s a shelf. The criticism gathered force because the critics had arrived, and they stayed because the plays rewarded the questions.

### Q: How has feminist criticism altered the standing of the feud in accounts of the play?

Feminist criticism promoted the feud from backdrop to engine. Before Kahn, the feud figured in accounts of the play as an unexplained given, an ancient grudge that set the lovers' difficulty the way weather sets a sailor's. After Kahn, the feud has a function and a curriculum: it manufactures the city's men, certifies their manhood in public violence, binds the households in a competition that marriage alliances are meant to manage, and supplies the causal chain, brawl, duel, forced match, tomb, that a fate-centred reading had credited to the stars. Later schools disputed her machinery's details and her psychology, but none returned the feud to the scenery. Even readings that centre the lovers' language now begin by acknowledging the institution their language had to survive, which is the measure of a standing altered permanently.

### Q: What separates the feminist Juliet from the Victorian Juliet of Jameson and Cowden Clarke?

The separation is the object of study. The Victorian Juliet is a person to be known better: Jameson defends her passion as love itself, Cowden Clarke invents her childhood so that readers may know her before the play begins, and both enlarge her interiority until she can bear the weight of her readers' sympathy. The feminist Juliet is a position to be analysed: the sole-heir daughter of a great house, priced in a market, trained by a nurse, pitched a husband by her mother, and granted supreme eloquence in the spaces the order cannot hear. The Victorians ask what Juliet felt; the feminists ask what made her feeling take the forms it took and what the forms cost. Something is lost in the exchange, the Victorians' unembarrassed love of the character, and the survey records the loss. What is gained is the city around her, without which her story cannot be explained.

### Q: Why does Mercutio matter to feminist readings centred on Juliet?

Mercutio matters because he is the curriculum's prize pupil and its loudest advertisement. The feminist account of the feud reads his wit as the training at play: the bawdy that prices women as bodies, the conjuration that reduces the beloved to anatomy, the virtuoso contempt for dreams and softness that keeps the school's standards polished. His death is the curriculum graduating into its consequence, since the duel he provokes is the manhood examination in earnest, and his curse on both houses is the pupil's dying recognition of the school. A reading centred on Juliet needs him for proportion: the play gives its most brilliant male speech to the feud's ideology in fancy dress, and against that brilliance sets a thirteen-year-old's exact, unadorned reasoning in a dark garden. The contrast is one of the feminist case's quietest and strongest exhibits.

### Q: Why is Lady Montague's offstage death an exhibit in feminist accounts of the play?

Lady Montague's death is an exhibit because of its economy. She appears in the first scene to restrain her husband with more sense than any man in the square, speaks her single line, and exits the play's attention. Her death is then reported by her husband in the final scene as a subordinate clause of his son's story: grief for Romeo's exile stopped her breath. No scene, no lament, no name spoken over her. Feminist accounts cite the handling as the order's bookkeeping made visible: a woman of the ruling generation dies of the feud's consequences, and the play's own allocation of grief prices her at two lines because her function in the system, wife and mother, has ended. The contrast with the sons' golden statues, promised in the same scene, completes the exhibit.

### Q: How have women editors changed the play that readers study?

Women editors have changed the play by changing which text, and which Juliet, reaches the reader. Jill Levenson's Oxford edition of 2000 printed the 1597 first quarto entire as a second text, recovering a swifter Juliet of action beside the monumental heroine of the familiar text, and built its account of the play from three centuries of performance, in which women actors had been remaking the role since the Restoration stage first admitted them. Dympna Callaghan's Bedford edition of 2003 changed the play's surroundings, printing it among the period's documents on marriage, desire, and daughters, so that a student meets Juliet inside the archive that formed her. Editing looks like custody and functions as authorship: the choice of copy-text, the glossing of a line, the documents admitted as context all decide in advance which feminist arguments a reader will be able to make.

### Q: Why do feminist critics resist letting the Prince's closing speech settle the play's meaning?

The resistance is to the speech's office. The Prince closes the play as Verona's chief magistrate, the officer whose edicts failed three times to govern the feud, and his summary converts a structural catastrophe into a story of woe, distributing punishment to all and therefore to no institution in particular. Feminist critics note what the official frame omits: no mention of the market that priced the daughter, the code that armed the sons, or the settlement the fathers conduct in the same scene, handshake and statues, before the summary is even delivered. To let the speech settle the meaning is to accept the state's edition of events. The feminist reading treats the closing lines as the last exhibit rather than the verdict: the order narrating its own failure as fate, in beautiful verse, at the scene of the transaction it is still conducting.

### Q: What has feminist criticism changed about the way the play is taught?

Feminist criticism changed the syllabus of questions. A classroom formed by the romance tradition asked whether the lovers were wise, whether fate or character killed them, and how the poetry achieves its beauty. A classroom formed by the feminist readings asks those questions later, if at all, and begins with the household: what a daughter was worth, why consent appears in Capulet's mouth as a part rather than a whole, what the servants' opening jokes teach the city's sons, and why the heroine's greatest speeches happen where nobody in power can hear them. Editions teach the change as surely as essays do: students now meet the play in volumes whose introductions treat gender as a primary category and whose contexts include the period's marriage documents. The tomb scene survives the new teaching. It reads differently when the class has priced the girl who lies in it.

### Q: How do feminist productions feed back into feminist criticism of the play?

Production is the criticism's laboratory. A staging that casts the feud's violence as glamorous confirms one reading; a staging that lets the audience watch Lady Capulet deliver the marriage pitch as her own trauma confirms another, and reviewers and scholars argue from what the stage made visible. Directors working the feminist questions have repeatedly disclosed text that commentary missed: the mothers' non-meeting becomes an event when a production places both women at the tomb's edge; the Nurse's abandonment of Juliet lands as policy rather than weakness when her fear of the household is staged. Criticism then absorbs the disclosures, cites them, and sends sharper questions back to the next rehearsal room. The feedback loop is why the survey treats performance history, and an edition like Levenson's that is built from it, as part of the feminist record rather than as its illustration.

### Q: What remains unsettled in feminist criticism of the play?

Three questions remain unsettled, and the survey has kept them open deliberately. The first is intention: whether the playwright designed the exposure his construction performs, a question the evidence cannot answer and the mature field has learned to quarantine. The second is residue: whether the lovers' feeling contains an element that no account of its manufacture explains, the point where Kottman's objection to Callaghan still presses and where Belsey's own theory concedes a remainder by naming desire the unspeakable part. The third is the stage itself: the original Juliet was a boy, and every claim about the heroine's voice must decide what that fact does to it. The questions are well mapped rather than resolved, and a survey's honesty consists in reporting the map accurately, fault lines included.

### Q: Why does the question whether Shakespeare was himself a feminist keep failing to settle the argument?

The question fails because it asks for evidence of a kind the record does not contain and the criticism does not need. No document reports the playwright's views on the standing of women; the plays are the only testimony, and the plays testify in both directions at once, giving Juliet the finest reasoning in Verona and the fathers the settlement over her body. Worse, the question smuggles in a modern category: feminism names an organised politics of a later century, and applying it to a commercial playwright of the 1590s measures him against a standard that could not have been stated in his theatre. The field's productive turn was to retire the biographical question and keep the textual ones, which have answers: what the construction exposes, what the desire is made of, what the ending transacts. Dusinberre's wager survives as the record of why the question once exhilarated, and its retirement is among the field's maturest acts.

### Q: Which single scene do the rival feminist schools all claim as evidence?

The orchard scene of 2.2 is the scene every school claims, and the claims map the field. Kahn reads the name refusal, "Deny thy father and refuse thy name," as the daughter attempting the exit the order forbids, a shedding of the patronymic that the play then proves impossible. Belsey reads the same speech as the play's seminar on signification, the heroine lecturing on names while the names close around her. Callaghan reads the scene's conclusion, the marriage proposal and the bounty speech, as desire arriving in the market's idiom and aiming at the market's sacrament. Novy reads the whole exchange as mutuality being built line by line. The schools agree on nothing except the address: the garden at night is where the play's feminism, whatever it is, must be decided, because it is the one place Juliet speaks at length outside her father's hearing.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch feminist map to argue that the play exposes the order that destroys its women?

An essay should use the map as a sequence of witnesses rather than as a menu. Begin with the row whose claim is hardest to dispute, Kahn's mechanism, and establish the causal chain from the text: the idiom of 1.1, the womanish rebuke of 3.3, the jointure handshake of 5.3. Then call Callaghan's row to forestall the obvious reply, that the lovers' feeling stands outside the system, by showing the desire's forms and aims arriving from inside it. Then set the endorsement objection at full strength, the statues as the order's decoration of its own crime, and answer it from the construction itself: a play that endorses its order does not concentrate its intelligence where the order cannot hear, nor price its heroine's corpse in her father's settlement vocabulary. The map supplies the order of witnesses; the scenes supply the testimony.
