Corporate law defeats more candidates by volume than by difficulty. The subject arrives as one enormous company statute, a second statute on insolvency that behaves like a code of civil procedure wearing commercial dress, a tribunal system borrowed from neither, and a shorter partnership statute that examiners treat as an afterthought until the year it carries a full question. Candidates respond to the volume in the only way that feels safe, which is to memorise, and the subject punishes memorisation more reliably than any other in this series, because the governing text changes by notification several times a year and a memorised threshold has a shorter life than the preparation cycle that produced it. The candidate who fails this subject rarely fails for ignorance of the law. The failure is architectural: one undifferentiated mass of provisions, held at one level of confidence, revised in the order the textbook happened to print them.

![Corporate law examination study, showing the four regimes of the Companies Act, the insolvency code, the tribunals and the LLP Act held as one map - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-30.webp)

The repair begins with a refusal. Corporate law for examination purposes is not one statute plus appendages; it is four connected regimes, each with its own text, its own forum habits and its own rhythm of change, and the candidate's first task is to hold the four separately before connecting them deliberately. The company statute governs formation, capital and governance. The insolvency code governs distress and exit. The tribunal structure adjudicates both. The limited liability partnership statute supplies the alternative vehicle that company law is perpetually compared against. A candidate who can say, of any provision put to them, which regime it belongs to, which forum would hear a dispute under it, and which layer of the text, Act or rules, carries its operative numbers, has already secured the structure on which every specific answer in this subject is built. What follows is a method for the whole subject: the map, the spine, the small set of numbers that function as names, the distinction between the Act and its rules that generates more lost marks than any other, the four historical moments that organise revision, the case law discipline, and a revision method designed for a text that moves.

## Corporate law is four regimes, not one statute

The syllabus of every examining body that tests this subject, whether a university law faculty, a professional institute for company secretaries and accountants, or a judicial services board, is written as if corporate law were a single subject, and the standard textbook reinforces the fiction by binding the material between two covers. The fiction is harmless for the teacher and expensive for the candidate, because the four regimes behave differently under examination conditions. The Companies Act 2013 is a governance statute: its questions are about who may decide what, with whose approval, on what disclosure, and with what consequence for default. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016 is a process statute with substantive teeth: its questions are about who may initiate, what freezes when a process opens, who decides the outcome, and in what order claims are paid. The tribunal regime is a forum subject: its questions are about jurisdiction, composition, appeal routes and the constitutional limits on replacing courts with tribunals. The Limited Liability Partnership Act 2008 is a design subject: its questions are about why a hybrid vehicle exists at all and what follows from building limited liability on a partnership agreement rather than on a share capital structure.

### What are the four regimes a corporate law candidate must hold at once?

Four regimes, held separately and connected deliberately. The Companies Act 2013 governs formation, capital and governance. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016 governs distress and exit. The National Company Law Tribunal and its appellate tribunal adjudicate both. The Limited Liability Partnership Act 2008 supplies the alternative vehicle. Each has its own text, forum habits and amendment rhythm.

Holding the regimes separately means, in practice, refusing to let knowledge earned in one regime answer a question set in another. The candidate who explains a resolution plan by analogy to a scheme of arrangement, or who reaches for the oppression and mismanagement jurisdiction when the facts describe a default and a creditor, has merged regimes that the examiner set side by side precisely to test whether they are kept apart. The connections matter as much as the separations, and they are specific. The insolvency code borrows its forum from the company statute, because the National Company Law Tribunal constituted under the Companies Act is the adjudicating authority for corporate insolvency under the Code. The company statute borrows its exit discipline from the insolvency code, because the winding up provisions of the 2013 Act were cut back once the Code supplied a working insolvency process, and the tribunal chapters of the company statute commenced on the same day the Code's forum came alive. The partnership statute connects by contrast: its absence of a board, of share capital and of the governance machinery that fills the company statute is itself the examinable point. A candidate who learns the connections as a short list of named bridges, rather than as a general sense that the subjects overlap, can move between regimes inside one answer without confusing them.

The classification discipline extends inside the largest regime. The Companies Act 2013 replaced a statute of 1956, and examination questions exploit the replacement constantly, because a candidate's answer reveals within two sentences whether the provisions being described belong to the old architecture or the new one. The honest way to prepare for that exploitation is to learn the replacement as a classification exercise rather than as a chronology: which subjects the 2013 Act carried over with renumbering, which it redrafted, which it changed in substance, and which it created outright. That classification is worked through provision by provision in [the comparison of the Companies Act 1956 and the Companies Act 2013](/2012/03/01/companies-act-1956-vs-2013-comparison/), which is the highest-yield single object in this cluster, and a candidate short of time should read it as a marking scheme for the whole company statute: the categories it uses are the categories in which comparison questions are set and answered.

There is a second reason the four-regime map earns its place at the front of preparation. Each regime changes at a different speed, and the speed determines the study method. The company statute is amended by Parliament at intervals of years and its skeleton is stable across a preparation cycle. The insolvency code was amended repeatedly in its first years and its case law is still young enough that a single Constitution Bench or three-judge decision can rearrange what a correct answer says. The tribunal regime changes through appointment controversies and constitutional litigation as much as through amendment. The partnership statute changed character once, through the decriminalisation amendment of 2021, and is otherwise the stillest water in the subject. A revision plan that treats the four regimes as one block of equal stability will over-invest in the still water and under-invest where the ground moves. The map is therefore not a courtesy introduction. It is the allocation decision from which every later choice in this guide follows.

## Reading the syllabus as a legal document

Preparation in this subject begins one step earlier than most candidates allow, with the syllabus itself, and the syllabus deserves to be read with the same layer discipline the statutes demand. A syllabus is a text issued by an institution, and like the Acts it governs, it has a stable layer and a moving layer. The stable layer is the architecture: the units into which the examining body divides the subject, the place of company law within a larger paper, the appearance of insolvency inside a company law unit or as a unit of its own, the weight the partnership statute is given relative to the company statute. That architecture changes at intervals of years and can be learned once, from the document itself rather than from any summary of it, because summaries preserve the topics and lose the proportions. The moving layer is everything the syllabus says or implies about currency: the amendments it treats as included, the cut-off it announces for changes in the law, the editions and materials it prescribes. That layer must be re-read at every cycle and taken from the body's own announcement, since two institutes examining the same statute in the same season may lawfully examine two different states of its text.

The proportions are the part candidates most often skip, and they are the part that decides the revision budget. A unit list that places the insolvency code as one unit among eight in a company law paper and a paper that sets the Code as a standalone subject are both described in coaching shorthand as covering the IBC, and they demand preparation differing by an order of magnitude. The count that matters is not the count of topics but the count of marks across a run of past papers, sorted by the demand each question makes. Five seasons of papers, classified by regime and by form, will tell a candidate more about the examination being prepared for than any prospectus, because papers reveal the working syllabus, the one the examiners actually hold, as against the published syllabus, which is the one the institution announces. Where the two diverge, and in a fast-moving subject they diverge whenever a syllabus revision lags the statute, the divergence itself is the risk to manage: prepare the published syllabus for content and the working syllabus for emphasis, and where a topic has left the papers before it has left the syllabus, demote it in the revision order rather than deleting it.

A syllabus also tells the candidate, if read closely, which layer of the law each unit inhabits. Units phrased in terms of institutions and doctrines, the duties of directors, the protection of minorities, the distribution of claims in liquidation, live in the Act layer and reward doctrinal preparation. Units phrased in terms of procedure and compliance, filings, meetings, returns, timelines, live substantially in the rules layer and reward the verification habit over the memory. The phrasing is a guide to the marking. A candidate who prepares a compliance unit as doctrine will write elegant answers missing every operative period; a candidate who prepares a doctrine unit as compliance will recite periods and never state a principle. Reading the syllabus as a legal document means nothing more mystical than this: construe its words as an examiner will perform them, note which of its statements are architecture and which are currency, and let that reading, rather than the order of a textbook's chapters, set the plan that the rest of this guide builds.

## The conceptual spine: from incorporation to exit

Underneath the four regimes sits one narrative that every syllabus in this subject assumes and few textbooks state: the life of a company from its creation to its disappearance, with the law attaching a distinct set of questions to each stage. The spine is the fastest organising device a candidate can build, because a provision whose stage is known is halfway to being understood, and a question whose stage is identified announces the regime, the forum and the likely provisions before its second sentence is read. Incorporation raises the questions of promotion, registration, the constitutional documents and the consequences of separate legal personality. Capital raises the questions of share issue, allotment, transfer, buy-back, reduction and the maintenance of the capital fund against which creditors lend. Governance raises the questions of directors and their duties, meetings and resolutions, accounts and audit, and the controls placed on those who manage other people's money. Protection raises the questions of the minority shareholder, the defrauded investor and the public interest: class actions, oppression and mismanagement, investigation and the fraud offence. Distress raises the insolvency questions of initiation, moratorium, collective decision and distribution. Exit raises the questions of winding up, dissolution and striking off, and of what survives a company's death.

The spine disciplines reading because the Companies Act 2013 is not arranged as a narrative, and a candidate who reads it in printed order meets the machinery of meetings before the concept of the share and the penalties before the duties they enforce. Read against the spine instead, and each chapter takes its place in a sequence the candidate already holds. The same spine explains why the insolvency code feels, to company lawyers, like a continuation of the company statute by other means: the Code begins exactly where the governance statute's competence ends, at the point where the company's problem is no longer how it is run but whether it should continue to exist at all. The tribunal sits across the whole spine, hearing incorporation disputes at one end and insolvency appeals at the other, which is why the forum cannot be left to a separate week's revision as an administrative topic. It is load-bearing at every stage.

Two habits convert the spine from a picture into marks. The first is to attach to each stage its two or three governing provisions by function before attaching any numbers: at the capital stage, the provisions on allotment, on buy-back and on reduction; at the protection stage, the provisions on class action, on oppression and mismanagement and on fraud. Function first, number second, because a question framed by facts will present a stage and a problem, and the candidate who retrieves by function finds the provision while the candidate who memorised a numerical index is still scanning it. The second habit is to learn, for each stage, the identity of the decision-maker the statute installs: the board, the shareholders in general meeting, the tribunal, the committee of creditors, the resolution professional, the Registrar. Corporate law questions are disproportionately questions about the wrong actor performing an act, a board doing what only shareholders may do, a creditor committee straying into what the adjudicating authority reserved to itself, and the candidate who holds the actor map for each stage answers those questions before the doctrine is even reached. The detailed architecture of the insolvency side of the spine, its initiation classes, its moratorium and its distribution order, is set out in full in [the complete guide to the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016](/2012/04/01/insolvency-bankruptcy-code-2016-guide/), and the study task is to hold that architecture at the level of sequence and function, returning to the guide for the provision-level detail a particular syllabus demands.

A warning belongs here, because the spine has a failure mode. A narrative this tidy tempts the candidate to answer the question that fits the story rather than the question on the paper, and examiners know the temptation and set against it: the incorporation question that is really about the promoter's pre-incorporation contract, the insolvency question that turns on a guarantee and therefore on the guarantor provisions rather than on the corporate process at all. The spine is a finding system, not an answer. It tells the candidate where in the subject a question lives and which provisions are candidates for use. The question still has to be read, and the discipline of reading it, against the pull of a well-rehearsed story, is a trainable skill that the worked approach later in this guide addresses directly.

## The spine stage by stage: what each stage is examined on

The spine earns a second pass at working depth, because each of its stages carries a characteristic examinable core, and a candidate who knows the core of each stage can predict the shape of a question from its facts. The incorporation stage is examined on personality and its exceptions: the company as a person separate from its members, the promoter's position before that person exists, the pre-incorporation contract that binds no company until adopted, and the circumstances in which the courts lift the veil the statute lowers, fraud and evasion being the durable categories. The capital stage is examined on maintenance: the rule that the fund subscribed by shareholders is held for creditors, expressed through the controls on buy-back, on reduction and on dividends paid otherwise than from profits, and through the allotment machinery that decides who holds the fund. Questions at this stage are usually accounting facts wearing legal dress, and the method is to find the fund and ask whether the transaction depletes it lawfully.

The governance stage is the largest and is examined on loyalty and procedure in roughly equal measure. Loyalty lives in the directors' duty provisions of Section 166, in the related party controls, and in the independent director institution, and its questions present a manager profiting from position and ask by what route the profit is recovered. Procedure lives in meetings, resolutions and filings, and its questions present the right act done by the wrong actor or on the wrong notice, which is why the actor map matters more at this stage than any other. The accounts and audit stage, which candidates treat as a professional annex, is examined in law papers on the regulator's design: auditor rotation, the prohibition on specified non-audit services, and the National Financial Reporting Authority as the state's answer to audit failure. The protection stage is examined on standing and forum: who may complain, members, depositors, the Central Government, and before the tribunal under which provision, the class action and the oppression jurisdiction running as parallel doors with different keys.

The distress stage is examined on sequence and classification, as the section on the Code's name provisions sets out, and on the moratorium's reach, because the freeze on admission is the fact every problem at this stage is built around: what stops, what continues, and who decides. The exit stage is examined on the residue: winding up where the Code does not reach, voluntary liquidation inside it, striking off for the defunct, and the survival of liabilities after dissolution, a small territory that papers visit precisely because candidates abandon it. Read as a whole, the stage map gives the candidate a diagnostic instrument. A question is located by its stage, the stage supplies the actor, the forum and the provision family, and the answer is then built from the provision's elements rather than from a memorised essay. The spine is sometimes criticised as a simplification, and it is one; its defence is that examinations are themselves simplified in exactly this shape, set stage by stage by paper-setters working from the same syllabus architecture the candidate is reading.

## Inside the company statute: how the 2013 Act is arranged

A candidate cannot verify what a candidate cannot find, and finding is a property of the statute's arrangement, which is itself worth an hour of study. The Companies Act 2013 opens with its definitions in Section 2, and the definitions are not a glossary but a control panel: related party in clause 76, key managerial personnel, promoter, subsidiary and associate are each defined there, and the operative provisions elsewhere in the Act borrow the definitions rather than restating them, so a question that turns on whether a person is a related party is answered in Section 2 before Section 188 is reached. The chapters then follow the spine with reasonable fidelity: incorporation, prospectus and allotment, share capital and debentures, registration of charges, management and administration, directors, meetings, accounts, audit, and then the tribunal-facing chapters on inspection, investigation, compromises and arrangements, oppression and mismanagement, and winding up. The candidate who learns the chapter map as a map, with the spine laid over it, can locate an unfamiliar provision by reasoning from its subject, which is the skill that substitutes for the index no one may carry into the hall.

Two features of the arrangement deserve particular attention because papers exploit both. The first is the schedule system. The Act carries operative content in its schedules, the code for independent directors in Schedule IV being the standard example, and the candidate who reads sections only will miss a provision the section incorporates by reference; the rule is to follow every incorporation the first time a provision is learned, and to note schedule content in the revision file at the provision it serves. The second is the dual regulation of the listed company. A listed company answers to the company statute and, on disclosure, governance and market conduct, to the securities regulator's listing regulations, and the two layers overlap on independent directors, related party transactions and audit in ways that generate the standard trap: a requirement attributed to the Act that in truth lives in the listing regulations, or the reverse. The layer discipline this guide teaches for Act and rules applies a second time here, and the safe answer names the layer it is relying on. Around the Act stands its administrative machinery, the Registrar of Companies as the registry of record, the regional and central levels of the ministry administering the Act, and the Serious Fraud Investigation Office for the cases the ordinary machinery cannot carry; a candidate should be able to say which piece of machinery a given default reaches, since procedure questions are frequently machinery questions in disguise.

## The numbers that function as names

Every subject has a small set of provisions that professional discourse treats as names rather than as citations. A company lawyer who says "a 135 company" or "a 29A problem" has not abbreviated a reference; they have invoked a whole legal situation in a single gesture, and the examiner, who is usually that lawyer on another day of the week, writes questions in the same dialect. The candidate must therefore know a short list of provisions by number in both directions: given the number, state the subject and the operative rule; given the situation, produce the number. The list is genuinely short, and its shortness is the point. On the company side it runs to five provisions. Section 135 is corporate social responsibility, the provision that makes specified large companies spend on social purposes and answer for the spending. Section 447 is the fraud offence, the provision whose breadth defines the criminal edge of the statute. Section 245 is the class action, the mechanism by which members and depositors sue as a class before the tribunal. Section 149 is the independent director provision, carrying the institution's definition, its code of conduct and its protected tenure. Section 188 is the related party transaction provision, the control on contracts between a company and those connected to its managers, read with the definition of related party in Section 2(76). A candidate who commands those five, with the oppression and mismanagement jurisdiction in Sections 241 and 242 held beside them as the tribunal's equity power, commands the vocabulary in which company law questions are actually phrased.

The insolvency side has its own name-set, and it is organised by the sequence of a case. Sections 7, 9 and 10 are the three doors into the corporate insolvency resolution process: financial creditors through the first, operational creditors through the second, the corporate applicant itself through the third, and the differences among the doors, particularly the operational creditor's demand notice and the bar on admission where a genuine dispute pre-exists, are among the most reliably examined mechanics in the Code. Section 14 is the moratorium, the freeze that descends on proceedings and enforcement when a process is admitted. Section 29A is the disqualification provision, the list of persons barred from submitting a resolution plan, whose width decides who may rescue a company and who may not. Section 53 is the distribution order, the waterfall that ranks claims in liquidation and against which the fairness of every plan is argued. Around those six, the candidate adds Section 12 for the process timeline and Section 31 for the approval that makes a plan binding, and the initiation-to-distribution sequence can be recited as a single sentence, which is the form in which it is most often needed.

Each name provision also has an examinable core, the part of it that questions are actually built from, and knowing the core matters more than knowing the section's full text. For Section 135 the core is the gateway and the destination of the money: which companies owe the duty, measured by the three financial lines, and where unspent amounts must travel, because both limbs are classification exercises a paper can set in four lines of facts. For Section 447 the core is the definition's breadth: fraud defined to include acts and omissions, concealment and abuse of position, committed with intent to deceive or to gain undue advantage, so that the offence reaches conduct no older provision named, and questions test whether the candidate can apply the definition's limbs rather than recite its punishment. For Section 245 the core is the double gate: the class must satisfy the numerical eligibility the provision prescribes, and the tribunal must be persuaded the action is brought in good faith, so the provision is examined as a filter as much as a remedy. For Section 149 the core is the independence machinery: appointment through the shareholders, a tenure protected against casual removal, a code in the schedule, and liability confined to acts done with knowledge or connivance, because the institution's design question, how to make a monitor independent of those monitored, is the question papers ask. For Section 188 the core is the approval ladder: the board's consent, the shareholders' resolution where the transaction crosses the prescribed scale, and the abstention of the interested party, a ladder whose rungs are the classic problem-fact variations. Learn the cores and the numbers have content; learn the sections entire and the cores are still missing.

Where a predecessor exists, the number must be known in both directions across the 1956 and 2013 statutes, because comparison questions are set in the seam between them. The oppression and mismanagement jurisdiction is the cleanest instance: Sections 397 and 398 of the 1956 Act became Sections 241 and 242 of the 2013 Act, widened and rehoused, and an answer that cites the old number in a new-law question announces a candidate prepared from stale material. Other members of the name-set have no predecessor, and knowing that is equally examinable. The class action was a creation of the 2013 Act, the fraud offence as a single named provision was a creation of the 2013 Act, and corporate social responsibility had no counterpart in the 1956 statute at all; the candidate who invents a lineage for them in an answer has added an error that a bare admission of novelty would have avoided. The rule is symmetrical: know the pairs where pairs exist, and know the creations as creations.

It is equally important to say what is not on the list, because the memorisation instinct, left ungoverned, treats every number as equally name-worthy and bankrupts the revision budget. The internal machinery provisions, the filing sections, the procedural subsections that practitioners look up in the bare Act as a matter of professional routine, carry no name-value in discourse and lose no marks when described by function. The examiner does not test whether a candidate can recite the section governing a register or a return; the examiner tests whether the candidate knows what the register proves and what the return triggers. The line between the two sets is the line between provisions that name a legal situation and provisions that operate a machine, and it is learnable in an afternoon. Spend the afternoon. Then defend the boundary against every coaching list that inflates the name-set to fifty or a hundred entries, because a name-set of a hundred is a contradiction: nothing functions as a name when everything does.

## Schemes and mergers: the tribunal's civil work

Between governance and distress stands a territory candidates underprepare because it belongs to no dramatic story: the compromise and arrangement jurisdiction in Sections 230 to 232 of the Companies Act, through which companies restructure debts, merge, demerge and rearrange share capital with the tribunal's sanction. It is examined steadily, because it combines every skill the subject tests: a statutory procedure with meetings and majorities, a discretionary judicial power disciplined by doctrine, and a commercial substance the candidate must be able to describe without advising on it. The mechanics should be held as a sequence. A scheme is proposed between a company and its creditors or members, or any class of them. The tribunal directs meetings of the classes, at which the scheme must win the statutory majority, approval by a majority representing three-fourths in value of those voting, a double test of heads and value that questions delight in unsettling. The tribunal then sanctions or refuses, on notice to the regulatory authorities, and the sanctioned scheme binds the dissentient minority as it binds the majority, which is the jurisdiction's whole point and its whole danger.

The doctrine that disciplines the discretion was stated by the Supreme Court in Miheer H. Mafatlal v Mafatlal Industries, decided in 1997 under the predecessor provisions and carried into the new Act's reading: the sanctioning forum acts in a supervisory and not an appellate capacity, it does not substitute its own commercial judgment for that of the classes who voted, and its inquiry is confined to whether the statutory procedure was followed, whether the classes were fairly represented and fully informed, whether the arrangement is one an honest and reasonable business person could approve, and whether it offends law or public interest. Those four inquiries, stated as propositions, are the answer to nearly every scheme question ever set, because problem facts are manufactured by breaking one of them: a class composed to manufacture a majority, a circular that conceals the promoters' interest, a valuation withheld from the voters. Beside the full route stands the fast track of Section 233, mergers between small companies and between a holding company and its wholly-owned subsidiary sanctioned through the Central Government's delegate rather than the tribunal, and Section 234, which opens the door to mergers with companies in notified foreign jurisdictions; the trio is examined as a set because it teaches the subject's recurring lesson, that the forum follows the stakes, tribunal for the contested and the large, administrative sanction for the small and the internal. A candidate who holds the sequence, the double majority and the four Mafatlal inquiries holds this territory entire, and it is territory the dramatic subjects, fraud and insolvency, cause most candidates to skip until a paper reminds them it exists.

## Definitions as doctrine: the sections that decide classifications

A disproportionate share of this subject's hardest questions are decided in the definitions, and the candidate who treats the definition sections as a preliminary glossary will lose them to the candidate who treats them as doctrine. The Code's classification of the whole process turns on one definition: financial debt in Section 5(8), a debt disbursed against consideration for the time value of money, with its inclusive list reaching commercial borrowing, debentures, lease and hire-purchase obligations treated as capital, and, by the amendment of 2018, amounts raised from allottees in real estate projects. Swiss Ribbons' equality holding is unintelligible without that definition, because the intelligible differentia the Court sustained is written inside it: the financial creditor's transaction is a lending of money against time, the operational creditor's, defined in Section 5(21) as a claim for goods, services, employment or statutory dues, is not. A problem question in this territory is answered by taking the facts to the definition's limbs one at a time, and the candidate who has the limbs, disbursement, consideration, time value, can classify a novel transaction the paper invents, while the candidate who memorised examples can classify only the examples.

The company statute's definitions repay the same treatment. Promoter in Section 2(69) is defined by three alternative routes, named in the prospectus, identified by the annual return, or exercising control in fact, and the third route makes the definition a doctrine about shadow control that questions on de facto directors and persons in accordance with whose directions the board acts are built from. Related party in Section 2(76) is a list whose limbs, directors and their relatives, key managerial personnel, firms and companies in which they hold stated positions, holding and subsidiary and associate companies, decide the reach of Section 188 before that section's approvals are ever considered. Control itself, defined through the right to appoint a majority of directors or to direct management and policy decisions, feeds the subsidiary and associate definitions and through them the consolidation and the governance obligations of groups. The study rule follows the pattern of this guide: definitions that classify are doctrine and belong in the proposition layer of the file; definitions that merely label belong in the reference layer; and the test for which is which is whether a dispute could turn on the definition's wording. Where it could, learn the wording.

## The insolvency ecosystem: the institutions around the Code

The Code is examined as a process, but it works as an ecosystem, and a candidate who can name the ecosystem's parts answers the institutional questions that papers use to separate prepared candidates from provision-collectors. The design, which descends from the report of the Bankruptcy Law Reforms Committee of 2015, rests on four supports. The adjudicating authority, the tribunal already treated in this guide, decides admission, approves plans and orders liquidation. The regulator, the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India, constituted in October 2016 before the process provisions commenced, licenses and disciplines the profession and writes the regulations that carry the Code's procedural detail, which is why the Code reproduces the two-layer problem inside itself: its regulations move faster than its text. The insolvency professionals are the process's operators, the interim and resolution professionals who take control of the debtor's affairs on admission, verify claims, run the committee of creditors and invite and test plans; the displacement of the board by the professional on admission is the Code's single most consequential institutional fact, the point at which control passes from the debtor's managers to the creditors' agent. The information utilities are the record layer, regulated repositories of financial information whose authenticated records of default the tribunal may act on, designed to convert the admission question from a trial about whether a debt exists into a verification against a record.

Around the four supports stand the creditor institutions through which the process actually decides: the committee of creditors, composed of financial creditors, whose vote by prescribed majority adopts or rejects a plan and whose commercial wisdom the courts protect; and the resolution applicant market, the bidders whose plans the committee judges, from which Section 29A excludes the disqualified. A candidate should be able to run a case through the ecosystem in one paragraph, application, record of default, professional appointed, board suspended, moratorium in force, committee formed, plans invited, vote taken, approval or liquidation, because the ecosystem in sequence is the Code's plot, and problem questions are set at its joints: the professional's conflict, the committee's overreach, the utility's contested record. The ecosystem is also where the Code's limits live, and evaluation answers draw on them honestly: a profession still maturing, a record layer still building coverage, a tribunal carrying a caseload its strength was not sized for. State the parts, state the joints, state the limits, and the institutional question in any of its forms has been answered at the level the examiner set it.

## Learn the Act, verify the rules

If one sentence carries this guide, it is the sentence that names the method: learn the Act, verify the rules. Indian company law is two-layered in a way candidates systematically underrate. The Act, passed by Parliament, states the obligations, the institutions and the consequences. The rules, made by the executive under the Act and revised by notification, carry the thresholds, the monetary limits, the time periods, the forms and the procedural detail through which the obligations operate. The two layers change by entirely different machinery. An Act changes by amendment, debated and voted, at intervals a candidate can track across a whole preparation cycle. Rules change by notification in the Gazette, without debate, at any time, and the standard preparation industry, textbooks, coaching notes and the threshold charts that circulate among candidates, is built almost entirely from the layer that changes silently. The distinction between what the Act requires and what the rules prescribe is, on the evidence of marking experience in this subject, the distinction that generates the most lost marks, because it converts knowledge that was accurate when memorised into error by the day it is used.

### Why do thresholds in company law change faster than the provisions they serve?

Because Parliament writes the obligation and delegates the number. A duty such as holding a meeting, filing a return or spending on social responsibility sits in the Act and changes only by amendment. Its monetary limit, time period and form sit in rules made under the Act, which a ministry can revise by notification without a parliamentary session.

The corporate social responsibility regime is the clearest worked example of the two layers in motion, and the clearest demonstration of why a memorised chart is a liability. The Act itself fixes the gateway in Section 135: a company crossing any of the stated lines of net worth, turnover or net profit, five hundred crore rupees of net worth, one thousand crore of turnover, five crore of net profit, owes the duty, and the duty's measure, two per cent of average net profits of the three preceding financial years, also sits in the Act. Those figures a candidate may learn with confidence, because Parliament must move to move them. Everything about how unspent amounts are handled, the separate account for ongoing projects, the periods within which money must move to specified funds, the set-off of excess spending, lives in the amendment Acts of 2019 and 2020 and in the companies rules on social responsibility as revised, a machinery that was redesigned twice before it fully commenced and that punishes any note taken from the first design. The full treatment of that machinery, including the correction that matters most, belongs to [the guide to CSR law and Section 135](/2012/03/15/csr-law-india-section-135/), and the study point is the correction itself: the transfer duty enacted in 2019 carried a criminal penalty that was never brought into force, and the operative regime, which commenced on 22 January 2021, enforces through monetary penalty instead. A candidate whose notes were fixed from the 2019 amendment as enacted would have carried an imprisonment provision into the examination that the law itself declined to commence. That is the moving target in its purest form, and no care in memorisation protects against it. Only verification does.

The insolvency code supplies the second example, and it is the sharper one because the moving number sits in the Act's own text as a delegated figure. Section 4 of the Code set the minimum default for initiating a corporate process at one lakh rupees as enacted, with a proviso allowing the Central Government to raise the floor by notification up to one crore. In March 2020, by notification S.O. 1205(E), the floor was raised to the full one crore. Nothing in the printed text of the Code changed; a candidate reading an unrevised bare Act, or a commentary printed before the notification, learns a threshold one hundred times too small, on the very provision that decides whether the process may begin at all. The lesson generalises across the subject. Wherever a provision states a figure that a proviso or a rule makes revisable, the figure is a rules-layer fact wearing an Act-layer costume, and it must be verified late, against the Gazette notification or the ministry's own consolidated text, not early against a textbook.

The administrative history of the company statute teaches the same lesson from a third angle, and it corrects a shorthand that circulates in preparation material. Electronic filing and the single electronic registry did not arrive with any replacement of the Act, nor with the amendment of 2000 with which they are sometimes credited. They arrived with the Companies (Amendment) Act 2006, which provided for director identification numbers and for filing in electronic form, and with the MCA21 programme through which the registry moved from paper files in state offices to one electronic system. A candidate who learns the sequence correctly learns something about the subject itself: that its deepest changes are as likely to come through the machinery layer as through the headline statute, and that an amendment trail read only for the famous years, 1956, 2013, 2016, will miss the year the registry changed its medium. Examiners reward the candidate who knows that the medium changed in 2006 for the same reason they reward the candidate who verifies a threshold: both answers show a reader of the record, not a reciter of the summary.

The practical method follows directly. Hold the structure of every obligation in permanent memory: who owes it, to whom, triggered by what, enforced where, with what consequence. Hold its numeric limbs, the limits, percentages, periods and fees, in a separate and deliberately provisional layer of the notes, marked with the source and the date of verification, and re-verify that layer against the official text in the final stage of revision, as close to the examination as the calendar allows. Where a syllabus or an institute prescribes a cut-off for amendments, that convention governs what the examiner may ask, and the honest position, which this guide states rather than guesses around, is that cut-off conventions differ between examining bodies and must be taken from the candidate's own institute's announcement, not from any secondary source. What does not differ is the obligation the method imposes: a threshold written in an answer should be a threshold the candidate has seen in the enacted text or its rules within the current revision cycle, and where that check has not been possible, the answer should state the structure and flag the figure as prescribed, which examiners reward, rather than assert a stale number with confidence, which they punish.

## Commencement: the third layer candidates forget

Between enactment and obligation stands a step that this subject uses more than most and candidates check less than any: commencement. The modern Indian statute is rarely born whole. The Companies Act 2013 was brought into force in tranches, a first set of ninety-eight sections in September 2013, the bulk of the Act on 1 April 2014, and the tribunal chapters together with the class action provision only on 1 June 2016, when the forum they named came into existence. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code commenced in the same staged fashion, its regulator first, its corporate process provisions on 1 December 2016, and its provisions on insolvency of personal guarantors to corporate debtors only in December 2019, while two entire territories of the enacted Code, the fresh start process for individuals and the cross-border insolvency provisions, were never brought into force at all. A provision can therefore be enacted, printed in every bare Act, discussed in every commentary, and still not be law in operation, and an answer that applies it as operative law commits an error of state, not of detail.

The examination consequence is precise. Where a paper's facts fall near a commencement boundary, the commencement is the question, whatever the question says it is about. The candidate's defence is a third column in the revision file, beside the structure and the numbers: the state of the provision, enacted, commenced, amended, omitted, or enacted and never commenced, with the date the state was last verified. The CSR correction taught in this guide is a commencement lesson before it is anything else: a penalty provision enacted in 2019 and never commenced misled every note that treated enactment as operation. The same discipline governs amendments, which are themselves commenced by notification and can sit enacted and inert for months. In this subject the question "what does the provision say" is only the second question. The first is "is the provision in force, in the form the facts require", and the candidate who asks it in that order has understood the statute's actual mode of existence, which is not a text but a text with a notification history.

The verification habit needs its sources named, because "check the official text" is advice a candidate cannot act on until the texts are identified. The hierarchy is short. At the top stands the Gazette of India, where amendment Acts, rules and notifications acquire their legal life; a figure that cannot be traced to a Gazette instrument or to a text that reproduces one faithfully is a rumour with a number in it. Below it stand the administering institutions' own publications: the Ministry of Corporate Affairs for the company and partnership statutes, which publishes the Acts as amended, the rules as notified and the circulars that gloss them, and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board for the Code, whose regulations and discussion papers carry the process detail. Below those stand the commercial bare Acts, reliable in proportion to the currency of their edition and to be checked, for any figure that matters, against the two layers above. The candidate need not read the Gazette daily; the discipline is narrower and entirely achievable. Every figure in the revision file carries the name of the layer it was verified in and the date, figures from the top two layers are trusted until the final sweep, and figures from anywhere else are treated as leads to be confirmed rather than as knowledge to be carried. That hierarchy, applied without exception, is the whole secret of the subject's accuracy problem, and it is a secret of method rather than of effort.

## Four moments that organise the whole subject

Chronology is the weakest organising device in a subject this size, because a sequence of forty amendment years cannot be held and need not be. Four moments carry the whole structure, and a candidate holding them can place any provision, amendment or case without a timeline. The first is 1956, the founding statute: the Companies Act 1956, Act 1 of 1956, with its 658 sections, built the post-Independence company regime on the English model and then accumulated, over nearly six decades, the amendments, the administrative machinery and the case law that defined what Indian company law was. Its replacement is the second moment. The Companies Act 2013, Act 18 of 2013, assented to on 29 August 2013 and brought into force in stages with the bulk of its provisions commencing on 1 April 2014, cut the statute to 470 sections in twenty-nine chapters and rebuilt its centre of gravity: from a permission-and-approval regime administered by the executive toward a disclosure-and-accountability regime enforced through independent directors, auditors under rotation, a statutory audit regulator in the National Financial Reporting Authority constituted in October 2018, and a fraud offence with real teeth. The expert committee chaired by J. J. Irani, whose report preceded the replacement, supplied much of its design philosophy, and the 2013 Act is best revised as an argument about how companies should be governed, not as a list of novelties.

The third moment is 2016, and it is a double event that candidates persistently halve. In that year the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, Act 31 of 2016, assented to on 28 May 2016 with its corporate insolvency provisions commencing on 1 December 2016, gave India a single process statute for corporate distress; and on 1 June 2016 the National Company Law Tribunal and the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal were constituted under Sections 408 and 410 of the company statute, dissolving the Company Law Board and receiving, in stages, the company jurisdiction of the High Courts and the remnants of the sick-companies regime. The two events are one design decision: a new process requires a specialised forum, and the forum was created under the older statute to serve both. The fourth moment is a sequence rather than a year: the decriminalisation amendments culminating in the Companies (Amendment) Act 2020 for the company statute and the Limited Liability Partnership (Amendment) Act 2021 for the partnership statute, in force from 1 April 2022, which converted swathes of procedural default from criminal offences into monetary penalties adjudicated within the department, expressly to reserve criminal process for fraud and injury. Candidates who place those four moments correctly can date nothing else and still answer the architecture questions; candidates who memorised forty dates and missed the four moments answer chronology questions and fail the architecture ones. The series thesis holds even in this register: the statute is the text, the notification history and the rules are the record, and in corporate law the record moves faster than in any other subject in the series, so the moments worth fixing are the ones that rearranged the text itself.

One discipline attaches to the four moments, and it is the discipline of the seam. Examinations are set in the joins between the moments: the provision that survived from 1956 into 2013 in altered dress, the jurisdiction that moved from a High Court to a tribunal bench, the default that was a crime in one decade and a fee-bearing lapse in the next. Revision organised by the moments should therefore spend its time less on what each moment contains than on what each moment changed relative to its predecessor, because that difference, stated crisply, is the answer to the standard question in this subject, which is rarely "what is the law" and almost always "what is the law, given what it replaced".

## The amendment trails, logged properly

The four moments organise the subject; the amendment trails fill it, and they must be logged rather than memorised, because a trail is a sequence of named changes and the examinable unit is the change, not the year. The method is a single line per amendment in the revision file: the amending Act, the provision it touched, the kind of change it made, and the pressure that produced it. Kept that way, the company statute's trail reads as an intelligible story. The amendment of 2015 tidied the replacement Act's own overreach, dropping the minimum paid-up capital requirement and making the common seal optional. The amendment of 2017 carried the Company Law Committee's second round of corrections into the text. The amendment of 2019 built the in-house adjudication machinery for penalties and inserted the first, criminal-backed version of the CSR transfer duty. The amendment of 2020, following the Company Law Committee's report of 2019, performed the decriminalisation proper, converting dozens of procedural defaults into civil penalties, recasting the CSR sanction as monetary before the regime commenced, and easing the compliance load on small and one-person companies. Four lines, and the candidate holds the whole post-2013 history of the statute as a sequence of decisions, each reversible in the mind back to the position it replaced.

The Code's trail is shorter and denser, and the same four-column log holds it. The amendment of 2018 confirmed the homebuyer within the financial creditor class by a deeming explanation and refined the disqualification provision's reach, the two changes the first year of litigation had shown to be necessary. The amendment of 2019 wrote the outer timeline of 330 days into Section 12 and strengthened the binding force of an approved plan, the change the Essar Steel court then read with its qualification. The amendment of 2020 inserted Section 10A, suspending initiation for defaults arising in the pandemic period, a provision whose entire meaning is a date range and which therefore tests the commencement discipline in its purest form. The amendment of 2021 added the pre-packaged insolvency process for micro, small and medium enterprises as Chapter III-A, a debtor-in-possession variant built beside the creditor-led process. Logged this way, the trail also teaches the subject's deepest habit: the Code is amended in response to its own case law and its own working record, so the trail and the doctrine must be revised together, each amendment read as a reply to the decision or difficulty that preceded it. A candidate who logs amendments without the pressure column can recite the trail and explain none of it; the pressure is the explanation, and explanation is what the papers buy.

## The forum is part of the syllabus

Candidates treat jurisdiction as a preliminary topic, to be cleared in the first week and forgotten. In this subject the forum is a continuing actor, and the tribunalisation story is examined as constitutional law, as company law and as insolvency law simultaneously. The design fact is simple: company and insolvency adjudication in India is tribunal adjudication. The constitution, bench structure, jurisdiction and record of the two bodies are profiled in [the guide to the National Company Law Tribunal and the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal](/2012/05/01/nclt-nclat-explained/), and the study task here is to hold the constitutional argument that makes the forum possible, because that argument, not the bench list, is what papers examine.

### Why does the forum matter as much as the statute in this subject?

Because a correct provision placed in the wrong forum reads as a wrong answer. Company and insolvency disputes begin before the National Company Law Tribunal, a tribunal rather than a court, with appeal to the appellate tribunal and then to the Supreme Court on questions of law. Examiners test the route, and the application marks follow it.

The constitutional line runs through a small set of decisions whose propositions a candidate must hold exactly. In S. P. Sampath Kumar v Union of India, decided in 1987, the Supreme Court accepted tribunals as institutional substitutes for the High Courts in their assigned fields, provided they were made effective and independent enough to bear the substitution. In L. Chandra Kumar v Union of India, decided in 1997, a seven-judge bench corrected the design's outer limit: tribunals remain subject to the writ and supervisory jurisdiction of the High Courts under Articles 226 and 227, judicial review being part of the basic structure, so a tribunal is a court of first instance and never the final word on its own legality. In Union of India v R. Gandhi, the Madras Bar Association case of 2010, the Court upheld the creation of the National Company Law Tribunal in principle while prescribing conditions on the qualifications and composition of its members, which is why the tribunal constituted in 2016 took the shape it did. In Rojer Mathew v South Indian Bank, decided in 2019, the Court struck at the executive's control over tribunal appointments and service conditions, holding that the independence of the adjudicator is not an administrative detail. Four propositions, four cases, one argument: Parliament may move adjudication out of the courts, but the moved forum must be independent, qualified and reviewable. State the propositions and the architecture of any tribunal answer is already built; the candidate who recites bench strengths and sanctioned posts without the propositions has memorised the layer that changes and missed the layer that is examined.

For examination purposes the forum carries a second, practical payload. Appeal routes in this subject are short, statutory and frequently asked: from the tribunal to the appellate tribunal, and from the appellate tribunal to the Supreme Court on a question of law, with the insolvency code running its own clock of thirty days for the first appeal. Limitation and forum errors are the classic application traps, and they are avoidable by the same method as threshold errors, because the route, unlike the roster of benches, is statutory and stable. Learn the route as part of each regime's spine, and treat any question that supplies facts about a dispute as asking two questions in sequence: what does the law say, and before whom does the saying happen.

The clocks deserve their own statement because they are statutory, stable and set as traps with unusual regularity. Under the Companies Act, an appeal from the tribunal lies to the appellate tribunal within forty-five days, with a further forty-five days condonable on sufficient cause, and from the appellate tribunal to the Supreme Court within sixty days on a question of law. Under the insolvency code the clocks run shorter and harder: thirty days to the appellate tribunal with a maximum of fifteen further days condonable, the cap being a hard one the Supreme Court enforced in National Spot Exchange v Anil Kohli, and forty-five days to the Supreme Court with fifteen condonable. The pair of regimes thus teaches one lesson in two dialects: the company statute's clocks forgive delay on cause shown, the Code's clocks barely forgive at all, because a process statute built on timelines cannot treat its own appellate timeline as elastic. A candidate who states the route without the clocks has answered the forum question halfway, and the missing half is the half with numbers in it, which the verification habit, applied to clocks as to thresholds, keeps accurate.

## The moving-target revision method

Every revision system in this subject fails in the same place: it treats all examinable material as equally perishable, and so either revises everything with equal anxiety or fixes everything early and trusts it. The moving-target method begins by sorting the material by its rate of decay, because the four categories of examinable content in corporate law change at measurably different speeds and demand opposite handling. Statutory provisions, the enacted text of the two Acts and the Code, change only by parliamentary amendment and are the safest material a candidate holds; they should be learned early, learned whole, and fixed. Case law changes by decision, in steps, and a settled proposition from a Constitution Bench is stable for a preparation cycle even though the stream of tribunal and appellate decisions beneath it is not; it should be learned as propositions with dates attached, and refreshed at the level of the largest courts only. Rules and thresholds change by notification at any time and are the least stable material in the subject; they should be learned last, verified against the official text, and deliberately left unfixed until the final revision stage. Procedural forms and filing mechanics change with the registry's systems and circulars, are examined lightly in theory papers and heavily in the practical life that follows them, and should be learned by structure rather than by snapshot. The table below states the method as a reference, and the rest of this guide assumes it.

| Category of material | How stable it is | How often it changes | Where to verify it | When a note on it is safe to fix |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Statutory provisions | High: the enacted text alters only by amendment passed by Parliament | At intervals of years, in identifiable amendment Acts | The bare Act as amended, read with the amendment Act that last touched the provision | Early in preparation, and permanently |
| Rules and thresholds | Low: limits, percentages, periods and fees sit in delegated legislation | At any time, by Gazette notification, several times a year across the subject | The rules as notified on the ministry and Gazette record, checked against the provision that delegates them | Only in the final revision stage, as close to the examination as possible |
| Case law | Medium: propositions from larger benches settle; tribunal-level readings move | In steps, whenever a higher court decides, with occasional rearranging decisions | The judgment itself or a reliable report of it, noting the bench and the year | Once the proposition and its court are fixed; recheck after any Constitution Bench decision in the area |
| Procedural forms and filings | Low to medium: the obligation is statutory, the mechanics are administrative | With registry systems, circulars and fee revisions | The registry portal's prescribed form and the rule that requires the filing | Late, and by structure: learn what the filing proves and triggers, not the screen layout |

The method has one operational rule that does most of the work, and it is worth stating as a rule rather than a suggestion. No number enters a candidate's final notes without a verification date written beside it, and no number is carried from an earlier cycle, a senior's notes or a coaching compilation without that date being renewed. The rule sounds clerical and is in fact the difference between the two failure modes of this subject: the candidate who trusted a stale chart and the candidate who never trusted any chart enough to use numbers at all. The first mode loses marks on accuracy; the second loses them on specificity, because an answer that says "a prescribed threshold" where the paper wanted the threshold reads as evasion even when the evasion was prudent. The verified, dated figure escapes both. Where verification genuinely cannot be completed, the honest middle course in an answer is to state the figure as the provision frames it, attributing the number to the rules as prescribed, and examiners schooled by a decade of notification churn accept the attribution for what it is: evidence that the candidate knows which layer of the law the number lives in.

## Case law: the proposition first, the citation second

The instruction that governs case law in this subject is easily misunderstood, so it deserves its own statement. In professional and law papers alike, a correctly stated proposition without a citation scores; a citation attached to a wrong proposition does not, and may score worse than silence, because it tells the examiner the candidate has handled the material at the level of names. The method follows: learn each leading case as a one-sentence proposition, in the candidate's own words, with the court's holding stated as narrowly as the record allows, and attach the case name as the proposition's label, never as its substitute. A revision file that stores propositions can be tested by covering the names; a file that stores names tests nothing but recognition. The candidate who can say what a case decided, under challenge, has the case. The candidate who can only say that the case is important has its bibliography.

On the insolvency side, five decisions carry the doctrinal weight of the Code and between them answer the standard questions. Innoventive Industries v ICICI Bank, decided in 2017, established that the existence of a default is the trigger for admission of a financial creditor's application, and that the Code, as a later parliamentary enactment with an overriding clause, prevails over inconsistent state law; it is the answer to every question about what a financial creditor must prove and what defences do not arise at the admission stage. Swiss Ribbons v Union of India, decided in 2019, upheld the constitutional validity of the Code across the board, including the classification between financial and operational creditors, which the Court sustained on the basis of intelligible differentia in the nature of the lending, and the disqualification scheme of Section 29A, sustained as a measure directed at those responsible for the default rather than as a punishment; it is the answer to every validity question and should be learned as a set of distinct holdings rather than as a general blessing. Committee of Creditors of Essar Steel v Satish Kumar Gupta, decided in 2019, held that the committee's commercial decision on a plan is not to be second-guessed on its merits, while reading the statutory timeline so that the outer limit of 330 days is the norm and not an inflexible guillotine, and tempering the distribution among creditor classes with a requirement of equitable treatment; it is the answer on commercial wisdom and on time. Pioneer Urban Land and Infrastructure v Union of India, decided in 2019, held that allottees in real estate projects are financial creditors entitled to initiate the process, reading the deeming explanation into the Code to that effect; it is the answer on homebuyers. Ghanshyam Mishra v Edelweiss Asset Reconstruction, decided in 2021, held that a resolution plan, once approved, binds all stakeholders and that claims not forming part of it stand extinguished, so that the successful applicant takes the company on a clean slate; it is the answer on finality and on the fate of undecided claims. Around the five, one more proposition earns its place for operational creditors: Mobilox Innovations v Kirusa Koncepts, decided in 2017, held that an operational creditor's application must be rejected where the debt is genuinely disputed on substantial grounds pre-existing the demand, which is the filter that makes the second door into the process narrower than the first.

Two disciplines govern the decisions below the leading set. The first concerns tribunal and appellate decisions, which accumulate in this subject faster than in any other in the series and which tempt candidates into collecting them. A tribunal decision is authority for its facts and a reading of the text; it is not the doctrine until a higher court adopts it, and a revision file should store such decisions under the provision they interpret, marked as readings, rather than among the propositions. When a High Court or the Supreme Court settles the point, the reading is promoted or discarded, and the file's layering performs the promotion mechanically. The second discipline concerns conflict: where benches have read a provision differently, as happened on the tribunal's power to recall its own orders and on the treatment of statutory dues in a plan, the candidate states the conflict as a conflict, names the readings by their courts, and identifies the larger or later authority without pretending the question is closed. Examiners reward the accurate map of a disagreement over a confident answer on an unsettled point, because the accurate map is what a practitioner actually needs from the same material.

On the company side the case load is lighter and older, and the candidate should resist inflating it. The tribunalisation line set out in the forum section carries the constitutional questions. The doctrine questions cluster around the tribunal's equity jurisdiction in oppression and mismanagement, where the governing ideas, that the conduct must be burdensome, harsh and wrongful in a continuing course rather than a single lost vote, descend from the English authorities the Indian courts adopted under the 1956 Act and carried into the new one; a candidate should state those ideas as doctrine and name a case only where the name is certain. That asymmetry between the two regimes is itself a syllabus fact: the Code is young, its meaning is being made by decision, and its papers therefore test judgments as doctrine; the company statute is old, its doctrine is settled into the text and its rules, and its papers test provisions, procedures and comparisons, with cases in a supporting role. Preparation time divided without regard to that asymmetry is preparation time misdivided, a point the verdict at the end of this guide takes up again.

## The distinctions that cost marks

Examiners in a doctrinal subject test distinctions because distinctions are where partial knowledge reveals itself, and this subject has a standing set that recurs across papers and institutes. The Act-versus-rules distinction has been treated already as the master key. The second is the pair of creditor classes under the insolvency code. A financial creditor lends against the time value of money and initiates on default alone; an operational creditor supplies goods or services, must serve a demand notice, and can be stopped at the door by a genuine pre-existing dispute; an answer that runs the two procedures together has surrendered the Code's central classification, the very classification Swiss Ribbons sustained. The third distinction is between admission and approval inside the process: admission is the adjudicating authority's act on proof of default, approval of a plan is the creditors' commercial act confirmed by the authority on statutory compliance, and the candidate who attributes the plan's merits to the tribunal, or the default inquiry to the committee, has reversed the Code's allocation of power. The fourth is between the company and the limited liability partnership as vehicles, which the H3 below places in the syllabus.

### Where does limited liability partnership law sit in the corporate law syllabus?

In the fourth corner of the map. The Limited Liability Partnership Act 2008 is a statute built beside the company regime rather than inside it, and syllabi treat it as the alternative-vehicle topic: incorporation, the partnership agreement, designated partners, conversion from firms and companies, and the contrast between a partner's limited liability and a director's duties under the Companies Act.

The partnership statute repays the candidate who learns it as a designed contrast rather than as a short company law. The Limited Liability Partnership Act 2008, Act 6 of 2009, in force in its bulk from 31 March 2009 with the conversion provisions following in May of that year, creates a body corporate whose internal government is a matter of contract in the partnership agreement, where the company statute imposes a mandatory governance code. There is no board to hold accountable, no share capital to maintain, no general meeting machinery; there are designated partners answerable for compliance, an agreement the state registers but does not draft, and liability limited by the statute's own terms, with the fraud exception stated in the Act itself for the partner who acts to defraud. The 2021 amendment then ran the company statute's decriminalisation logic through this statute a year later, converting minor defaults to penalties and creating a small partnership category with low default ceilings on contribution and turnover, and a comparison answer that treats the two statutes' reform sequences as one story told twice, with a year's lag, will outscore an answer that treats the partnership statute as a footnote. The fifth standing distinction closes the set: between decriminalised default and fraud. The reform sequence of 2019 to 2021 redrew the enforcement character of both statutes by moving procedural lapses into monetary penalty adjudicated by officers of the department, and the candidate must be able to say what did not move: Section 447 fraud, investigation, and the offences involving public interest and injury remained criminal, because the reform's stated logic was to reserve prosecution for culpable conduct, a logic an evaluation answer should be able to argue from both sides, as the reform's defenders present it, a compliance dividend for honest enterprise, and as its critics present it, a dilution of deterrence whose answer lies in the penalty record rather than in assertion.

## Evidence in an answer: using the record without inventing it

Doctrinal answers run on text and cases; evaluation answers run on evidence, and the evidence discipline of this series applies in the examination hall exactly as it applies on the page. Three kinds of material may be used, in descending order of safety. The first is the design document: the committee reports that built the statutes, the Irani Committee's report behind the 2013 Act, the Bankruptcy Law Reforms Committee's report behind the Code, the Company Law Committee's reports behind the amendments of 2017 and 2020. These are citable as authority for what a design was meant to do, they are stable, and an evaluation answer that grounds its account of purpose in the design document has an anchor no marker can dispute. The second is the institutional record stated as pattern: pendency and vacancy in the tribunal system, the share of processes ending in liquidation rather than resolution, the direction of disposal times. Patterns may be stated as patterns, attributed to the reporting institution where the candidate knows it, and the moment a pattern hardens into a precise figure the candidate must know the figure's source and base or let it go; a recovery percentage without its denominator is the single most common invented fact in this subject's scripts, and examiners who mark the record articles' territory know the real figures too well to be bluffed. The third is the single example, the famous case or company, usable as illustration and never as proof; the answer that says "as the Essar Steel process showed" about timelines illustrates, and the answer that builds a general claim on it overreaches. Hold the three kinds apart, attribute what is borrowed, and prefer the design document to the anecdote at every choice, and the evaluation answer acquires the quality markers privately call judgment: the visible ability to tell the difference between what is known, what is reported and what is merely said.

## The debates an evaluation answer must be able to run

Evaluation questions in this subject draw on a small set of genuine debates, and the candidate should hold each in its strongest two-voiced form rather than as a settled opinion. The first is the control debate inside the insolvency design. The Code chose creditor control: management is displaced on admission and the committee of creditors decides the company's fate, a design the Bankruptcy Law Reforms Committee defended on the ground that a debtor left in possession has every incentive to gamble with other people's money. The contrary case, pressed in the challenges and in the commentary, holds that displacement destroys the knowledge only incumbents hold, deters honest promoters from early filing, and converts a resolution statute into an auction of the firm; Swiss Ribbons records both arguments and answers them with the legislation's economic character, but a candidate should be able to state the losing side as its makers state it, because evaluation questions are set precisely where a court has held one side and the debate continues anyway. The second is the disqualification debate around Section 29A: whether barring defaulting promoters from bidding protects the process from moral hazard or merely hands the company to rivals at a discount the creditors bear, a question on which the provision's defenders point to the conduct that caused the default and its critics to the value destroyed when the best-informed bidder is excluded. The third is the homebuyer debate: whether treating allottees as financial creditors corrects a real asymmetry, households having paid for homes undelivered with no seat at any table, or imports thousands of individual claimants into a commercial process and strains its timelines, the argument Parliament settled in 2018 and the Court sustained in 2019 without ending the underlying disagreement about what the process is for.

The company statute carries its own pair. The social responsibility mandate divides opinion between those who defend Section 135 as a disclosure-and-spending discipline that yokes large companies to public purposes while leaving the choice of purposes substantially to the board, and those who criticise it as a tax in substance, levied without the safeguards of one, and better replaced by honest taxation and public spending; the provision's history of amendment, and particularly the retreat from criminal enforcement before the regime commenced, is the evidence both sides cite. The decriminalisation sequence divides opinion between the compliance case, that criminal process for procedural lapse punished the honest and crowded the courts, and the deterrence case, that a penalty priced into the cost of business deters less than a prosecution threatened, a disagreement the penalty and prosecution records will eventually inform and assertion cannot settle in advance. An evaluation answer in this subject is therefore a small exercise in the stance this whole series requires: each side at its strongest, in its own voice, the settled law distinguished from the open argument, and the candidate's verdict given at the end with its reasons showing. The candidate who rehearses the debates in this form owns a stock of answers no amendment can obsolete, because debates survive the changes they are about.

## How questions are framed, and how to work one

Question papers in this subject are more predictable in form than in content, and the forms can be named. The definitional demand asks what a provision or institution is and what it does, and is answered by the provision's elements in the provision's order. The distinction demand names two things the syllabus places side by side, creditor classes, vehicles, old Act and new, and is answered by deciding criteria, not by parallel description: an answer that describes A fully and then B fully has not performed a distinction until it states the ground on which the law separates them. The proposition demand states or implies a holding and asks for its basis and limits, and is answered by the one-sentence proposition, its case, and the boundary of what the case did not decide. The problem demand gives facts and is answered by the spine: identify the stage, the regime, the actor, the forum and the provision, in that order, and the conclusion follows the analysis rather than preceding it. The evaluation demand asks whether a reform worked or a design is defensible, and is the only form in which the stance material belongs, the strongest case on each side stated in its own voice before the candidate's verdict, which must then be given and defended rather than implied.

The dual-text problem that runs through the subject, a 1956 provision and its 2013 successor, a code provision before and after amendment, has a method of its own, and it is shared with the criminal-law transition: [the criminal law reform exam guide](/2011/03/15/criminal-law-reform-exam-guide/) works the same two-text discipline from the other side of the series, and the candidate who has handled either transition owns the method for both. State the old position in one sentence with its number, state the new position in one sentence with its number, state the change as a change of a named kind, renumbering, redrafting, substantive alteration or creation, and only then evaluate. The sequence sounds elementary and is violated in most scripts, which evaluate a change they have not yet stated, or state the new law in the old law's vocabulary. A worked comparison answer makes the sequence visible. Asked how the 2013 Act changed the position of the minority shareholder, the answer that scores opens with the old door: Sections 397 and 398 of the 1956 Act, a petition against oppression and mismanagement, discretionary relief, a jurisdiction exercised sparingly. It then states the new architecture in its own terms: Sections 241 and 242 carrying the jurisdiction forward with wider standing, Section 245 adding the class action the old Act never had, and the tribunal as the single forum for both. It then classifies: the oppression jurisdiction is a substantive continuation in a new house; the class action is a creation; the two together convert minority protection from a petitioner's remedy into a designed system. Evaluation, offered last and briefly, notes what the design still lacks in practice and stops. Every element of that answer was available to every candidate in the room. What separates it is order, and order is a method, not a talent.

This guide states the law in general terms and teaches an examination method; it is legal information and study guidance, not advice on any individual case, candidature or filing, and a reader with a live matter should take advice on its own facts, since limitation periods and forum rules in this subject are unforgiving.

## The traps the papers set by design

Certain errors recur so reliably in this subject that they should be learned as a catalogue, the way a pilot learns failure modes. The first is the uncommenced provision: a duty or a penalty enacted and printed but never brought into force, applied by the candidate as living law; its defence is the state column in the revision file. The second is the inherited number: a provision cited by its 1956 number in a 2013 answer, or by its pre-amendment form in a question set after the amendment; its defence is the both-directions drill on the name-set. The third is the wrong door: an operational creditor run through the financial creditor's procedure, a financial debt analysed as a trade claim, the demand notice and the dispute filter forgotten; its defence is classification before procedure, always. The fourth is the guarantor surprise: facts that look like a corporate process question but turn on a personal guarantee, where the Code's guarantor provisions, commenced years after the corporate provisions, and the holding in Lalit Kumar Jain v Union of India, decided in 2021, that the liability of a personal guarantor is coextensive and that notifying the guarantor provisions was within Parliament's power, decide the matter on a different track from the corporate debtor's own process. The fifth is the date-boundary trap of Section 10A: a default placed by the facts in the suspended pandemic period, where initiation is barred altogether and permanently for that default, so that the entire correct answer is a commencement-and-dates answer wearing a merits costume.

The remaining traps are quieter. The listed-company trap attributes a listing-regulation requirement to the Act or an Act requirement to the regulations, and is defended by naming the layer relied on. The waterfall trap argues a plan's fairness from the liquidation ranking in Section 53 without noticing that a plan is judged on the Code's own approval conditions and equitable treatment, the ranking being the comparator and not the test. The moratorium trap overstates the freeze: Section 14 stops proceedings and enforcement against the debtor, and the candidate must know the standard exceptions and limits, that the freeze does not protect the guarantor's assets, and that it ends with the process. The credit trap repeats an unsourced recovery percentage or caseload figure from the preparation market; its defence is this guide's standing rule, that outcome figures belong to the record and are quoted, if at all, with their source and their base. A candidate who has walked the catalogue once, slowly, owns a checklist that costs nothing in the hall and pays on every paper, because traps are not set for the ignorant. They are set for the hurried, and the catalogue is a device for slowing the right moments down.

## Worked problems: the method under pressure

Methods are believed only when they survive facts, so consider the standard problem this subject sets in its insolvency territory. A supplier is owed a sum for goods delivered; the buyer company answered the supplier's invoices for a year with complaints about quality, raised a debit note before any demand was made, and ignored the statutory demand notice when it came; the supplier files as an operational creditor and the company resists. The undisciplined answer argues fairness. The methodical answer runs the spine. Stage: distress, at the door of the process. Regime: the Code, operational creditor route, Section 9, not Section 7, because the debt arose from goods supplied and not from money disbursed against time. Actor and forum: the supplier applies, the tribunal decides admission. Provision and filter: the demand notice was required and was served; the question is the dispute, and under the Mobilox proposition the tribunal asks whether a genuine dispute on substantial grounds pre-existed the notice, which the quality complaints and the debit note, both predating it, are capable of establishing. Conclusion, reached only at the end: the application is vulnerable at admission, the supplier's remedy lies in the ordinary forums, and the Code's door was built narrower for this class precisely to keep disputed trade claims out of a process designed for undisputed default. Every step of that answer is a retrieval from the map, and none of it required a fact the question did not supply.

The evaluation form repays the same treatment. Asked whether the tribunal system has delivered what the court system could not, the answer that scores refuses the invitation to a verdict in the first paragraph. It states the design claim: specialised, single-window adjudication of company and insolvency matters by a forum built for commercial questions. It states the constitutional settlement that disciplines the design, Sampath Kumar's substitution, L. Chandra Kumar's retained review, the appointment conditions of the Madras Bar Association line and Rojer Mathew. It then tests the claim against the record a candidate may state without inventing figures: the forum's chronic vacancies and the judiciary's repeated directions on appointments, the staged transfer of jurisdiction that left company matters divided between tribunal and High Court for years, and the countervailing fact that the insolvency process, which exists only because the forum does, has run at a scale the court system never offered it. The verdict, when it arrives, can afford to be exact: the design is constitutionally sound and operationally under-built, and the gap between those two judgments is the subject's standing criticism of the state as an institution-builder. An answer built that way can be disagreed with, which is the mark of an evaluation; an answer built as an assertion can only be marked wrong or right, which is the mark of a summary.

## Answer architecture: how a strong script is built

Content decides what an answer can earn; architecture decides how much of it the examiner sees. The architecture that serves every form in this subject has four moves, and they are the same moves whether the paper allows three hours or forty minutes. The opening move states the answer before the argument: the proposition the question turns on, the classification the facts fall under, the change the comparison asks for, compressed into the first sentences so that the examiner reads the rest as elaboration rather than as a search. The second move lays the governing framework in the law's own order, provision's elements in the provision's sequence, definition before operation, Act before rules, original before amendment, because examiners mark against the statute's structure and an answer aligned with it is cheap to credit. The third move performs the question's specific work, the distinction drawn on its deciding criterion, the facts taken to the definition limb by limb, the two sides of the evaluation stated at strength. The closing move returns to the question and stops: a sentence that answers it in terms, and nothing after, because a script that ends by answering reads as controlled and a script that trails into summary reads as unfinished even when it is longer.

Within that architecture, three habits separate the top band from the competent. The first is citation discipline of the kind this guide teaches: section numbers where they function as names or where precision is the point, propositions where cases are used, and silence where a reference is uncertain, since a script's credibility is a single quantity that one invented citation spends entirely. The second is the visible handling of layers: the answer that says "the duty is in the Act; the figure is prescribed and verified as follows" advertises the exact competence this subject tests, and examiners reward the advertisement because it is the thing itself. The third is proportion: marks follow the question's parts, and the candidate who gives the definitional limb of a problem the same pages as its contested limb has misallocated the scarcest resource in the room, which is not knowledge but time. None of this is decoration added to knowledge after the fact. In a subject where every candidate in the top half holds substantially the same material, architecture is not the presentation of the answer. It is a large part of the answer.

## Three readers, three preparations

This guide has spoken to the candidate, but the brief of a study article in this series runs to three readers, and the other two deserve their paragraph each. The teacher's problem in this subject is not knowledge but sequence: a syllabus revised annually and a statute revised oftener make any fixed lecture order obsolete in detail while the architecture endures, and the sequence that survives is the one this guide is built on, regimes first, spine second, name provisions third, the moving layers last and explicitly labelled as moving, so that a cohort taught in one year can be re-pointed to the changed figures in the next without re-teaching the structure. The confusions a teacher should pre-empt are known in advance, because they are the standing distinctions: Act against rules, creditor against creditor, admission against approval, company against partnership, and a lecture that names them as confusions before the students meet them in a paper has done more than a lecture that covers more ground. The law student's problem is the opposite of the professional candidate's: doctrine comes easily and operation feels beneath notice, and the repair is to insist that in this subject the operation is doctrine, that a moratorium, a waterfall and an approval ladder are arguments about power stated in procedural form, and that the student who reads Section 53 as a ranking of social claims rather than as a payment schedule has understood the Code's politics as well as its law. Each reader, in the end, is asked for the same holding: the structure firmly, the numbers lightly and verifiably, the cases as propositions. The preparations differ in emphasis. The holding does not.

## The viva: the subject spoken aloud

Professional examinations in this field increasingly end in a room rather than on a page, and the spoken form tests the same holdings under a different pressure: there is no time to compose, so what survives is what was learned as structure. The viva rewards the candidate whose knowledge is stored as propositions and sequences, because a proposition can be spoken in one breath and defended in the next, while a memorised paragraph collapses at the first interruption. The disciplines transfer directly. Answer the question asked in the first sentence, as on paper. Classify before narrating: the examiner who asks what happens on admission is listening for moratorium, displacement and committee in that order, and a candidate who begins with the sequence earns the follow-up questions a rambling answer never reaches. Where a figure is asked and the figure lives in the rules, say so and give the structure with the figure as verified; the spoken equivalent of the dated note is the phrase "as prescribed, and as last notified", which signals layer awareness instead of bluffing a stale number with confidence the examiner can hear. Where a premise in the question is wrong, a section misnumbered or a case misdescribed, the candidate corrects it once, plainly and without triumph, and answers the corrected question; vivas are designed to discover whether the candidate will follow an error politely into the ditch, and the correction, delivered as assistance rather than as victory, is among the strongest signals the room offers. The transition material, old Act to new, code as enacted to code as amended, is viva gold, because it can be probed at any depth and the candidate who holds it as classification rather than chronology can stop at exactly the depth the examiner sets. Speak the map, and the room, like the paper, becomes a place where the method is visible.

## A reading order for the cluster this guide caps

A study guide does not replace the cluster it organises, and the candidate who wants the depth behind this method should read the cluster in the order the method implies rather than in the order of publication. Begin with the company statute's pillar treatment, for the governance architecture entire: the institutions, the duty structure, the audit design and the fraud edge. Move to the replacement account and then the comparison, because the seam between 1956 and 2013 is where comparison questions live, and the comparison's classification, renumbered, redrafted, changed, created, should be in the hand before the pillar is re-read. Take the social responsibility treatment next, as the worked example of the two-layer problem, while the Act-versus-rules distinction is fresh. Cross then to the insolvency pillar for the Code's architecture, and read its implementation record immediately after, while the provisions are in mind, because the record article is where the provisions acquire their evidence: what the timelines became in practice, what the recovery figures are and, as importantly, what they are figures of, since the record's discipline of attribution is the candidate's protection against the stray statistic that circulates without a source. Read the tribunal profile after the two statutes it serves, the partnership pillar as the designed contrast, and the sick-companies repeal last of all, as the cluster's history lesson: the regime whose failure, a moratorium that sheltered default and a board that could not decide, explains why the Code was built with clocks, displacement and a waterfall. Read in that order, the cluster teaches itself, each article answering the question the previous one raised; read in any order, it remains nine good articles. The difference is the difference between a library and a course.

## A revision file that can be updated rather than rewritten

The material form of preparation decides whether the moving-target method survives contact with a real revision calendar. A bound notebook and a printed compilation both freeze the law at the moment of their making, and in this subject a frozen record is a wrong record on a schedule the candidate does not control. The working alternative is a single personal file, organised by the spine and the regimes rather than by the textbook's chapter order, in which each obligation is stored as its structure, its provision number, its numeric limbs and its verification date, so that when a notification changes a threshold, one figure in one place is amended and the file is true again. A file of that kind earns its keep only if it is private, portable and editable in place, which is the discipline [VaultBook's account of how journalists protect their sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) describes for source material and which a candidate's threshold file needs for the same reason: one controlled copy, amended in place, never a stack of superseded printouts in which the stale figure survives beside its replacement. The previous-year question work that the file supports should be organised by demand rather than by year: collect the distinction questions together, the proposition questions together, the problem questions together, and the file begins to show what the map predicted, that each demand has a method and that the methods are few.

## The final stage: verification, rehearsal and the order of last work

The last stage of preparation has a different job from every earlier stage, and confusing the jobs is the commonest calendar error in this subject. Earlier stages build: the map, the spine, the name-set, the propositions. The final stage tests and verifies, and its order of work is the moving-target method applied to time. First, the verification sweep: every numeric limb in the revision file, every threshold, percentage, period, fee and clock, re-read against the enacted text, the notified rules and the commencement record, and stamped with the new date or corrected; this sweep is deliberately placed last because its product decays fastest, and a figure verified in the final stage is the freshest figure the calendar allows. Second, the proposition audit: the case entries read aloud as propositions with names covered, any proposition that cannot be stated without its name demoted to reading until it can, and any citation whose proposition is uncertain struck from the file rather than carried as a risk. Third, rehearsal under the paper's own conditions: whole answers to past questions, written to time, classified afterwards by the error they contain, layer errors separated from structure errors separated from retrieval failures, because each class has a different repair and the remaining days should be spent on the class the rehearsal actually found.

Two habits govern the final days themselves. The file is closed to new material except verified corrections; a new compilation, a borrowed summary or a last threshold chart encountered in the final stage enters the file only through the verification sweep, never around it, since the final stage is exactly when stale material does its damage. And the candidate sleeps with the architecture rather than the numbers: the last reading is the map, the spine, the name-set and the propositions, the stable layer on which every answer is built, because the numbers verified in the sweep are retrievable in the hall precisely to the extent that the structure holding them is calm. This ordering is not superstition. It is the recognition that the examination tests a structure with figures attached, and that preparation, at the end, should mirror the thing tested.

## The verdict for candidates

The verdict of this guide is its title sentence, and it can be stated here with its full weight. Corporate law is the subject in which the text itself is part of the syllabus: the candidate is examined not only on the law but on the law's two-layer structure, its recent replacement, its young and still-forming case doctrine on the insolvency side, and its enforcement character redrawn by decriminalisation. The candidate who prepares it as four regimes on one spine, who learns the small name-set of provisions to the standard of reflex, who holds every threshold as provisional until it is verified against the enacted layer, who stores case law as propositions and cases as their labels, and who works each question by its form before its content, is not merely better prepared than the candidate who memorised more. That candidate is prepared for the subject that actually exists, the one whose numbers move. Divide the remaining effort accordingly: the company statute's structure and name-set first and permanently, the insolvency sequence and its five decisions next and as doctrine, the tribunal's constitutional propositions with them, the partnership statute as a designed contrast, and the whole numeric surface of the subject last, verified, dated, and held lightly enough to change.

One choice remains, and it is made in the hall rather than at the desk: where a paper offers optional questions, the selection is the last exercise of the method. Choose by form before content, because a candidate's marks follow the demands they have rehearsed, and a familiar form on a thinner topic will ordinarily outscore an unfamiliar form on a favourite one; the distinction question in the insolvency territory a candidate has classified a dozen times is a safer instrument than the evaluation question in the company territory the candidate loves but has never argued aloud. Choose also by layer: in the first hour, when accuracy is cheapest, take the questions whose limbs are verified and whose propositions are reflexes, and leave the questions that ask for judgment, the evaluations and the open problems, to the settled middle of the paper, when the hand is warm and the map is in front of the mind rather than being reconstructed under pressure. Read every option before committing to any, because papers in this subject hide their kindest question in their least familiar costume, a doctrinal demand dressed as a problem, a comparison dressed as a definition, and the candidate who commits on the first reading answers the costume and not the question. The method this guide teaches was never only a way of storing the law. It is a way of meeting the law's representatives, its questions, in the order of one's own choosing, and the choosing, done by form and by layer, is the final dividend the structure pays.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How should you study company law for professional examinations?

Study it as four regimes on one spine rather than as a single statute. Build first the map: the Companies Act 2013 for formation, capital and governance, the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code for distress and exit, the tribunal system that adjudicates both, and the Limited Liability Partnership Act as the alternative vehicle. Attach every provision to a stage of a company's life, incorporation, capital, governance, protection, distress, exit, and learn the small set of provisions that function as names to the standard of reflex. Hold every threshold, period and fee as provisional until it has been verified against the enacted text or the rules as notified, and store case law as one-sentence propositions with the case name as the label. Revise by demand type, distinction, proposition, problem, evaluation, because each form has its own method.

### Q: Which Companies Act sections are worth memorising?

The provisions that function as names in professional discourse. Section 135, corporate social responsibility, with its gateway thresholds and its two per cent spending duty. Section 447, the fraud offence that marks the statute's criminal edge. Section 245, the class action by which members and depositors sue together before the tribunal. Section 149, the independent director provision, carrying the definition, the code and the protected tenure. Section 188, the related party transaction control, read with the definition in Section 2(76). Beside those five, hold Sections 241 and 242, the oppression and mismanagement jurisdiction. Each must be known in both directions, number to subject and situation to number. Beyond that set, provisions should be learned by function and retrieved by the stage of the company's life they govern, because the machinery sections carry no name-value and examiners test what they do rather than where they sit.

### Q: How do you keep track of Companies Act amendments?

Track the two layers by their own machinery. Amendments to the Act arrive as amendment Acts, debated in Parliament and collected in the statute's own trail, and they are few enough to log individually with the year and the change each made. Changes to the rules arrive as Gazette notifications from the ministry administering the Act, several times a year, without debate, and these are tracked by keeping a dated verification layer in the revision file: every threshold, period and fee recorded with the source it was read in and the date it was checked. Before an examination, re-verify that layer against the bare Act as amended and the rules as notified, and treat any figure in a textbook or coaching compilation as unverified until that check is done. The habit matters more than any single amendment, because the layer that changes silently is the layer that stale material reproduces.

### Q: Which Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code judgments are most frequently tested?

The decisions around which the Code's doctrine and the preparation built on it concentrate. Innoventive Industries v ICICI Bank, from 2017: default alone triggers admission for a financial creditor. Swiss Ribbons v Union of India, from 2019: the Code is constitutionally valid, including the creditor classification and the Section 29A disqualifications. The Essar Steel decision, also from 2019: the committee of creditors' commercial decision is not reviewed on its merits, and the 330-day outer limit is a norm rather than a guillotine. Pioneer Urban Land and Infrastructure v Union of India, from 2019: homebuyers are financial creditors. Ghanshyam Mishra v Edelweiss, from 2021: an approved plan extinguishes claims left outside it. Mobilox Innovations v Kirusa Koncepts completes the working set on the operational creditor's dispute filter. Learn each as its proposition first and its name second.

### Q: How do you answer a question comparing the Companies Act 1956 and 2013?

Answer it as a classification, not as two descriptions. Open with the old position in one sentence, with its 1956 number where a pair exists, then the new position in one sentence with its 2013 number, then classify the change as one of four kinds: renumbering, redrafting, substantive alteration, or creation without a predecessor. The oppression and mismanagement jurisdiction, Sections 397 and 398 becoming Sections 241 and 242, is the model of substantive continuation in a new house. The class action and corporate social responsibility are creations, and the answer should say so rather than invent a lineage. Only after the changes are classified should evaluation begin, and it should be brief and tied to the classification. Parallel description, old Act in one paragraph and new Act in the next, leaves the examiner to perform the comparison the question asked the candidate to perform, and is marked accordingly.

### Q: Is the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code tested in law examinations?

Yes, and in a register different from the professional papers. Law faculties absorbed the Code into company and commercial law teaching because it rearranged doctrines a law course cannot avoid: the nature of a creditor's right, the effect of a collective process on individual remedies, the constitutional limits of economic legislation. Law papers therefore test the Code as doctrine, through its leading judgments, the validity holdings in Swiss Ribbons, the admission rule in Innoventive, the finality rule in Ghanshyam Mishra, and through evaluation questions on design choices such as the exclusion of defaulting promoters. The candidate should prepare the Code's case law to proposition standard, its provisions by their sequence from initiation to distribution, and its thresholds as provisional figures to be verified, exactly as for the professional papers, while expecting the questions to ask why the Code is built as it is rather than only how a filing runs.

### Q: What is the commonest error in company law answers?

The confusion of the Act with the rules made under it, usually in the form of a threshold quoted from memory that a notification has since moved. The candidate states a monetary limit, a percentage, a time period or a fee with complete confidence, the figure was accurate in the textbook the candidate learned from, and it is wrong on the day of the examination because the operative number lives in delegated legislation that changed without debate. A close relative of the same error is the 1956 citation in a 2013 answer, the predecessor's number surviving in notes taken from older material. Both errors are failures of layer discipline rather than of knowledge, and both are preventable by one habit: learn the structure of every obligation permanently, hold its numeric limbs separately with a verification date, and re-verify the numbers against the enacted text and the notified rules in the final stage of revision.

### Q: How much case law does company law require?

Unevenly, and the unevenness is the answer. The company statute itself requires little: its doctrine settled into the enacted text and the rules over decades, so its papers test provisions, procedures and comparisons, with the tribunalisation decisions, Sampath Kumar, L. Chandra Kumar, the Madras Bar Association case of 2010 and Rojer Mathew, carrying the constitutional questions. The insolvency code requires a great deal, because it is young and its meaning is being made by decision; its five or six leading judgments are doctrine and must be held as propositions. The candidate should therefore budget case law by regime rather than by subject, learning the tribunal line exactly, the Code's decisions to proposition-and-limits standard, and company-law cases only where a name is certain, since a correct proposition without a citation scores and a citation attached to a wrong proposition does not.

### Q: Should a candidate read the bare Act or a commentary first for company law?

Both, in an order that respects what each is for. Begin with guided material, a textbook or lectures, to acquire the map: the four regimes, the spine, the location of the name provisions. Move to the bare Act as soon as the map exists, because the bare Act is the examined object, its arrangement, its provisos and its delegation clauses are themselves examinable, and a candidate who has only read about the statute cannot see where the Act ends and the rules begin. Return to commentary for doctrine, criticism and the working of provisions in decided cases. Treat every commentary statement of a threshold as a claim to be verified in the text, since commentary is printed from the layer that changes silently and ages faster than the doctrine around it. The sequence is map, text, commentary, verification, and reversing the last two steps is how stale figures enter otherwise strong answers.

### Q: How can a candidate check whether a threshold in a coaching note is still correct?

Work from the delegation outward. Find the provision the threshold serves and identify the layer it lives in: if the figure is stated in the Act itself, it changes only by amendment and the Act as amended settles it; if the provision speaks of a prescribed limit, a period as notified, or a figure a proviso lets the government revise, the figure lives in the rules and must be read there. Read the rules as notified in the Gazette or in the ministry's consolidated publication of the Act and rules, and check commencement notifications where a change has been announced, since an announced revision and a commenced revision are different states. The insolvency threshold is the standing warning: Section 4 of the Code carried one lakh rupees in its enacted text and one crore after notification in March 2020. Date the verified figure in the revision file, and re-verify in the final revision stage.

### Q: How much of the Companies Act 1956 does a candidate still need to learn?

The comparison layer, not the statute as a working text. The 1956 Act governs nothing a candidate will be asked to apply as living law, but three parts of it remain examinable. Its provisions are needed wherever a comparison question is set, as the first term of the classification: the candidate must be able to state the old position in one sentence with its number, as with Sections 397 and 398 on oppression and mismanagement. Its case law survives where the 2013 Act re-enacted the substance, because doctrine built under the old sections informs the reading of their successors. Its administrative history matters where the subject's machinery changed under it, as electronic filing and director identification numbers did through the amendment of 2006. Beyond those three uses, time spent on the 1956 text is time taken from the statute that will actually be examined, and the proportion should be small and deliberate.

### Q: Which Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code sections must be known by number?

The initiation-to-distribution sequence. Section 4, the minimum default threshold, known together with the fact that its figure was raised by notification. Section 7 for the financial creditor's application, Section 9 for the operational creditor's, with its demand notice and dispute filter, and Section 10 for the corporate applicant's own. Section 12 for the process timeline and its outer limit. Section 14 for the moratorium that freezes proceedings on admission. Section 29A for the persons disqualified from submitting a resolution plan. Section 31 for the approval that binds all stakeholders to a plan. Section 53 for the distribution waterfall in liquidation, the order against which plan fairness is argued. Known in both directions and in sequence, those numbers recite the whole process in one breath, which is the form in which a problem question asks for them.

### Q: How should a candidate answer a question on the decriminalisation of company law offences?

Build the answer on the sequence and the boundary. State the sequence first: the amendment of 2019 building adjudication of penalties inside the department, the Companies (Amendment) Act 2020 converting procedural and technical defaults across the company statute into monetary penalties, and the Limited Liability Partnership (Amendment) Act 2021 doing the same work for the partnership statute a year later. State the boundary next: what did not move, fraud under Section 447, investigation, and offences involving injury and public interest remained criminal, so the reform distinguished culpable conduct from lapse rather than abandoning enforcement. Then evaluate from both sides in their own terms, the compliance argument that criminal process for a late filing deterred enterprise without protecting anyone, and the deterrence argument that a fee-bearing lapse invites calculation. Close with a verdict that engages the boundary: the reform is defensible exactly to the extent that the fraud edge stays sharp.

### Q: How do the demands of company law differ between law examinations and professional examinations?

They differ in where they put the weight. Law examinations weight doctrine and judgment: the validity of the insolvency code, the constitutional basis of the tribunal, the design of minority protection, questions that reward a proposition, its case and its limits, argued in essay form. Professional examinations for company secretaries and accountants weight operation: the procedure a provision sets running, the approval a transaction needs, the form and period a filing carries, applied to problem facts under time pressure. The professional candidate therefore needs the rules layer in greater working detail and must verify thresholds as a practising habit, while the law candidate needs the case law deeper and the evaluation material stronger. Both need the same map, the same spine and the same name-set. A candidate preparing for one family with the other family's method has usually mastered the material and misjudged the demand, so the first preparation decision is to read the target paper's own past questions by form.

### Q: What should a revision file for corporate law contain, and how should it be organised?

Organise it by the spine and the regimes, not by the textbook's chapter order, and build every entry to be amendable in place. Each obligation gets one entry holding four things: its structure, who owes it, on what trigger, enforced where, with what consequence; its provision number, and its predecessor's number where a pair exists; its numeric limbs, thresholds, percentages, periods and fees; and the date on which those limbs were last verified against the enacted text or the notified rules. Case law gets proposition entries, one sentence per holding with the case name and year as the label and the limit of the holding stated. Previous-year questions are filed by demand, distinction, proposition, problem, evaluation, so the file teaches the forms as well as the content. Kept as a single controlled copy and updated by amendment rather than rewritten each cycle, the file survives the notification churn that makes printed compilations unreliable.

### Q: How should a candidate handle an amendment that arrives after the syllabus cut-off?

Take the cut-off from the candidate's own examining body and from nowhere else, because conventions differ between institutes and no secondary source can state another body's rule. Where the body's convention fixes the law at a date, prepare the fixed position as the answer's foundation. Learn the amendment itself as a change, in the classification this guide uses: state the position the cut-off fixes, state the amended position, and name the kind of change. That preparation is safe under either convention, because an examiner working to a cut-off meets an answer grounded in the fixed position, and an examiner who admits the change meets a candidate who can state it precisely. What must never be done is to answer from the amendment while ignoring the cut-off, or to assert a threshold from the new rules without having verified that the amendment has commenced, since announced changes and commenced changes are different legal states and the gap between them is a known trap in this subject.

### Q: Is it worth learning LLP Act section numbers for professional examinations?

A small set, learned by function, is worth it; an index is not. The partnership statute is short and its papers test design and contrast rather than citation density, but four locations carry name-value. The liability provisions, Sections 27 and 28, which limit the liability of the firm and the partner and state the fraud exception, are the statute's centre and should be known by number. The conversion provisions, Sections 55 to 58 with their schedules, govern the migration of firms and companies into the form and anchor the comparison questions. Designated partners, the compliance answerability at the statute's heart, and the partnership agreement, its constitutional document, should be known by function and found instantly in the text. Beyond that set, describe provisions by what they do. The contrast that earns marks is structural, company against partnership, mandatory governance code against registered contract, and numbers support that contrast rather than replacing it.

### Q: How do you write an examination answer on the constitutional validity of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code?

Structure it as the court structured it in Swiss Ribbons. Open with the challenges as they were pressed: that the classification between financial and operational creditors violated equality, that the disqualification of promoters under Section 29A was punitive and retrospective in effect, and that the process displaced established rights without adequate safeguard. Take each challenge to its holding. The classification survived because the two creditor classes differ intelligibly in the nature of their lending, the terms on which money is given against time, and the evidence of default each can produce. Section 29A survived as a provision directed at the persons whose conduct brought the company to default, keeping the process a resolution mechanism rather than a route back for those who caused the failure. Credit the reasoning where the judgment itself locates it, in deference to economic legislation tested against manifest arbitrariness rather than judicial preference. Close with the limits: validity decided, questions of working and outcome left open.

### Q: How should a candidate use previous-year questions in a subject whose governing text keeps changing?

Use them for form and doctrine, and re-verify them for figures. A previous-year question is reliable evidence of the demands an examining body sets, the distinction, proposition, problem and evaluation forms recur across cycles, and working old questions by form teaches the methods faster than any summary. It is unreliable evidence of figures and sometimes of text: a question set before an amendment may embed the older threshold, the older period or a provision since recast, and model answers circulating with it carry the same date. So work the question, then audit its premises against the enacted text and the notified rules, and note in the revision file which of its limbs have moved. Organised by demand rather than by year, a run of past questions also reveals the standing distinctions and the recurring cases, which is the closest thing to a marking scheme the subject offers. Treat the questions as a map of the examiner's habits and the bare Act as the authority on the law, never the reverse.

### Q: How should a candidate divide revision time between company law and insolvency law?

Divide it by the target paper's own weighting first, and by the two regimes' different decay rates second. Read the syllabus and a run of past papers to fix the proportion the examination itself sets, because a professional paper weighted toward compliance and a law paper weighted toward doctrine demand different splits and no general rule can substitute for that count. Within the split, allocate by kind of work rather than by page count. The company statute takes the larger block of first learning, because it is the bigger text, but its stability means the block is built once and maintained. The insolvency code takes a smaller block of first learning and a recurring block of return visits, because its case law moves and its propositions must be refreshed as larger benches decide. The tribunal's constitutional propositions ride with the insolvency block. The partnership statute takes one compact block, learned as a contrast. Threshold verification across both regimes is a final, separate block, done last and once.
