Teaching the violence of Romeo and Juliet is the point at which the school play stops being a romance with interruptions and becomes what its own Prologue announces: a story in which "civil blood makes civil hands unclean" (Prologue, line 4, Folger Shakespeare Library edition). The play contains six deaths. It opens with a street brawl and a prince threatening to execute the next brawlers he catches. Its most quoted scene of wit ends with a man dying under his friend's arm and cursing both houses. Its hero kills three times over in effect, once in a duel, once at a tomb, and once by his own hand, and its heroine dies by hers. A teacher who treats this material as decoration around a love story has misread the proportions of the text, and a teacher who treats it as a thrill to be marketed to reluctant readers has misread its direction. The verdict of this article is that the play is its own best safeguard. Taught whole and in sequence, its violence arrives already framed by the consequences the text attaches to it: a peacemaker's intervention, a ruler's sentence, a dying man's curse, an official inquiry, an exile, a reckoning in which two fathers compete in grief over the bodies. Glamour is not a property of the play. Glamour is a property of the excerpt. It enters the classroom when a lesson, a film clip, or a worksheet lifts a fight out of the reckoning that follows it and lets the swordplay stand alone as the exciting part. The rule that follows from the text is therefore a rule of sequence and completeness rather than of censorship or celebration: teach no duel without its audit, stage no fight without staging what the fight costs, and let the play's own accounting set the terms on which its violence is exciting.

That rule sounds simple and is not, because every pressure on the unit pushes the other way. The fights are the scenes restless classes ask for. The duel in Act 3 Scene 1 is the moment the plot turns, and turning points invite emphasis. Screen versions compress the play toward its fights, since fights photograph better than audits. Anthologies and study aids reduce scenes to their outcomes, and the outcome of a fight is a body, which reads as excitement in summary whatever it meant in sequence. One way to feel the safeguard's strength is to notice how saturated the play is with legal process. Romeo and Juliet contains a prince's edict and its stated penalty, a formal challenge delivered by letter, a hearing with witness testimony and a prosecution speech, a sentence of exile pronounced with reasons, a second inquiry in the final scene with a confession, documentary evidence in the form of a letter, findings of fact, and a closing distribution of punishment. No other Shakespeare tragedy is built so completely out of the machinery of judgement, and the machinery is not decoration. It is the play's answer to the feud, present from the first scene's edict to the last scene's audit, and it means that a teacher never has to import a moralising frame from outside the text. The frame is the text's own second plot. Verona's young men live inside the code; Verona itself lives inside the law; and the tragedy is the story of the code repeatedly outrunning the law until the law, at the end, counts what the delay has cost. Teaching the violence with the process attached is therefore not an act of pedagogical caution laid over an exciting play. It is simply teaching both of the play's plots instead of one.

Against those pressures the teacher holds one asset no excerpt can match: the whole text, in order, with its consequences attached at full length. The play spends more lines on the aftermath of its central killing than on the killing. It gives the last word on violence not to a fighter but to a magistrate counting the cost, and its final image of the feud is not a sword but a handshake and two statues. Teaching the violence without glamour means, in practice, refusing to let the class leave any fight before the play itself has finished with it.

One boundary governs everything below. The deaths in this play include two self-killings, and they are handled here the way the framework requires: soberly, at the level of the play's framing and the classroom's care, with no attention to method beyond the bare facts the text itself states, and with the contagion scholarship that a sibling study in this series has assembled left standing at passage rather than re-argued. What a class needs from the tomb scene is the play's verdict on the feud, delivered over the bodies by the Prince. What it does not need is any lingering on the mechanics by which the lovers die. The distinction is the play's own: Shakespeare gives the tomb scene's length to grief, discovery, and judgement, and gives the deaths themselves a few lines each. A lesson that follows the text's proportions has already solved most of its problem.

## Six Deaths, Counted in Order: The Cost the Play Keeps Presenting

The first discipline the framework imposes is a count. Six people die in Romeo and Juliet: Mercutio, Tybalt, Paris, Romeo, Juliet, and Lady Montague, whose death is reported in the final scene by her husband: "Grief of my son's exile hath stopped her breath" (5.3). The count matters because teaching habitually shrinks it. The lovers' deaths dominate the posters; Mercutio and Tybalt are remembered as plot machinery; Paris is forgotten so regularly that his absence from student essays is a standing joke of the staffroom; Lady Montague, who dies of the feud without ever drawing a weapon or speaking a line in anger, vanishes almost entirely. A unit on the play's violence that begins by counting all six, and by asking what killed each of them, has already corrected the glamour distortion at its root, because glamour depends on selective memory. The feud's cost, counted whole, includes a wit, a duellist, a suitor, two children, and a mother who died of grief offstage while the men were still talking about honour.

The distribution of the deaths carries the lesson further. Only two of the six die in anything resembling a fair fight, and neither of those fights is fair for long. Mercutio dies under Romeo's intervening arm, stabbed by a man aiming at someone else, in a brawl he entered as a joke. Tybalt dies in a revenge killing minutes later, by an opponent who had refused him courteously in the same scene. Paris dies at a tomb, in the dark, against a man he believes to be a vandal, in a fight the text barely stages before it ends. The two self-killings close the sequence, and Lady Montague's reported death reframes all of them: the feud kills by grief as readily as by steel, and it kills the generation that started it as surely as the generation that inherited it. Presented in this order, as a ledger rather than a highlight reel, the violence of the play reads the way the Prince reads it in the final scene, as a scourge laid on the houses' hate. The word the play keeps returning to is not glory but waste: of wit, of youth, of a city's peace, of two families' futures.

Mercutio's death is the fulcrum, and the play marks it with the most famous anti-glamour device in the canon. The dying man does not praise the code that killed him or forgive the friend whose body screened the blade. He curses the system entire: "A plague o' both your houses!" (3.1), a sentence he repeats as the wound wins, converting his death into an indictment of the feud rather than a ornament of it. He dies, moreover, still joking, and the joke is the mechanism by which the audience is made to feel the price: the play's chief source of verbal pleasure is withdrawn mid-sentence, and the laughter in the theatre stops with a completeness that no sermon could achieve. A classroom that has enjoyed Mercutio for two acts experiences his death as a loss of something the class itself valued. That experience, engineered by the text, is the opposite of glamour. Glamour would require the death to enlarge him. The text instead diminishes the world by his absence and sends his friends out under a curse.

Tybalt receives the opposite treatment, and the pair of treatments together make a lesson. The play gives its most accomplished fighter no elegy at all. Lady Capulet's grief in the inquiry scene is real, but its grammar is prosecutorial: she arrives at the hearing demanding a life for a life, and her nephew's death enters the record as a claim to be settled rather than a loss to be mourned. Nobody in Verona praises Tybalt's art once he is dead, and the code he served with such fidelity has no ceremony for him, because the code's accounting counts honour and standing, not persons, and a dead duellist is an instrument already spent. Students who admired Tybalt's discipline in the feast scene meet its reward in the inquiry scene, where his own household converts him into evidence within minutes of his death. The contrast with Mercutio is the teaching point. The wit is mourned by the structure of the play itself, in the curse and in the tragedy's change of weather; the swordsman is processed. A class that sets the two exits side by side has learned to read the code's promises against the code's payouts, which is the analytical habit the whole unit exists to build.

The count has one more discipline to teach, which is that it must be built before any fight is studied. A class that begins the unit by assembling the ledger of six, name by name, with the instrument and the circumstance of each death recorded, has installed the destination before the journey, and every subsequent fight is read in the knowledge of where the feud's road ends. The exercise costs half a lesson and repays it at every stage, because it converts each later scene into evidence for a conclusion the students have already drawn in provisional form. It also inoculates against the poster tradition's arithmetic, in which two photogenic deaths stand for the whole. Six is the play's number, and the unit that keeps six in view from the first week has made glamour arithmetically difficult, since glamour's first move is always to forget the deaths that do not decorate the story.

### What does Mercutio's curse accomplish that a lament for him could not?

A lament would honour Mercutio and leave the feud untouched, turning his death into a noble loss the honour code knows how to absorb. The curse refuses absorption. It names both houses as author of his death, denies either side the comfort of mourning him as a partisan, and attaches his authority to a judgement the play then executes.

The final scene completes the accounting the curse begins. The Prince conducts what amounts to a public inquiry, hears the Friar's confession and the letter's evidence, and delivers the play's official verdict: "See what a scourge is laid upon your hate, / That heaven finds means to kill your joys with love" (5.3), closing with the flattest summary in Shakespeare, "All are punished" (5.3). The fathers' reconciliation is real and it is worthless as compensation, a handshake over five bodies, sealed with competing promises of golden statues. Shakespeare could have ended the play on the lovers. He ended it on an audit and a building project, on authority totalling the bill and on wealth converting guilt into monuments. A teacher who keeps the class in the room for that ending has taught the violence in the frame the playwright built for it, and a teacher who ends the unit at the tomb, on the romance, has excerpted the play at exactly the point where its own argument begins.

## Honour at Street Level: Insult, the Wall, and an Imported Fashion

The play's violence begins at the bottom of the social scale and in the pettiest register imaginable, and the lesson on honour should begin there too. The first fight is started by servants, over a bitten thumb, in a quarrel Gregory states with perfect clarity: "The quarrel is between our masters and us their men" (1.1). Nobody in the opening scene can name the feud's cause, because the play never supplies one; what the scene supplies instead is a machinery of insult in which manhood is measured by refusal to be displaced. Sampson's boasting fixes the scene's geography of status in a single repeated image, the wall: the weaker party is thrust to the wall, and to be a man is to hold the wall against all comers. Robert Appelbaum's essay "'Standing to the Wall': The Pressures of Masculinity in Romeo and Juliet" (Shakespeare Quarterly, 1997) takes that image as its title and its thesis. Masculinity in the play, Appelbaum argues, is not an inner quality the young men express but a set of rules prefixed in advance, a posture a man must assume in public whether or not he believes in it, so that the street's code operates on servants and gentlemen alike before any of them has chosen anything. The brawl is therefore not an eruption of feeling. It is a performance of compliance, staged for an audience of peers, and its pettiness is the point: the smaller the provocation, the clearer it becomes that the script, not the insult, is doing the work.

The opening scene contains a third party to the feud that lessons often skip, and the framework restores it to view: the citizens of Verona. The brawl draws them into the street with clubs, and their cry as they come is the play's first verdict on the honour system, "Down with the Capulets! Down with the Montagues!" (1.1). The city that hosts the code has already priced it. The citizens are neither house's men; they are the shopkeepers and householders whose peace the brawls keep breaking, and their intervention reframes the spectacle before any critic touches it: what reads from inside the code as standing and courage reads from the street as a public nuisance imposed by two families on everyone else. The classroom use of the citizens is to give students a standpoint inside the fiction that is neither Montague nor Capulet nor the law. Discussion of any fight can be run from their doorways: who pays for this quarrel in broken mornings and closed shops, and what standing does the code's currency buy among the people forced to accept it. The play plants that standpoint in its first hundred lines, and the Prince's edict, arriving moments later, is the state ratifying what the street has already shouted. Honour's glamour depends on choosing one's vantage point inside the houses. From the citizens' corner of the square, there was never any glamour to begin with.

That reading gives the classroom its first usable distinction, between violence as expression and violence as assignment. Verona's young men do not fight because they are angry; they become angry because a fight has been assigned to them by the positions they occupy, as Montague men, as Capulet men, as men who cannot be seen to step aside. The history of the duel supports the distinction at documentary strength. Markku Peltonen's The Duel in Early Modern England: Civility, Politeness and Honour (Cambridge University Press, 2003) demonstrates that the duel of honour was not a survival from a rougher medieval world but a recent import, a Renaissance fashion carried into England from Italy and France together with the courtesy literature that theorised it, and wrapped from the start in a paradox: its defenders claimed that readiness to fight was what made men polite, since a gentleman who might be called out would mind his manners. The same decades produced the counter-attack, a Jacobean anti-duelling campaign in print and proclamation that severed duelling from civility and treated the duel as murder dressed in etiquette. The play's first audiences, in other words, watched Verona's street code with a live controversy in their ears. The duel was not their immemorial custom. It was a foreign fashion with a sales pitch, and Shakespeare's text handles it with the suspicion the pamphleteers taught: Mercutio's catalogue of Tybalt's fencing vocabulary, "the immortal passado! the punto reverso! the hai!" (2.4), is a Londoner laughing at an imported technique's jargon, the duelling master's terms quoted the way a schoolboy quotes a pretentious older brother.

The print quarrel behind Peltonen's record is concrete enough to put in students' hands. Vincentio Saviolo, an Italian fencing master teaching in London, published his practice of the rapier together with a treatise on honour and honourable quarrels in 1595, giving the imported code its most systematic English statement: a taxonomy of insults, of the given lie in its several grades, and of the responses each grade obliged. George Silver's Paradoxes of Defence, printed four years later, attacked the foreign masters and their rapier teaching as a mortal danger to Englishmen, defending the older English weapons against the fashionable import. Between Saviolo's system and Silver's resistance, the first audiences of this play had a controversy in pamphlet form to set beside the fiction: the duel as a technology with advocates, vendors, and critics. Mercutio's mockery of Tybalt's duelling vocabulary is the play taking Silver's side of the argument for a laugh, which means the laughter of the first performances was already an act of criticism. The classroom inherits that position ready-made. Students do not have to be argued out of reverencing the duel by a teacher's disapproval; they can be shown the period arguing with itself, and invited to notice which side of the argument the play's deaths support.

The classroom value of Peltonen's record is that it kills the romance of tradition. Students inclined to excuse the feud as simply how things were done in a more honourable age meet a documentary record in which the age itself was arguing about the practice, prosecuting it, and mocking its vocabulary. Tybalt, the play's purest duellist, is also its most formal correspondent: his challenge to Romeo travels by letter, through the correct channels, a piece of etiquette the text mentions with dry precision. Honour in Verona is paperwork and choreography before it is passion. Teaching the duel through its history converts it from an exciting survival into a case study in how fashions in violence are manufactured, marketed by fencing masters and courtesy books, and enforced by the fear of seeming afraid. That conversion is the single most reliable anti-glamour instrument the unit possesses, because it gives students a way to respect the play's intelligence about the code while declining the code's invitation entirely.

The challenge letter repays a closer look, since it is the code's etiquette caught in the act of writing itself down. Benvolio reports the fact with a clerk's flatness: Tybalt has sent a letter to Romeo's father's house, and Mercutio supplies the interpretation, a challenge, for certain (2.4). In the duelling culture Peltonen documents, the written cartel was a formal instrument with rules of its own, a stage in an escalation that contemporaries understood as a procedure, almost a litigation, conducted in the language of honour. Shakespeare's staging of the fact is dry to the point of comedy, and Mercutio's riff on the challenger, the Prince of Cats with his nine lives and his imported fencing terms, reduces the procedure's author to an animal of reflex and claws. The classroom point is that the feud's deadliest instrument in Act 3 is not a sword but a piece of stationery: a form correctly filled in, delivered to the correct address, obliging its recipient under rules both parties' households recognise. Students who grasp the letter grasp the unit's thesis in miniature. Verona's violence is bureaucratic before it is passionate, which is why it can be audited, and why the play keeps answering it with rival paperwork, edicts, reports, confessions, and a letter of explanation that arrives, catastrophically, one quarantine too late.

### Why does the play give its first sword fight to servants rather than to gentlemen?

Because the code's reach is the subject. A feud that can set two liveried servants fighting over a gesture neither understands has already demonstrated that honour in Verona is not a gentleman's philosophy but a household infection, transmitted downward through livery and loyalty until men are bleeding for a quarrel their own spokesman admits belongs to their masters.

The feast scene of Act 1 supplies the counterweight the unit needs before it reaches the duel, because it shows the code's brakes working in the person least expected to apply them. Tybalt, hearing Romeo's voice at the Capulet feast, moves to fetch his rapier, and his uncle stops him cold: the intruder shall be endured, the feast's peace kept, the old man's authority asserted over the young man's honour in terms that leave no appeal. Capulet's reasons are not pacifist. He protects the reputation of his hospitality and the order of his house, which is to say he defends one part of the honour system against another, and the scene's drama is the spectacle of the code restraining itself for the code's own sake. Tybalt withdraws with a stored threat, sweetness converted to gall, and the audience has watched the feud's senior generation demonstrate the skill its juniors never learn: an insult can be swallowed when swallowing it serves the house. The lesson for the violence unit is structural. The play does not oppose honour to peace as abstractions; it shows honour containing both the duel and the restraint, and men choosing between its instruments. When Tybalt later presents his challenge and Romeo refuses it, the class has already seen a third possibility enacted by the feud's own patriarch, and the tragedy sharpens accordingly: the brakes existed, and the young men declined to use them.

Benvolio's role in the opening scene completes the frame the lesson needs. He enters the brawl as its peacemaker, sword drawn to part the fighters, and his line became, centuries later, the name of the most fully documented school programme built on this play: "Part, fools! / Put up your swords. You know not what you do" (1.1). The text positions its own anti-violence spokesman inside the first minute of fighting, sword in hand, using the instruments of the code against the code. Even the Prince's sentence for the brawl is part of the frame: "If ever you disturb our streets again, / Your lives shall pay the forfeit of the peace" (1.1). The play opens, that is, with the law's price list for street violence read aloud in advance, so that every later fight occurs under a sentence the audience has already heard. A unit that teaches Act 1 whole has installed the entire interpretive apparatus before the first drop of blood: insult as assignment, honour as fashion, peacemaking as the text's own position, and the death penalty for brawling announced by the city's ruler. The violence that follows is never unframed for a class that starts at the start. A fuller critical account of how Verona's masculinity organises this machinery is given in the series study of [masculinity and violence in the play](/2024/02/16/masculinity-and-violence-analysis/), whose ground this lesson applies rather than re-surveys.

## Act 3 Scene 1: The Duel That Turns a Comedy into a Killing

The central fight repays the slowest reading the unit can give it, because the scene is constructed as a sequence of refusals, each of which the code converts into a reason to proceed. Benvolio opens it as the voice of risk assessment: the day is hot, the Capulets are abroad, a meeting will not escape a brawl (3.1). Mercutio answers with his tavern speech, a comic disquisition on the friend who quarrels over a nut, a cough, a beard's trimming, delivered while the danger he describes walks toward him. Tybalt arrives with the etiquette of the formal challenge and addresses Romeo through it. Romeo, an hour married and newly Tybalt's kinsman, refuses with a courtesy so complete that the code can only read it as shame: he protests he never injured Tybalt, and loves him better than Tybalt can devise (3.1). Mercutio supplies the code's verdict on the refusal, "O calm, dishonourable, vile submission!" (3.1), and draws in his friend's place. Within a page the joke has become a wound, the wound a curse, and the curse a second killing, as Romeo, his peacemaking turned fatal, pronounces his own diagnosis: "O sweet Juliet, / Thy beauty hath made me effeminate / And in my temper softened valor's steel!" (3.1). The scene's anatomy is a machine for demonstrating how a culture processes a refusal to fight into two corpses, and a scene-by-scene study of its construction is available in the series account of [Act 3 Scene 1, the duel that breaks the play](/2023/09/08/act-3-scene-1-mercutio-tybalt-duel/).

The tavern speech rewards a full stop in the lesson, because it is the code's psychology stated as comedy. Mercutio's catalogue of quarrels, with a man for coughing in the street and waking a dog, over the trimming of a beard, over hazel eyes and cracked nuts and the creaking of a shoe, is a diagnostic instrument disguised as a tease: the duellist of honour does not wait for provocation but manufactures it, since the posture requires regular exercise to remain credible. Mercutio addresses the speech to Benvolio, the play's peacemaker, and accuses him of being as quarrelsome a Jack as any in Italy, which is the joke's serious payload. If even Benvolio can be fitted with a duelling temperament by a sufficiently attentive friend, then temperament is not the variable the lesson should track. The variable is the script, which can find a quarrel in any material, including a cough. A class that has laughed through the catalogue and then watched Tybalt enter on its last line has felt the mechanism engage: the speech describes the machine, and the machine arrives on cue to demonstrate itself. Shakespeare times the entrance like a proof.

Susan Snyder's essay "Romeo and Juliet: Comedy into Tragedy" (Essays in Criticism, 1970) gives the teacher the structural fact that organises the whole unit. The play, Snyder argues, does not begin as a tragedy but becomes one: its first half runs on comedy's machinery of wit, disguise, and erotic pursuit, and the reversal at its centre is so radical that it amounts to a change of genre. Mercutio's death is the hinge on which the genres swing: "in Mercutio's sudden, violent end, Shakespeare makes the birth of a tragedy coincide[s] exactly with the symbolic death of comedy." The pedagogical consequence is precise. The laughter of the first two acts is not decoration the duel interrupts; it is the price the play has charged in advance, so that the audience's own pleasure becomes the measure of the loss. A unit that hurries students to the duel to reach the exciting part has destroyed the mechanism, since the duel's weight is manufactured out of everything the class has laughed at since the first scene. Snyder's reading converts sequencing from a timetable preference into an interpretive necessity: the comedy must be taught at full value, enjoyed without apology, because its destruction is the lesson.

Harley Granville-Barker, in his Preface to the play, made the companion observation from the character's side of the same hinge: Mercutio's death precipitates the change in Romeo, who enters the scene as the comedy's lover, all lyric patience and refusal, and leaves it as the tragedy's revenger, fluent in the code he had declined an hour before. The two observations describe one mechanism from two vantage points. Snyder watches the genre turn; Granville-Barker watches the hero turned by it; both fix the turn at the same corpse. For the classroom the conjunction is clarifying, because it tells the teacher exactly what the duel must not be allowed to become. If the scene is excerpted as a fight, students see two deaths and a chase. If it is taught as the hinge both critics describe, students see a world change its laws at the moment a blade lands: comedy's rules, in which wit settles everything and nobody bleeds, are repealed in public, and tragedy's rules, in which every act is audited and every debt collected, take their place. The violence is the instrument of the repeal. That is why the scene feels momentous in sequence and merely exciting in excerpt, and why the framework treats the difference as the whole subject of the lesson.

Coppélia Kahn's "Coming of Age in Verona" (Modern Language Studies, 1977-78) supplies the second half of the scene's meaning, and her disagreement with Appelbaum is the critical argument the unit should stage openly. Where Appelbaum sees a regime of rules prefixed in advance, trapping every man in the posture of the wall, Kahn reads the duel as a rite of passage scripted by the patriarchal order: in Verona, a male comes of age by separating from the household of his birth and proving himself within the fraternity of men, and the proof the fraternity recognises is the willingness to fight for the name. Romeo's refusal is, on this reading, a refusal of manhood itself as Verona defines it, and Mercutio's taunt of effeminacy tells the truth about the stakes. The killing of Tybalt then registers as Romeo's forced initiation: the order accepts him as a man at the exact moment the acceptance destroys him, and the Friar's later lecture, with its taunts about womanish tears, confirms that the whole city grades his grief by the same code. Kahn's Romeo does not stumble into violence. He is graduated into it, with honours, by the only curriculum Verona's men respect.

### Why does Benvolio's account of the fight leave Lady Capulet unconvinced?

Because his narrative, the play's nearest thing to an official report, quietly rearranges the sequence to protect his friend, passing lightly over the interval in which Romeo's intervention placed him between the blades. Lady Capulet names the distortion from the opposite interest, charging that affection makes him false, and the Prince must sentence without a clean record from anyone.

The two critics divide the scene between them, and the adjudication matters for what a teacher does on Monday. Appelbaum wins the street: the opening brawl, the thumb, the wall, the servants bleeding for a quarrel that is not theirs, all behave exactly as his prefixed-rules model predicts, and no rite of passage is needed to explain a servant's compliance. Kahn wins the aftermath: the killing of Tybalt changes Romeo's status in every eye that matters, his own included, in precisely the way an initiation changes status, and Appelbaum's posture model cannot explain why the Friar, the Prince, and Romeo himself all treat the post-duel Romeo as a different category of person. The classroom conclusion is that the script has two gears. Pressure, Appelbaum's gear, starts the fight; passage, Kahn's gear, makes it irreversible, because a community that confers manhood through violence has no ceremony for handing the status back. A student who can operate both gears can explain why Mercutio's death could not be absorbed as an accident: the code needed it to mean something, and the meaning it chose was Romeo's manhood, purchased at the price of everything else he had. The contrast between the two young men the code processes in this scene, the one who refuses and the one who demands, is drawn out in the companion study of [Romeo and Tybalt as two models of Verona manhood](/2023/03/31/romeo-vs-tybalt-masculinity/).

The scene's second half deserves as much classroom time as the fight itself, since it is the play's first audit and the model for the framework's core rule. Benvolio narrates; Lady Capulet prosecutes; the Prince cross-examines the air and sentences: Romeo is exiled, and the closing doctrine is a sentence against leniency itself, "Mercy but murders, pardoning those that kill" (3.1). The audit is imperfect, partisan, and conducted over a body, and it is still the scene the excerpting habit deletes. A fight taught without its inquiry teaches students that violence is an event. The play insists it is a process, with a report, a hearing, a sentence, and a doctrine, and the insistence is where its realism lives.

One detail of the sentencing deserves its own minute in class, because it governs how the final scene's audit should be read. The Prince does not sentence as a detached magistrate. Mercutio was his kinsman, and he says so in the sentence itself: "My blood for your rude brawls doth lie a-bleeding" (3.1). The law's voice in Verona is a bereaved voice, and the doctrine against pardoning killers is pronounced by a man counting his own dead. The point cuts in two directions, and students should be given both. It strengthens the sentence's moral weight, since the ruler who lost kin to the feud still stops at exile where the code demanded a life. It also marks the sentence's limit, since a fine and an exile imposed by an interested prince leave the feud's structure intact, and the play's remaining deaths are the interest on that leniency. The framework's interest in the detail is practical: the audit scenes repay being taught as human proceedings, with interests and griefs in the room, rather than as verdicts handed down by the plot. A class that notices the Prince's stake in Act 3 is prepared for the Prince's despair in Act 5, where the audit widens to include the auditor, and his own failure to enforce the first scene's sentence is entered in the record against him.

## On Their Feet: Active Methods and the Governance of Energy

The strongest tradition in Shakespeare pedagogy holds that scenes like this one are learned in the body or not learned at all. Rex Gibson's Teaching Shakespeare (Cambridge University Press, 1998), the handbook of the Cambridge School Shakespeare project he founded, states the principle on which the active approach rests: the plays are scripts, "to be played with, explored, actively and imaginatively brought to life by acting out," and a classroom that keeps students seated before a fight scene has denied them the very evidence the scene contains, the felt difference between a refusal spoken from a chair and a refusal maintained while a friend draws a sword beside one's ear. Gibson's authority in British and Commonwealth classrooms is difficult to overstate; the series he edited runs to thirty-one titles with sales past a million, and the Globe's head of learning, Fiona Banks, has called him the father of the active approach. For the violence unit, his case is at its strongest exactly where the risk is highest. A class that has physically walked the geometry of Act 3 Scene 1, who stood where Romeo stands when Mercutio steps past him, possesses a knowledge of the scene's trap that no amount of annotation delivers. The intervention that kills Mercutio is a spatial fact before it is a moral one.

Ayanna Thompson and Laura Turchi's Teaching Shakespeare with Purpose (2016) supplies the necessary counterweight, and the disagreement between the two positions is genuine rather than theatrical. Their charge against unexamined active method is that performance is not innocent: students asked to inhabit a scene rehearse its values with their bodies, and a class that stages the duel as a romp has practised the honour code's pleasures without ever being asked to inspect them. Energy is not understanding, and the louder the lesson, the more completely the code's script can hide inside the fun of performing it. The adjudication this article reaches is that Gibson wins on method and Thompson and Turchi win on governance, and that the two victories are compatible in a single sequence. The fight is staged, because Gibson is right that the scene's knowledge is physical. It is staged slowly, framed before and after by the text's own audit materials, because Thompson and Turchi are right that an unframed staging teaches the code instead of the play. The practical consequences of that settlement run through the Surrey programme examined below, whose design was built, apparently independently, on exactly this verdict: bodies first, but bodies inside a structure of consequence. The broader craft of teaching this play to the age group that receives it is treated in the series guide to [teaching Romeo and Juliet to teenagers](/2030/01/25/teaching-romeo-and-juliet-to-teens/), whose methods this article's framework assumes.

The adjudication has a concrete protocol, and it is worth stating as practice rather than as principle. The class stages the street scene in three passes. The first pass is Gibson's: the scene on its feet, at walking pace, for the geometry alone, who stands where, who can see what, where the intervention must physically happen for Mercutio to die under Romeo's arm. The second pass freezes at the decision points, the arrival, the refusal, the taunt, the draw, the intervention, and at each freeze the students speak the frozen character's calculation aloud, the drama classroom's thought-tracking turned to the code's accountancy: what does the posture require at this second, and what would it cost to decline it. The third pass replays the scene with a single variable changed, Mercutio stepping back, Romeo stepping aside, Benvolio arriving a breath earlier, and the class audits the replay against the text: which changes the play's world permits, and which it forecloses by everything established before. The protocol is Thompson and Turchi's governance made operational. Performance generates the energy; the freezes and the replay subject the energy to inspection while it is still warm; and the audit of the replay returns the class to the play's own audit, Benvolio's report and the Prince's sentence, with the felt knowledge of how narrow the exits were. Gibson directed this tradition's institutional form as well as its theory: the Shakespeare and Schools project he led from 1986 put the active approach into the hands of the teachers who would carry it into ordinary classrooms, and its longevity is evidence that the method survives contact with the timetable. What the protocol adds to Gibson is the discipline his celebrants sometimes skip, and what it takes from his critics is the admission that energy unexamined is the code's favourite hiding place.

### Why must a classroom fight be rehearsed at the speed of trust rather than the speed of anger?

Because the educational content of the exercise is the difference between the two speeds. Anger in Verona is instantaneous and unrepeatable; it happens once, at full pace, and somebody dies. Choreography is the same movements made deliberate, negotiated, and repeatable without harm, so the rehearsal itself demonstrates the deliberation the characters never perform.

The cheering problem belongs here, because it is the moment the governance question stops being theoretical. Students cheer fights. They cheer them in corridors and they cheer them in classrooms, and a teacher staging Act 3 Scene 1 will hear the cheer at the moment of contact unless the lesson's structure has made the cheer expensive. The play offers the instrument: Mercutio dies joking, and then goes on speaking, and the speech runs long enough for any cheer to die in the throat of the student who made it. A teacher who holds the scene open through the whole curse, who lets the silence after "worms' meat" (3.1) sit in the room before anyone moves, has used the text's own timing to convert appetite into attention. The cheer is not a disciplinary failure to be punished. It is the code's script operating in the classroom exactly as Appelbaum describes it operating in the street, and the lesson's finest moment is the one in which the class catches itself performing Verona.

The deeper point about the cheer is that it identifies the audience as the code's engine, in the classroom as in the piazza. Appelbaum's model requires witnesses: the wall is held because someone is watching it held, and the posture pays only in the currency of observed standing. A class performing the scene is therefore never merely representing Verona's audience problem; it is reproducing it, live, in miniature, with the same rewards on offer for the same performances. The teacher's advantage is that the reproduction can be examined while it happens, as no street crowd's behaviour can. The freeze techniques of the protocol above exist for exactly this: to stop the room at the second the reward is paid, the laugh, the cheer, the intake of breath at the draw, and to ask the room what it has just paid for. Students who have been caught mid-payment, and have laughed at being caught, carry a piece of self-knowledge out of the unit that transfers far beyond Shakespeare. They have watched their own applause doing the feud's work, and they have the play's word for the mechanism. Few lessons in any subject offer so clean an experiment on so available a subject.

## The Craft of the Fake Fight: What the Fight Directors Know

Beneath the pedagogy sits a profession whose entire existence refutes glamour, and its literature deserves a place in the unit's preparation. Stage combat, as its practitioners codified it in the later twentieth century, is the art of manufacturing the appearance of violence out of its opposites: distance, timing, eye contact, and consent between partners. The Society of American Fight Directors was founded in 1977 under David Boushey to professionalise that art and to certify its teachers. J. Allen Suddeth's Fight Directing for the Theatre (1996) and Dale Anthony Girard's Actors on Guard (1996), the standard manuals of the discipline, are unambiguous about its first principle: the fight is an illusion whose safety is engineered, and whose storytelling value depends on the audience believing in a contest that the performers are in fact conducting as a cooperation. Girard's history of the craft complains that fights staged by fencing coaches without theatrical training were incoherent compilations of sporting moves, exciting to nobody and legible as nothing. The professional standard inverts the classroom's temptation. A good stage fight is not the most athletic passage in the show. It is the most controlled, the one in which every apparent spontaneity has been counted, cued, and slowed to the tempo at which partners can protect each other.

The British tradition of the craft makes the same point from the classroom's side of the footlights. William Hobbs, whose Techniques of the Stage Fight appeared in 1967, a decade before the American society's founding, arranged the fights for the 1968 Zeffirelli film through which generations of school students have met this play, and his standard was legibility: a fight, in his practice, is a passage of storytelling in which each exchange must read as an intention and its answer, or the audience is watching noise at speed. The manuals agree on the mechanics that produce the legibility, and two of them transfer to teaching without alteration. The first is the knap, the sound of a blow manufactured by the receiving partner, striking a palm or a padded surface at the moment of apparent contact, so that the violence the audience hears is produced by the person the violence pretends to injure. The second is the discipline of the offer: an attack is never launched at a partner but presented to a partner, along a line and at a distance the receiver has already accepted, so that the scene's danger exists only in the audience's perception. A teacher does not need to drill either technique to use what they prove. They are exhibits. The most convincing stage violence ever filmed was manufactured out of the victim's cooperation and a sound effect made by the victim's own hands, and a class that knows the fact possesses a permanent instrument of disenchantment, applicable to every screen fight it will ever be shown.

### Who controls a stage fight, the fighter who strikes or the partner who receives?

The partner who receives. In the discipline's standard practice, the apparent victim sets the timing, offers the target, and signals readiness, while the apparent aggressor follows the cues the receiver gives. Choreographed violence runs on the authority of the person it pretends to endanger, which is why its rehearsal teaches consent as a technique rather than as a slogan.

That inversion is the deepest instructional asset the violence unit owns, and it should be made explicit to students rather than left as a rehearsal-room secret. Verona's violence is everything stage combat is not: unconsented, uncued, irreversible, and driven by the aggressor's reading of the moment. The classroom's violence, properly taught, is a demonstration of an alternative physics of conflict, in which the endangered party holds the authority, speed is a decision rather than a passion, and the whole encounter can be stopped by either participant at any point. A teacher assembling the unit's evidence, the edition's text, the fight manuals' principles, the honour historians' record, and the evaluated programme reports discussed below, is keeping a research file of exactly the kind that a local-first archive such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/) exists to hold together, and the file's organisation is part of the lesson's integrity: claims about violence, like the fights themselves, should be checkable by the person asked to trust them. The professional literature also supplies the unit's vocabulary of refusal. Fight directors speak of the fight call, the slowed rehearsal run before every performance at which the choreography is walked at reduced speed to confirm that bodies and tempers are fit for it. A classroom equivalent, in which the class re-walks its staging at half speed before any full run and names anything that feels unsafe, imports the profession's safety culture into the schoolroom without importing its weapons.

## Put Up Your Swords: A Documented Programme Puts Romeo on Trial

The strongest evidence that the framework's verdict describes real classroom physics comes from a programme built outside the academy altogether. Put Up Your Swords, created by the Guildford Shakespeare Company in 2024 alongside its production of the play, takes its name from Benvolio's intervention in the first brawl and its structure from the play's own sequence of fight and audit. The company developed the programme after a young person was stabbed in 2023 in a park near its offices, an origin the company states plainly in its own accounts: the programme was born from a local wound, not from a curriculum strategy. It runs for eight weeks with Year 9 pupils, the age group that receives the play in English schools, in weekly sessions, and it is delivered with partners whose presence announces its seriousness about consequences: the Office of the Police and Crime Commissioner for Surrey, the University of Surrey, Fearless, the youth service of the charity Crimestoppers, and charities founded in memory of young victims, among them the Kiyan Prince Foundation and Charlie's Promise. In its first run it reached six Surrey schools and, by the company's count, more than two thousand pupils.

The design is the framework's verdict translated into a timetable. Pupils work through the play's text and through professional stage combat sessions, learning the fights as the fight directors' discipline teaches them, as engineered illusion. They hear speakers with lived experience of knife violence. They examine a mocked-up crime scene. Then the programme performs its decisive act of sequencing: the pupils' own staging of the play follows Shakespeare's script up to the street fight of Act 3, and at the point of the killing the fiction is arrested. The street is cordoned. Investigators work the scene. And the unit's climax is not a performance of the tomb but a trial, held at the University of Surrey's law school, in which Romeo is prosecuted for murder and the pupils must argue, from the text they have studied and the law they have been taught, what his act was and what it deserves. The programme, in short, refuses to let its students leave the duel before the audit, and it builds the audit out of institutions, police, university, courtroom, that carry a weight no worksheet can imitate.

The pilot's mechanics, as reported, deserve recording because they show the verdict applied at the level of the timetable rather than the slogan. Sessions ran weekly at ninety minutes in the first six schools. The pupils' reworking of the play included an exercise the reports describe in the language of alternative outcomes: working in small groups, the pupils restaged the killing scene as a set of divergent possibilities, examining the causes of the outcome Shakespeare wrote and rehearsing the points at which it could have been avoided, the dramaturgical equivalent of the replay pass in the protocol above, arrived at independently by practitioners facing real knives rather than examination rubrics. The course ended in performance, the pupils' finished scenes shared with an invited audience of peers, family, and the wider school community, so that the work's final form was a public account rendered to the people the violence would have touched. The company's creative learning producer, Kerrie Driscoll, stated the premise the whole design serves: too many young people face life-changing consequences because they did not believe they had safer choices. The programme's wager is that a four-hundred-year-old play, kept whole, is a machine for manufacturing the belief that choices exist, by staging a street in which every character at every second had one and showing, scene by scene, what the failure to take it cost. That wager is this article's verdict wearing work clothes, and its presence in a police-partnered programme gives the teacher a citation that no literary argument can supply: the sequence rule is not only an interpretation of the play but a practice with partners, sessions, and published outcomes.

The programme's name is itself a curriculum decision, and teachers citing it should notice the choice. It is not called after the duel, the tomb, or any line of Mercutio's wit. It is named for the peacemaker's intervention in the first brawl, the moment the play's own anti-violence position is spoken by a young man with his sword out, beating down the weapons of men older in service than himself. The branding instructs before the first session meets: the hero of the piece, for the programme's purposes, is the one who parts the fighters, and the pupils' eight weeks are spent inside a story whose title has already told them which figure to study. The pairing with the company's stage production completes the design. Pupils encountered the play as a professional performance and as a classroom text at once, and the stage combat sessions closed the triangle, placing the actors' engineered illusion between the fiction and the street. A pupil who has watched professionals fight the duel beautifully, then learned from the fight director how the beauty was manufactured, then restaged the scene and stopped it at the killing to build a prosecution, has met the play at every level the framework distinguishes: as excitement, as craft, and as evidence. The programme is, in effect, the play's sequence institutionalised by people who had buried the alternative.

Its published evaluation figures require the grading this series applies to all effect claims: they are the company's reported results from an independent evaluation, stated here as claims with a source rather than as established measurements. On those terms they are striking. The proportion of pupils who understood that carrying a knife does not make its carrier safer rose, on the published figures, from 50 per cent before the programme to 83 per cent after it. The proportion who knew where to find support rose from 35 to 74 per cent. At one participating school, the proportion of pupils willing to report a knife being carried rose from 18 per cent to 82 per cent. A teacher judgement reported alongside the figures holds that the programme reached pupils who struggle in conventional classroom settings. None of this establishes that Shakespeare prevents knife crime, and the article's verdict claims nothing of the kind. What the figures support, at the strength the documents allow, is narrower and still valuable: a unit built on this play, sequenced as fight followed by trial, taught by theatre practitioners with police and university partners, measurably shifted the knowledge and stated intentions of the pupils who completed it, as its evaluators recorded. The play did the work the framework predicts, because the programme kept the play whole in the one respect that matters: it kept the consequence attached to the act.

### What did the Surrey programme change first, attitudes to knives or willingness to use the systems around them?

On its published figures, both moved, but the largest recorded movement was relational rather than attitudinal: willingness to report a carried knife at one school rose from under a fifth of pupils to over four fifths, a change in the pupils' relation to authority and to each other, larger than the gain in factual understanding about carrying.

The programme's limits deserve the same plain statement as its results. Eight weeks is a short exposure. Attitude questionnaires measure what pupils say to evaluators, not what a frightened fourteen-year-old does at a bus stop. The programme's Surrey setting, in a county whose police commissioner describes knife crime rates at roughly half the national average, is not the setting of the schools where the need is most desperate, and transfer to those settings is a hypothesis, not a finding. A teacher citing the programme should cite it as what it is: the best documented demonstration available that this play, taught whole and followed into a courtroom, can carry a violence unit without glamour and with measurable movement on its own stated aims. That is a considerable thing. It is not a proof, and the framework does not ask it to be one.

## The Clip Problem: Screen Violence and the Severed Reckoning

The likeliest route by which glamour enters a modern classroom is not the stage fight but the screen excerpt. Film versions of the play are teaching staples, and the two most used in schools frame the violence in instructively different ways, but the deeper problem is common to every screen use: excerpts circulate. A duel detached from its film circulates further still, as a clip, a trailer, a montage of the exciting parts, and the clip is the excerpt in its purest form, consequence-free by construction. Baz Luhrmann's 1996 film makes the mechanism visible because its design was so deliberate. The film keeps Shakespeare's words, in which men call for their rapiers and long swords, by remaking the weapons as handguns whose makes and models carry sword names engraved on them, so that a character can demand his sword and be handed a pistol that bears the word. The device is intelligent and it is also a perfect emblem of the risk: the play's violence is re-costumed as the most glamourised weaponry of its decade, photographed with a music video's love of speed and impact, and the beachfront duel becomes the film's showpiece, the sequence its marketing led with. None of this makes the film unusable. It makes the film's fights unusable as excerpts, since the film itself, watched whole, surrounds its showpiece with the same audit the play supplies: the Prince's analogue arrives by helicopter, the exile is pronounced, the bodies accumulate. The film kept whole argues the framework's case. The film in clips argues the opposite, and the difference is entirely the teacher's editorial decision about where the screening stops.

### What changes when the duel is watched without the scene that follows it?

Everything the play was doing. With the audit attached, the fight is evidence in a process that ends in exile and a doctrine against pardoning killers. Without it, the fight is a result, two falls and a winner's grief, and grief without judgement reads as the glamour of intensity: the strongest feeling in the room, unexplained and unpriced.

The rule for screen use follows the rule for everything else in this article. A screening of a fight is a reading of the fight, and it is complete only when the reckoning has also been screened or read. Teachers who show the 1968 Zeffirelli duel, staged with rapiers on Italian streets and performed by actors close to the characters' ages, are showing a fight whose energy is balletic and whose aftermath in the film is correspondingly swift; the lesson's obligation is to restore in discussion the weight the film's pace removes, beginning with the question the clip never asks: whose body is carried off, and who is left to explain the street to the Prince. The question costs nothing and changes everything, because it returns the students to the text's own distribution of attention, in which the explanation scene is longer than the fight.

Zeffirelli's film, kept whole, even stages the cheering problem inside itself, which makes it the most instructive screening the unit owns. Its duel, arranged by William Hobbs on a sunlit street, gives Mercutio a death that begins as performance: wounded, he keeps the jest running, and the gathered crowd, and by design the cinema audience with it, laughs at the fall as clowning before the blood shows and the laughter stops in the throat. The film therefore contains its own correction. A class screened the sequence complete has been put through the exact experience the framework describes, appetite first and repricing after, and the discussion can begin from the students' own laughter rather than from any warning: at what second did the laugh become impossible, and what information ended it. No lecture on glamour achieves what that question achieves, because the students have caught themselves in the act the play anatomises, enjoying a posture while its cost was still offscreen. Luhrmann's film offers the same structure at the opposite temperature, its showpiece framed by sirens and a sentencing delivered to camera. The two films between them exhaust the screen's options, and both, watched to the end of the audit, argue the framework's case against the clip that either could supply.

The clip has an industrial form that teachers should name for students, since naming it is half the defence: the trailer. A trailer is an excerpt engineered by professionals to sell excitement, and for this play it sells the fights, the balcony compressed to a glance, the tomb to a candle, because fights are what trailers are for. Students arrive in the classroom pre-taught by marketing, carrying a version of the play in which the duel is the centrepiece and everything else is atmosphere, and part of the unit's work is simply to make that pre-teaching visible as a commercial artefact with authors and interests. The exercise costs one lesson's opening minutes: compare what a trailer selects with what the play contains, set the selections side by side as proportions, and ask what the missing four fifths consist of. The answer, audits, letters, rebukes, inquiries, is the framework itself, discovered by the students in the gap between the advertisement and the work. A class that has performed that comparison once has acquired a habit it will apply to every adaptation it meets afterwards, which is the nearest thing to a permanent vaccination against glamour that a literature unit can administer.

## The Quieter Violence: Threats, Coercion, and the Father's Hand

A violence unit that confines itself to swordplay will miss the play's most consequential violence, which is domestic, verbal, and committed by a father against a daughter who never raises a weapon. Act 3 Scene 5 contains the play's most frightening speech, Capulet's answer to Juliet's refusal of Paris, in which the household's government drops its mask: she will marry on Thursday or be dragged there; if she is his, he will give her to his friend; if not, she may hang, beg, starve, die in the streets, and he will never acknowledge her (3.5). The speech's physical edge is stated in a phrase of terrible casualness, "My fingers itch" (3.5), the father's hand confessing its wish to strike while his tongue discusses logistics. Lady Capulet's contribution is quieter and colder: "I would the fool were married to her grave!" (3.5). Between them, the scene establishes that the feud's violence has an indoor branch, in which the weapons are disinheritance, destitution, and the withdrawal of acknowledgement, and its victim is the play's heroine, whose subsequent actions, the feigned obedience, the potion, the tomb, are all decisions made inside the narrowing corridor this scene builds.

Teaching this violence requires a different register from the duel, and the framework marks the difference plainly. The street fights can be staged, slowed, choreographed, and laughed about in the laughter's proper place. The coercion scene should not be staged as spectacle at all, and its classroom treatment belongs to reading and discussion, at the distance the text itself keeps: Shakespeare gives the threats to rhetoric rather than to blows, and the scene's power is in its language of property and disposal. The sensitivity here is not decorative. A classroom contains students for whom a parent's ultimatum is not a historical artefact, and the lesson's design should make engagement possible without personal disclosure, keeping the analysis on the text's evidence: what Capulet claims the right to dispose of, how the Nurse's defence is silenced, how Juliet's kneeling is answered. The play rewards that discipline, because its analysis of coercion is exact. Capulet does not doubt his love. He experiences his daughter's refusal as an attack on his government, and the scene shows a household discovering that its affection was conditional on obedience all along. Students who have read Act 3 Scene 5 closely do not need the lesson on honour to be imported from the street. They have watched honour's domestic form destroy the family it claimed to protect, three acts before the tomb finishes the work.

The scene's silencing of the Nurse completes its anatomy of the household, and it should not be passed over in class. The Nurse, who has served the family for decades and raised its daughter from weaning, attempts the one defence Juliet receives from any adult in the scene, and Capulet cuts her down with a contempt he would not use to a servant he feared: "Peace, you mumbling fool!" (3.5). The household's order is thereby displayed entire. The father disposes; the mother, who in the play's first act had presented the marriage as her daughter's advancement, withdraws into the grave line; the family's oldest retainer is gagged; and the daughter kneels, is interrogated about her gratitude, and is answered with the conditional grammar of property, his to give if hers to obey. No blade is drawn, and the scene is the play's most complete demonstration that its violence was never principally about blades. The feud's external form kills six people in the street and at the tomb. Its internal form, the government of the household by ultimatum, manufactures the desperation that puts two of the six there. A violence unit that teaches the coercion scene with the same care as the duel has understood the play's distribution of blame, which falls at least as heavily on the quiet rooms as on the loud streets.

## The Cell Scene: Despair, Rebuke, and the Friar's Grading of Grief

Between the duel and the deaths stands a scene the framework insists on teaching at full length, because it is the play's own demonstration of how despair is handled inside the fiction. Romeo, in the Friar's cell, receives the sentence of exile as a death worse than death and collapses into a grief the text refuses to flatter. He begs to be told in what part of his anatomy his name lodges, so that he can sack the hateful mansion (3.3), and the weapon he turns toward himself is taken from him by the Nurse while the Friar talks. What follows is the rebuke, and the rebuke is the scene's gift to the classroom: the Friar grades Romeo's despair by the code's own standards and finds it wanting on every count, his tears womanish, his temper unseemly, his intended self-violence a perjury against the life the law has spared. The speech is unsparing and it is also the play's model of an intervention: an older voice refusing the glamour of despair, inventorying the blessings the despairing man cannot see, and converting collapse into a plan with errands, visits, and a future tense.

The classroom handling follows the scene's own. The discussion stays on the judgements the text makes, the Friar's grading, the Nurse's physical interruption, Juliet's parallel despair reported from the other household, and on the fact that the play treats this despair as an error to be corrected by argument rather than as a depth to be admired. A class that has studied the rebuke closely has been given the anti-glamour instrument for every later scene of private anguish, because the Friar's method is transferable as an analytic habit: name the blessings, name the error, restore the future. The framework records the scene as the duel's necessary sequel in the unit's order. Act 3 Scene 1 shows violence done to others audited by the law. Act 3 Scene 3 shows violence contemplated against the self audited by friendship, at greater length and with greater tenderness, and a unit that skips the cell to hurry from the fight to the tomb has removed the play's own bridge between public violence and private despair.

## Teaching the Deaths: Paris at the Tomb and the End of the Feud

The deaths in Act 5 arrive in the play's most compressed writing, and their classroom handling should honour the compression. Paris dies first at the tomb, keeping a vigil the audience understands better than Romeo does, and Romeo, having killed him, performs the play's strangest act of restitution: he carries the body into the tomb and lays it in the monument, honouring the request of a man he has just killed, whose name he learns only as the corpse cools. The episode is the framework's test case for the forgotten death. Students routinely cannot recall that Romeo kills Paris at all, and the amnesia is diagnostic: Paris's death serves no romance, advances no theme a poster can carry, and exists purely as cost, a good man killed by a grieving one in the dark over a misunderstanding neither survives. Restoring Paris to the count is restoring the play's arithmetic, in which violence at the end is not climax but leakage, the feud's habits discharging themselves in men who had no quarrel worth the name.

The Friar had stated the whole pattern in advance, at the play's other ceremony. His warning at the wedding, "These violent delights have violent ends / And in their triumph die, like fire and powder" (2.6), is the closest the text comes to a thesis sentence on violence, and its placement is the teaching point: Shakespeare puts the warning inside the marriage service, spoken by the man conducting it, to a groom too happy to hear it. The play's violence is not a rival subject to its love. It is the same energy audited, delight and end in a single couplet, and a unit that teaches the wedding scene well has taught the tomb scene's interpretation before the class arrives there.

### Why does the play place its clearest warning about violence inside a wedding scene?

Because the warning's subject is not fighting but intensity without governance. The Friar is blessing a marriage contracted in secret and in haste, and his image of fire and powder consuming in the kiss of their meeting describes the lovers' haste and the feud's hatred with the same words, refusing to separate the play's heat into good and bad kinds.

Mantua deserves a paragraph before the tomb, because the play is careful about how the feud exports its violence into exile. Romeo in Mantua is a man removed from every audit Verona runs: no prince, no friar at hand, no inquiry within reach, only a servant's report and a memory of an apothecary's poverty. The purchase he makes there is written by Shakespeare as a transaction between two exhausted men, desperation buying from destitution, and the framework's table records the scene accordingly: briefly taught, as economics and isolation, and left behind for its consequences. Meanwhile the explanation that would have saved everyone is stopped in a doorway by the machinery of a frightened city: Friar John, sent with the letter, reports that the searchers of the town, suspecting plague in a house he visited, sealed up the doors and would not let him forth (5.2). The detail is the play's bleakest joke about systems. Verona and Mantua between them possess edicts, friars, letters, watches, and laws, an entire civilisation of prevention, and the catastrophe threads its way through the one unguarded gap, a quarantine cordon manned by frightened officials. Students tracing the chain learn the framework's last structural lesson: the feud kills its final three victims not in a fight at all but through a failure of communication inside a system built to prevent exactly this, which is why the play's imagination keeps returning to process. Where process works, in the hearings and the audits, people are sentenced and spared. Where it fails, in a sealed doorway, people die unheard.

The lovers' own deaths close the sequence, and the framework's treatment of them is fixed by the boundary stated at the head of this article. They are discussed as the feud's final cost and the Prince's evidence, in the play's own proportions, which give the deaths a handful of lines and the aftermath two hundred. The classroom emphasis falls where Shakespeare's falls: on the Watch discovering the scene, on the Friar's confession, on the letter that arrives too late in every sense, on the fathers' handshake and the statues, and on the Prince's audit, which distributes the punishment upward to the households and their hate rather than downward to the children. A sibling study in this series has assembled the scholarship on imitation and suicide contagion and graded its evidence, and its record stands behind this article's restraint without being re-argued here: the deaths are taught as waste within a fiction that frames them as the price of a feud, never as a summit of romantic achievement, and the distinction between the play's framing and the poster tradition's framing is itself one of the lesson's explicit topics. Students leave the unit able to say who killed each of the six, and in the play's own judgement the answer is the same for all of them: the feud did, operating through hands that the code, the household, and the haste of the young supplied.

The statues deserve the unit's final minute of suspicion, because they are where the glamour tradition begins, inside the play, performed by the guilty. Within moments of the audit's close, the fathers are competing again, in the only currency left to them: Montague will raise Juliet's statue in pure gold, and Capulet answers that Romeo's shall stand as rich beside it (5.3). The conversion is instantaneous and should be named in class for what it is. Two men who could not keep their children alive undertake to make them beautiful forever, in precious metal, at public expense of memory, and the feud's energy survives its settlement by changing its medium from steel to gold. Posterity obliged them. The lovers' afterlife as icons, on posters, covers, and Valentine merchandise, is the fathers' project completed by the culture, and the play contains the scene of its founding, placed after the Prince's audit precisely so that the audience can watch commemoration being used to close an account that the audit had left open. Teaching the ending whole means teaching the statues as the first excerpt, the original act of glamour, gilding the deaths before the bodies are buried. The Prince's closing couplet, which names the story a tale of woe and gives the last word to judgement rather than to the monuments, is the text's own refusal of the gift shop.

The discovery sequence that precedes the audit has its own classroom value, since it shows the feud's violence being processed by the city's ordinary machinery at its most ordinary. The Watch, roused by Paris's page, find the bodies and respond with the plain procedures of their office: they seize the Friar on suspicion, send for the Prince, and secure the ground, and their unadorned speech, all business and bewilderment, is the sound of the real world re-entering a scene the romance would prefer to keep private. Students should be directed to the tonal drop. After acts of verse pitched at the feud's intensity, the final scene keeps interrupting itself with constables, pages, servants, and a father arriving with news of a sixth death, until the stage holds a cross-section of the entire city standing over the consequences. That crowded, procedural stage is the play's last image of what violence actually produces: not a tableau of lovers but a public inquiry with too many witnesses and not enough explanation. The lesson that ends the unit there, amid the paperwork of grief, has honoured the text's proportions to the final line. A class that ends there has been shown glamour being born and has been handed the instrument for declining it, which is all the framework ever promised.

## The InsightCrunch Violence Framework

The argument of this article is gathered below as the InsightCrunch violence framework, a scene-by-scene schedule of the play's violent matter, the glamour risk each item carries in a classroom, the frame the play itself supplies against that risk, and the practice the frame dictates. The framework's single principle is printed in its first row and repeated in every other: the play audits everything it stages, and the lesson fails only when it separates an item in the left-hand column from the item the play attaches to it in the third.

| Scene or matter | The glamour risk | The frame the play supplies | The classroom practice |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The opening brawl (1.1) | Servants' swagger read as comedy of toughness | Benvolio's peacemaking and the Prince's death sentence for brawling, delivered in the same scene | Teach the whole scene, sentence included; name insult as assignment, not expression |
| The feast and Tybalt's rage (1.5) | The villain's menace read as style | Capulet's restraint: the city's peace kept by an old man's swallowed pride | Read the restraint beside the rage; ask what the code costs its keeper |
| The challenge and its jargon (2.4) | Fencing vocabulary as connoisseurship | Mercutio's mockery of the imported duellist's terms | Teach Peltonen's record: the duel as fashion with a sales pitch, mocked in its own decade |
| The wedding warning (2.6) | The couplet quoted as decoration | The Friar's fire-and-powder thesis, spoken over the marriage itself | Set the warning as the unit's interpretive key before any fight is staged |
| The duel and its inquiry (3.1) | The fight excerpted as the play's peak | The curse, Benvolio's partial report, Lady Capulet's prosecution, the Prince's sentence and doctrine | Never teach the fight without the inquiry; choreograph slowly, receivers in control, full sequence in order |
| Romeo in the Friar's cell (3.3) | Despair read as romantic depth | The Friar's rebuke and his grading of griefs by the code's own standards | Read the rebuke whole; keep the discussion on the text's judgements, not on method |
| Capulet's threats (3.5) | Domestic coercion overlooked beside swordplay | The speech's own language of property, disposal, and disowning | Reading and discussion only; analysis fixed on the text's evidence, no personal disclosure invited |
| The apothecary (5.1) | Desperation read as resolve | Romeo's own description of the transaction as a bargain with poverty | Teach the scene as economics and exhaustion, briefly, and move to its consequences |
| Paris at the tomb (5.3 in sequence from 5.1) | The forgotten death, invisible in summaries | Romeo's restitution: the killer carries his victim in and lays him in the monument | Restore Paris to the count of six; ask why summaries delete him |
| The reckoning (5.3) | The ending consumed as romance | The Prince's audit, the fathers' handshake, the statues, "All are punished" | Close the unit on the audit; require every final judgement to cite it |

The framework is deliberately conservative in one respect: it adds nothing to the play. Every safeguard in the fourth column is a decision to keep something the text already contains, the sentence inside the brawl scene, the inquiry after the duel, the audit after the tomb. Its claim is that glamour in the teaching of this play is never a problem the text creates and always a problem the timetable creates, by the innocent-looking decisions to start at the fight, to stop at the death, to screen the showpiece, to skip the report. Reverse those decisions and the play does the rest, because the play was built by a writer who understood the appetite for swordplay intimately, catered to it generously, and charged for it in full.

Used as a planning instrument, the framework reduces to four questions a teacher can put to any lesson in the unit before it is taught. Does the lesson contain a fight without the reckoning the play attaches to it. Does it stage anything at a speed that forbids inspection. Does it leave any of the six deaths out of the count, or treat any of them as decoration. Does it end on excitement when the play ends on judgement. A lesson that passes the four questions will not glamourise the play's violence, whatever its methods, and a lesson that fails any one of them will drift toward glamour however careful its intentions, because the drift is structural rather than moral. That is the framework's final claim, and the reason it is offered as a table rather than as an exhortation: the difference between teaching the violence and merchandising it is written into the order of scenes, and order can be checked.

Two things the framework is not should be stated to prevent its misuse. It is not a content warning, since it removes nothing, softens nothing, and advises no student to leave any scene unread; its whole argument is that the scenes are safe in the play's company and dangerous only in isolation. And it is not a censorship schedule in reverse, a list of the passages a class must linger on for their own good. The lingering is instrumental. The reckonings are taught at length because they are where the play keeps its meanings, its finest courtroom writing, and its judgements of the code, and a teacher who taught them at length for those reasons alone, without a thought for glamour, would satisfy the framework in full. The framework merely notices that the play's literary virtues and its safeguards are the same features, and that a unit faithful to the first set inherits the second without further effort.

## Verdict: Sequence Is the Safeguard

The complication the brief set, dramatic excitement against glamorisation, resolves once the two are located correctly. Excitement is the play's own instrument, present in the text at full strength and inseparable from its teaching power: the brawl is funny, Mercutio is exhilarating, the duel is terrifying, and a unit that muted those effects in the name of safety would lose the students and falsify the work. Glamour is not a stronger dose of the same thing. Glamour is excitement with the bill removed, the fight remembered as style because the curse was cut, the duel admired as choreography because the inquiry was skipped, the deaths consumed as romance because the audit was never read. The line between the two is therefore not a line of taste, policed by a teacher's sensibility. It is a line of sequence, policed by the order in which the class meets the material, and it can be drawn by rule: nothing violent in this play is taught alone. The Surrey programme's results, graded as claims, suggest the rule works where it is institutionalised. The fight directors' discipline shows the rule embodied in a craft, where the pretence of violence is governed by the consent of the endangered. The critics show the rule written into the play's meanings, where violence is a script of pressure and passage whose costs the text itself totals. Teaching the violence without glamour is, in the end, a modest discipline with an immodest payoff: keep the play whole, and the play teaches its own violence better than any warning pasted over it, because it lets students feel the excitement at full strength and then, in the same hour, hands them the bill.

The standing objection deserves its answer in the open. Sequence sounds like a counsel of perfection under examination pressure: the assessments reward extract work, the timetable is short, and the audit scenes can look like the dispensable connective tissue between the set pieces. The objection misreads where the marks live. Benvolio's report is a set-piece of partisan narration that examiners have always rewarded students for interrogating; the Prince's doctrine is a thesis sentence the play argues for five acts; the inquiry after the duel is the scene in which every theme the syllabus names, authority, honour, justice, mercy, is put under oath. Nothing in the framework asks a teacher to spend a minute that attainment does not repay. It asks only that the minutes be spent in the play's order rather than the anthology's, on the finding that the order is where the meaning and the marks both are. The excerpt will keep circulating outside the school gates, in trailers and tattoos and quotation books, and students will meet the duel a hundred times in the wild with no audit attached. The unit's one irreplaceable gift is the whole play, in sequence, once, taught as if the order mattered, because on all the evidence assembled here, from the critics to the fight manuals to a Surrey courtroom, the order is the safeguard.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Why do teachers treat the Act 3 duel as the point of greatest risk in the unit?

Because the scene concentrates every distorting pressure the unit faces into a single lesson. It is the plot's turning point, the fight students have heard about before they arrive, the passage most often excerpted by films and study aids, and the moment at which the play's comedy converts into its tragedy. Taught alone, it delivers spectacle: a witty man falls, his friend takes revenge, and the class leaves with the code's version of events. Taught in the play's sequence, with the comedy before it enjoyed at full value and the inquiry after it read at full length, the same scene delivers the play's argument about what the code costs. The risk is never the scene. The risk is the timetable that isolates it.

### Q: What does the play itself do to keep its fights from reading as glamour?

It attaches a reckoning to every one of them, at the level of structure. The opening brawl is answered inside its own scene by a peacemaker and by a prince who sentences future brawlers to death. The central duel is followed by a dying curse on both houses, an official inquiry, an exile, and a doctrine against pardoning killers. The tomb fight is followed by the killer carrying his victim in and laying him in the monument. The final scene is an audit that ends with the words "All are punished." A reader who takes the scenes in the play's order never meets a fight whose bill has not been presented. Glamour requires editorial help: the reckoning has to be removed before the fight can shine.

### Q: How does the Guildford programme Put Up Your Swords use the play against knife crime?

The Guildford Shakespeare Company built the programme in 2024 alongside its production of the play, after a young person was stabbed near its offices the year before. Over eight weeks, Year 9 pupils study the text, learn the fights in professional stage combat sessions, hear speakers with lived experience of knife violence, and examine a mocked-up crime scene. The staging of the play then follows Shakespeare up to the street fight of Act 3 and stops at the killing: the street is cordoned as a crime scene, and the unit ends with Romeo tried for murder at the University of Surrey's law school, with pupils arguing the case from the text and the law.

### Q: What did the Surrey programme's published evaluation figures actually measure?

They measured pupils' knowledge and stated intentions before and after the eight weeks, as recorded by the programme's independent evaluation and published by the company. On those figures, the share of pupils who understood that carrying a knife does not make the carrier safer rose from 50 to 83 per cent; the share who knew where to find support rose from 35 to 74 per cent; and at one school the share willing to report a carried knife rose from 18 to 82 per cent. The figures are attitude and knowledge measures reported by the programme, not observations of behaviour, and the article grades them as claims with a source rather than as proof that theatre prevents violence.

### Q: Why do fight directors insist that stage violence is an illusion built on trust?

Because the craft cannot function on any other basis. The standard manuals of the discipline, J. Allen Suddeth's Fight Directing for the Theatre and Dale Anthony Girard's Actors on Guard, both published in 1996, describe a fight as a cooperation disguised as a contest: distance is measured, targets are offered, cues are exchanged, and the partner who appears to be in danger controls the timing of the action that endangers them. The Society of American Fight Directors, founded in 1977, exists to certify that discipline. For a classroom the point is transferable without any weapons at all: choreographed violence runs on the consent of the endangered, which is the precise opposite of the violence the play depicts.

### Q: What is the difference between staging a fight and teaching one?

Staging aims at the audience's belief; teaching aims at the students' understanding, and the two aims dictate different speeds. A staged fight is performed at the tempo of conviction, fast enough to be misread as real. A taught fight is built at the tempo of inspection: walked slowly, stopped at the decision points, repeated with the partners exchanged, and stopped again at the moment where the text's consequences begin. The classroom version is not a poorer performance of the same event. It is a different event, whose content is the analysis the speed makes possible, and whose discipline borrows the profession's own rule that the choreography is rehearsed at half speed before it is ever run at full pace.

### Q: How does Coppélia Kahn's reading change what a teacher does with Romeo's refusal to fight?

Kahn's essay "Coming of Age in Verona" reads the refusal through Verona's definition of manhood: to decline the challenge is to decline the rite by which a male in that city becomes a man, and Mercutio's cry of "vile submission" states the code's marking scheme aloud. A teacher working with Kahn slows the lesson at the refusal rather than at the fight. The class examines what Romeo's courtesy costs him socially, why his new kinship with Tybalt cannot be spoken, and why the killing that follows functions as a graduation the city recognises. The fight then reads not as Romeo losing his temper but as Romeo being processed by a system that had only one certificate to issue.

### Q: What does Robert Appelbaum's phrase "standing to the wall" explain about the opening brawl?

The phrase names the brawl's real subject, which is position rather than anger. In the first scene the wall is the place of advantage, women are thrust to it as the weaker party, and a man's standing is measured by his refusal to be moved from it. Appelbaum's essay argues that masculinity in the play is a set of rules prefixed in advance, a posture assumed in public before any individual has chosen anything, which is why servants with no stake in the feud fight over a bitten thumb: the quarrel is between their masters, as Gregory says, but the posture is compulsory for the men who wear the livery. The brawl is compliance performed for witnesses.

### Q: Why does Susan Snyder's comedy-into-tragedy argument matter to the order of a unit?

Because Snyder locates the play's tragic mechanism in its sequence. Her 1970 essay argues that the play begins in comedy's patterns and becomes a tragedy at Mercutio's death, the moment "the birth of a tragedy coincide[s] exactly with the symbolic death of comedy," in her phrase. If she is right, the first half's laughter is not relief from the violence but the substance the violence destroys, and a class that has not laughed has nothing to lose when Mercutio falls. A unit that rushes to the duel to reach the serious material has therefore removed the tragedy's fuel. Snyder's argument converts teaching order from a scheduling choice into an interpretive one.

### Q: How did the duel of honour reach Verona, and why does its foreignness matter in class?

On Markku Peltonen's documented account, the duel of honour was a Renaissance invention rather than a medieval survival, carried from Italy and France into England together with courtesy books and fencing masters, and defended by a theory that readiness to fight made men polite. Early modern England argued about the import from the start, in pamphlets and in a Jacobean anti-duelling campaign. The foreignness matters pedagogically because it breaks the spell of inevitability: students who meet the duel as a recent fashion with a sales pitch, mocked in the play itself through Mercutio's catalogue of fencing jargon, are free to analyse it as a manufactured practice instead of revering it as the natural behaviour of a more honourable age.

### Q: What should a teacher do when students cheer the duel?

Treat the cheer as evidence, not as misconduct. The cheer is the honour code's script operating in the classroom exactly as the critics describe it operating in the street: an audience of peers rewarding the posture of violence before its cost is known. The play supplies the corrective in its own timing. Mercutio, once wounded, goes on speaking, jokes failing one by one, until the curse replaces the comedy and the room's appetite turns. A teacher who holds the scene open through the whole of that speech, and lets the silence after it stand, allows the text to reprice the cheer in front of the students who made it. Discussion can then begin from the class's own experience of the shift.

### Q: Why is showing a film duel without the following scene a distortion of the text?

Because the following scene is part of the fight's meaning in every version that keeps the play's structure. The play follows its duel with a curse, an inquiry, a prosecution, and a sentence of exile; the major films preserve equivalents of that sequence. A clip that ends at the falls has not shortened the duel but replaced it, converting a process that ends in judgement into an event that ends in excitement. The distortion is measurable in what students retain: classes remember the winner of a clipped duel and forget the doctrine the Prince pronounces over it. Restoring the reckoning, by screening or by reading, restores the text's proportions, in which the explanation of the fight runs longer than the fight.

### Q: How does the lesson handle Romeo's killing of Paris, the death students most often forget?

By treating the forgetting itself as the first exhibit. Paris dies at the tomb, in the dark, in a fight the text dispatches in a few exchanges, and summaries routinely delete him because his death serves neither the romance nor the feud's main line. The framework restores him to the count of six and lingers on Romeo's restitution: the killer learns his victim's name and request only after the killing, then carries the body into the tomb and lays it in the monument himself. Taught this way, Paris becomes the unit's purest case of violence as waste, a death with no grievance behind it, committed by a man who had come to the tomb to die rather than to kill.

### Q: What place does Lady Montague's reported death have in a lesson on the feud's cost?

She is the count's proof that the feud kills by more instruments than steel. Lady Montague never fights, never threatens, and speaks almost nothing in the play, yet the final scene reports that grief for her exiled son stopped her breath the night before. Her death widens the lesson's definition of the feud's violence from the duels to the whole system of consequences: exile is a sentence on a family, not only on a son, and the generation that authored the feud dies of it as surely as the generation that inherited it. A count of the dead that omits her has accepted the code's own accounting, in which only deaths by weapons register.

### Q: How are the lovers' deaths discussed responsibly in a classroom?

Inside the play's framing and in the play's proportions. The text gives the deaths themselves a few lines each and gives the discovery, the confession, the letter, and the Prince's audit the remainder of the scene, and a lesson that follows those proportions is already most of the way to responsibility. The deaths are discussed as the feud's final cost and as the evidence in the audit that follows them, not as the fulfilment of the romance and never with attention to method. The distinction between the play's own framing, in which the deaths are waste, and the poster tradition's framing, in which they are a summit of love, is made an explicit topic, so that students can see the two traditions competing for the same scene.

### Q: Why does the framework refuse both cutting the fights and celebrating them?

Because the two refusals are the same refusal. Cutting the fights protects students from the play by falsifying it, leaving them with a romance whose ending arrives from nowhere and whose cultural prestige they cannot interrogate. Celebrating the fights exposes students to the code by endorsing its own valuation, in which the duel is the play's peak and the reckoning its paperwork. The framework's alternative is the play's alternative: the fights at full strength, in sequence, with the consequences the text attaches to them read at the same length and with the same care. What is refused in both cases is the excerpt, the one instrument by which either failure is produced.

### Q: What does the InsightCrunch violence framework record, scene by scene?

It records, for each piece of violent matter in the play, four things in a single row: the scene or situation itself, the specific glamour risk that item carries in a classroom, the frame the play supplies against that risk, and the classroom practice the frame dictates. Its rows run from the opening brawl to the final reckoning, and they include the matter excerpting usually deletes: the wedding warning, the domestic coercion of Act 3 Scene 5, the reported death of Lady Montague, the forgotten killing of Paris. The framework adds nothing to the play; it is a schedule of what the play already attaches to its violence, arranged so that a teacher can check a lesson against it before the lesson is taught.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch violence framework to argue that the play audits its own violence?

By working down the framework's rows and showing that each violent set piece is answered, inside the play's own structure, by an instrument of judgement: a peacemaker inside the brawl, a sentence pronounced before the bloodshed, a curse inside the duel, an inquiry after it, a restitution at the tomb, and an audit over the final bodies. The essay's strongest evidence is the play's distribution of length, since the reckonings consistently run longer than the fights they judge. Set against that record, the glamour associated with the play's violence can be shown to belong to the excerpting tradition of posters, clips, and summaries, which removes the audits, rather than to the text, which insists on them.

### Q: What evidence would show that a violence unit had glamourised the duel despite its intentions?

The evidence would appear in what students retain and repeat. A glamourised unit leaves behind the fight's choreography and the winner's name, quotations from the witty exchanges before the wound, and an account of the scene in which Mercutio's death registers as the price of excitement rather than as an indictment. It shows in essays that reach the tomb without passing through the inquiry, and in discussions where the code's vocabulary of honour is used without distance. The contrary evidence is equally legible: students citing the Prince's doctrine, counting all six deaths including the two the posters forget, and describing the duel by beginning with its consequences. Retention is the audit a unit cannot fake.

### Q: Why does the play give its last word on violence to the Prince rather than to a lover?

Because the play's subject, in its own final accounting, is the city and not only the couple. The Prince has opened the action with a sentence against brawling and closes it with an audit of the sentence's failure, and between those two speeches the whole cost of the feud is laid out for judgement. Giving him the last word converts the ending from a private tragedy into a public finding: the scourge is laid on the households' hate, all are punished, and the reconciliation of the fathers is recorded as a transaction over bodies rather than celebrated as healing. A unit that ends where the play ends leaves students with a verdict to argue with, which is the condition the framework wants every discussion of the violence to end in.
