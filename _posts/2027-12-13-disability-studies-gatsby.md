Count the bodies in this novel that work properly. Tom Buchanan has one, and Fitzgerald spends a paragraph on its muscle and its leverage. Jordan Baker has one, and she is a professional athlete who declines a cocktail because she is in training. After that the list runs out. George Wilson is introduced as "spiritless" and "anaemic" and ends the book unable to stand without holding the doorposts. The men of the valley are described as already crumbling. Myrtle Wilson's body is her only asset and is destroyed on a road. Disability studies and Great Gatsby criticism have not often been put together, and the reason is that readers assume the novel contains no disabled characters. The lens does not need one. It needs a text that sorts people by bodily capacity, and this one sorts relentlessly.

The reading this article defends can be stated in a sentence. In Fitzgerald's novel, physical and mental breakdown is a class marker: the bodies that fail are the bodies of the powerless, the bodies that gleam with vigor belong to the people who can afford them, and the health of the elite is subsidized by the exhaustion of everybody downwind. That is not a metaphor the novel invents. It is a description of an industrial economy in 1922, and Fitzgerald put it on the page in the form of a valley where men shovel ash into their own lungs so that the parties on the shore can continue.

## What Disability Studies Is, in Plain Terms

Disability studies emerged as an academic field in the 1980s and 1990s out of the disability rights movement, and its founding move was to separate two things that ordinary language runs together.

The medical model treats disability as a defect located in an individual body, a problem to be diagnosed, treated, cured, or managed. On this account a person is disabled because their body does not function as bodies are supposed to function.

The social model, articulated in Britain by disabled activists in the 1970s and developed in academic form by Michael Oliver in the following decade, relocates the problem. Impairment, on this account, is a fact about a body. Disability is what a society does with that fact: the stairs without a ramp, the job that will not accommodate, the assumption that a certain speed of movement is standard. Disability, on the social model, is produced by environments and arrangements rather than by bodies.

Three further concepts do most of the analytical work in literary criticism.

The norm is the first, and its history matters. Lennard Davis argued in the mid-1990s that the very idea of a normal body is a nineteenth-century invention, arriving with statistics and with the eugenic projects that statistics enabled. Before there was an average there could be no deviation from it. The novel Fitzgerald wrote sits in the decades when that idea was at its most politically aggressive in the United States.

The normate is the second, a term introduced by Rosemarie Garland-Thomson to name the position of the unmarked, standard body against which others are measured, a position that is culturally constructed and never neutral. Compulsory able-bodiedness, developed later by Robert McRuer, names the system that makes that position mandatory rather than merely common.

Narrative prosthesis is the third, from the work of David Mitchell and Sharon Snyder, and it is the concept most directly useful for reading fiction. Their argument is that literature leans on disabled and non-standard bodies as a device: an anomalous body enters a story to supply meaning, to symbolize a moral condition, or to drive a plot, and is then removed once its work is done. The disabled figure is a crutch the narrative uses and discards.

### What is a disability-studies reading of The Great Gatsby?

A disability-studies reading examines how the novel distributes bodily capacity, treating breakdown, exhaustion, and vigor as socially produced rather than natural. It asks which bodies count as standard, who is permitted to be healthy, how the text uses failing bodies as plot machinery, and what the arrangement reveals about power.

## The Questions the Lens Asks of a Text

Applying the lens is a matter of asking a stable sequence of questions and letting the passages answer.

Which bodies does the text describe, and in what terms? Description is never neutral in fiction, and a novel that lavishes anatomical detail on one character and grants another only a set of subtractions has already made an argument.

What counts as a standard body in this world, and who occupies that position? The normate is usually invisible, which is why locating it requires attention to what goes unremarked.

How does the text explain breakdown? Whether a collapse is attributed to individual weakness, to moral failure, or to conditions is the difference between the medical model and the social model operating inside a narrative.

Does the text use non-standard bodies as machinery? If a character's illness exists chiefly to move a plot or to symbolize something about a healthy character, narrative prosthesis is at work.

Who gets access to care, rest, and repair? This is the question that converts a disability reading into a social analysis, because access is distributed by money.

And finally, what does the text do with its damaged bodies at the end? Cure, death, and disappearance are the three conventional exits, and which one a novel chooses is a statement of its values.

## Disability Studies and The Great Gatsby: The Making of George Wilson

Wilson is the novel's central case, and the lens reads his arc as a documented deterioration with an identifiable cause.

He is introduced in chapter two as "a blond, spiritless man, anaemic, and faintly handsome." The word "anaemic" is a medical term, and Fitzgerald uses it as a character description, which is the medical model operating as characterization: a diagnosis substituting for a person. Spiritless removes vitality. The sentence subtracts twice and then concedes an attractiveness immediately qualified into nothing.

His environment is then described in terms that explain the diagnosis without the novel ever connecting them. He lives and works in a valley where ashes take the forms of houses and chimneys and rising smoke and finally of "ash-grey men, who move dimly and already crumbling through the powdery air." When a line of gray cars stops, those men swarm up with leaden spades and stir up "an impenetrable cloud, which screens their obscure operations from your sight." That is a description of an occupational hazard. Men working unprotected in airborne particulate, for years, will present as "anaemic" and "spiritless", and Fitzgerald has placed the cause two paragraphs away from the symptom without asserting the connection.

He completes the picture with a detail readers usually pass over. After Myrtle speaks to him, Wilson goes toward the office and mingles immediately with the cement color of the walls, and Nick reports that "A white ashen dust veiled his dark suit and his pale hair" as it veiled everything in the vicinity, except his wife. The environment is depositing itself on the man. He is being turned into the material he lives in, and the exception granted to Myrtle marks her as the one body in the valley still resisting.

By chapter seven Wilson has moved from chronic to acute. Tom stops for gas and the proprietor emerges and "gazed hollow-eyed at the car." Asked what is wrong, Wilson says, "I'm sick," then "Been sick all day," then "I'm all run down." Three statements, all self-report, all vague, all delivered to a man who responds by demanding service. Tom's diagnosis is offered later: he tells Nick that Wilson is so dumb he does not know he is alive.

The final stage arrives in chapter eight. Michaelis finds Wilson sick in his office, pale as his own pale hair and shaking all over, and advises him to go to bed. Wilson refuses on the grounds that he would lose business. There is the social model in one exchange: the impairment is real, the barrier is economic, and rest is a commodity Wilson cannot purchase. After Myrtle's death he stands swaying in the doorway, holding the doorposts with both hands, and "neither heard nor saw" the man speaking to him. He fixates on a swinging light. He gives out his high, horrible call.

### How does a disability lens read George Wilson's breakdown?

It reads the deterioration as environmentally and economically produced rather than as personal weakness. Wilson is introduced with a medical adjective, works unprotected in airborne ash, cannot afford to rest when sick, and is finally reduced by the inquest to a man deranged by grief. The novel supplies every cause and never connects them.

The reduction at the end is the most disability-conscious moment in the book, and it is delivered as bureaucratic procedure. Nick reports that the inquest confined Wilson to a man deranged by grief so that the case might remain in its simplest form, and that it rested there. A legal process converts a complex history of chronic illness, poverty, and acute loss into a single psychiatric label, and the label serves an institutional convenience rather than a truth. Any reader who has followed the disability rights literature will recognize the move immediately.

## The Valley of Ashes as a Disability Zone

Fitzgerald's most famous piece of landscape writing is, read through this lens, an account of an industrial region that manufactures impaired bodies.

The description in chapter two is worth taking slowly. The valley is "a fantastic farm where ashes grow like wheat into ridges and hills and grotesque gardens." Farm and wheat are agricultural words applied to waste, which converts the place into a site of production whose product is refuse. The men who work there are described as growing out of the ash with a transcendent effort, which places them at the end of the same process that produces the ridges and hills. They are the crop.

The air is the medium of harm. Fitzgerald mentions "the spasms of bleak dust which drift endlessly over it," and the impenetrable cloud raised by the leaden spades. Above it all, an advertising sign for an optician watches over what Nick calls the solemn dumping ground. A billboard selling corrective eyewear presides over a landscape that damages the eyes and the lungs of everyone in it, and Fitzgerald arranged that irony deliberately.

The only child in the valley appears for one clause: "a grey, scrawny Italian child was setting torpedoes in a row along the railroad track." Gray describes the deposit already on the child. Scrawny describes the effect of the conditions. Fitzgerald spends four words establishing that the process which produced Wilson is producing a next generation, and then moves the car along.

The geography completes the argument. The valley sits between West Egg and New York, and everyone in the novel passes through it without stopping, on the way from where they live to where they enjoy themselves. Tom stops only to buy gas and to visit his mistress. Nick's summary of the place is that it is contiguous to absolutely nothing. The people whose bodies are being consumed are positioned on the route the healthy travel, visible from the train, and structurally invisible.

That is the load-bearing observation of this entire reading. The valley is not a symbol of moral decay set beside a story about rich people. It is the physical location where the cost of the rich people's lives is being paid, in lungs and in eyes and in the difference between a body that gleams and a body that crumbles.

## The Ability Norm the Elite Display

Against the valley, the novel sets a display of health so emphatic that it stops looking natural once the contrast is visible.

Tom is the normate, and Fitzgerald constructs him out of capacity. Nick records the enormous power of that body, the great pack of muscle shifting under his coat, the boots strained at the lacing, the body capable of enormous leverage. Tom's history is athletic: one of the most powerful ends ever to play football at New Haven, a national figure in a way. His hobbies require animals and equipment: a string of polo ponies brought from Lake Forest. His decline is registered in the same vocabulary, when Nick observes that "his sturdy physical egotism no longer nourished his peremptory heart." Even the man's disappointment is described as a nutritional failure of his physique.

Jordan supplies the second version. She is a slender, small-breasted girl with an erect carriage that she accentuates by throwing her body backward at the shoulders like a young cadet. She is a golfer who plays in tournaments. She refuses a cocktail because she is "absolutely in training," and Nick, watching her, notes that her body asserted itself with a restless movement of her knee. Assertion is the same word the novel uses for Tom. Bodily capacity is a form of speech in this book, and only some people are given it.

Gatsby occupies an interesting middle position. He is physically capable, tanned, described as balancing himself on the dashboard of his car with "that resourcefulness of movement that is so peculiarly American," a phrase that ties bodily competence to national identity. But his health, like everything else about him, is acquired. The boyhood schedule specifies dumbbell exercise and wall-scaling at a fixed time each morning. Gatsby built his body the way he built his biography, from published instructions, and the lens notes that the able body is a thing a poor boy in 1906 understood he would have to construct.

### Why does the novel prize the able body of the elite?

Because bodily vigor is presented as the visible form of social power. Tom's muscle and leverage, Jordan's athletic carriage and training, and Gatsby's constructed fitness are all described as assertions. The valley's workers, who have no power, are described as crumbling. Capacity and standing are the same measurement taken twice.

The clearest evidence that the novel treats ability as a class possession is what happens when the elite are damaged. Daisy bruises a knuckle and it becomes a dinner-table anecdote. Tom's nose is not broken by anybody. Jordan is in a car accident that Nick uses to characterize her carelessness rather than to injure her. Gatsby is shot, which is the only serious bodily harm any wealthy character in the novel suffers, and it is administered by a poor man with nothing left. Injury travels in one direction until the last chapter, and when it finally reverses, the reversal ends the book.

## Eckleburg: An Optician's Sign Over a Dust Valley

The eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg are usually read as God, as the death of God, as the moral vacancy of the age, or as the surveillance of commerce. Under this lens they become something more specific and more uncomfortable: an abandoned advertisement for the correction of impaired vision, placed above the site that impairs it.

Fitzgerald's description repays attention to its medical facts. The eyes are "blue and gigantic," and "their retinas are one yard high." Retina is a clinical term, and it is the wrong term for a painted sign, which has no retinas. The eyes "look out of no face, but, instead, from a pair of enormous yellow spectacles which pass over a nonexistent nose." Then the history: "some wild wag of an oculist set them there to fatten his practice in the borough of Queens, and then sank down himself into eternal blindness, or forgot them and moved away."

An oculist advertised his practice above a landscape that fills the air with particulate, which would generate exactly the patient population his business required. Then, in Nick's telling, he went blind himself. The commercial logic and the medical irony are both in the sentence. Whether the oculist actually lost his sight or simply vanished is left open, and Nick's alternative, that he forgot them and moved away, is the more devastating possibility: the sign advertising the repair of eyes was abandoned by the man who could repair them, and the eyes remain, dimmed by many paintless days, brooding over the dumping ground.

The valley's residents have no oculist. What they have is the picture of one. When Wilson, in the worst hours of his life, presses his face to a window and sees the eyes emerge from the dissolving night, he tells Michaelis that God sees everything. Michaelis corrects him: that is an advertisement. Wilson does not accept the correction. A man whose body and mind have been consumed by an industrial environment turns for witness to a derelict billboard for eye care, and the novel lets the substitution stand without comment.

Read this way, the most famous symbol in American fiction is a piece of medical infrastructure that failed. That reading does not cancel the theological one; it grounds it. The reason a discarded optician's sign can stand in for an absent God in this novel is that both are things the people of the valley need, have been promised, and do not have.

## Myrtle, Jordan, and the Bodies of Women

The lens changes what it finds when it turns to the women, and the change is instructive.

Myrtle is described almost entirely as a body, and the description is a study in surplus vitality contained in a place designed to extinguish it. She is in her middle thirties and faintly stout, and Nick notes that she carried her flesh sensuously as some women can, that her face contained no facet or gleam of beauty, and that there was an immediately perceptible vitality about her as if "the nerves of her body were continually smouldering." Vitality is the exact quantity her husband lacks and her valley removes. The white ashen dust veils everything in the vicinity except Myrtle. She is the one body in the ash that has not been converted.

The novel then destroys that body in the most physically explicit passage Fitzgerald ever published, and the destruction is described from outside, by observers, in the language of an accident report. Myrtle's interiority is never available. Her vitality was her single asset and her only route out, she spent it on a man who could buy a healthy body of his own, and the road took it. A disability reading notes that the novel's most vital character is also its most physically vulnerable, because vitality without money buys nothing but attention.

Jordan is the counter-case and the one that tests the reading. She is athletic, trained, hard, and Nick describes her with the vocabulary of controlled capacity: erect carriage, cadet posture, a body that asserts itself. She is also, Nick eventually decides, incurably dishonest, and he links her dishonesty to her physical self-sufficiency, remarking that dishonesty in a woman was something he never blamed deeply and then blaming her. A woman with an able body and no need of anyone is treated by the narrator as a moral problem.

Daisy occupies a third position. Her body is described in terms of voice and motion rather than capacity, and her one injury is a bruised knuckle displayed at dinner. She is the only character who is systematically protected, and the protection is financial. When she kills a woman with a car, the consequences are absorbed by other bodies.

The pattern across the three is consistent with the class reading. The woman with vitality and no money dies. The woman with capacity and independence is judged. The woman with money is insulated. Ability in this novel does not protect anybody who cannot pay for its consequences.

## Narrative Prosthesis: Illness as Plot Machinery

The hardest question a disability reading must ask of a novel is whether the text uses damaged bodies as equipment, and Fitzgerald's plot answers yes.

Consider the mechanics of the ending. Myrtle runs into the road because she believes the yellow car contains Tom. Wilson, having locked her upstairs and been shattered by her death, is given a piece of information by Tom and walks across Long Island to shoot Gatsby and then himself. Every hinge in that sequence turns on Wilson's condition. His sickness in chapter seven establishes that something is wrong. His shaking and pallor in chapter eight establish incapacity. His dissociation establishes that he can be directed. His breakdown supplies the motive, the instability, and the willingness. Fitzgerald needed a man who could be pointed at a target, and he built one across three chapters.

Mitchell and Snyder's argument is precisely this: narratives lean on anomalous bodies for their structural work and then remove them. Wilson is removed. He shoots himself in the grass, and Nick reports that "the holocaust was complete." The word completes an inventory. The character has finished his function.

The same test applied to the elite produces the opposite result. Nobody's illness moves the plot in East Egg. Tom's decline is atmospheric. Daisy's unhappiness is a mood. The one wealthy body that is damaged, Gatsby's, is damaged by the plot rather than driving it, and it is damaged by the poor man whose breakdown was the machinery.

### How does illness function as a plot device in the novel?

Wilson's deterioration supplies the ending's mechanics. His sickness establishes something wrong, his shaking and dissociation establish that he can be directed, and his breakdown supplies motive and instability. Fitzgerald built the instrument across three chapters, used it, then removed it, which is exactly the pattern disability critics call narrative prosthesis.

Naming this is not an accusation against Fitzgerald. It is a description of how the novel is built, and the description sharpens rather than diminishes the book, because Fitzgerald was unusually explicit about what produced Wilson. A less honest novel would have made him simply weak. This one shows the ash, the unprotected labor, the impossibility of rest, the economic dependence on his wife's lover, and the bureaucratic erasure at the inquest, and then uses the result as a mechanism. The novel indicts the process it employs.

## The Eugenic Weather of 1922

A disability reading of this novel cannot skip the dinner-table scene in chapter one, because that scene is the moment the historical context walks into the dining room and announces itself.

Tom recommends a book. "Civilization's going to pieces," he says, and asks whether Nick has read "The Rise of the Coloured Empires by this man Goddard." He summarizes: the white race will be submerged unless the dominant race watches out, and the dominant race has produced science and art and all the things that go to make civilization. Nick registers something pathetic in his concentration.

The book Tom describes does not exist under that title, but an established line of interpretation reads it as a composite of two real sources whose names Fitzgerald blended. Lothrop Stoddard published The Rising Tide of Color Against White World-Supremacy in 1920, a bestseller arguing exactly what Tom summarizes. Henry H. Goddard was an American psychologist known for intelligence testing and for work on what the era called feeble-mindedness, whose research was used to support institutionalization and sterilization policy. Fitzgerald put a racist demographic panic into the mouth of his most powerful character and attached it to a name associated with the classification of minds.

That matters for a disability reading because American eugenics in the 1920s was not primarily about race in isolation. It was a program of sorting human beings by measured capacity, and its targets included the poor, the institutionalized, immigrants, and people classified as mentally or physically unfit. The Immigration Act of 1924 restricted entry on grounds that mixed nationality with fitness. Compulsory sterilization statutes were being enacted across states during the decade in which the novel is set and published. The idea that some bodies and minds were burdens on a healthy national organism was, in these years, respectable science with legislative consequences.

Fitzgerald did not write a novel about eugenics. He wrote a novel in which the man with the finest body in the book reads a work of racial pseudoscience at dinner and repeats its thesis to his guests, while thirty miles away men who are already crumbling shovel ash into an impenetrable cloud. Placing those two facts in one book is an argument even if the author never states it, and the argument is that the vocabulary of fitness and unfitness in this period was a vocabulary of power dressed as biology.

Nick's response to Tom's speech is worth noting, because it establishes the novel's distance from the position. Daisy winks. Jordan changes the subject. Nobody argues, and nobody agrees. Fitzgerald renders the ideology as a social embarrassment produced by a man who is anxious about slipping, which is exactly the diagnosis a critical reading would offer.

### Is it anachronistic to apply disability studies to a 1925 novel?

The academic field is recent, but its subject matter was contemporary. Eugenic sorting by fitness, industrial injury, mass war disablement, and institutional classification were live in the 1920s. The lens supplies vocabulary for phenomena Fitzgerald observed directly, which is different from imposing concerns the period did not have.

## The War and the Disabled Veteran

One further historical layer belongs to this reading. The First World War produced disabled survivors in numbers no previous conflict had generated, and the men in this novel are of that generation.

Gatsby and Nick are veterans. Neither is described as physically injured, and the novel is notable for containing no visibly disabled ex-serviceman at all, in a country that in 1922 had a great many. That absence is itself a finding. Fitzgerald's Long Island is a place where the war has been converted into medals, anecdotes, and restlessness rather than into bodies, which is what a wealthy summer colony would look like. The men whose bodies came back altered were not at Gatsby's parties.

What the novel does register is the psychological residue, and here the disability lens and the trauma lens converge. Gatsby says he tried very hard to die. Nick says he came back restless. Neither describes an injury, and both describe an alteration. The lens notes that non-visible impairment, then as now, is the kind most easily denied and least likely to be accommodated, and that both men are affluent enough to convert their alteration into lifestyle rather than into a claim on anybody.

Wilson, who did not go, is the man in the book whose body has been consumed by his work rather than by a war. The contrast is instructive. The nation had, by 1922, built an apparatus of recognition around the disabled soldier, however inadequate. It had built nothing around the man in the garage. The novel's most damaged body belongs to the character with the least claim on public sympathy, and Fitzgerald arranged the sympathies of his own narrator to match: Nick pities Gatsby and never once considers Wilson's condition as anything but an obstacle and then a threat.

## The Butler's Nose: Occupational Injury as Dinner Entertainment

The single most concentrated piece of evidence for this reading occupies half a page of chapter one and is almost always read as a joke. It is a joke. That is what makes it evidence.

Daisy leans toward Nick during a lull and offers a family secret. "It's about the butler's nose. Do you want to hear about the butler's nose?" The story follows. The man was not always a butler; he used to be the silver polisher for people in New York who owned a service for two hundred. He had to polish it from morning till night, and Daisy reports that "it began to affect his nose." Jordan supplies the next line as a formula, saying that things went from bad to worse, and Daisy repeats the formula and finishes: he had to give up his position.

Read the content stripped of the delivery. A working man performed a repetitive task involving polish and metal for so many hours a day, over so long a period, that it produced a permanent injury to his face, and the injury cost him his livelihood. He now holds a lesser position in the household of the woman recounting it. That is an industrial disease narrative, complete with cause, progression, and economic consequence, and it is told at a dinner table on East Egg as a piece of light gossip designed to fill an awkward pause after Tom's race-panic speech.

Notice who narrates it and how. Daisy is the teller, and Nick reports that for a moment the last sunshine fell with romantic affection upon her glowing face and that her voice compelled him forward breathlessly as he listened. The prose is doing the same thing the anecdote is doing: converting a man's ruined face into an occasion for the beauty of the woman describing it. Nick is charmed. Jordan collaborates in the telling. Nobody asks the butler's name, and the butler is in the room.

The structural placement is exact. Fitzgerald puts this story immediately after Tom's speech about the dominant race producing all the things that go to make civilization. The silver service for two hundred is one of those things. The nose is what producing it cost. Fitzgerald did not need to comment, and he did not.

A disability reading treats this half page as the novel's thesis in miniature. Bodily damage in this world happens to servants, is produced by labor, is converted into entertainment by the people the labor served, and terminates in a loss of position that the sufferer absorbs alone. Everything that later happens to George Wilson at length happens to the butler in six sentences.

## Nerves, Heat, and the Body in Chapter Seven

The novel's climactic chapter is organized around bodily distress, and once the lens is in hand the physical writing in it stops being atmosphere.

Fitzgerald opens with the weather as a medical condition. "The next day was broiling, almost the last, certainly the warmest, of the summer." The heat is then attached to bodies. Nick's train seats are described as hovering on the edge of combustion. The woman beside him perspires into her white shirtwaist and then, as her newspaper dampens, lapses into deep heat with a desolate cry. In Daisy's house the butler announces the salon needlessly, and Nick observes that in this heat every extra gesture was an affront to the common store of life. Daisy and Jordan lie on an enormous couch, in Fitzgerald's phrase "like silver idols weighing down their own white dresses against the singing breeze of the fans," and say together that they cannot move. Jordan says she cannot stand this any more. Daisy asks what they will do with themselves this afternoon, and the day after that, and the next thirty years.

Heat exhaustion is the condition being described, and Fitzgerald distributes it by class in the same way he distributes everything else. The rich characters suffer it as discomfort and complaint, cooled by fans and ice and a rented suite. Wilson suffers it as collapse. When Tom stops for gas the proprietor is hollow-eyed and reports being sick all day and all run down, and he is standing in the same heat with a garage to run and no possibility of lying down.

The Plaza suite scene then stages a confrontation between men in a room where everyone is physically failing. Fitzgerald tracks the bodies: the mint julep, the ice, the open windows, the hot whistles of the National Biscuit Company from below, the wedding march audible through the floor. Nick reports on the drive home that human sympathy has its limits, that Tom's voice was as remote from Jordan and himself as the foreign clamor on the sidewalk, and that they were content to let the tragic arguments fade with the city lights behind. Exhaustion is being offered as an explanation for moral withdrawal.

That is the chapter's quiet argument, and it is worth an essay paragraph. The characters who can afford to be exhausted retreat into their exhaustion. Wilson, who is genuinely ill, cannot retreat anywhere, and by the end of the same day his wife is dead and he is holding the doorposts of his office. The identical weather produces a bad afternoon for one group and a catastrophe for the other, and the difference is money.

### How does the heat of chapter seven function under this lens?

Fitzgerald distributes an identical physical condition unequally. The wealthy experience the heat as discomfort managed by fans, ice, and a rented suite, and later use their exhaustion to excuse moral withdrawal. Wilson experiences it as collapse he cannot afford to treat, and by nightfall the same day has destroyed him.

## Who Provides Care, and Who Receives It

A disability reading eventually arrives at care, because care is where a society reveals what it thinks bodies are worth, and Fitzgerald's novel is unusually clear on the question.

Almost nobody in this book cares for anybody. The exceptions are precise and they are all poor. Michaelis, the young Greek who runs the coffee joint beside the ash heaps, sits with Wilson through the entire night, tries to get him to eat, looks for a friend to call, urges him to bed, and stays until another man comes to relieve him at six in the morning. He does this for a neighbor he barely knows, in a place with no institutions, and he is the one figure in the novel who performs sustained, unpaid, competent care.

Set against that, the wealthy characters organize care only for themselves. Tom's household has servants. Daisy has a nurse for her daughter, who is produced for display and taken away. Gatsby dismisses his entire staff and replaces it with people Wolfshiem sends, because he needs discretion rather than service. When Gatsby is shot, the household includes a butler, a chauffeur, and a gardener, none of whom prevented anything, and the gardener finds Wilson's body afterward.

The clearest illustration is what happens to Gatsby's own body after death. Nick reports that he found himself on Gatsby's side and alone, and that every practical question was referred to him. Daisy leaves with no forwarding address. Wolfshiem writes that he cannot get mixed up in it. Klipspringer telephones about a pair of tennis shoes. The father arrives from Minnesota with a photograph. Nobody comes. A society that will not attend to a wealthy man's corpse was never going to attend to a garage owner's lungs.

The absence of medical infrastructure across the entire novel is worth noting as a finding rather than an oversight. There is no doctor in it. Doctor Webster Civet appears once as a name on a guest list and is reported drowned in Maine. Doctor T. J. Eckleburg is an advertisement, and an oculist rather than a physician. The only medical presence in the book is a sign, and the only medical treatment administered on the page is the cold bath and spirits of ammonia given to a drunk bride in Louisville by a maid and a friend, to get her into a dress in time for a wedding. Care in this world is improvised by women and by immigrants, and is directed at making a body presentable rather than at making it well.

## One Scene Read Closely: The Night in Wilson's Office

The lens proves itself at the level of the sentence, so it is worth running the method through a single sustained passage without skipping. The night Michaelis spends with Wilson in chapter eight is the longest continuous depiction of a body and mind in crisis that Fitzgerald wrote, and almost every paragraph rewards the reading.

The framing is the first observation. Nick did not see this. It reaches the reader because Michaelis was the principal witness at the inquest, which means the novel's most detailed account of impairment is legally obtained testimony from a man of the valley about another man of the valley. Nobody from East Egg is present, and nobody from East Egg ever hears it. The information exists in the book only because a coroner required it.

The scene opens with Michaelis crossing the road to check on a neighbor, which is the first act of care in the sequence and the pattern for everything that follows. He wants Wilson to eat and Wilson refuses. He wants Wilson to sleep and Wilson refuses. Michaelis suggests a church, asks whether Wilson belongs to one, and proposes telephoning a priest, and Wilson has no church and no priest. There is no institution in this man's life to call.

Then the interrogation of the room. Michaelis asks how long Wilson and Myrtle had been married, and Wilson answers twelve years, and Fitzgerald has the older man look around the office as though the answer were somewhere in it. Wilson repeatedly returns to the drawer where he found the expensive dog leash wrapped in tissue paper, which is the evidence of his wife's other life. He handles it, sets it down, and comes back to it. Repetitive handling of a single object during acute distress is a behavior the passage records without naming, and a reader with the lens in hand can see what the prose is doing.

The physical presentation escalates in stages Fitzgerald marks precisely. Early in the evening Wilson is described as rocking himself back and forth on a chair in the doorway. Then his voice drops to a mutter. Then he becomes still for long intervals. By the dawn passage he has stopped responding to speech at all: his glazed eyes turn out to the ash heaps, where small gray clouds take on fantastic shapes and scurry in the faint dawn wind. Glazed is the same adjective Fitzgerald applies to Tom's eyes at the garage after the accident, which is the one place in the novel where a wealthy character and a poor one receive the same physical description. Tom's glaze lasts a paragraph. Wilson's does not lift.

Then the theological improvisation. Wilson says he spoke to Myrtle, that he told her she might fool him but she could not fool God, that he took her to the window and told her God knew everything she had been doing. Michaelis, standing behind him, sees with a shock that Wilson is looking at the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg, which "had just emerged, pale and enormous, from the dissolving night." Michaelis says it is an advertisement. Wilson does not respond to the correction and stands a long time with his face close to the window pane, nodding into the twilight.

Every element of a disability reading is in that paragraph. A man in acute crisis reaches for a witness. The only candidate his environment supplies is a derelict commercial sign for the correction of eyesight. A neighbor with no training offers the accurate factual correction, and the correction has no purchase, because what Wilson needs is not information. The scene is often read as the novel's statement about God, and it is, but it is first a statement about what is available to a poor man at four in the morning in Queens in 1922 when his mind gives way, which is a neighbor, a billboard, and nothing else.

The exit is as telling as the collapse. By six o'clock Michaelis is worn out and grateful for the sound of a car, one of the watchers from the night before returning as promised. He cooks breakfast for three, eats with the other man, notes that Wilson is quieter, and goes home to sleep. When he wakes four hours later and hurries back, Wilson is gone. Informal care, provided by exhausted volunteers in shifts, with no capacity to observe continuously and no authority to prevent anything. The novel does not editorialize about this. It reports it, and the reporting is the indictment.

One last detail deserves attention because it is easy to miss. Fitzgerald notes that Wilson's movements that morning were traced by boys who had seen a man behaving strangely, and by motorists to whom he had looked queer. The community observed his condition accurately and had no mechanism for doing anything about it. Between the accurate observation and the shooting there is nothing at all, and the nothing is the point.

### What does the night in Wilson's office reveal about care?

It reveals that the valley has no institutions. Wilson has no church, no priest, and no friend, so an exhausted neighbor sits with him in shifts, offers food and sleep he refuses, and corrects a delusion with a fact that cannot help. Volunteers observe his deterioration accurately and possess no means of intervening.

## The Reading This Produces

Assemble the parts and the claim can be named. Call it the breakdown-as-class-mark reading.

In The Great Gatsby, bodily and mental breakdown functions as a sign of powerlessness rather than of individual weakness. The novel establishes an ability norm through the muscular authority of Tom and the trained capacity of Jordan, positions that vigor as the visible form of wealth, and then locates every failing body in the valley of ashes or in the people who serve the shore. Wilson's deterioration is given a complete causal chain, from airborne particulate to economic dependence to the impossibility of rest, and is then reduced by an inquest to a phrase. The body in this novel bears the cost that the gilded world offloads, and the geography of Long Island is arranged so that the cost is paid on the road between the places where the money lives and the place where it is spent.

The reading does three things other approaches do not.

It explains why Fitzgerald gave the valley an anatomy. The ash grows like wheat, the men crumble, the air is described as producing spasms, the dust veils clothing and hair. That is not the vocabulary of moral symbolism. It is the vocabulary of exposure, and once a reader sees it as such the valley stops being a metaphor for the decay of the American dream and becomes the place where the dream is physically manufactured.

It explains the Eckleburg billboard's specificity. Of all the products Fitzgerald could have chosen for an abandoned advertisement, he chose corrective lenses, and he put the sign above a landscape that damages eyes. The symbol is precise rather than arbitrary.

And it explains the ending's machinery without recourse to coincidence. Readers often complain that the plot turns on an implausible series of accidents. It does not. It turns on a man whose capacity to be manipulated was built by his conditions, and Fitzgerald spent three chapters building it.

### How does the body bear the cost the elite offload?

Directly and physically. Ash produced by the city and the industries that enrich East Egg is dumped in a valley where unprotected men shovel it, breathe it, and are described as already crumbling. Wilson's anemia, exhaustion, and eventual collapse are the accumulated form of that transfer, and the inquest erases the history in a phrase.

The reading also reframes the novel's title character. Gatsby is usually read as the man who could not buy the one thing he wanted, and that is true. Under this lens he is also the man who bought a body. The boyhood schedule prescribed dumbbell exercise and wall-scaling at fixed morning hours, alongside the study of electricity and needed inventions, and a boy on a Minnesota farm wrote it down because he had understood that the able body was a credential he would have to acquire rather than inherit. He acquired it. Fitzgerald then gives him the vocabulary of physical competence, describing his resourcefulness of movement as peculiarly American, and gives him a swimming pool he uses once, on the last day of his life, because his gardener wanted to drain it before the leaves came.

That is the completion of the argument. In this novel a poor boy can construct an able body through discipline, and it will not protect him, because the protection was never in the body. It was in the money and the family standing behind it. Tom, who did nothing to earn his physique, remains intact. Gatsby, who built his, is shot in the water. Wilson, who could afford neither, breaks down and is erased by a coroner's phrase. Ability is not distributed by effort in this book, and Fitzgerald arranged three men across the range to prove it.

## The Counter-Reading: The Novel Has No Disability Dimension

The strongest objection is the one most readers hold instinctively. There are no disabled characters in The Great Gatsby. Nobody uses a wheelchair, nobody is blind, nobody is described as having a named condition. Wilson is sick for a day and then bereaved. Reading the book through disability studies is importing a framework onto a text with no material for it.

Four answers meet the objection.

The first is definitional. Disability studies does not require a diagnosed character any more than a class reading requires a character who describes himself as proletarian. It requires a text that sorts bodies by capacity, and this one sorts explicitly and repeatedly, granting muscle and leverage to one man, training and carriage to one woman, and a set of medical subtractions to another man.

The second is textual. The novel uses clinical vocabulary. "Anaemic" is a diagnosis. "Retinas" is an anatomical term applied to a billboard. Run down, sick, deranged by grief, and worn-out are all descriptions of bodily and mental states, and they are distributed by class with perfect consistency.

The third is structural. The plot's mechanism is a man in breakdown, and Fitzgerald documents the breakdown's production rather than asserting it. A novel that shows the ash, the labor, the lack of rest, and then the collapse has supplied a social-model account of impairment whether or not it had the term.

The fourth is historical. The decade in which the book is set and published was the peak period of American eugenics, and Fitzgerald put a man reading racial pseudoscience at the head of his most powerful family. Fitness discourse is in the novel's air.

Something survives from the objection and should be kept. This reading is not about representation, because there is no disabled community in the novel to represent or misrepresent. It is about how a text distributes capacity, which is a narrower and more defensible claim. An essay that promises a reading of disabled characters in Gatsby will not survive its first paragraph. An essay that promises a reading of how the novel produces and disposes of failing bodies will.

## The Limits of the Lens

Four limits are worth naming.

The first is proximity to Marxist reading. Nearly every finding here converges with a class analysis, and a careless essay will simply produce a Marxist reading in medical vocabulary. The distinguishing move is to keep the body itself in view: a Marxist reading explains the transfer of wealth, a disability reading explains what the transfer does to lungs, eyes, and nerves, and why the novel describes those effects at such length.

The second is the absence of disabled voices. Wilson never speaks about his own condition beyond three flat statements. The novel offers no perspective from inside an impaired body, which means the lens is always reading a text about damaged people rather than a text by or for them. That limitation should be stated rather than papered over.

The third is over-reading. Not every description of a body is a disability observation. Gatsby's tan and Daisy's voice do not become evidence merely because a lens has been picked up. The findings that hold are the ones concentrated in the valley and in Wilson, and an essay is stronger for admitting that the lens has a limited catchment in this novel and is decisive within it.

The fourth is the risk of instrumentalizing Wilson twice. Fitzgerald used him as machinery, and a critic who uses him only as an illustration of a theoretical concept has repeated the operation. The corrective is to read him as a person the novel documents: a man who worked, who wanted to move West, who could not afford to lie down when he was ill, and whose four years of neighborly presence Michaelis recalls at the inquest.

## How to Build an Essay Around This Lens

Argue about distribution, not about a character's illness. A thesis that says Wilson is sick is a plot summary. A thesis that says the novel distributes bodily capacity along class lines and uses the resulting breakdown as its plot mechanism is arguable, checkable, and could be wrong.

Choose four passages from different registers. The introduction of Wilson in chapter two supplies the medical vocabulary. The valley description supplies the environmental cause. Tom's introduction supplies the normate. The inquest phrase in chapter nine supplies the institutional erasure.

Analyze single words. The novel's "anaemic," plus crumbling, spasms, veiled, hollow-eyed, run down, and deranged. Each is a diction choice that encodes a condition, and examiners reward the reader who works at that scale rather than paraphrasing scenes.

Handle the anachronism openly and early. State that the academic field is recent, that its subject matter was contemporary, and that eugenic sorting, industrial injury, and mass war disablement were live realities in the 1920s. Then proceed.

Say what you are not doing. One sentence distinguishing this from a Marxist reading will do more for an essay than three paragraphs of general theory.

## Closing Verdict

Disability studies is the least obvious lens to bring to this novel and one of the most productive, because it asks a question the book answers on almost every page and that no other approach asks directly: who in this world is permitted to have a body that works?

The answer is that health is a possession of the wealthy, that the vigor Fitzgerald lavishes on Tom and Jordan is described in the same vocabulary of assertion he uses for social power, and that the bodies which fail are located, without exception, among people who cannot afford to rest. George Wilson is not a weak man who breaks. He is a man whose environment, occupation, poverty, and dependence produce a documented deterioration, which the novel then uses as its plot mechanism and the inquest erases with a phrase.

The verdict this article defends is that breakdown in The Great Gatsby is the mark of the powerless, and that Fitzgerald built the causal chain into the book even though he had no framework for naming it. Above the valley he placed an abandoned advertisement for the correction of vision, and beneath it he placed men shoveling the dust that ruins eyes. A reader who takes that arrangement seriously will never again read the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg as a free-floating symbol of a vanished God. They are a sign for a service the people below them needed, were sold, and never received, and the man who eventually mistakes them for God is the man the novel has spent seven chapters dismantling.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is a disability-studies reading of The Great Gatsby?

A disability-studies reading examines how the novel distributes bodily and mental capacity, treating breakdown, exhaustion, and vigor as socially produced rather than as natural facts about individuals. It does not require a diagnosed character. It requires a text that sorts people by what their bodies can do, and Fitzgerald's novel sorts constantly: muscle and leverage for Tom, athletic training for Jordan, and a series of medical subtractions for George Wilson. The lens asks which body counts as standard, who is permitted to be healthy, how the text explains collapse, whether it uses damaged bodies as plot machinery, and who can afford rest and repair. Applied here it produces a reading in which health is a class possession.

### Q: How does a disability lens read George Wilson's breakdown?

It reads the deterioration as produced by environment and economics rather than by personal weakness, and it notes that Fitzgerald supplied every link in the chain. Wilson is introduced with a medical adjective, "anaemic," alongside "spiritless." He works unprotected in a valley where airborne ash is described as veiling his suit and hair. When he is sick in chapter eight he refuses to go to bed because he would lose business, which locates the barrier in money rather than in his body. By the final stage he sways in a doorway, holds the doorposts, neither hears nor sees, and fixates on a swinging light. The inquest then reduces the whole history to a man deranged by grief.

### Q: Why does the novel prize the able body of the elite?

Because bodily capacity is presented as the visible form of social power. Fitzgerald describes Tom's body in terms of enormous power, a great pack of muscle, and leverage, and reports that his sturdy physical egotism once nourished his peremptory heart. Jordan has an erect carriage she throws backward at the shoulders like a young cadet, plays tournament golf, and refuses a cocktail because she is absolutely in training. Nick says her body asserted itself. Assertion is the same verb the novel attaches to Tom's dominance. Against this, the workers of the valley are described as crumbling. Capacity and standing are one measurement taken twice, which is why the healthy are also the powerful.

### Q: How does bodily breakdown mark the powerless in the novel?

Every failing body in the book belongs to someone without money. The ash-gray men move dimly and are already crumbling. The one child in the valley is gray and scrawny. Wilson is "anaemic," run down, hollow-eyed, and finally unable to stand unsupported. Meanwhile the wealthy sustain almost no bodily damage: Daisy bruises a knuckle and makes it a dinner anecdote, Jordan's car accident characterizes her rather than injuring her, and Tom's decline is registered as a mood. Injury travels in one direction throughout the novel, from the shore toward the valley, and the single reversal, Gatsby's shooting, is carried out by the poorest man in the book and ends the story.

### Q: Does The Great Gatsby have a disability dimension at all?

It does, though not in the form readers expect. There is no character with a named condition, no wheelchair, no blindness. What there is instead is a text saturated with clinical vocabulary distributed by class. "Anaemic" is a diagnosis. "Retinas" is an anatomical term Fitzgerald applies to a painted sign. Run down, sick, worn-out, and deranged by grief are all descriptions of bodily and mental states, and they attach exclusively to the poor. The plot's mechanism is a man in breakdown whose production the novel documents in detail. A reading that promises disabled characters will collapse immediately; a reading that examines how the novel produces and disposes of failing bodies has abundant material.

### Q: How does the body bear the cost the elite offload?

Physically and geographically. The ash that enriches nobody in the valley is the residue of the city and the industries that fund East Egg, and it is dumped where poor men shovel it with leaden spades into an impenetrable cloud. Fitzgerald describes the resulting dust as veiling clothing and hair, and describes the men themselves as growing out of the ash and already crumbling in it. Wilson's anaemia, exhaustion, and collapse are the accumulated form of that transfer. The valley sits on the road between where the money lives and where it is spent, so the people paying the cost are visible from every car that passes and structurally invisible to everyone inside them.

### Q: What is the medical model versus the social model of disability?

The medical model locates disability inside an individual body as a defect to be diagnosed, treated, or managed, so a person is disabled because their body fails to function normally. The social model, developed by British disabled activists in the 1970s and given academic form by Michael Oliver, separates impairment from disability: impairment is a bodily fact, while disability is what a society does with it through inaccessible environments, inflexible work, and assumptions about standard capacity. Fitzgerald's novel contains both. It labels Wilson with a medical adjective, which is the medical model as characterization, and it shows the ash, the unprotected labor, and the impossibility of rest, which is a social-model account without the term.

### Q: How do the ash-gray men figure in a disability reading?

They are the novel's clearest image of industrially produced impairment. Fitzgerald describes the valley as a fantastic farm where ashes grow like wheat, and says the ash takes the forms of houses and chimneys and finally, with a transcendent effort, of ash-gray men who move dimly and are already crumbling through the powdery air. The men are presented as the final product of the same process that makes the ridges and hills. When cars stop, they swarm up with leaden spades and raise an impenetrable cloud that screens their operations from view. Unprotected labor in airborne particulate produces exactly the presentation Wilson displays, and Fitzgerald placed cause and symptom two paragraphs apart.

### Q: Are the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg relevant to disability studies?

They are central to it. The billboard is an abandoned advertisement for an oculist, a specialist in correcting impaired vision, placed above a landscape that fills the air with dust. Fitzgerald describes the eyes as blue and gigantic with retinas one yard high, looking out from enormous yellow spectacles over a nonexistent nose. He then supplies the history: a wag of an oculist set them there to fatten his practice in Queens and sank down himself into eternal blindness, or forgot them and moved away. A sign promising the repair of eyes was abandoned above the people who most needed the service, and when Wilson mistakes it for God, Michaelis corrects him and the correction does not land.

### Q: How does a disability lens read Jordan Baker's athletic body?

Jordan is the novel's second normate and its test case. She is a professional golfer with an erect carriage, described as throwing her body backward at the shoulders like a young cadet, and she declines a cocktail because she is absolutely in training. Nick notes that her body asserted itself with a restless movement of her knee, using the same verb of physical speech the novel gives Tom. She is the one woman in the book with disciplined bodily capacity and no dependence on anyone. The narrator's response is suspicion. He decides she is incurably dishonest and links that judgment to her self-sufficiency, which suggests that able-bodied independence in a woman reads to him as a moral defect.

### Q: What does Myrtle Wilson's vitality mean under a disability lens?

It marks her as the one body in the valley that has not yet been converted into ash. Fitzgerald describes her as faintly stout, carrying her flesh sensuously, with a face containing no facet or gleam of beauty but an immediately perceptible vitality, as if "the nerves of her body were continually smouldering." He then specifies that the white ashen dust veiled everything in the vicinity except his wife. Vitality is precisely the quantity Wilson lacks and the valley removes. It is also her only asset and her only route out, and she spends it on a man who can buy health outright. The novel then destroys that body on a road, from a distance, in the language of an accident report.

### Q: Which passages should I cite for a disability-studies essay?

Four passages from different registers will carry a full argument. The introduction of Wilson in chapter two supplies the medical vocabulary and the subtractive description. The valley description that opens the same chapter supplies the environmental cause, the crumbling men, and the impenetrable cloud. Tom's introduction in chapter one supplies the normate, with its muscle, leverage, and enormous power. The inquest sentence in chapter nine, reducing Wilson to a man deranged by grief so the case could rest in its simplest form, supplies the institutional erasure. Add the Eckleburg description for the failed medical infrastructure and Wilson's refusal of bed rest for the economics of care.

### Q: What are the limits of a disability reading of this novel?

Four are worth naming. The findings converge heavily with a Marxist reading, so an essay must keep the body itself in view to avoid producing class analysis in medical vocabulary. There is no disabled perspective anywhere in the text, since Wilson never reflects on his own condition beyond three flat statements, which means the lens always reads a book about damaged people rather than one written from inside impairment. The lens over-reads easily, since not every bodily description is evidence. And there is a risk of using Wilson twice: Fitzgerald made him machinery, and a critic who treats him only as an illustration repeats the operation.

### Q: How does disability studies differ from a Marxist reading here?

They share almost all their evidence and differ in what they explain. A Marxist reading of the valley of ashes explains the extraction of value, the alienation of labor, and the way the shore's wealth depends on the valley's work. A disability reading explains what that arrangement does to lungs, eyes, and nerves, and asks why Fitzgerald spent so many words on the physical condition of the people involved rather than on their wages or their hours. The practical test in an essay is whether the argument would change if the workers were paid better. A Marxist reading says yes; a disability reading points out that they would still be breathing the ash.

### Q: Is it anachronistic to apply disability studies to 1925 fiction?

The academic field dates from the 1980s and 1990s, so its vocabulary is later than the novel. Its subject matter was not. American eugenics was at its most politically effective during the decade the novel describes, sorting people by measured fitness with legislative consequences including the immigration restrictions of 1924 and compulsory sterilization statutes in many states. The First World War had produced disabled survivors on an unprecedented scale. Industrial injury was routine and largely uncompensated. Fitzgerald put a man reading racial pseudoscience at the head of his most powerful household. The lens supplies terms for phenomena the novel observed rather than importing concerns the period lacked.

### Q: How does illness function as a plot device in the novel?

It supplies the entire mechanism of the ending. Wilson's sickness in chapter seven establishes that something is wrong, his pallor and shaking in chapter eight establish incapacity, his dissociation establishes that he can be directed, and his shattering supplies motive and instability. Tom then provides a piece of information and Wilson walks across Long Island. Fitzgerald built the instrument across three chapters, deployed it, and removed it, and Nick's phrase when the gardener finds the body, that the holocaust was complete, reads like the closing of an inventory. Disability critics call this pattern narrative prosthesis: a story leans on an anomalous body for structural work and discards it afterward.
