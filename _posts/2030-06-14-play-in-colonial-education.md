Romeo and Juliet entered the colonial classroom as paperwork before it entered as poetry. Across the British Empire the play was prescribed, printed, annotated and examined inside an education system whose architects stated their purpose in plain language: to manufacture a class of colonial subjects who would be, in Thomas Babington Macaulay's words of 1835, "Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals, and in intellect." The tragedy of two disobedient children, punished by death while their fathers repent over the bodies, was an almost perfect instrument for that design, and generations of students in Calcutta, Freetown, Mombasa, Port of Spain and a hundred lesser school towns met it first as a set book, under examination conditions, in editions prepared for minds the empire intended to form.

![A colonial-era schoolroom edition of the play open beside examination papers - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-04.webp)

The verdict of this article can be stated at the outset, because the documents support it in both directions and the honest answer is double. As policy, the brief's premise is confirmed: the play was used, deliberately and on the record, as a tool of colonial education, a carrier of the English language, of English moral furniture, and of the canon's authority to certify who counted as educated. As history, the same record convicts the policy of failure at its point of proudest confidence. The students the system examined memorized the play, quoted it back in essays written to please their examiners, and then, within a single generation in Bengal and within living memory elsewhere, translated it, rewrote it, indicted the curriculum that had assigned it, and built national literatures partly out of its materials. The instrument worked as a credential and failed as a cage. What follows weighs the machinery first, the textbook second, the student's actual encounter third, and the reassessment last, grading each claim by the strength of its evidence, because the colonial archive is eloquent about intentions and patchy about this play in particular, and the difference matters.

A word on method, since the grading recurs throughout. The colonial archive is a paper empire: minutes, despatches, calendars, commission reports, publishers' catalogues and examination papers survive in profusion, while the classroom itself, the hour in which a boy in Agra or Freetown construed the orchard scene, survives hardly at all. The historian of this subject therefore knows the system's intentions far better than its afternoons, and any honest article must mark the seam. Where a document names this play, the article says so and leans on it. Where a document proves the machinery that carried Shakespeare in general, the article says that instead, and refuses the silent promotion of a canon into a title. Where only memory speaks, in a memoir, a tribute or a writer's recollection of his schooldays, the article labels the evidence as memory, with a memoir's warmth and a memoir's limits. The grades assigned in the profile at the end are simply this method made visible, and readers who distrust the verdict are invited to audit the grades rather than the rhetoric.

## Why Romeo and Juliet? The Tragedy a Discipline-Minded School Could Use

No administrator sat down and chose this play from the whole canon by pure chance, and the text itself explains its own employability. Verona, as the Chorus announces, holds "Two households, both alike in dignity" (Prologue, line 1), and the city's ruler opens the action by pricing disorder: "If ever you disturb our streets again, / Your lives shall pay the forfeit of the peace" (1.1). The plot then exhibits, in sequence, every offense a paternal order most fears. A daughter is disposed of in marriage by negotiation between men: "But woo her, gentle Paris, get her heart" (1.2), Capulet instructs his suitor, in a scene where Juliet herself does not speak. A secret marriage defies both houses. A daughter who kneels and pleads is answered with the threat of the street: "An you be mine, I'll give you to my friend; / An you be not, hang, beg, starve, die in the streets" (3.5). The disobedient are then destroyed, the households reconciled over the corpses, and authority closes the book. For a curriculum whose stated aim was the formation of obedient intermediaries, a tragedy in which youthful rebellion ends in a vault and parental authority survives, chastened but enthroned, was preachable in a way that, say, the regicide of Macbeth or the filial ingratitude of Lear was not.

Yet the same text carries the audit that undoes the lesson, and it is placed in the mouth of the ruler himself. The Prince's closing verdict does not blame the children's passion. It blames the fathers' hatred: "See what a scourge is laid upon your hate" (5.3.291), and its summary is universal and includes the throne: "All are punish'd" (5.3). A schoolmaster preaching obedience from this play had to teach his class a closing speech in which the governing power arraigns the governors. The Friar's warning, "These violent delights have violent ends" (2.6), could be extracted as a moral for the young; the Prince's audit could not be extracted at all without indicting the old. This doubleness is not a modern critic's imposition on an innocent text. It is the play's own structure, available to any attentive schoolboy in any colony, and the history of the play's colonial afterlife is in large part the history of readers who noticed that the set book judged its setters. The curriculum chose the play for the discipline on its surface. The students kept it for the judgment underneath.

The surface offered one more convenience that should not be missed: the play's disorder begins at the bottom. Its first scene is a street brawl between servants, thumb-biting escalating to drawn swords, the city's laboring class performing its masters' grudge in the marketplace until the Prince restores order by decree. A colonial classroom, whose pupils were being trained to staff the lower rungs of an administration that feared the street above all things, was handed in the play's opening minutes a complete fable of its own anxieties: servants aping their betters' quarrels, private hatred becoming public riot, and salvation arriving only as the ruler's sentence. The scene could be taught, and was teachable, as the case for the Prince. What the same scene also shows, to a pupil whose own city was governed by decree, is a ruler whose authority rests on threatening death for a third offense, and whose peace, by the final scene, has failed in his own family. The fable grades its own moral, for any reader patient enough to reach the end of the set book.

## Before the Play: English Literature as a Substitute for the Bible

The machinery was assembled in India first, and its strangest feature is the one Gauri Viswanathan established in Masks of Conquest: Literary Study and British Rule in India (1989): English literature was taught as a systematic subject in Indian classrooms of the 1820s before it was institutionalized as a subject in England itself, and it was installed there to do a job the colonial state was forbidden to do directly. The evangelical wing of the design had stated its hopes a generation earlier, in Charles Grant's Observations on the State of Society among the Asiatic Subjects of Great Britain, written in 1792 and laid before Parliament in the charter debates: Grant, a Company director, argued that Britain owed its Indian subjects moral reformation and that English language and learning were the instruments by which a degraded society, as he judged it, could be remade. The Observations supplied the moral charter; the classroom supplied the method. Between Grant's pamphlet and the 1813 Act's education clause, the elements lay ready: a conviction that Indian character was the proper object of British policy, a bar on pursuing that object through open religion in state institutions, and a literature waiting to be conscripted as the object's respectable instrument. The Charter Act of 1813 had set aside a lakh of rupees for education and admitted missionaries to India, but the East India Company governed a population whose religions it had pledged not to disturb, and official policy barred the open Christian instruction of Indian students in state-supported institutions. Evangelical administrators wanted moral transformation; the state wanted order; neither could put the Bible at the center of a government classroom. Literature solved the problem. A secular canon, taught as the record of a people's moral imagination, could carry the formation of character while, in Viswanathan's formulation, suppressing its affiliation with the Christianity whose values it quietly transmitted. Shakespeare stood at the head of that canon, the mirror in which the student was to see the ideal Englishman and measure his own distance from him.

### Why could the colonial state not teach the Bible openly in its Indian classrooms?

Because the Company's rule rested on a declared policy of non-interference in the religions of its subjects, so the moral instruction the administrators wanted had to travel in disguise. English literature, and preeminently Shakespeare, became the disguise: a curriculum of character whose Christian furniture arrived as culture rather than doctrine.

The point deserves emphasis because it governs everything that follows in this article. The play was not in the colonial classroom because a syllabus committee admired it, though many did. It was there because a specific institutional problem, the need to govern morals without governing religion, had been solved by inventing a school subject, and the subject needed supreme texts. Hindu College in Calcutta was teaching English literature in the 1820s, a generation before Oxford founded its English school; the discipline of English studies was, in its curricular form, a colonial invention exported home.

The chronology of that export deserves precision, since it reverses the usual story of a metropolitan subject radiating outward. Oxford did not establish an honours school of English language and literature until 1894, and Cambridge did not examine its English tripos until the years after the First World War. In the decades when English literature had no university home in England, it had syllabi, textbooks, examinations and a captive readership in India. The reason is the one Viswanathan gives: in England the subject had to fight classics for a place, while in the colony it had a job to do, and the job funded it. English studies acquired its institutional form, its canon, its examination techniques and its claim to form character, in the place where forming character was a technology of rule. When the subject finally came home, it arrived with its colonial habits packed inside it: the set text, the moralized gloss, the examined appreciation. A student meeting this play in a twentieth-century classroom anywhere in the empire was meeting a discipline that Calcutta had helped to invent. When later sections of this article describe examination papers in Sierra Leone or school readers in Trinidad, they are describing the descendants of this Indian settlement of the 1820s and 1830s, in which a play about Verona became one of the load-bearing texts of a governing technology.

## Duff's Institution: The Missionary Wedge in Practice

The substitute-for-the-Bible argument can sound abstract until it is watched at work in a single institution, and Alexander Duff's General Assembly's Institution in Calcutta shows the mechanism with the casing off. Duff, the Church of Scotland's first missionary to India, arrived in May 1830 and opened his school on 13 July of that year, in a house at Jorasanko lent by a Hindu patron, with the reformer Rammohun Roy's encouragement smoothing its acceptance among Hindu parents. Where the state was barred from doctrine, Duff was not, and he built the freestanding version of the same design: an English-medium school teaching the full range of secular subjects to university standard, with the Bible read and explained daily in the higher classes, aimed deliberately at the sons of the affluent and the high-caste, whose conversion, he calculated, would carry society with it. He called the method his own downward filter: reach the classes who influence others, and let the influence descend.

Duff's theory of the medium was more radical than the state's. He held that English learning was itself solvent: in the very act of acquiring proficiency in English, he argued, the student becomes "tenfold less" the child of what Duff called pantheism, idolatry and superstition than before, because the language carried Christian habits of thought inside its literature and its science. The curriculum was the conversion strategy. A boy who came for the commercial value of English would imbibe, through its books, a universe in which his ancestral religion appeared as a "stupendous system of error," and would arrive, by reasoning his teachers staged for him, at the faith they had planted at the center. Duff wrote the case up for the public in his pamphlet A New Era of the English Language and Literature in India, and the state's 1835 resolution adopted the medium while dropping the missionary's explicit aim, which is the precise difference between Duff's classroom and Macaulay's system: the mission school could say what it was doing; the government school did the same work under the mask Viswanathan describes.

### What did Duff expect English learning itself to do to a Hindu student's religion?

He expected it to dissolve the religion from inside. Duff taught that proficiency in English reordered the student's terms of reference, because English literature and science arrived steeped in Christian assumptions; the pupil who mastered the language would find his inherited beliefs indefensible in the only vocabulary his education had left him, and would reason his way to conversion.

The results, recorded honestly, were mixed in the pattern this article keeps meeting. Duff's institution prospered, drew the sons of the elite, and made celebrated converts, among them Kali Charan Banerjee and Lal Behari Dey, men formed at the center of the design. It also educated, in the same classrooms and by the same methods, graduates who carried the formation into public life against the missionary's purposes: Surendranath Banerjee, founder of the Indian National Association, passed through the orbit of this educational world without the conversion it was engineered to produce. The wedge split the wood, but the pieces did not fall where the wedge aimed. Between Duff's school, where the aim was avowed, and the state's schools, where it was masked, the play's classroom stood downstream of both: a text of the English canon, taught for its moral furniture, in institutions whose founders disagreed about everything except the instrument.

Dey's later career gives the mission classroom its most ironic exhibit. Lal Behari Dey, the convert Duff's design had worked exactly as drawn, grew into one of the earliest Indian writers of English prose fiction and social description, author of Bengal Peasant Life (1874), a portrayal of the Bengali countryside written in the acquired language for the acquired language's readers. The wedge had been driven to make an English Christian. It made an English author, one who turned the instrument on the society the wedge-makers administered, describing the peasantry's world from inside it in the colonizer's tongue. The pattern is the adjudication's, met early and at the mission's own hearth: the formation was real, the use was the graduate's.

## Macaulay's Minute and the Class of Interpreters

The settlement received its charter on 2 February 1835, when Macaulay, as president of the Committee of Public Instruction, wrote the Minute that settled the Orientalist-Anglicist controversy in favor of English. The document is not coy. It dismisses the learned literatures of India and Arabia in the most quoted sentence of colonial education history: "a single shelf of a good European library was worth the whole native literature of India and Arabia." It then states the social design with equal clarity. "It is impossible for us, with our limited means, to attempt to educate the body of the people. We must at present do our best to form a class who may be interpreters between us and the millions whom we govern; a class of persons, Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals, and in intellect." Lord William Bentinck, the Governor-General, endorsed the Minute within weeks, recording beneath it on 7 March 1835 his "entire concurrence to the sentiments expressed," and the funds followed the words: English-medium instruction for the few, who would then, in the theory later called downward filtration, refine the vernaculars and carry knowledge to the many.

Filtration is the hinge between the Minute and the play. A system designed to form a thin intermediary class through a prestigious foreign literature needs texts that certify: difficult enough to mark the graduate, prestigious enough to reward the effort, morally legible enough to form the character the design required. Shakespeare's plays met the specification so exactly that within decades they were the standard furniture of Indian college courses and, in time, of examination systems across the empire. Macaulay's own literary formation ran through the same canon; his essays treat Shakespeare as the summit of the national literature the interpreters were to internalize. What the Minute supplied was not a reading list but a market: a guaranteed, examination-driven demand for English literary texts, taught to students whose careers depended on mastering them, in institutions from Calcutta to Freetown that the rest of this article follows. Article 429 in this series examines what the modern Indian boards did with that inheritance; the colonial machinery that built the inheritance is the subject here, and its first classroom was already open when Macaulay wrote.

The settlement was moderated within four years, and the moderation is part of the record. Under Lord Auckland, Bentinck's successor, the administration of 1839 restored a measure of patronage and stipends to the Oriental institutions and encouraged the translation of English works into Indian languages, conceding in practice what the Orientalists had argued in theory, that a literature dropped from a height reaches nobody. The concession changed the weather without changing the climate. English remained the language of advancement, the examinations remained the gate, and the set plays kept their places at the gate's center; but the state's own retreat from the Minute's purity is early evidence that the design met its limits first in the administrators' budgets and only later in the students' politics. Bombay, meanwhile, had built the western presidency's version of the machine under Mountstuart Elphinstone's patronage, its college teaching the same English canon to the same certifying end, so that by the time the universities opened in 1857 the literary curriculum was a three-presidency system and not a Calcutta eccentricity.

What was displaced deserves a paragraph, because the Minute's victory was a choice between two curricula, not a gift to an empty country. The Orientalists, led in the argument by H. H. Wilson, had built the state's patronage around Sanskrit and Arabic learning, the Calcutta Madrasa, founded under Warren Hastings in 1781, the Benares Hindu College, founded under Jonathan Duncan in 1791, and the translation of European science into classical and vernacular languages. Macaulay's settlement starved that system: stipends to its students were to end, its presses to fall silent, its funds released to English education alone. The play's classroom was thus also a replacement classroom. Students who might have spent their youth on Kalidasa under state patronage spent it, after 1835, on Shakespeare under examination, and the substitution was understood at the time as civilizational, which is why Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay's essay of 1873, setting Kalidasa's Shakuntala beside Shakespeare's Miranda and Desdemona to argue the comparison on equal terms, reads as an act of recovery: a product of the new curriculum using its tools to repatriate the old one's masterpiece into the same critical room.

## Trevelyan and the Manufacture of Dependence

Macaulay's brother-in-law supplied the doctrine's fullest statement, and it is franker than the Minute. Charles Edward Trevelyan, whose groundwork fed the Minute itself, published On the Education of the People of India in 1838, a book-length defense of the settlement that describes its intended product in a sentence no postcolonial critic would dare invent: "There is no class of our subjects to whom we are so thoroughly necessary as those whose opinions have been cast in the English mould." Dependence, not enlightenment, is the stated yield. The English-educated Indian, severed by his education from the intellectual authorities of his own civilization, would require the British connection to make sense of his own mind, and would therefore hold it fast. Trevelyan's famous traveler's detail runs the other way and complicates the picture honestly: the steamboats on the Ganges, he reported, were boarded by boys "begging, not for money, but for books," and he pointed to Hindu College, founded by the voluntary contributions of its Indian patrons, as proof that the desire for English literature and science was indigenous and eager before the state supplied it. Both things are in the book, the design for dependence and the evidence of demand, and the article's verdict requires holding them together: a genuine Indian appetite for English learning was conscripted into a design whose author valued it precisely because it manufactured political dependence. Shakespeare's place in that design is the place of the mould itself, the supreme literature in which opinions were to be cast.

The mould metaphor clarifies what a set play was for. A mould is not read; it is poured into. The curriculum's texts were expected to shape the student's taste so completely that English judgments would feel like his own judgments, which is the interior condition Trevelyan calls necessity. Against this design stands the stubborn material fact that a play is not a mould but a machine for generating disagreement, and this play in particular arrives with its authority figures arraigned by its final speech. The examiners could standardize the questions. They could not standardize what a clever boy in Calcutta or Freetown would conclude from the Prince's verdict, and the sections that follow trace that unstandardized residue through the system's own records.

## Hindu College: The Classroom Where the System Began

Hindu College, founded in Calcutta on 20 January 1817 by a coalition of Indian reformers and British sympathizers, is the documented birthplace of the colonial Shakespeare classroom, and its record corrects any simple picture of imposition from above. The college was an Indian initiative before it was an imperial instrument: the city's Hindu elite wanted English learning for their sons, and the institution they founded taught English literature from its early years, with lectures delivered in English from 1826. Reports of the curriculum in 1827 and 1828 list Milton and Shakespeare among the authors studied, and the college's surviving syllabus descriptions name Shakespeare's plays, Richardson's Selections, Bacon's Essays and Milton's poems as the furniture of its English literature course. Sustained literary teaching of exactly the kind Viswanathan describes was underway in Calcutta while Macaulay was still in England.

The teachers made it vivid. Henry Louis Vivian Derozio, appointed in 1826 at the age of seventeen, taught a curriculum whose recorded contents ran from Goldsmith's histories and Pope's Homer to Paradise Lost and, in the record's exact and tantalizing phrase, "one of the tragedies by Shakespeare." His students, the circle remembered as Young Bengal, responded to English literature not with obedience but with an intoxicated free-thinking that alarmed the college's managers enough to dismiss him in 1831. His successor generation fared better with the authorities: David Lester Richardson, whose reading aloud so impressed Macaulay that the Minute's author praised the sound of English verse in an Indian classroom, encouraged his students to perform, and in 1837 those students staged The Merchant of Venice. Hema Dahiya's study of Shakespeare at Hindu College traces the succession of its Shakespeare teachers, Derozio, Richardson and H. M. Percival among them, and Rangana Banerji's essay on the uses of Shakespeare in colonial Bengal takes its title from the period's own boast: "Every College Student Knows By Heart."

The syllabus's own wording preserves the pedagogy's first stage: the extract. Hindu College's English literature course is recorded as comprising "Richardson's Selections, Shakespeare's plays, Francis Bacon's Essays, Milton's poetical works, Addison's Essays" and their companions, and the sequence is instructive. Before the whole play came the selection, the bound gathering of beauties through which a student in a second language could be led into the canon by its most quotable doors. Richardson, the college's own teacher, compiled the selections his classroom used, an early instance of the pattern the Deighton section documents at industrial scale: the man who taught the text also manufactured the form in which it was met. The extract stage mattered beyond Calcutta. It is the same device the Royal Readers carried to Caribbean elementary schools and the same device by which, across the system, the canon was administered in doses calibrated to the pupil's certified level, beauties first, whole tragedies at the examination summit. The play reached many of its colonial readers in pieces before it reached them entire, and the pieces had been chosen by the system's own editors.

### Which Shakespeare tragedy did Derozio teach, and does the record name it?

The record does not name it. The surviving account of Derozio's Hindu College curriculum lists Paradise Lost "and one of the tragedies by Shakespeare" without identifying the play, and honesty requires leaving the blank unfilled rather than writing this play's title into a syllabus that does not contain it.

That distinction is the first lesson of the colonial archive. For Hindu College, the documentation is strong for Shakespeare, strong for the performance culture Richardson built, and silent, at title level, for Romeo and Juliet. The play enters the Indian record by name through other doors: the examination system that the new universities built after 1857, and the textbook trade that supplied it. The record turns next to the examination, the empire's most effective literary machine.

The college's graduates show what the classroom made possible once the syllabus was inside them. Michael Madhusudan Dutt, educated in the Hindu College world, began as an English poet, wrote his first plays under Shakespeare's shadow, and then carried the formation into Bengali, introducing blank verse to the language and building its modern tragedy out of Hindu epic material: the curriculum's instrument reforged, within one career, into a national literature's founding tools. His contemporary judgment survives in a letter of 1860, arguing that European drama dealt in hard realities and lofty passion where the Bengali drama he inherited offered softness and romance, a colonial classroom's hierarchy of taste spoken by its most gifted product, and already turned to the project of raising his own language to the standard. Hindu College did not make Dutt obedient. It made him ambitious in two languages, which is a fair summary of the system's actual yield across the century.

Delhi College supplies the northern variation. Refounded in the 1820s on the site of an older madrasa, it combined English teaching with an ambitious programme of translating Western works into Urdu, so that the new learning entered its classrooms wearing two languages at once. The institution matters here because it breaks the binary the Minute tried to impose: in Delhi, for a generation, English literature and Oriental learning were not enemies but colleagues, and the students who passed through encountered the English canon inside a bilingual institution that treated translation as scholarship rather than surrender. The Anglicist settlement closed that experiment's funding logic, but the Delhi record stands as evidence that the classroom empire contained, from the start, an alternative arrangement in which Shakespeare might have been a guest instead of a governor.

## The Examination Empire: Set Books from Calcutta to Freetown

Policy formed the class; examinations policed it. The universities founded at Calcutta, Bombay and Madras in 1857, on the model urged by the education despatch of 1854, were examining bodies before they were teaching ones, and their calendars converted English literature from an accomplishment into a credential. They taught almost nothing themselves. Their affiliated colleges, scattered across each presidency, did the teaching to the university's syllabus and presented their students for the university's papers, so that a single calendar in Calcutta governed the reading of hundreds of classrooms its authors never saw. Above the universities stood the summit credential of all: the Indian Civil Service examinations, held in London from the 1850s, at which the future rulers of Indian districts were examined in subjects that included English literature and history. The ladder thus ran unbroken from the schoolroom's set play to the proconsul's desk, every rung an English paper, and a young man's Shakespeare was not an ornament on the ladder. It was the ladder's material.

The London summit cut both ways, and its grievance belongs in this record. The civil service examination's location, its syllabus weighted toward the English public-school curriculum, and a maximum entry age lowered in the 1870s to a point that favored boys fresh from English schools, made the summit formally open to Indian candidates and practically fenced against them. Satyendranath Tagore, the first Indian to enter the service, passed in 1863; the trickle that followed became a standing nationalist exhibit, proof that the empire's examinations certified a fairness they were arranged not to deliver. The literary ladder therefore taught two lessons at once, and taught them to the same students: mastery of the canon was the price of advancement, and mastery would not be honored at the advertised rate. No classroom text caused that discovery. The examination system delivered it, with the set plays as its most respectable furniture, and the discovery's alumni include a striking share of the men who would later dismantle the system in prose the system had taught them to write.

The universities' own first products make the same point at degree level. Among the first two men to take the BA of the new University of Calcutta in 1858 was Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay, who would become the founding novelist of Bengali prose and the author, in 1873, of the essay that set Kalidasa's Shakuntala beside Shakespeare's heroines as an equal. The examining university had existed for one year when it graduated the writer who would do more than any other to give Bengali its modern literary prose, and his formation ran through the syllabus this section describes: the English canon mastered under examination, then turned, with full knowledge of its weight, to the revaluation of the Sanskrit canon the Minute had priced at a shelf. If a single biography were required to stand for the system's yield, the first BA class of Calcutta supplies it. A prescribed play, studied for a paper, answered in English prose under time pressure, became a unit of career currency across the empire's clerkships, schoolmasterships and subordinate services.

The class of interpreters, in the event, turned out to be an army of subordinates. Below the thin covenanted service stood the vast uncovenanted establishment: deputy magistrates and subordinate judges, revenue clerks and record keepers, schoolmasters, inspectors, translators, the paper machinery of the Raj staffed overwhelmingly by Indians educated in the system's own classrooms. This was filtration's real harvest, not the proconsular elite the rhetoric imagined but a literate intermediary stratum numbering in the tens of thousands, whose qualification was exactly the certificate the literary curriculum issued. For this stratum the set play was neither ornament nor imperial sacrament. It was a line on a certificate that decided a family's income for a generation, and its prestige in the culture followed from that fact. A curriculum's deepest root is the salary it feeds, and the canon's Indian entrenchment, which outlasted every policy reversal from Auckland's moderation to the Hunter branching, is unintelligible without the payroll beneath it. The system's designers had wanted interpreters between rulers and ruled. They got them, at the price of making interpretation itself an Indian profession, practiced daily at the state's expense and, in the end, at the state's expense in the second sense as well.

The universities themselves were the despatch's children. Charles Wood's education despatch of 1854, the document Indian historiography calls the Magna Carta of English education in India, ordered a complete ladder: education departments in every province, graded schools, grants to private effort, and at the top, universities at the three presidency capitals modeled on London's examining pattern. The ladder's logic was the Minute's logic, systematized. Every rung was to be inspected, examined and certified, and the certificate at each level purchased admission to the next. A literary curriculum sat naturally inside such a machine, because literature, unlike laboratory science, could be examined from a book, at scale, by post, in any district town with a hall and an invigilator. The play's portability was part of its utility: a tragedy that required no apparatus, no apparatus budget and no local expertise beyond a teacher who had himself passed the same papers could be prescribed from Calcutta to the smallest mofussil college and examined to a common standard. Standardization was the despatch's gift to the set book, and the set book returned the gift by giving the standard a human face. The set-book system had two properties that mattered for this play. It standardized a small canon, since examiners rotated a limited shelf of approved texts, and it exported the standard wholesale, since colonial institutions affiliated to British universities or to British examining boards taught to papers they did not set. The play's presence in the system is documented at both ends of that chain: in the annotated editions manufactured for the Indian market, examined in the next section, and in the affiliation arrangements that carried identical examinations overseas.

Two companion settlements completed the machine in the same decades, and both raised the set play's value. The first was linguistic: Persian, the inherited language of Indian administration, was displaced in the later 1830s, and English installed at the superior levels of the courts and the revenue system, so that the language of the schoolroom's canon became the language in which the empire addressed its subjects when it judged and taxed them. A student construing Shakespeare was no longer acquiring a foreign accomplishment; he was acquiring the master's tongue in its most prestigious register, the register in which his own petitions would one day need to be written. The second settlement was the system's own first review. The Hunter Commission of 1882, charged with judging what the 1854 despatch had built, reported a lopsided structure: primary schooling starved while the subsidized literary ladder flourished, and it urged that mass vernacular education be funded and the secondary course branched into literary and practical streams. The recommendations conceded the Minute's design flaw from inside the machine, that filtration filtered mostly for the ladder itself. The set plays were untouched by the concession and indeed by the branching, since the literary stream was precisely where their certifying value resided, which is why the commission's membership matters to the next section.

Fourah Bay College in Freetown is the purest African case. Founded by the Church Missionary Society in 1827 to train teachers and clergy, it affiliated to the University of Durham in 1876, and the terms of affiliation were exact: students at Fourah Bay studied the same curriculum and took examinations identical to those administered to Durham's own students. For ninety years, until the affiliation ended in 1967, a student in Sierra Leone could earn an English degree without leaving West Africa, at the price of meeting English examiners on English ground, inside a liberal-arts curriculum of Latin, Greek, Hebrew, Arabic, history, natural science and modern languages in which English literary study traveled as part of the Durham inheritance. The college drew students from across Anglophone West Africa, and its graduates staffed the schools and services of four future nations. Whatever play stood on a given year's Durham papers stood, by the same post, on the papers at Fourah Bay.

### What did it mean in practice that Fourah Bay students sat Durham's own examinations?

It meant the examination paper was the colony's real curriculum. A syllabus can be adapted locally; an identical external paper cannot. Fourah Bay's affiliation made an English university's judgment the measure of a West African degree, so Durham's texts, standards and literary values governed what was taught in Freetown.

The same machinery, at school level, was the Cambridge Overseas School Certificate, the examination through which the senior classes of colonial secondary schools were taught to papers set in England. The record from the British Southern Cameroons states the pattern plainly: senior classes used the programme of the Cambridge Overseas School Certificate Examination, with emphasis on English language and literature, and in the literature paper, consciously dubbed "English literature," the compulsory study of authors such as Shakespeare, Chaucer, Dickens and Tennyson. In Northern Rhodesia the final school examination was likewise set in England, and candidates competed for the Cambridge certificate or the London matriculation. In the Caribbean, as Giselle Rampaul's research traces, Shakespeare entered first through the extracts printed in the Royal Readers used in the region's elementary schools and then, at the elite secondary schools, as full plays required for the Cambridge examinations that gated colonial careers. England's own schools would eventually rebuild this examination culture for themselves, in the system the series examines in [Romeo and Juliet in the UK GCSE](/2030/03/08/romeo-and-juliet-uk-gcse/); the colonies sat its imperial original, set at a distance, marked to a metropolitan standard, and indifferent to local circumstance by design.

Against this centralizing record stands one documented counter-current, and it must be weighed rather than waved away. The 1925 memorandum on education policy in British Tropical Africa, issued after the Phelps-Stokes commissions had criticized the bookishness of African schooling, advised adapting education to the life and languages of the communities served. The advice was real, and in primary schooling it had effects. But the secondary track that led to certificates, clerkships and Fourah Bay kept its English papers, because the credential's market value was its Englishness. Adaptation governed the bottom of the system; the Cambridge programme governed the top, and the top is where set plays live. The two policies coexisted for forty years without the memorandum dislodging the examination, which is the clearest evidence in the African record of what the machinery was for: not the diffusion of useful knowledge downward, but the certification of a literary standard upward.

## The Annotated Play: Deighton's Edition and the Textbook as Primer

A set book requires an edition, and the edition is where the curriculum touches the student's hands. For this play in the Indian market, the documentary trace is unusually concrete. Macmillan published Romeo and Juliet with introduction and notes by Kenneth Deighton in 1893, a volume of some two hundred pages in the publishing system that supplied the empire's classrooms, and the edition was reprinted into the following decades; a 1916 issue survives with Deighton's apparatus intact, and its explanatory notes remained in circulation as a standard reference long after. The editor was not a London man of letters decorating a classic. The Royal Literary Fund's archive records Kenneth Deighton as Principal of Agra Government College and a commissioner of the Indian Education Commission: a senior officer of the very system that prescribed the texts he annotated, preparing, from inside the machinery, the instruments through which Indian students would meet the play.

Joya Mannan's study of Macmillan's English Classics, "Publishing Shakespeare in India," reads Deighton's Shakespeare editions as an attempt to use their extensive explanatory notes as a primer on Englishness for Indian readers: the notes do not merely construe the text, they tutor the colonial student in the manners, assumptions and moral reflexes of the culture that produced it, at the level of the gloss. The apparatus is the curriculum in miniature. A student who worked through Deighton's Romeo and Juliet learned the play, and learned, note by note, what an English reader was expected to feel about a father, a feud, a marriage, a suicide. This is Viswanathan's mask at the scale of the footnote: the moral governance of the classroom, executed not by proclamation but by annotation, in a book the student bought because an examination required it.

Deighton's career places the edition inside the system's own review of itself. The Indian Education Commission of 1882, chaired by W. W. Hunter, was the Raj's great stocktaking of the education the 1854 despatch had built, and the Literary Fund's record names Deighton among its commissioners: the editor of the school Shakespeare had sat on the body charged with judging the school system. The conjunction is emblematic rather than incidental. Colonial education was a small world, in which the men who reviewed the machinery also manufactured its textbooks, and the standards by which Indian schooling was judged were written by the same hands that glossed its set plays. When the system congratulated itself on the spread of English literature, it was grading its own apparatus, in both senses of the word.

### Why did Macmillan's English Classics fail to conquer the Indian market they were built for?

Mannan's answer is that the publisher misread a field that already existed. India had its own Shakespeare culture before the series arrived, in colleges, in Bengali print, in its own teachers and editions, and the pedantic apparatus that treated Indian readers as empty vessels met buyers who were anything but empty.

The episode refines the article's verdict in an important way. The textbook layer of the colonial project, the layer most directly designed to form the student's inner life, is the layer where the record shows friction, refusal and appropriation earliest. The play prospered in India; Deighton's primer did not prosper as designed. Students sat the examinations, bought what the examinations required, and read past the notes to a text they proceeded to make their own, a sequence the next sections document in the students' own traces.

## The Play They Thought They Were Teaching: The Curriculum's Moral and the Text's Reply

Between the policy and the student stood an interpretation, the reading of the play that the curriculum carried with it into the classroom. It is worth reconstructing exactly, because the reassessment argues with it still. In the reading the system promoted, the play was a cautionary tale in a ordered world. The fathers arrange, the friar counsels patience, the children rebel in secret, and the rebellion is answered by death with an arithmetic neatness that a schoolmaster could diagram: haste kills, disobedience kills, the feud's ancient grudge is the only villain permitted full size, and the survivors' reconciliation restores the moral order the young had broken. Deighton's notes, on Mannan's showing, tutored this reading at the level of feeling, teaching the student which sympathies an English reader was expected to supply. The lovers, in this frame, are a warning dressed as a romance, and Juliet's end is the wages of a daughter's self-disposal. It is a coherent reading. It is also, in the judgment this article argues, a reading the play tolerates only if the class stops before the speeches that break it.

### What happens to Juliet's desire inside the curriculum's moral reading?

It has to be muted, because the play gives its heroine the most ardent speech of wanting in the canon. "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds" (3.2) is a bride awaiting her wedding night, impatient and explicit in its longing, unrebuked by the text. A moral frame built on disobedience punished cannot digest a heroine whose desire the verse celebrates.

The muting is the tell. A curriculum confident of its text does not need to manage its heroine's soliloquy; a curriculum using the text needs the soliloquy quiet, because Juliet in 3.2 is not repenting, not erring in any way the scene marks as error, but burning with a sanctioned marriage's consummation while the audience is asked to burn with her. The same pressure applies at the play's other pole. Her first words about Romeo run against every loyalty the classroom preached: "My only love sprung from my only hate! / Too early seen unknown, and known too late!" (1.5). The line's judgment falls on the hate, not the love: the inherited enmity is the accident, the love the substance, and a student formed by the feud-ridden societies of the colonial world, divided by caste, sect, race and clan in arrangements the empire administered and profited from, did not need a teacher's permission to hear the application. The curriculum's moral required the play to be about the danger of young feeling. The play, read whole, is about the criminality of old hatred. Both readings sit in the same set book, and the colonial history of the text is the history of the second reading escaping the first, in classroom after classroom, at the pace of the students' own circumstances.

There is a further inheritance the moral frame could not tax, and it is the one students carried out of the hall in their mouths. The play is a school of feeling's rhetoric: Juliet's "My bounty is as boundless as the sea, / My love as deep" (2.2) gives a young speaker a grammar for an interior life conducted in defiance of the house's arrangements, and Romeo's hyperboles, mocked inside the play itself, nonetheless teach the idiom in which private conviction outbids public duty. An examination can test a paraphrase of such speeches. It cannot confiscate the competence they build, the ability to imagine one's own longing as a subject worthy of the language's highest register. The selections and readers that introduced the canon by its beauties were, in effect, distributing this competence in extracts, to children the system intended to make clerks. What a clerk does with a tragic heroine's grammar of desire is not the system's to schedule, and the literary history of every colonized language in this record shows the competence being spent, within a generation, on the novel, the essay and the national epic, forms in which interior lives judged their worlds rather than the reverse.

This is also where the edition question and the interpretation question join. An annotated school edition does not merely explain a text; it patrols the border between the two readings, deciding by emphasis, by silence and by the moral temperature of its notes which play the student is holding. Mannan's account of Deighton's apparatus is therefore the key document of this section: the notes are the curriculum's moral, materialized as typography. That the Indian market could refuse the primer while keeping the play shows the border patrol failing in the most literal commercial sense. The students bought the tragedy and declined the escort.

The curriculum's approved adult deserves his own audit, because he is inside the text. Friar Laurence is the play's credentialed intermediary, the educated man between the houses, and the system could point to his counsel as its own voice in Verona: "Wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast" (2.3), he tells Romeo, in the idiom of every schoolmaster who ever set the play. His couplets are the play's most extractable matter, sententiae that a selections culture could lift cleanly for the copybook: the Friar warning that "they stumble that run fast," preaching that violent delights have violent ends, moralizing young men's love as lying in their eyes rather than their hearts. A curriculum mining the play for governable wisdom would quarry the Friar first, and the quarry is real; his speeches are the tragedy's built-in commonplace book. The trap is that the commonplaces belong to the plot's chief engineer of disaster, a man whose every wise sentence is spoken in service of a scheme the play destroys. The extracts, severed from the catastrophe, taught prudence. The whole play taught what the prudent man's machinery did. Which of the two a student received depended on whether his classroom gave him the anthology or the tragedy, and the examination, whatever the syllabus intended, kept setting the tragedy. He is also the author of the catastrophe's mechanism. His secret marriage, his potion, his letter entrusted to a man the plague detains: the tragedy runs on the intermediary's well-meant machinery, and it ends with the Friar standing before the Prince, in his own words, "both to impeach and purge" himself (5.3), at once the plot's engineer and its penitent. A colonial pupil attentive to his set book met, in the Friar, the exact figure his own education was manufacturing, the interpreter between rulers and ruled, and watched the interpretation fail at every joint. The resonance was available without any teacher naming it, which is the property that made the book ungovernable: its most respectable character is its most instructive failure.

## What Colonial Students Actually Encountered

Between the memorandum and the graduate stood the lesson, and the lesson's texture is recoverable from three kinds of evidence: what students wrote, what they remembered, and what their teachers' employers feared. Viswanathan's most quoted evidence is of the first kind. Reading Indian students' examination essays, she found young men performing gratitude to the colonial power for improving what they had been taught to call, in the examiners' own vocabulary, the "native mind and condition": students describing themselves, in English essays written for English examiners, with the colonizer's word for themselves. It is the single most damning document of the system's interior success, and it should be quoted in every honest account: the machinery could, at its best, make the examined speak of themselves as the examiners did.

The second kind of evidence complicates the first. Banerji's title, "Every College Student Knows By Heart," records the period's other truth: the learning was real, deep and retained for life, carried in memory by men who would staff the professions, the press and eventually the nationalist movement. Learning by heart is ambiguous evidence. It can be the rote that officials feared, and it can be possession, the text taken into the student's own keeping, beyond the examiner's reach. Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, remembering his Kenyan schooling in Decolonising the Mind (1986), supplies the receiving end in one sentence: at his school, Shakespeare's greatness was presented as "one more English gift to the world alongside the bible and the needle." The simile's contempt is the student's retrospective verdict on the gift framing, delivered by a writer the colonial school system educated and then lost to its own purposes. A published biographical tribute to Burjor Jal Avari, who grew up in Mombasa and took his Cambridge Overseas School Certificate there in the 1950s, preserves the timetable behind the slogan: learning tables by heart to forty, reciting the dictionary page by page, and "cramming line by line Shakespeare's immortal plays" as the ordinary discipline of an Asian schoolboy in colonial Kenya, rewarded with a scholarship to England. The encounter, in other words, was not an afternoon with a classic. It was years of memorized English, of which the plays were the prestige portion, and it formed the examinable self that the system's critics and its graduates describe from opposite sides.

The third evidence is the employers' anxiety, and Sanjay Seth's Subject Lessons: The Western Education of Colonial India (2007) makes it the center of the story. Colonial officials and Indian nationalists alike worried, for opposite reasons, that the students were learning by rote and therefore acquiring no true knowledge, and that Western education had plunged its recipients into a moral crisis, torn between the knowledge of the classroom and the beliefs of the home. For Seth, these twin anxieties are the telling documents: they register the system's failure to produce the modern subject it presupposed, the self Macaulay's design required and the classroom could not manufacture. The interpreters were indeed formed. They were formed anxious, divided and critically minded, which is not what the Minute ordered.

Memorization had a public stage as well as a private discipline. The colonial school year displayed itself at prize days and public examinations of the spoken kind, where recitation was the pupil's proof made audible, and the set author's speeches were the standard exhibition pieces of the culture: the boy who could deliver his Shakespeare by heart before the inspector, the parents and the district officer was performing the system's success in the system's own terms. Rangana Banerji's title, "Every College Student Knows By Heart," is drawn from the period's pride in exactly this display. The performance cuts the same way as the private cramming did. A speech learned for the inspector belonged to the learner's tongue thereafter, available for occasions no inspector convened, and the archive of anti-colonial oratory is salted with the set books' cadences. The system's proudest classroom ritual was also its least controllable legacy, since a memorized text cannot be unmemorized when its owner changes sides.

The encounter had a linguistic texture that the policy documents never mention and the 1962 conference finally stated aloud. The colonial lesson in a difficult English text proceeded by construal: the teacher glossed, the class parsed, the notes were copied, the passage was rendered into the vernacular and back, and the examination rewarded the rendering. Ashmead's report of the British Council conference records the arithmetic of the mismatch in India: the official equipment expected of a high-school pupil ran to some hundreds of English structures and a few thousand words, "but with this equipment Indian students are unable to read such standard literary works as those of Shakespeare, Lamb, Shelley, or Keats." The sentence is the system's confession at ground level. A curriculum that prescribed the supreme texts of the language to pupils officially equipped with a phrase-book's vocabulary had built failure into the daily lesson, and the failure was absorbed, characteristically, by the students' industry and the bazaar of aids that grew up to serve them: the annotated edition, the copied note, the memorized paraphrase. Deighton's primer was one respectable stratum of that aid economy. Beneath it stretched the unrespectable strata the examiners deplored and the students required, and the play reached most of its colonial readers through the strata, not through the syllabus's clean intentions.

Seth's archive widens the point beyond the schoolboy. His chapters follow the colonial education debates into the questions the system could not settle: how to educate Indian women without unhousing them from the domestic order the reformers claimed to respect, how Muslim communities were to be drawn into institutions their leaders suspected, and how nationalists demanded the very education they denounced, seeking it and criticizing it in the same breath. The pattern generalizes the classroom's doubleness. At every level, the knowledge arrived as an instrument and was received as a problem to be renegotiated, and the renegotiation, not the instrument, is what the historical record preserves. A set play sat inside that traffic as a small, portable instance: prescribed from above, glossed by the system, and then argued with, translated, memorized for purposes the prescribers had not listed, by readers whose relationship to the book was never the simple one the Minute had budgeted for.

## Africa and the Caribbean: The Same Machinery, a Thinner File

Honesty about evidence requires this section to begin with a grading. For India, the record names colleges, teachers, dates and the editor of this play's own textbook. For Africa and the Caribbean, the surviving documentation is rich about the machinery and thinner about this play by title, and the article writes the two regions at the strength the record supports, without converting a general Shakespeare curriculum into an undocumented syllabus of Romeo and Juliet productions and prescriptions.

What is documented for Africa is the system's spine. Mission schooling carried English letters from the start: Fourah Bay's parent body, the Church Missionary Society, founded the college to train the teachers and clergy through whom English education would multiply itself, and the 1882 education ordinances in the Gold Coast and Lagos formalized state support for a missionary curriculum that glorified British literature. The college fed on its own society's grammar school at Freetown and drew its students regionally, from the Gambia, the Gold Coast and Nigeria as well as Sierra Leone, so that its Durham papers functioned as a West African standard and not merely a Sierra Leonean one. Its graduates include Sir Milton Margai, who took his Durham bachelor's degree at Fourah Bay in 1921 and became the first prime minister of independent Sierra Leone: the affiliated college had, by the plainest biographical route, educated the head of the government that ended the affiliation's world. In East Africa the pattern repeated a generation later, when Makerere, founded as a technical school in 1922, grew into a university college whose students were prepared for the external degrees of the University of London, carrying the same examination logic to Kampala. At the top of the system sat the identical Durham examinations at Fourah Bay and the Cambridge certificate in the secondary schools, with Shakespeare a compulsory author in the literature paper of the Southern Cameroons programme and the set-book culture general across the region's elite schools. The Gold Coast's flagship made the fusion explicit: Achimota School, opened under Governor Gordon Guggisberg in the 1920s with the educator James Aggrey among its founding leadership, was built to join African and Western learning in one elite institution on the English public-school pattern, houses, playing fields, examinations and all, and its alumni carried the blend into the Gold Coast's national politics, Kwame Nkrumah among them. Achimota is the adaptation debate institutionalized: the same government that circulated memoranda urging education fitted to African life endowed a school whose distinction lay in how completely it reproduced the metropolitan article. The set canon lived inside that contradiction undisturbed, because the certificate, not the memorandum, was what the parents were buying. South Africa's mission institutions belonged to the same world at one remove: Lovedale, founded by the Glasgow Missionary Society in the Eastern Cape in 1824, joined a famous printing press to its classrooms and made literary production, in English and in Xhosa, part of a mission education whose graduates carried print culture across the region. The play's title surfaces in the African record through the stage door rather than the syllabus: the translations and local stagings through which African theatre-makers seized it are the subject of [African Stagings and Adaptations](/2030/05/31/african-stagings-adaptations/), and this article claims for the classroom only what the classroom's documents prove, which is the machinery, the standard, and the remembered experience of students like Ngũgĩ and Avari, not a fabricated list of African set-book years for this title.

The Caribbean file has the same shape with one addition: the primer stage is documented in detail. Rampaul's research shows Shakespeare entering Caribbean schooling early and low, through the Royal Readers, the graded elementary readers of the Royal School Series used across the region after emancipation, which were, in her account, vehicles for cultivating British values, stocked with canonical poems, with historical passages celebrating British victories, and with Shakespearean extracts: Royal Reader 3 carried a story of Prince Hal, "The Prince and The Judge," drawn from Henry IV, and Royal Reader 5 printed a gathering of "Choice Quotations." Above the readers stood the elite secondary schools, Jamaica College in Kingston and Queen's Royal College in Port of Spain among them, giving a classical British education whose summit was the Cambridge examination, for which full plays were required. The structure beneath the readers deserves its own sentence, because it divided the region's children by design: Barrymore Bogues, describing the system Rampaul studies, distinguishes the rural missionary schools, which trained the children of the emancipated for agricultural labor, from the older urban schools, which trained an elite through the classical curriculum. The Royal Readers reached both tiers with the colonizer's excerpts; the full plays waited at the top of the ladder, where the few were certified. Shakespeare in the Caribbean classroom was thus not one experience but a staircase, extracts for the many, whole tragedies for the examined few, and the staircase was the policy. C. L. R. James, who entered Queen's Royal College in the years before the First World War, is the region's standing example of what that education formed: a colonial schoolboy saturated in the English canon who grew into one of the century's great anti-colonial intellects, his mind stocked by the very curriculum he would spend a career arguing with. His schoolmate generation's summit was the island scholarship, the colonial prize that sent the region's best examinee to Oxford or Cambridge, and its most consequential winner sat the same benches a little later: Eric Williams, who won Trinidad's island scholarship from Queen's Royal College in 1932, read history at Oxford, and returned to write Capitalism and Slavery and to lead Trinidad and Tobago into independence as its first prime minister. The examination staircase thus produced, at its top, the historian who indicted the economic foundations of the empire that had examined him. As evidence about the system's interior effects, the Caribbean record at its highest rung runs the same way as the Indian: the credential was real, the formation was real, and the loyalty the formation was supposed to purchase did not survive the graduate. The play itself is not named in the surviving Caribbean records this article relies on, and the gap is left visible, as the profile below grades it: in the Caribbean the documentary trail proves the canon, the readers and the examination, and grows thin exactly where a syllabus page naming Verona would settle the question.

The classroom's echo in the region's public culture is documented, and it belongs here as the encounter's outer ring. Rampaul's work traces how the Shakespeare quotation, installed by the schools as the certificate of eloquence, circulated in Trinidad's calypso as a public currency: to quote the set author aptly was to display the education the colony had made prestigious, and the same quotation culture, in her account, turned in time from display to mockery, the schoolroom's treasure spent on satire of the schoolroom's world. The trajectory is the Caribbean miniature of this article's whole argument. A canon installed as the measure of the educated became, by the ordinary operations of wit, a resource for judging the installers, and it did so in the vernacular art form least subject to any syllabus. What the examination certified, the calypso audited.

## Imposition or Appropriation? The Critics Adjudicated

Three named accounts divide the interpretation of this record, and the article's verdict requires choosing among them rather than blending them into mush. Viswanathan's is the strongest statement of imposition: English literary study was introduced as a mask of conquest, a surrogate religious instruction that governed by forming taste and conscience, and her evidence, from the 1820s curriculum to the students' self-describing examination essays, is archival and specific. Seth's account cuts across hers at the point of effect. The colonial archive, he shows, is full of the system's own doubts: the rote-learning panic, the moral-crisis literature, the fear that the product was mimicry without substance. A mask that its wearers kept reporting as ill-fitting was governing less completely than the design intended, and the failure is not incidental to the history; it is the history, since the anxious, divided graduate, Macaulay's unintended child, became the critic of the knowledge imposed on him. Seth closes Subject Lessons by naming the irony: the hope that "Macaulay's bastard children will have contributed to the critical appropriation of a knowledge that was once imposed upon them."

Suddhaseel Sen's Shakespeare in the World (2020) presses the objection further, from the receiving culture's side. Reading India's Shakespeare through hegemony and mimicry alone, Sen argues, leaves out the writers and audiences who took the plays up for their own purposes from the beginning. The chronology supports him uncomfortably early for a pure imposition thesis. Bengali writers were remaking the plays within a generation of Hindu College's founding: a Bengali version of The Merchant of Venice appeared in 1853, a version of this play, Charumukh Chitrahara Natak, in 1864, and Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar's prose Comedy of Errors, Bhrantibilas, in 1869, the work of the very class the colleges were forming, executed in their own language for their own readers. The Indian stage that grew from this print culture is another article's territory; what belongs here is the sequence. The classroom's first Bengali grandchildren were not clerks reciting the canon but authors raiding it. Gandhi's Hind Swaraj (1909) then supplied the political indictment of the whole educational transaction from within the class it had created, and by 1962 the imperial center was doubting its own instrument: the British Council's conference on English literature overseas, reported by John Ashmead, heard delegates from twenty-five countries question whether the literature could survive overseas at all, and recorded the ground-level absurdity that Indian high-school students, officially equipped with a few thousand English words, were expected to read Shakespeare, Lamb, Shelley and Keats.

The adjudication this article reaches is therefore layered, and each layer is assigned to the critic who proved it. On design and mechanism, Viswanathan wins outright: the documentary chain from the 1813 Act through the Minute to the examination hall shows a governing technology, and no honest reading of Macaulay can soften it. On effect inside the classroom, Seth wins: the system's own archive testifies that the subject it tried to manufacture would not hold still, and the anxiety literature is a better guide to outcomes than the Minute's confidence. On the sequel, Sen wins: appropriation was not a late, postcolonial correction but a contemporary fact, visible in Bengali print by 1864, and any account that begins the seizure of the play at independence is refuted by the dates. Mannan's publishing evidence binds the three together: even the textbook, the most controlled layer of the apparatus, was refused, adapted and outflanked in its intended market. Imposition is the true verdict on the policy. Appropriation is the true verdict on the history. The play's colonial career is the record of the second verdict overtaking the first.

The strongest counter-case should be stated before it is answered, because it is built from this article's own evidence. A defender of the imposition thesis at full strength can reply that the appropriations were themselves conducted inside the colonizer's categories: Bankim weighed Kalidasa in Shakespeare's scales, the Bengali remakings measured themselves against the English original, the nationalists demanded the examination system be opened rather than abolished, and even the counter-system of 1905 taught in the shadow of the credential it refused. On this reading, the mask governed most completely precisely where it appeared to be seized, since the seizure confirmed the canon's authority to be worth seizing. The reply fails on the evidence of direction. A category can be occupied and then vacated, and the record shows the traffic running one way: the scales Bankim borrowed were used to acquit Kalidasa, the examination the nationalists entered was used to staff a movement against the examiners, and the canon's authority, once made the prize, could be spent on purposes its installers abhorred. Occupation that ends in eviction of the landlord is not tenancy. It is the history this article has documented, at the pace of three generations.

The organized refusal arrived on schedule. Bengal's Swadeshi movement after 1905 built a national education movement in explicit counter-design to the colonial system, with its own schools and its own council, demanding Indian control of Indian education as a first article of self-rule; the Jadavpur institution that survives from that movement began as a college founded to teach outside the Raj's examining shadow. The refusal's existence completes the adjudication's evidence. A curriculum that had in fact formed its subjects as designed would not have required a counter-system within seventy years of the Minute; a curriculum that had failed entirely would not have been worth refusing. The colonial classroom had done something more dangerous than either: it had taught a generation to read closely, in English, and then watched that generation apply the close reading to the curriculum itself.

The profile below assembles the article's evidence in one place, grading every row for this play specifically, so that a reader can see at a glance where the documentation names Romeo and Juliet itself and where it proves the machinery that carried Shakespeare generally. The grades are the argument in shorthand. Rows graded direct rest on documents that name this play. Rows graded machinery rest on documents that prove the system, the canon or the examination, without naming this title. Rows graded encounter rest on student testimony. Keeping the grades honest is an archival discipline: the sources survive as scattered calendars, catalogues, commission reports and memoirs, and the working method behind the profile, filing each claim beside the evidence that supports it, is the discipline that a research tool such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/) exists to serve, since a claim separated from its grade is a claim waiting to be overstated.

Two cautions belong with the table. The first is survival bias: paper survives where institutions kept it, and the Indian archive, maintained by a paperwork state and mined by two generations of scholars, is incomparably richer than the records of a Cameroon mission school or a Trinidad elementary classroom, so the table's Indian density reflects the archive's habits as well as the history's weight. The second caution runs the other way. A thin file is not an empty classroom. The pupils who construed Shakespeare in schools whose papers burned, flooded or were never kept were taught the same canon under the same examinations as the pupils of Calcutta, and the grades mark the evidence, not the experience. The profile claims what it can prove and displays what it cannot, which is the most a reference table on this subject can honestly do.

A third caution is chronological. The rows span a century and a half, from Grant's pamphlet to the 1962 conference, and the machinery they describe changed its operators, its intentions and its justifications across that span: the mission wedge of 1830, the examining state of 1857, the adaptation memoranda of the 1920s and the doubting conference of 1962 are four regimes, not one. What persisted through all four was the set book itself, carried from regime to regime by the certificate's momentum, which is why the play's curriculum presence is best understood not as a single policy's lifespan but as an institution's. Policies were announced and reversed. The examination renewed the prescription annually, in silence, and it is the annual renewal, more than any founding document, that the profile's dates collectively record.

## The Verdict: An Instrument That Audited Its Owners

The play in colonial education was, as the brief charges, an instrument of empire: chosen by a system that needed prestige texts to form and certify an intermediary class, glossed by that system's own officers, examined under that system's papers from Calcutta to Freetown to Port of Spain, and remembered by its students as a gift they were expected to be grateful for. Every element of that sentence is documented. But the instrument had a property its administrators either missed or could not remove without removing the text itself. It ends with authority arraigning authority, with a prince informing two great houses that heaven has killed their joys with their hate, and with the guilt distributed upward, to the fathers, the feud, the city and the throne, "All are punish'd." A curriculum can prescribe a book; it cannot prescribe which speech a clever student will carry away. The colonial record shows the students carrying away the audit.

The sequel confirms the reading. The class the Minute designed produced the Minute's critics; the textbook market refused its primer while absorbing its play; Bengal was remaking the tragedy in its own language by 1864; and the postcolonial reassessment, from Viswanathan's archive to Seth's irony to Sen's recovery of the appropriators, is itself written in the idiom the classrooms taught, by scholars standing in the line of the examined. The modern Indian settlement of the play, which this series records in [Romeo and Juliet in the Indian Curriculum](/2030/03/22/romeo-and-juliet-indian-curriculum/), is unintelligible without this colonial prehistory, and the worldwide classroom that inherited the whole arrangement is surveyed in [The Play in Global Classrooms Today](/2030/06/28/play-in-global-classrooms/). The empire taught the play to teach obedience. The play, examined closely by the obedient, taught them where the guilt in Verona actually sat. That is the verdict the documents support: the instrument worked, and it worked, in the end, on its owners.

One boundary of the verdict should be drawn before the file closes. This article has judged the colonial career, not the play's whole educational life, and the distinction matters because the machine outlived the empire that built it. The examining universities, the set-book lists, the annotated editions and the certificate examinations did not vanish at independence; they were inherited, argued over and slowly rebuilt by the successor states, and the play's place inside them became a question each new nation settled for itself, in Nairobi and Delhi and Port of Spain, on evidence this article has graded as belonging to other inquiries. What the colonial record settles is narrower and, for that reason, firmer. A system was built to govern through a book. The book was this tragedy, among a small shelf of others, and its own closing judgment was on the governors. The system certified its students. The students, in the end, certified the book, and decertified the system. The paperwork survives to prove both halves.

What remains unproven should be named as plainly as what is proven, because the unproven residue is where the subject's future work lies. No surviving syllabus page has been produced in this inquiry naming this play for a specific African or Caribbean school year; no Indian examination paper on the play has been quoted verbatim here, because the argument did not need one and the standard this article set itself was to cite only what it had verified. Between Deighton's edition, which fixes the play in the Indian textbook trade beyond argument, and the memoir testimony, which fixes Shakespeare in colonial schoolrooms beyond nostalgia, stretches a middle distance of calendars, catalogues and board papers that future archival work can still narrow. The verdict does not depend on that narrowing. The machinery is documented, the edition is documented, the encounter is documented, and the sequel is documented in print from 1864 forward. On the evidence graded in the profile, the colonial classroom used Romeo and Juliet as its designers intended, and got, from the same book, the judgment its closing lines pronounce on the elders of Verona: the audit of the governors, by the governed, in the governors' own tongue. A syllabus is a promise about the future disguised as a list of books, and this syllabus kept its promise in the one way its makers never intended: it promised that those who mastered the book would judge, and they did.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the Charter Act of 1813 set aside money for in Indian education?

The Act set aside one lakh of rupees a year for the revival of literature and the promotion of knowledge among the inhabitants of British India, and in doing so it opened the argument that dominated Indian education for a generation. The clause was ambiguous enough to fund Oriental learning or English learning, and the Orientalist-Anglicist controversy of the following two decades was fought over its meaning. The Act also admitted Christian missionaries to India under license, while the Company's policy of religious non-interference barred open Christian teaching in state-supported classrooms. That combination, money for education, missionaries at the gate, doctrine barred from the desk, created the vacancy that English literature, with Shakespeare at its head, was installed to fill, as Gauri Viswanathan's Masks of Conquest (1989) demonstrated from the administrative record.

### Q: Why did Hindu College begin teaching English literature before the colonial state required it?

Because the demand came first from Calcutta's own Hindu elite, who founded the college in 1817 to give their sons the English learning that government employment and commerce increasingly rewarded. The colonial state did not invent the appetite; it inherited and then organized it. Hindu College was a collective Indian effort, supported by reformers in the circle of Rammohun Roy and by British sympathizers such as David Hare, and its lectures were delivered in English from 1826, nine years before Macaulay's Minute directed state funds toward English instruction. The college's reports list Milton and Shakespeare among the authors studied by 1827 and 1828. The sequence matters for judging the whole system: English literary education in India began as a transaction between ambitious Indian families and an available curriculum, and the imperial design of 1835 was imposed on a classroom that Indian initiative had already built. The demand side is the first corrective to any account in which the curriculum simply descends upon a passive city: Calcutta asked for the books before the state thought to weaponize them.

### Q: Who was Kenneth Deighton, and what did he do to this play?

Kenneth Deighton was a senior officer of Indian education, recorded in the Royal Literary Fund's archive as Principal of Agra Government College and a commissioner of the Indian Education Commission, who edited Shakespeare for the imperial textbook market. His Romeo and Juliet, published by Macmillan with his introduction and notes in 1893 and reprinted into the following decades, put the play into the hands of Indian students inside an apparatus written by the prescribing system itself. Deighton edited a shelf of the canon for Macmillan's English Classics and kindred series, construing the texts for the colonial classroom. His edition matters because it is the point where the play, the textbook trade and the education department meet in a single documented object: a tragedy annotated for Indian examinees by the principal of an Indian government college.

### Q: In what sense did Deighton's notes work as a primer on Englishness?

In the sense Joya Mannan establishes in "Publishing Shakespeare in India" (2023): the explanatory notes teach far more than the words mean. Mannan argues that Deighton's apparatus functions as a primer on Englishness for Indian readers, glossing the play's manners, households, affections and moral reflexes until the student has been tutored in what an English reader is expected to feel, judgment by judgment, at the level of the footnote. The text supplies Verona; the notes supply England. A student could pass an examination on the play while absorbing, note by note, a training in the sensibility of the ruling culture, which is why Mannan treats the editions as instruments of assimilation rather than neutral schoolbooks. Her further finding completes the picture: the Indian market, which already possessed its own Shakespeare culture, refused or remade the primer, and the assimilation the notes were built to perform failed on its own commercial ground.

### Q: What examinations did a student at Fourah Bay College sit in the 1880s?

A student at Fourah Bay College sat the University of Durham's own examinations, under Durham's standards, without leaving Freetown. The college, founded by the Church Missionary Society in 1827, affiliated to Durham in 1876, and the affiliation's terms were unusually strict: the same curriculum and examinations identical to those administered to Durham's students in England. The syllabus was the liberal-arts course of the period, Latin, Greek, Hebrew, Arabic, history, natural science, French and German, with English literary study traveling inside the Durham inheritance. The arrangement lasted until 1967 and drew students from across Anglophone West Africa. Its educational meaning is precise: the credential's worth in colonial employment depended on its identity with the English original, so the examiner in Durham governed the classroom in Sierra Leone more completely than any local syllabus could have done. Among the graduates the arrangement credentialed was Sir Milton Margai, who took his degree there in 1921 and led Sierra Leone to independence forty years later.

### Q: How did Shakespeare first enter Caribbean classrooms, and through which books?

He entered through graded elementary readers, principally the Royal Readers of the Royal School Series, before he arrived as whole plays. Giselle Rampaul's research on Shakespeare in Caribbean education shows the readers, used across the region's schools after emancipation, serving as vehicles for British values: canonical poems, historical passages celebrating British victories, and Shakespearean extracts, including a Prince Hal story, "The Prince and The Judge," in Royal Reader 3 and a gathering of "Choice Quotations" in Royal Reader 5. The child met the author first as moralized excerpts inside a primer of loyalty. Full plays came later and higher, at elite secondary schools such as Jamaica College and Queen's Royal College, where they were required for the Cambridge examinations that gated colonial careers. The sequence, extract in the elementary reader, whole play at the examination school, is the Caribbean form of the empire's general pattern.

### Q: What did Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o remember about the way Shakespeare was presented at his school?

He remembered the presentation as a lesson in gratitude. In Decolonising the Mind (1986), Ngũgĩ recalls that at his school in Kenya, Shakespeare's greatness was put before the pupils as "one more English gift to the world alongside the bible and the needle," a formula that placed the playwright in a catalogue of imperial benefactions for which the colonized were to give thanks. The sentence is the student encounter at its most economical: the play arrived wrapped in a story about the generosity of its givers, and studying it was framed as receiving a present. Ngũgĩ's career, as a writer who later renounced English for his fiction and campaigned against the colonial syllabus, is the remembered classroom's own refutation. The gift framing failed with the very student it describes, and his memoir preserves both the framing and its failure in a single recollection.

### Q: How did a schoolboy in colonial Mombasa describe the place of Shakespeare in his education?

As memorization, rewarded. The published biographical tribute to Burjor Jal Avari, who was schooled in Mombasa and sat the Cambridge Overseas School Certificate there in the 1950s, describes a formation built on recitation: multiplication tables learned by heart to forty, pages of the Oxford English Dictionary recited, and, in the tribute's phrase, "cramming line by line Shakespeare's immortal plays" as an ordinary part of an Asian schoolboy's discipline in colonial Kenya. The cramming worked in the system's own terms. Avari's certificate results won him a full British government scholarship to study in England in 1955, and he later returned to Kenya as a teacher and syllabus reformer. The memoir evidence matters because it records the encounter's texture, neither the Minute's theory nor the critic's verdict, but years of English lines carried in a child's memory, and the scholarship at the end of them.

### Q: What was the filtration theory behind educating a small English-taught class first?

Filtration was the doctrine that education should be poured into a narrow class at the top and allowed to filter downward. Macaulay's Minute states it without embarrassment: since the state could not attempt to educate the body of the people, it must form a class of interpreters, English in taste and intellect, and leave that class "to refine the vernacular dialects of the country" and render them fit vehicles for carrying knowledge to the mass of the population. The theory made a virtue of exclusion. A small, prestigious, English-medium sector, with Shakespeare among its certifying texts, would produce the intermediaries; the intermediaries would translate the knowledge downward; the state would pay only for the top. In practice the filtration was slow and leaky, the vernacular refinement happened fitfully, and the doctrine's real achievement was the one it stated first: a credentialed class, formed on English literature, set between the rulers and the ruled. The doctrine is the syllabus's economics stated as philosophy, and the prestige of the set play was one of its working instruments.

### Q: Why do the examination essays quoted by Viswanathan trouble her readers?

Because in them the system appears to succeed from the inside. Viswanathan quotes Indian students' examination essays in which the writers express gratitude for the improvement of what they call, borrowing their examiners' vocabulary, the "native mind and condition": young Indians describing their own people, in English, in a high-stakes examination, with the colonizer's word "native." The essays trouble readers because they cannot be dismissed as propaganda imposed from outside; they are the examined performing the identity the examination was designed to certify, in the setting where certification was decided. Viswanathan uses them as the intimate evidence for her title's argument, that literary study worked as a mask of conquest, governing most thoroughly where it appeared as education. Set against them, Sanjay Seth's evidence of official anxiety about rote learning and moral crisis reads as the necessary counterweight: the performance of gratitude and the formation of a grateful subject were not the same achievement.

### Q: What did the British Council's 1962 conference on English literature overseas conclude?

It concluded, in substance, that the imperial instrument was in trouble on its own terms. The conference at King's College, Cambridge, in July 1962, reported by John Ashmead, was the first gathering of its kind after more than a century of overseas literature teaching, and it heard delegates from twenty-five countries express doubts and dissatisfaction with the teaching of English literature abroad. Its commissions found school pupils in Afro-Asian countries officially equipped with a few thousand English words and expected, on that equipment, to read Shakespeare, Lamb, Shelley and Keats; they judged overseas literary research poor and the certificate in English literature a weak predictor of anything. The report's remedy was modernization, visual aids, new methods, a literature re-equipped to survive overseas. Read against the nineteenth-century record, the conference is the metropolitan center confessing, a century on, that the classroom empire of English letters had been built faster than it could be staffed.

### Q: What is the earliest evidence that Indian readers were remaking this play rather than merely receiving it?

A Bengali version of the tragedy, Charumukh Chitrahara Natak, published in 1864, less than fifty years after Hindu College opened its doors and within the lifetime of the first generations it taught. The date is the evidence. Remaking was not a postcolonial sequel to a passive colonial reception; it was contemporaneous with the reception itself, the work of the classroom's own graduates writing in their own language for their own public. The surrounding record thickens the point: a Bengali Merchant of Venice had appeared in 1853, and Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar's prose Comedy of Errors, Bhrantibilas, followed in 1869. Suddhaseel Sen's Shakespeare in the World (2020) builds his correction of the hegemony account on exactly this chronology, arguing that readers who chose the plays and refitted them to local forms were never merely the curriculum's products. The classroom supplied the text; Bengal supplied the sequel, in Bengali.

### Q: How did the Cambridge Overseas School Certificate govern classrooms it never visited?

By making the examination, not the syllabus, the effective curriculum of every school whose pupils needed the credential. Senior classes in colonial secondary schools were taught to the certificate's programme, set in England and marked to English standards, so a paper in Cambridge decided what was read in Buea, in Lusaka and in Port of Spain. The Southern Cameroons record states that the senior classes used the Cambridge programme with emphasis on English language and literature, Shakespeare among the compulsory authors of the paper officially styled "English literature"; Northern Rhodesia's final school examination was likewise set in England. A syllabus can be negotiated locally, annotated, evaded. An external examination cannot, because its value to the pupil is precisely that it is external. The certificate thus governed at a distance more completely than any inspectorate: schools policed themselves, and their pupils' life chances enforced the policing. An examination set abroad is a governor that never sleeps, never tours and never negotiates, and its papers reached classrooms no inspector ever entered.

### Q: What did the 1925 memorandum on education in British tropical Africa advise, and why did the set plays survive it?

The memorandum advised adaptation: education fitted to the life, languages and occupations of the communities served, in place of the bookish, examination-driven schooling the Phelps-Stokes commissions had criticized. The advice was official, considered and largely sincere, and in primary education it had real effects. The set plays survived because the memorandum addressed the bottom of the system while the certificate governed the top. Secondary schooling existed to deliver pupils to the Cambridge examinations and to institutions like Fourah Bay, and the market value of those credentials was their Englishness: an adapted certificate would have been a devalued one. For four decades the two policies coexisted, adaptation in the elementary school, Shakespeare in the senior forms, without the memorandum dislodging the examination. The survival is diagnostic. Where the system's purpose was certification, the literary standard was the product, and no policy of adaptation could be allowed to touch it.

### Q: Why was a tragedy about disobedient children so easy for a discipline-minded curriculum to prescribe?

Because its surface plot is a sermon against the exact offenses the curriculum existed to prevent. Verona opens under a ruler who prices street disorder at death, proceeds through a marriage negotiated between men while the bride is silent, punishes a secret wedding and a daughter's defiance with exile and death, and closes with the households reconciled under authority and the young offenders in the vault. A schoolmaster could point the moral without inventing it: disobedience ends in the tomb, and the fathers, not the children, stand reconciled at the end. The play's feudal household, its watch, its friar and its prince formed a complete little commonwealth of fathers and rulers in which every rebellion is fatal. That the same ending also arraigns the fathers is the complication the next question takes up; the prescription was made on the surface, and the surface was genuinely, usefully severe.

### Q: Which figures does the play's closing audit blame for the catastrophe: the children or their elders?

The elders, and the text says so in the ruler's own voice. The Prince's closing speech arraigns the feud, not the love: "See what a scourge is laid upon your hate" (5.3.291), addressed to Capulet and Montague, and its sentence falls universally, "All are punish'd" (5.3), on the fathers who kept the grudge, the households that fed it, and the Prince himself, who lost kinsmen to his own leniency. Romeo and Juliet are mourned as sacrifices, "poor sacrifices of our enmity," in the Prince's phrase, victims of the elders' war rather than criminals against it. For the colonial classroom this was the unteachable residue inside the set book. A lesson in obedience could be drawn from the children's deaths; the play's own verdict assigns the guilt upward, to the generation that governed Verona, and any student who read to the end received both documents in the same volume.

### Q: Why did learning the play by heart cut both ways in the colonial classroom?

Because memorization is both submission and possession, and the record shows it functioning as each. As submission, it was the rote the officials themselves feared: lines crammed for examinations, as Burjor Jal Avari's Mombasa schooling remembered them, Shakespeare recited line by line alongside the dictionary and the multiplication tables, knowledge performed for a certificate rather than possessed as thought, the anxiety at the center of Sanjay Seth's Subject Lessons. As possession, it was what Rangana Banerji's title celebrates, "Every College Student Knows By Heart": the text carried in memory for life, quotable in the courtroom, the newspaper and the nationalist pamphlet, beyond any examiner's control. The same act of memory certified the student to the system and armed the student against it. Which of the two it was depended on the graduate, and the system's misfortune was that it could not set an examination to distinguish them.

### Q: Did the colonial system prescribe this play everywhere at once, or is the record uneven?

The record is uneven, and pretending otherwise would falsify it. It is strongest for India, where the documents name the college, the curriculum, the examination system and the editor of this play's own school edition. For Africa it proves the machinery in detail, Durham's identical examinations at Fourah Bay, Shakespeare compulsory in the Cameroon literature paper, the certificate culture of the elite schools, while naming this title in the classroom rarely; the play's African trail runs chiefly through translation and the stage. For the Caribbean it documents the primers and the examination schools and the canon they carried, and grows thin at the level of the individual syllabus page. The InsightCrunch colonial-education profile grades every row accordingly, direct where a document names the play, machinery where it proves the system. An honest map of empire-wide prescription is a map of strong Indian evidence, strong systemic evidence elsewhere, and named gaps, and the gaps are part of the finding. Prescription followed the examination map rather than the imperial map, and the two maps were never the same shape.
